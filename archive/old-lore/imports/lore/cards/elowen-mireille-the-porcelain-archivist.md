## Lore · The Life Behind the Card

*Written 2026-09-24 by the character-lore pass from this card and the record of The Withering courts. What those sources do not state is new here.*

### Origin

Elowen Mireille was born into the house of Mireille, a minor noble house of the western quarter whose people were archivists. The house kept the papers of greater houses in a town of pale stone. In the Withering Era those papers were supply contracts written at the old rates, and a contract of that kind had become an inheritance. Stewards came from the great houses to consult them. While the clerks went down for the boxes, the stewards were given tea.

Her mother kept the tea. Elowen carried the tray as soon as she could hold it level. She learned to set each cup where the last had stood and to pour from the same height, so that nothing at the table changed but the talk. The stewards talked to the girl with the tray. They told her things they kept from the clerks. She wrote none of it down. When her mother asked, she gave it back in the order it had been said.

The house owned one heirloom of note, a brooch cut as a rose. It came down to her. She has worn the Rose Brooch of Mireille ever since.

### The Making

The Ivory Ledger Society took her from that table. It placed her in households among the noble and merchant circles of the west. She went where it sent her. In each new house she tied on a black silk apron and poured from her own service, the same cup and the same pour every afternoon, made identical on purpose. Guests at her table said more than they meant to. She asked them nothing. The houses called her the Quiet Teacup and Lady Sugarveil.

Something she overheard once was larger than she was built to hold, and she held it anyway. The force behind it was one she had no business surviving. It did not break her. She came out of it forgetting nothing. Every conversation she marked afterwards came back to her whole, with the temper it was spoken in.

### The Cost

The Society placed her in the household of a Greaves tin house before its head died. Through the winter she poured every afternoon in the widow's parlour. Marceline Vireaux sat with the widow and the heirs through the weeks after the burial. One afternoon the heirs spoke a promise their father had made, years before, to a house of the Sable Rose Consortium. Elowen marked it.

Cyranthia Valez called once, to condole. While she sat in the parlour, the widow and the chief creditor agreed terms aloud. Elowen marked that afternoon too. She could read the heirs, the widow and the creditor, down to what their shoulders did when a sum was named. In everything Cyranthia said she found nothing out of true. Under Marceline's mourning hat she could read nothing at all, from the first week to the last.

In the spring the house's credit failed inside a week. The creditors took the shafts and the pumps; the supply book was already bound to the Consortium. She poured through all of it. She has spent neither afternoon.

The households trust the girl with the teapot. She keeps what they give her. A truth she lets out can be let out once, and never again. She can release only what she has heard herself, marked and kept. The few she has spent surfaced in public, at other people's tables, and each time the room reached its own conclusion. Nobody could say she had accused anyone. A tin house fell around her cup, and she let out nothing.

She has put her trade in her own words: "People reveal themselves when they feel safe. I make them very safe."

### Where They Stand

Elowen serves the Ivory Ledger Society. She is embedded in its households under the Society's direction, and noble and merchant circles across several households are under some form of her standing observation. She wears the brooch and the apron and pours from the same service.

Her latest table was in a merchant household of the slate belts, where the caravans of the Quiet Route halt. A caravan guest sat at it with a gold-clasped tome. The guest's posture showed she was listening harder than she spoke, and when the table talked freely, the guest said less. Seri Zeliro-Vanthe was the first person Elowen had poured for who never said the thing where she was sitting. Elowen marked the afternoon anyway.

She has not forgotten a single word of what she overheard, and she has told no one which conversation it was. Nobody knows how many stored conversations she holds that she has decided never to spend. The courts call her the girl who always knows. Nobody has ever proven where the knowing comes from.

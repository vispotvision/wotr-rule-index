## Lore · The Life Behind the Card

*Written 2026-09-24 by the character-lore pass from this card, the archived scene it appears in, and the record of Old World roads and chains. What those sources do not state is new here.*

### Origin

Torven Greis was born on the Inner World frontier, the second generation of a Concord family that had come off the Old World's western coast in the late Long Reckoning. Passage from that coast put in at Chainward Landing. Inland of it, the frontier roads were cart tracks, and the forest grew back over any stretch nobody kept open.

The frontier lived on what moved along those tracks. Iron came down from the Ring smelters, and salt went back the other way. Salt was money and food at once. A stamped bar settled a debt anywhere in the northern quarter, and in a bad winter a family could eat it.

He went onto the roads young. At seventeen he was a carter's runner on a supply convoy taking structural iron south from the Ring smelters to the port. He learned which stretches of track the forest took back first.

### The Making

The convoy came to a gorge in the thaw, with the river under it running high. The ward on the bridge footing had thinned past holding, and nobody had paid for the inspection. The bridge went out under the lead wagon. Fourteen tons of smelted iron went into the water.

The convoy master had been running the route on credit extended by a Nalūn factor. The loss ruined him. Torven watched a man who owned a hundred wagons become a man who owned nothing, because of a ward that cost six meals to maintain. He understood two things at once: the road was a more dangerous creditor than any bank, and whoever owned the places where the road was safe owned the road itself.

He bought his first way-station with borrowed salt before he was nineteen.

For the first fifteen years he loaded his own wagons. A wagon tongue caught him in his twenties and left a crook near the bridge of his nose. He threw two punches in his adult life, both at men stealing from his wagons, and connected once. The fighting he hired.

In those loading years he took on a young Hobgoblin chain-fighter out of the Expanse to ride escort on an iron run. The fighter gave a name and no island. At a ford in the melt the river rose while the lead wagon was in it, and the wagon began to go. The Hobgoblin threw a weighted chain round its tongue and planted on the bank. He held it against the water while the teamsters cut the team free and dragged the load back up. No iron went into the river. Next morning Torven walked the ford. He paid the contract as written, entered the fighter's name against the run, and wrote the ford into his supply book as one that goes out in the melt. He walked that crossing every spring after, and no Greis wagon crossed it in the melt again.

He took Accord certification because rank is collateral, and a man running supply contracts across three jurisdictions cannot borrow without a token. The Measurewright who certified him noted in the margin that the instrument was functioning correctly. A sealed contract told him more. He could feel whether a Nalūn instrument would hold: a sound one felt settled, and one whose terms were drifting felt loose. The chainlords noticed it in him first. He called it experience. On that feeling alone he walked away from four deals, and all four counterparties defaulted within the year.

For years he talked too much in a negotiation, filling every pause his counterparties left. Later he left the pauses and let the other side fill them. The factors at Ironlink Hold came to trust the Greis supply book as they trusted few instruments made outside Nalūn. In twenty-seven years of contracted supply he never missed a delivery date.

### The Cost

Dalla Greis ran the Chainward Landing office while he ran the roads. She died of a fever. The ward on her well-stone should have caught it. Within the year he rebuilt the ward system of every way-station on his network, and the spending nearly broke the house. The Nalūn factors saw it and extended his credit line without being asked. From then on his stations were kept to three times the standard the Accord requires. He did not speak of her.

He never stopped expecting the next bridge to fail. He inspected his own way-stations, reviewed his own supply books and walked his own routes. When he found a flaw in something he had built, he fixed it with his own hands and trained nobody to find the next one. The house grew to forty-seven employees and one man who believed he had to see every load arrive. The chainlords trusted their system because the Wellsprings enforced it. Torven trusted no system he could not walk.

Their son Edsen wanted nothing of the supply routes and went as apprentice to a shipwright at the Landing. Torven did not discuss it. He did not discourage it either. What he meant to leave was roads that stayed warded and stations that stayed staffed, a network that would run whether or not he was alive to walk it.

Three times he declined a buyout. The third time, he told a Nalūn factor: "A man who owns the wards on the road is rich until he dies, and if he dies, the road falls apart, and then nobody is rich. That is the whole of the business."

In the years the eastern corridors stood partly closed, the mountain settlements went months without salt, iron stock or medicine. Alkia of the Thornridge settlements came to him then, to speak about roads, and wards, and what a man with wagons owes to the people who depend on them.

### Where They Stand

Nalūn sealed at Kharven-Seat in the sixth year of the empire. The instrument conceded one thing: the Iron-to-Salt Corridor, its way-stations, its licensing, and the ground the rail ran on. The Greis house had held that road on the strength of knowing which fords go out in the melt and which ward-posts have a tender who drinks. Torven Greis lost eleven way-stations in a morning and was paid for every one of them. Edward Lambert valued them himself, above market. The empire bought the stations in a single instrument, and it turned nine of them into rail halts and two into nothing at all.

He walked away richer than his father had died, with no trade left in his hands. He took the money into the thing the wire had made, and the Greis name is on the northern line's freight contracts. He has never once described the transaction as a purchase. He calls it "the morning he was paid to stop existing."

He still dresses for the road, in the wolf-pelt papakha and a scarf wound high over the jaw. The seal-token of his first contract is clipped to his belt. When a deal turns, he goes still and the scarf comes up. Edsen is at the Landing. He does not speak of Dalla.

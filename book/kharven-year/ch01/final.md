# The Week After

*Kharven-Seat, the lower hall and the passage behind it. Seven days after the signing. Ice-out.*

The instrument lay where it had been signed, and nobody had moved it, because nobody had decided where it went.

Seven days on the boards. The vellum had taken the hall's damp and wanted to curl at the corners, and somebody, Lambert, had set a river stone on each corner to hold it flat, and the stones had left four grey rings that were now part of the document for as long as the document lasted. The seal was face up. The last name on it had gone from black to the brown that ink goes when it has stopped being wet and started being old. His own. He could read it upside down from the head of the table, and did, each time the lamp leaned, without meaning to.

The roof gave water. The whole north slope of the lower hall let go at Ice-out, then the south, and the sound of it running down the outside of the walls into the yard had been going since before light and would go until dark. Under that, the carts. The death-house at the Seat had been opened on the fourth day after the signing, when a pick went into the upper burying ground to the depth of a forearm, and it was being emptied a body a morning in the order the bodies had gone in, and the families came down for their own and carried them up the hill themselves. The Waiting was over for the winter's dead. A man in the yard said *left, left, your left* to another man on the far end of a board, and a wheel went through the ruts, and the water came down.

Sodoku Moto had been awake since the second hour.

He had taken the head of the table standing, as he had at the signing, with the grain returns of the southern reach spread in front of him, forty leaves, and his hands flat on the boards either side of them. He had read the third column three times. He had the figure. He was going to read it a fourth time because the figure had left him between the foot of the column and the top of it, and that had happened twice already this morning, and he had not counted the mornings on which it had happened before this one. His hands stayed flat. He had begun keeping them flat in the winter and had not asked himself why, and was not going to ask this morning either.

The shutters were still up against the thaw wind, so the hall was lit by blubber lamps at an hour when it should have had the sky, and the smell was the lamps and wet wool and the ink Lambert's clerks had been grinding since dawn, and under all of it the stone, which in this building had never once been warm all the way through.

Edward Lambert sat at his right. Gloves on, collar done up to the throat, hair cut close to the skull and thinning at the crown, the wax tablet he no longer wrote on lying face down beside his hand with the stylus laid across it exactly parallel to the table's edge. He had read three thousand and eleven names aloud in this hall before the seal went on. His voice had not given out and he had not delegated one.

Yoko Mishiro sat on the bench against the wall behind Sodoku's left shoulder, where she had sat, or stood, or crouched, for nine years, whenever there had been a wall. Her ears were up. Her tail lay along the bench and did not move. She had her record on her knee, the plain book and the stub of lead, and she had not opened it, and she had not written anything at this table in the seven days since the night she had written four lines in it after the seal went on and shut it and put it inside her coat.

He had seen her write them. He had not seen them.

Tabitha Hallenfeld sat halfway down the left side with her coat still on. Broad through the shoulders and thick through the waist, sun-dark and wind-rough in the face, crow's feet, hair brown with grey going through it and pulled back hard under two wooden pins, and the heavy wool coat road-stained to the knee and belted with the strap that carried her knife and the cylinder of route manifests she went nowhere without. She had come in from the Hallenfeld junction on the sixth day. She had missed the signing because she had been on a road when it happened, and the roads did not pause for signings, and she had said so at the door in those words and had not apologised.

At the foot of the table, in a chair with arms that was too big for him and that nobody had offered to change, sat Miku Tenrai Moto. Fourteen. Long in the bone the way the Tenrai went long, all wrist and neck, hair cut short at the nape the way the household had cut it and nobody at the Seat had thought to cut differently, a face that had not yet settled on what it was going to be. He wore a hide-coat somebody had made down to his size and had made well. His hands were in his lap, one on top of the other. He held that chair by right of a forfeiture he had made at nine years old in the mud of the Brine crossing, and he had been told on the first morning of the table that he could leave it whenever he pleased, and he had not left it once in seven mornings, and he was not looking at the instrument.

He was looking at the king's hands.

Sodoku had noticed it on the second morning and settled it on the third. The boy watched his hands because at the Brine crossing he had watched what they did, and a boy who has seen that watches the hands for the rest of his life. It did not need answering. He kept them flat.

The factor of the Council of Links had been at the Seat since before the signing. He had asked for nothing in seven days, and asked now.

He was a narrow man of fifty with the pallor of a life lived under lamps and rock, hair gone the grey of old salt and cut close, and a soft unweathered face on which the wind had never once been given the chance to work. Under the hide-coat somebody had lent him, which did not meet across the chest, he wore a coat of good black cloth cut for a place with no weather. His hands were clean. The ink was on the scribe standing behind his chair, a boy of twenty with a slope of board on a strap and the first two fingers of his right hand black to the second knuckle. Round the factor's wrist on a cord hung an iron disc the size of a thumbnail. He turned it with his thumb, under the table's edge, without looking at it, and had been turning it for seven days.

"The Council of Links asks, with respect to the seal and to the hand that closed it, that a fair copy of the instrument be made." He had the voice of a man reading a price off a board, courteous, with nothing in it that could be bargained down. "For Vaultmere. For entry, my lord. Entry. I am asked to say the word twice, because the Council is aware that entry and sealing are two questions and it does not wish to be understood as having asked the second."

The lamp leaned. He waited for it to straighten.

"Once the chain-cities have entered a thing at Vaultmere, my lord, they do not get to forget it. Nobody does. The Council would like to be in that position with regard to this one."

Seven days. Sodoku had said to Lambert in the winter, over this table, that when the thing was tested it would be tested from outside, and that the salt would do it, because the salt was the one signatory that could turn a promise into a working. Here was the salt's factor with his thumb on a disc asking for a copy. *There it is.* He did not move his hands.

"It has lain seven days under four stones," Lambert said, to the table generally. "It needs entering somewhere. There are three places it can go, and one of them is a pillar in this house with a nail in it, and I would like it understood that I have an opinion on the order of the three and have not been asked for it."

The clerk of the Bench had arrived on the third day with a case and a letter and had been given a chair. She waited for Lambert to finish, as a clerk waits, and not one breath past it.

A woman of forty in the grey of the Division that had lent her to the Bench, hair the brown of a wet plank and scraped back off a high forehead, a long jaw, a document case of black leather across her knees with the three chairs' marks stamped into the flap and the leather gone white where the flap rubbed. Ink in the creases of both thumbs. A voice that had not once, in four days, risen above the level of the water coming off the roof.

"The Bench is told that Mistress Mishiro wrote in her own record on the night of the signing, after the seal," she said. "Four lines. The Bench asks to see them."

He did not turn. Lambert did not look past him at the bench. The clerk had, when she asked, and was looking at her tablet now. The boy's eyes had not left his hands.

"The Bench may not have them," Yoko said.

Behind him the bench gave no sound. Not a page.

The clerk wrote on her tablet. Four words, perhaps five, at the speed of a woman who had written the same words before in other halls. "The Bench will note that it asked," she said, and closed the tablet, and the stylus went back into its loop.

Sodoku said nothing. It was his hall and the Bench had been refused in it. The water came down the walls.

"Make the copy," he said.

Lambert's hand moved toward the tablet and stopped. "I will make it. It is four leaves. The clause on the children of the defeated lines runs to a page and a half. I am not giving a page and a half of that to a clerk with a cold. It will be the evening before it is"

"Hallenfeld carries it."

Tabitha's hand went to the cylinder at her belt before her face did anything.

"Vaultmere," she said.

"Vaultmere."

She looked at the boards a moment and then at him, and gave the road the way she gave every road, as a count. "South road's under a foot of water at the fourth ford and stays there till the grass comes. So. Ironwood March. The Hallenfeld junction. Rimward in nine days if the Cuttings are open, and nobody has told me the Cuttings are open, so call it twelve to the counting city and I will send you the true number from the junction." She stopped. "I have never been below ground."

He knew where the four houses sat. Nalūn had fourteen and Lambert had bought through four of them, and he had known the four names since before the first cart of grain came north, and the counting city was where the four kept their day-books, and he did not say any of that. He said the next thing to Lambert without turning his head toward Lambert.

"The road is hers. The days on it." A leaf of the returns had come loose under his hand and he put it back. "Where she stops. Who she asks. Nobody at this table writes her a list."

Lambert's hands went behind his back. He had done it in the seventh month, in this hall, in front of the whole assembly, when the same woman had asked him a question. He did it now in the same order, the right hand first and the left closing over it, and stood with his face doing nothing.

"Then the copy is dry by the evening bell," Lambert said, "and she leaves when it is dry, and I will enter it that the Hallenfelds arrive late to everything and that on this occasion the copy kept her waiting."

"The copy can take its time," Tabitha said. "The road won't."

At the edge of his eye, on the bench, Yoko's nose moved. Once, toward Lambert, and back.

Under the table's edge, the factor's thumb had stopped. The disc hung still on its cord. Sodoku saw it stop and did not know what he had seen, and put it beside the figure he had lost, and went back to the column.

The boy had not moved. He had watched the hands through the factor and the clerk and the road, and his own were still one on top of the other in his lap, and the Bench's clerk looked at him once, the way clerks look at a thing they have not been given a form for, and looked away.

---

The table broke at noon and the hall emptied. The factor and his scribe went to the rooms they had been given, the clerk to hers with the case under her arm, Tabitha to the stable to look at a horse's near fore that she said she did not like. Lambert went into the side room with the instrument's four leaves and the four river stones and a clerk to grind ink, and left the door open. Through it for the rest of the day came the sound of a man writing slowly.

Sodoku stayed at the head of the table on his feet with the returns.

He read the third column a fourth time and had it and wrote it in the margin so he would not need a fifth, and went on to the fourth column, and the shutters came down at the fifth hour and the hall got the sky for what was left of the afternoon, grey and low and running. Yoko did not leave the bench. The boy did not leave the chair. Twice a clerk came to the door of the side room with a question about a word and twice Lambert answered without looking up, and at some point in the afternoon somebody put a bowl of broth at Sodoku's elbow and at some later point somebody took it away.

The carts went on in the yard. Two in the afternoon, then one, then the yard was quiet and only the water came down.

The Seat's death-house had had a keeper for six hundred years and had not had one for five. The keeper laid out the body and opened the window and kept the three days and said the words at the door when the family came, and the words were the keeper's and were not written anywhere, and the last keeper had died in the collapse with nobody trained under him. The words went with him. What the families said now at the door was whatever they could remember of having heard it once, and it was different in every house, and nobody in the Seat could tell you which house had it right. Ada Foss had kept her husband three days in a room ten feet by twelve and had said something at the door. Lambert had asked her what, and had said so at this table. She had told him it was not his.

That was the winter's dead, carried up a hill by their own people, and the hill had four stones on it, and Sodoku Moto did not look at the hill and did not look at the door and read the fourth column.

---

The passage behind the hall was the one place in the Seat he walked without somebody at his elbow, twice in a day, from the hall to the stair and back. Brida Ashwell had been standing in it some evenings since the winter, and was standing in it now.

It smelled of blubber lamp and old stone, the cold-stone smell of a building that had never been warm through, and of the tallow in the sconce that nobody had scraped since the Thin Weeks, and under that, tonight, of wet wood forced, the sour smoke that gets into a coat and stays. The frost-grit was gone off the flags. At Ice-out the stone sweated instead, and his boots found the wet and he shortened his step for it. Behind the altar-room door the night-stone ticked as it cooled.

She was leaning her whole side on the stick. Short and dense through the body, russet hair pinned at the base of the skull with the two bone pins, the satchel with its buckles re-set so many times the leather round the hardware had gone soft and shapeless, and the flush across her cheeks and nose that had been weather once and had been something else for longer than the stick. In the lamp it had grey at its edges. Her eyes went over him before they came up to his face, the way they went over everybody.

"I am not going to ask how you are."

"Good."

"I am going to tell you." She shifted the stick a hand's width and put her weight back on it. "A man who has not slept a full night in a year is spending a thing he cannot see the bottom of. It is the last third of the night. No. It is what a body puts by in the last third of the night, that is what I mean, the part that does the mending, and you have not had it since the ninth hour, and a body will let you take that from it for a long while because a body is generous and it is stupid." She stopped. "Then it stops being generous."

He stood with the returns under his arm.

"The hands go first. You have that. You are keeping them flat on the boards so nobody sees it, and I have seen it, and so has the boy, and I would put money on the fox. You walk this passage on the outsides of your feet now. You did not in the autumn." Her free hand went up, two fingers, toward his throat, and stopped a span short of it, and came down. "Then the eyes stop holding a column. You have that. Then one morning you stand up and the floor is a hand's width from where you left it, and you have not got that yet, and you will." The water ran down the outside of the wall behind her. "It comes due in the Thin Weeks. It always comes due in the Thin Weeks. Everything in this country does."

He said nothing.

"You stood through the signing. Greymane stood through it, and that is his hip and his business, and I have said my piece to him and he has told me where to put it. You have a chair at the head of that table. You have not sat in it in seven mornings." She looked at the returns under his arm and at his hand on them. "When did you last sit down. Not the chair. Down."

"How's your stack?"

Something went across her face and went off it.

"Full, and dry to the bottom."

"Good," he said, and went past her toward the hall and the returns, and heard the stick take her weight again behind him, twice, and then not.

---

The hall had the lamps lit again. Lambert was at the side table with four leaves in front of him and a sander in his gloved hand, and the original lay on the boards under its four stones where it had lain for seven days, and beside it Tabitha's cylinder stood open and empty with the manifests pulled out of it and stacked on the table in the order of the roads. She had her coat belted. She had the two wooden pins out and was putting them back in, tighter.

"Dry," Lambert said, and rolled the four leaves on the table with the heels of his hands, and slid the roll into the cylinder, and capped it, and held it out.

Tabitha took it and put it inside the coat against her body, where the roads were kept, and did up the belt over it.

"Twelve," she said. "I will send the true number from the junction."

The door let the thaw wind in and the sound of the water, and shut on both.

Sodoku went to the head of the table and put the returns down and put his hands flat on the boards either side of them, and found the fourth column, and read it. Yoko was on her bench with the book on her knee. At the foot of the table the boy was in his chair with his hands one on top of the other in his lap, and had been there since the second hour, and was not looking at the king's face.

He was looking at the hands.

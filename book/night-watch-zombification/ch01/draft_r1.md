# The Docket Head

*Cutler Row: the Night Register's back room, then the day desk. The same night, from the fourth division to the sixth.*

She laid the pen across the top of the form, square to its edge, and took her hand away.

The scullery still smelled of vinegar from the wash-tray, and under the vinegar the sourness from the drain, where the gutter water running through the grating had not yet carried off what he had left in the sink. The bench glass was cold under the heel of his hand. Rain ticked on the window in no rhythm at all. The three lamps hissed at three different pitches, and above them and below them the building had no hum in it anywhere.

Wystan turned the form round with one finger and read the head of the docket.

She had written it in the lineage-hall hand, each stroke laid down in the order some master had once stood behind her chair and made her lay it, and the ink had gone into the Division's paper clean. Surname, generation character, given name, courtesy name. All four were there. He read them once forward and once backward, which was how he checked any name he might one day have to swear to, and blotted them with the corner of the pad, and did not say any of them out loud.

"That will do."

"It is the only hand I have."

"It is a good hand. Nobody will be able to say you did not know what you were signing."

Her mouth held. She had folded her arms again, the right hand tucked under the left elbow, and the left thumb was going round the outer band of the bracelet where the proof-mark had been struck badly.

He wrote *assay of record* under her name, and under that his own, *W. Ashmore, Warden of Duty, for the Night Register*, and the division. The pen wanted to shake. He would not let it, on a docket. He set the heel of his wrist on the bench edge and wrote the last word from the fingers alone, small, and it came out legible.

The gloves lay across the day book where he had squared them before he touched the wedge. He put them back on. The left went on wrong the first time, two fingers crowded into one sleeve of the leather, and he pulled it off and did it again without looking up.

The fresh tins lived under the sink in a lidded crate packed with straw, so that nothing would knock the anchoring out of true before issue. He took one out and turned it under the third lamp. Pale brass, unmarked, the lid ground to the body so close that it seated with a small breath of air going out of the join. A Division sealwright he had never met had laid Fixatio into the metal. It did the one thing it did. What went into the tin stayed as it went in.

He looked at the pale band on the wedge's crown face.

It stood clear of the older tissue exactly as it had when he first lifted the lid. A quarter inch, by his eye. He did not measure it again. The number was already in ink in the margin, *crown +¼ in sealed, 11d*, and the same eye an hour later would only give him the same number with less sleep behind it. Something in the tin had gone on running under a seal that was sold to the courts as a promise that nothing would. He had written that down too, in fewer words.

"You are not going to touch it," Yinzhi said.

"I am going to move it with the forceps, gloved, from one tin to the other, and then I am going to put wax on it." He picked up the forceps. "If you still want to stand in front of the bench, this is where it would help."

She stayed where she was. He had not expected otherwise.

He took the wedge at the cut end, where it was fibrous and firm, and lifted it off the glass. It weighed almost nothing. He carried it across the gap between the two tins at the height of the bench, never over his own hand, and lowered it onto the floor of the fresh tin without letting the soft face brush the sides. He opened the forceps. He drew them out straight. The lid went on, and the breath went out of the join.

The chapter's tin sat empty beside it with a crumb of broken seal still stuck to the lip. He did not wash it. He stood it upright at the far end of the sill, away from the wash-tray, where nobody could put it in the vinegar by mistake.

The Register's wax came out of the forms drawer: brass wax, a dull yellow stick the Division issued to its officers and to nobody else. He softened the end in the lamp and drew it round the join in one pass. Resin came up off it, and under the resin a metal smell he had lived with for eleven years and still could not have named. Then he took his brass out of his waistcoat.

It was the size of the ball of his thumb and worn smooth at the rim. The Division's device on one face. On the other, in letters too small for anybody to read who had not been told they were there, the title nobody in the districts used.

He struck it into the wax across the join.

A clean strike wanted a still hand for a slow count of five, and he did not have one. He set his elbow on the bench and laid his left hand over the back of his right and pressed, and the tremor went down into the bench instead of into the seal. When he lifted the brass, the device stood in the wax sharp to its edges.

"There," Yinzhi said. It was the word she had used over the coil.

"There." He turned the tin a quarter turn so that the lamp raked the seal, and went round it once with his eye. "From this division it is not the Society's. If the chapter wants it back they may ask the Division in writing, and the Division will say no."

"They will not want it back."

"They will want it back the day it is worth something." He opened the day book at a clean page, ruled off under the night's last entry, and wrote.

> *Register file, Ferriby. Exhibit one. Wedge of crown mass, one hind, North Ferriby cut. Cut in the cut, sealed there by the assay under chapter wax, the 9th. Chapter's Fixatio tin found whole. Chapter seal broken this night at Cutler Row by the assay, in the officer's presence. Contents moved by forceps, gloved, to a Division tin; brass wax; struck. Assay of record as at the docket head. On opening: crown +¼ in sealed, see margin.*

She had come round to his shoulder to read it. He let her.

"You have put down that it grew."

"What I saw."

"Brack will say the tin was bad, or that I..." She stopped, and started again lower. "He will say I sealed it wrong, standing in the rain."

"Brack will not read that far." He blotted the page. "I am writing it for whoever does."

The wax was still in the front room.

He took the second lamp by its base and went through, and she came after him with her hands in her coat pockets. The front room was as they had left it: the insert face down on the blotter, the chair shoved back, the four pins in the plaster above the desk where the Marches sketch had hung. Two pieces of the chapter's seal lay on the blotter by the inkwell, where she had set the tin down to break it. The other two were under the desk. One lay face up on the boards, with half the Timberline device in it.

He went down on one knee, which his knees disputed. He lifted each piece with the forceps onto a sheet of clean paper and carried the paper back through at the height of his chest, both hands flat beneath it, and left the lamp for her to bring.

On the bench he laid the four pieces out and nudged them together with the forceps until the broken faces met. They met well enough. The chapter device closed across two of them with a hairline through its middle, and the groove the join had pressed into the underside ran along all four in one straight line.

"You said you would want them later," Yinzhi said.

"Custody." He folded the paper the druggist's way, the long sides in and then the ends under, so that nothing inside could slide out whichever way it was held. "The day somebody wants to say this tin was opened on the road, I want four pieces of chapter wax that fit a join, and a paper round them dated before anybody asked."

He wrote on the outside, *Chapter wax, Timberline. Broken by the assay at Cutler Row in the officer's presence. Four pieces.* Then the day and the division in full, in the sloped hand the day clerks complained about. He laid the fold on the sill beside the tin and squared it to the edge of the stone.

His mouth still tasted of bile and vinegar. Under both, faintly, it tasted of green.

A spirit kettle stood at the end of the sink, and above it on the shelf a tin of grounding tea, the coarse black leaf the medical house gave to anybody who had sat too long near an Echo. He kept it for the same reason they did. He filled the kettle from the jug and lit the spirit and stood with his back to the bench while it came up, and neither of them said anything. The gutter ran. A cart went by somewhere past the river with its tailboard loose, and a dog began to bark at it and thought better of it.

He made two cups. He gave her one and took the other to the window, and he held it there by the rim, the thumb on one side and two fingers on the other, so that none of the heat came near his palm. The black glass gave him back a tall, narrow man with his hair tied at the nape and hanging to the waist, dark red where the lamp caught it, and a white line through the left eyebrow. He looked past all of that at the rain.

Yinzhi held hers in both hands and put her face in the steam.

He drank. The tea tasted of tea, black and bitter and too hot, and under it the green was still there. It was the same green that had come up off the wedge when he lifted the lid. He had rinsed his mouth twice at the sink. He rinsed it a third time with the tea and swallowed, and the taste sat at the back of his tongue exactly where it had been, no stronger and no weaker.

He had nothing to put against that. He did not write it down.

"Your bracelet," he said.

She looked at her wrist.

"Is it still pulling?"

She took a moment over it. The thumb had found the badly struck mark again and was working at it, back and forth. "No," she said. "Not since the sink. It went quiet when you were at the sink."

"Good."

He finished the tea and set the cup in the sink by its rim, and it did not rattle.

The letter came next. He had meant it for a wire.

He stood at the bench with the pen and counted it out on the fingers of his left hand. *Tally. Keep walking. Measure everything. Who set marker. Register.* Nine words. The wire took nine, and would have it at the Timberline chapter by noon, and the chapter's secretary would read it out to whoever happened to be at the bench, and Tally would get it third-hand with the words in some other order. Nine words would buy him a man walking and measuring. They would not buy him the same man, measuring in the same way, writing it down at the time.

He took a sheet of the Register's paper instead.

> *To Tally, N., North Ferriby walk, by the Timberline chapter.*
>
> *Your insert of the 14th has been read at the Night Register and is open here.*
>
> *I ask you to walk your line again as you walked it before the 11th, and to measure whatever you find on it, lying or standing, with the same rule and in the same way. Write each one down at the time, in a notebook you keep on your person, with the day and the division. I want a second dataset from the same observer. Do not change your method to please me. Where you find nothing, write that you found nothing, and where.*
>
> *Tell me who set the parish marker on the Ferriby line, and when, if anybody knows. Tell me what the animals on that line put their mouths to: water, licks, troughs, anything that is set out and filled.*

He reached the foot of the page and stopped with the pen off the paper.

The clerk at the bulletin office had told Tally the Night Register was a person. It had been true for four years, since the Division wanted a particular officer somewhere else and found him a scullery on Cutler Row with a main that went off at the eleventh division. A desk could be folded into another desk by one line in a Prime Warden's minute. A letter signed with a name would go on being about the name.

He signed it *Night Register, Cutler Row*, dropped a thumb of brass wax beside it, and set the brass in the wax. Then he folded it in three.

"Who carries it?" Yinzhi said.

"The Society's bag goes back with the morning bundle."

"I go past the bulletin office. I could..."

"You could go home."

She had finished her tea. She stood with the empty cup in both hands and did not put it down.

"What happens now," she said. "To the... With me. On the docket."

"Nothing, tonight. Nothing tomorrow, or the day after." He capped the ink. "If it comes to a hearing, and it will not come soon, somebody will stand up and say the assay was eight months capped and burned two coils and cannot say what she read. You will tell them what you read. That is the whole of what an assay of record does."

"I can tell them now."

"I know you can. Go home and sleep, and tell them in a year."

"The chapter will ask where the tin is."

"Tell them it is under my brass at Cutler Row and they may write."

"And you."

"The day desk, at the sixth."

She looked at the sill, at the tin and the fold of paper squared beside it, and back at him.

"You said forty-two," she said.

"Forty-two."

"Then you counted." She put her cup in the sink next to his and did not straighten it. "Eat something, Master Wystan. At the sixth. Something that is not tea."

He heard her go down the outer stair, both flights, and the street door, and then the rain had her.

He turned her cup in the sink so that it stood level with his.

The fifth division went while he was writing up the night, and he counted the bell off toward the river and got it right. He turned back through the day book to the page he had been reading when she knocked, where the margin said *edges* in his hand, and under it *ask Tally who set the marker*. The second half was done. He sat with the first half.

Eleven hinds and a stag inside a quarter mile. The last of them twenty-two paces short of the parish boundary marker. Clean ground on the far side, and Tally had walked it and turned and walked it again, because he was the sort of man who checks the far side twice. Four ravens in the birch that would not come down. And a residue the hind herself had signed, because her own Lattice had run the working to the end.

Somebody had put it into her. That was the part with a hand in it.

*A dose goes where it is put.*

He wrote it under *edges*, in smaller letters, and then he wrote out the rest of it underneath in the plain sentences a memorandum would need. Whatever the formulator had put into those animals had stopped where he stopped putting it, because a man who works at a distance from his own work has to choose the distance, and every site in the four counties would show the same hard margin once somebody walked it with a rule. The Swale walks would have an edge. The two chapters in the eastern timber would have edges. Whatever the Marches man had tried to draw would have one. A man who kept away from what he made would leave his edges behind him wherever he went, as reliably as a signature.

He read it over. It held.

At the back of the book, on the pages he kept for them, he entered the forty-second minute beneath the other forty-one. *Hind, North Ferriby cut. Not frightened. Did not suffer.* He left the line after it clear and ruled it off.

The main came back at the sixth division.

It started under the flags as a note too low to be a note and came up through the legs of the bench and into the window glass, which took it and held it. The kettle lid buzzed once on the kettle. Out in the front room the meter beside the door ticked, and he knew without looking that its needle had come off the pin under the Board's seal and the bill for Cutler Row was running again. His shoulders came down. He had not noticed they were up.

He wrote the time in the margin: *main on, 6th.*

Then he took the tin. He lifted it the way he lifted everything, two fingers and a thumb at the rim of the lid and the base, and he took the docket form in the other hand, and he went out through the front room and along the passage, where the hum ran under the boards the whole length of the house, and up the four steps to the front of the building where the Division kept its day desk.

The day desk was a long counter under the street windows with a brass rail across the front of it for the public to stand behind. The stove in the corner was cold. The chalk slate showed yesterday's filings, and the docket book stood on its lectern at the near end, shut, with the ribbon hanging out of it at the current page.

He set the tin on the counter and opened the book.

Ferriby was four pages back. A day clerk had entered it in the copying hand, the Bureau's return pinned to the leaf beside it, signed in its own ink: *Wellspring bleed, Verdantia, tertiary expression, no operator indicated. No further action. No file. E. Brack, Lattice Classification Bureau, northern office.* Two lines had been ruled under it in red. In the margin, in the day desk's hand, *closed.*

He did not disturb any of it. He dipped the day desk's pen and wrote beneath the red.

> *Memorandum of the Night Register. The filing above is opened this division as a Register file, Ferriby. Exhibit one is held at Cutler Row under the officer's brass. The assay of record is named at the head of the Register docket, laid in. Chapter wax is retained, four pieces, for custody. The measurement above is not disputed. W. Ashmore, Warden of Duty.*

In the margin, level with Brack's line, he wrote one word. *edges.*

He folded the docket form once, with her name inward, and laid it in the book at the page.

The street door let in rain and a day clerk. He was a man of fifty or so, bald across the crown with a short fringe of grey above the ears, clean-shaven, his face gone red from the walk. He was heavy through the belly under a brown day-coat buttoned one hole wrong, and he had black sateen oversleeves on to keep the ink off his cuffs, with a wen at the side of his nose that he touched when he was thinking. He shook his hat out on the mat.

"Still on the main, Master Ashmore?"

"Since the sixth."

"Good. Good. Our lane was off half the night, the whole of it. Wife sat up with the lamp." He came down the counter peeling his gloves. "What have you got there, then?"

"Ferriby."

The clerk leaned in over the book where Wystan's finger lay. His lips moved on the Bureau's line. "Bleed. No operator. That's Brack's, that is." He nodded at it, satisfied. "That's closed, that one. Came back the other day."

He shut the book over the memorandum, over the folded docket and the one word in the margin, took it off the lectern in both arms and slid it into its place on the shelf behind the counter, spine out. Then he went to the stove and got down on his knees in front of it with the spill-jar.

"You'll want this lit, Master," he said, to the grate. "You look perished."

Wystan picked up the tin by its rim, two fingers and a thumb, and carried it back down the passage over the hum to the sill, and set it beside the fold of chapter wax, and laid his brass on the lid.

# Twenty-Two Drops

*Greyshaft Nine, the coldhouse. The second division to the ninth.*

The hook came down off the rail with the ninth hind on it and Nuvalik took the weight on her shoulder.

She did it the way she did everything, without asking anybody to help and without looking to see whether help was coming. The carcass slid off the iron and folded. It went over her back and down onto the slate table in one motion, and it landed with the sound of a wet coat dropped on a floor, which was not the sound a hind of that size should make. Eleven days of the ferment had taken the connective net out of her and left the meat where it was. Structurally liquid. Cellularly whole. Vesk had the phrase off Nuvalik and had been using it all week as though he had made it himself.

He stood at the bench with his hands out in front of him and watched the hide settle over the bones.

The room had no hum in it. The main under Greyshaft Nine had been capped four years, and the standpipe housings along the west wall stood there in their brackets with nothing in them, iron gone the colour of an old kettle, capped and unbilled and quietly rusting into the slate. There was no meter by the door. Nobody in this building had been asked *still on the main* since the year the Board struck the row off its schedule. What the coldhouse had instead was the ice press turning over next door, and the drip in the far corner where the ceiling had gone soft. Twenty-two drops a minute.

He had counted it once, a year ago, out of boredom, and had never got it back out of his head.

"The second division," Nuvalik said. "She is on the table."

"She is on the table." Malphas was on the gauge bench with his coat folded beside him, in the same place he had been sitting since the first division. "Vesk."

"Lord."

"How long."

"The matrix is in. It has been in since the eleventh hour." Vesk turned round to the bench and put both palms flat on either side of the flask, which was how he kept from picking it up before he meant to. "I want an hour on it standing. Not because it needs an hour. Because the last two I carried at half an hour and the fine matter came out of suspension in my hands, and if it comes out in her it goes in at the flank and stays at the flank, and then we have proved nothing except that I am in a hurry."

"An hour, then."

"Less than an hour. A little less."

Nobody said anything to that. Vesk heard himself hedge it and did not much mind. He had lost the habit of minding.

The flask sat under the third lamp with the formulation green in it, the third dilution, faintly cloudy, the suspended matter turning over slowly in a colour that belonged to a pond in July. Into that he had put the contractile matrix. Not over it, not after it. Into it, at the dilution, so that the thing that grew and the thing that pulled came off the same pour and arrived in the same tissue at the same time. That was the whole of the last four days and he had not slept through any of them.

He looked at his hands on the bench and made them close, and they got two thirds of the way and stopped.

Two grafts. The first had taken cleanly and nobody would know to look. The second had taken in the way that a thing takes when it has been asked twice, which was well enough for holding and badly for gripping, and the nails on the two fingers that took had come in ridged and yellow and had never stopped growing since. He cut them every fourth day. They were at the end of the fourth day now, standing past the fingertip with a curve in them like a tool that had been made for something. He put them under the bench edge where the lamp did not reach.

"Foss."

"I am here." Foss did not look up. He had the coil out again, in pieces, the jeweller's glass screwed into his eye, and the brass of the gauge-housing open beside him with the slate back off it and the seal broken on a quarterly inspection that had stopped coming nine years ago.

"I want the Arbitration Division's field manual."

The glass came out of Foss's eye.

"Which one."

"The one their Wardens of Duty are issued. Not the published articles. Not the Codex extract and not the lecture notes some chapter clerk sells at the bulletin office." Malphas had his forearms on his knees and was looking at the floor between his boots. "The manual has a section on attribution and the section has a paragraph in it that nobody signed, and every officer who works a residue works it off that paragraph, and I have never read the paragraph, and I have been designing against it for two years out of what other people tell me is in it."

"You cannot buy that."

"I did not say buy."

"You cannot get it at all, is my point, and I will say why, because the why is the useful part." Foss turned round on the stool with a piece of coil still in his fingers. "It is a numbered issue. Every copy has a number cut into the board and the number is against a name in an issue book, and when a man is struck off or dies or goes to the Bench his copy goes back and the clerk rules a line under it. I have watched it done. I had a numbered instrument off the same kind of book for eleven years and the day they struck me they wanted the instrument before they wanted the oath back."

"Then somebody's copy has not gone back."

"Somebody's copy is under a floor with a dead man over it, and the issue book says so, and the day it comes up out from under that floor is the day the book gets read." Foss put the coil down. "And if you tell me to wire for it, then hear the rest of it first. A wire is nine words. Nine words, and the boy at this end reads them, and the boy at the far end reads them, and the wire office keeps the flimsy at both ends for a turn of fourteen days. Nine words is nine words whichever end of them you are standing at."

"Then not a wire."

"Then not anything." Foss picked the coil back up. "I will ask a man. He will say no and I will ask a second man. It will be a season."

"A season is acceptable."

Vesk had been listening with half of himself and counting the drops with the other half. He got to twenty-two and lost the thread of what he had been about to say and found the pleasure of it instead. A season. The Lord said a season the way other men said tomorrow.

"Lord," he said. "May I ask why the paragraph."

"Because I do not know what it says."

"Yes. But the residue reads clean. Foss has proved it out three ways and I have never once seen a coil do anything but sit flat over one of ours."

"The residue reads clean under the practice they have now. Their practice is written in that manual and their manual is amended, and I would like to read the paragraph before somebody amends it, so that when it is amended I know which sentence moved." Malphas did not raise his head. "A man who designs against what he is told about a rule has a copy of the rule in his head and no way to check it. I have four of those. I intend to have three."

That was the end of the answer and Vesk knew it. He went back to the flask.

Nuvalik had the ninth hind's hide back at the shoulder. She worked with her sleeves pushed past the elbow and her apron on, and she had not once asked anybody to bring the lamp nearer, and in eleven days Vesk had not seen her stand up straight to rest. The knife went where she put it. The crown growth stood up off the spine and the flanks in a grey-green mass, nine inches at the best of it, and she cut round the mass and not through it, because Vesk had asked for that on the first night and she had done it every night since without once being reminded.

"Nuvalik."

"Say it."

"The fourth hind's shoulder." He had his palms on the bench. "The Lord said contraction. Twice, he said. I have had nothing like that off any of the others and I want to know what it looked like. Whether it was the whole muscle or a part of it. Whether it went and came back."

The knife stopped. She wiped the blade on her apron before she spoke, which was the one thing about her that frightened him, and it did not frighten him less for happening every day.

"Everything the fourth hind gave me is written down."

"In the book."

"In the book."

"Then I will read the book."

"Read the book." She went back into the shoulder. "It is on the shelf over the press where it has been since the first night and nobody has taken it down but me."

Vesk let it go. The book was there and he would read it, and pushing Nuvalik only ever got him a shorter answer than the one before. Behind him Malphas said nothing at all. He did that most days and Vesk had never once been able to read it. The press turned over. The dog that belonged to nobody had stopped barking somewhere in the last hour and the quiet where it had been sat in the room like a fourth man.

*Twenty-two,* he thought, and started again at one.

"Your sky," he said. "You said it about the ravens. The sky finishes what the ground starts."

"Where I was raised, the sky finishes what the ground starts, and when the sky refuses, people walk out to see why." She said it the same way both times, flat, as a thing she had been given rather than a thing she was making. "That is not mine. It is older than me and it will be older than whoever has this name after me."

"After you."

"The name I wear was worn by three people before me. That is how it is done at home. You carry a name and you spend it, and when you are done the name goes on to somebody who is not born yet, and what they get is what you left in it." She turned the shoulder open. "I have spent all three of mine in this room. I am telling you because you asked about the sky and the two are the same question."

He did not know what to do with that, so he did what he did with most things now, which was to put it down and pick up the pleasure instead. In the village where he was raised they put stones on the graves. Flat ones, out of the beck, one a year for nine years, until the nine were on and the ground was finished. He had asked why at six years old and had been told, and had thought even then that it was a poor way to settle a question. Nuvalik's people had a sky that finished things and his had a heap of stones. His had been wrong. He had spent his life since finding out how wrong, and tonight the ninth hind was on the table.

"The hour is up," Nuvalik said.

"It is not."

"It is near enough up that you are looking at the flask instead of at me."

He laughed, once, which came out of him at the wrong volume in a room that cold, and picked the flask up in both palms.

That was the way he carried anything now. Two hands flat, the flask standing between them, held out in front of the chest the way a server carries wine. He had learned what his hands would and would not do and had stopped negotiating with them. Eight paces from the bench to the table. He took them slowly and watched the fine matter turn over and stay turning, in suspension, not a grain of it down at the shoulder of the glass.

"Foss. The glass, if you will."

"I am not putting my eye over that."

"You will put your eye over it because you have the steadiest hands in this building and because if the pour goes in wrong I want it said by somebody who will say it." Vesk set the flask on the table by the hind's opened shoulder. "The Lord will want it said as well."

"Ilmar," Malphas said.

Foss came, with the glass in his fist and an expression he did not trouble to arrange.

The growth had built its own carriage. That was the part nobody outside this room would credit and the part Vesk had stopped trying to explain to anyone in it. The Green Pulse does not repair. It grows, continuously, off whatever ambient conversion it can reach, and it has no notion of a finished size. Put growth into a matrix with the scaffold eaten out from under it and it does not put the old scaffold back. It lays a fresh one to a plan of its own, and because it laid the plan along the vasculature the dose had travelled, the fresh scaffold came up with channels in it, following the old roads because the old roads were where the material was. Eleven days of that in a cold room had given the ninth hind a second circulation. There was no pump on the end of it.

Vesk did not need a pump. He needed a gradient, and a thing that grows makes its own.

"At the flank," he said. "Not the wound. The wound gives it a cavity and a cavity is a puddle. I want it in the new tissue where the channel is finest, because the finest channel is where growth is still going on, and growth pulls."

"Growth pulls," Foss said.

"It pulls the way every green thing in a dry pot pulls. The dry side of the pot is where the root goes. That is all this is. It goes toward what it can still use and it keeps going until there is nothing left to use." He had his hands out in front of him. "Hold the hide, please. Both thumbs. There."

The pour took a long time and he was the only one in the room who enjoyed it.

He worked it in at four places along the near flank, a little at each, and between each he stopped and gave it the count of forty and watched the colour go out of the surface as the channels took it away inland. That was the thing. That was the whole thing. On the first night, with the third dilution and no matrix, the colour had sat where he put it for a quarter of an hour like paint on a wall. Tonight it went. Not fast. But it went, and it went on going after he took his hands off it. The new scaffold was carrying. It had been carrying since before tonight and nobody had asked it to.

"Say what you see," he said.

"I see it clearing." Foss had the glass down at the hide. "Uneven. It is going faster at the two forward pours than the two back."

"Say why."

"I will not guess."

"Guess wrong, then, and I will correct you, and you will remember the correction longer than the fact."

"It is going faster forward because the animal died forward." Foss straightened up. "The trunk quit last. The forward tissue is where the old road was best kept and the growth had the best material to build on, so the channel is finest and most numerous at the shoulder and the girth, and the finest channel is the strongest pull. Which is your sentence, not mine, and I dislike it, and it is correct."

"It is correct."

"It is filthy, and it is correct, and those are two separate findings and I want both in the book."

Malphas stood up off the gauge bench.

He was not a large man. He filled nothing when he arrived and nothing when he moved, and in twelve days Vesk had watched for the thing that men like him were supposed to have and had not seen it once. No pressure in the room. No note under the floor. Nothing in the slate, nothing in the brass, nothing in the cold. What there was instead was the one thing Vesk could not get past: the Lord had been sitting on an iron bench in a room the housing gauge had at four degrees since the first division, with no gloves on and his coat folded beside him and not under him, and his fingers were the colour of a living man's fingers.

Eleven days he had watched for it. The twelfth had started at the second division and he had never asked.

The press threw its warm air along the east wall and the gauge bench stood in the middle of it, and that was the whole of why the Lord's hands never took the cold. He had worked it out on the fourth night. He had been pleased with it and had not said so, because a man does not tell his Lord where he sits.

"Nuvalik," Malphas said. "The ninth hind is the freshest and she is the one I want. Say what she is now that she is on the table."

"She is meat with a new net in it and the net can shorten." Nuvalik had her knife in her hand and did not look at the carcass. "That is what you have bought. No Crystal. No self. Nothing in her that wants anything, and instead of it a tissue that shortens when one end of her is emptier than the other. Put the empty end somewhere and she goes there. Not because she chooses it. Because that is what shortening does."

"And the speed."

"The speed is whatever the tissue allows and I will not have a number for it until she is on the floor."

"Days," Foss said. "Four to six. You said four to six."

"Four to six without an anchor, and there is no anchor in her, and there will not be one." Malphas walked up to the table. "An anchor is a binding. A binding takes a name. A name is the only thing in this world the Accord can prosecute, and I have not put one in her, and this is the first night where that decision costs us something, because a thing with no name in it cannot be told where to go. It can only be given somewhere emptier than where it is."

"The main," Vesk said. He heard it come out of him too loud again. "The capped mains. Lord, the whole row here is capped, the housings are dead the length of that wall, this building is the emptiest place in four streets and she is lying in the middle of it."

"Yes."

"Then she will not need four days. Eleven feet is all she needs."

"She will need to get up," Malphas said, "which she has not done."

That went into Vesk like cold water. He was grateful for it, and he had not known he wanted correcting until it arrived. He put both palms on the table edge and looked down at the hind.

Proving rot was finished. Eleven days on the rail and no smell and nine carcasses signed by nobody, and it had been beautiful work and it had been an argument about what a thing is. This was not that. From the second division tonight the work was an argument about what a thing does, and everything that had been elegant about it was now only the ground it stood on.

"Write the night up," Malphas said. "Vesk's pour, the four places and the order of them, Foss's clearing at the shoulder, Nuvalik's shortening in her own words. Not a summary. The words."

"In the book," Nuvalik said.

"In the book, and in ink, and with the division. I want the whole of this project written down under the name I gave it, by four hands, in one book, because the file somebody builds out of us will be built off what is written and I would rather it be built off this than off a clerk's recollection of it."

He put two fingers against the ninth hind's flank, above the pour, and pressed. The hide moved under the pressure like the skin on cooling milk. Then he took his hand back and looked at the fingers, the way a man checks whether a thing has come off on him.

"The ninth," he said.

Foss went back to the coil and the broken gauge-housing. Nuvalik wiped the blade and set it down and went to the shelf over the press for the book, and the press turned over, and outside the dog did not bark.

Vesk stayed at the table with the empty flask standing between his palms.

The hide at the shoulder drew up.

It was small. A hand's breadth of it, no more, a slow tightening that came up and held for the length of a breath and let go, and nothing at all was touching her. He stood with the flask between his hands and waited for it to come again and it did not come again. He did not know whether that had been the matrix, or the slate under her taking her weight over as the meat found its level, or his own eyes at the end of the fourth day without sleep. He had nothing to put against it either way.

He did not call Foss back to put the glass on it.

The drip went on in the corner and he found he was counting it again. He let himself get to twenty-two. Then he went to the shelf for the book and wrote the division in the margin.

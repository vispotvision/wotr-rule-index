# The Morning Check

*The Division's medical house, then the Arbitration Division's northern office on Cutler Row: the day desk, the passage, the Prime Warden's room. Day 1, from noon to the ninth division.*

He woke at noon with the shutters closed and the house humming under him.

The main ran all day in this street. It came up through the bed frame and the boards and into the jug on the washstand, a low steady note he had stopped hearing years ago, and he heard it now only because he was listening for something under it. There was nothing under it. A cart went by on the setts. Somebody below was beating a rug over a sill. The light came round the shutter in two thin yellow lines and lay across the foot of the bed.

He held his hands up in front of his face.

They shook. They always did at this hour, a fine fast tremor that began somewhere in the wrists and came out at the fingertips, worse in the right than the left. The brown stain across the backs of them looked darker by daylight than it ever did by lamp. The medical house had a name for the shaking. The Division had entered it in his file under a different heading, in the copying hand, as a disciplinary observation.

He gave them the count of sixty. They were no better at sixty, and he got up.

His jaw ached, and the long muscles over his shoulders. He had kept himself down all night, asleep, the way he kept himself down awake, and this was what it came to in the mornings. He had stopped counting it some years ago. It was spent before he opened his eyes.

He sat on the edge of the bed and did the check before anything else.

The sheet was in the inside pocket of his coat on the chair, folded in four. He left it there. The minute came first and the paper after, because a record read first is a record remembered, and after that the minute would only agree with it. He shut his eyes and went to it.

It was where he had left it.

An iron handle, cold, under a right hand that was not his. A lamp somewhere behind. A count going on under the breath, already at thirty-one when the minute opened. The legs tired, and the back. The head clear and a little bored. Bread in the mind, a dark loaf with butter on it, wanted and put off until after. And the words, aloud, to nobody on the landing: "Sixth. They said the sixth. Hold it to the sixth."

Then the paper.

He unfolded it on his knee. His own hand, younger and smaller, in a brown ink that had since gone the colour of tea. *Keld, O. Door on the landing. Handle iron, cold. Right hand. Lamp behind. Count at thirty-one. Legs tired. Not afraid. Bread, dark, butter. Says: "Sixth. They said the sixth. Hold it to the sixth."*

He read the paper against the minute, word by word, once forward and once backward.

It had not moved.

He checked the other thing too, which was whose it was. It was Keld's. The hand on the handle was Keld's hand and the bread was Keld's bread, and he had not once caught himself remembering it as his own. He folded the sheet on its old folds, which were soft as cloth now, and put it back in the coat.

There was half a dark loaf on the shelf. He ate the heel of it standing at the shutter with the light in lines across his chest, and it was stale, and he ate all of it because Yinzhi had told him to.

The medical house stood two streets over, behind the Division's stables, a long whitewashed building with its windows kept shut against the yard. He had no token to show at the door. His brass was lying on a tin lid on Cutler Row, where he had left it at the sixth division, and he gave his name at the porter's hatch with nothing in his hand.

The porter knew his face. The man looked at it a moment, the way a wire-boy reads a face before he can read the wire, and let him up.

Keld's room was on the upper floor at the end. Whitewash, a cot, a washstand, a peg with the Division coat hanging on it and the silver-grey at the shoulder still bright, because nobody had worn it through anything for years. In the coat's breast, Wystan knew, was Keld's token. Silver. Nobody had ever come to take it back.

Keld sat at the window in a chair turned to face the street.

A big man, gone loose in the frame. The shoulders were still wide and the chest had fallen in under them. The orderlies kept his hair cropped to the skull and shaved him every second day, and today was not the day, so the grey showed along the jaw. A clean shirt. Grey Division trousers, a blanket across the knees though the room was warm. His hands lay on the sill, square and heavy, with the knuckles scarred white from something that had happened before Wystan ever knew him.

"Master Keld."

Keld did not turn. He was watching the street.

Wystan took the stool from beside the cot and set it down to the left of the chair, a yard back, where he always set it. He sat. He did not touch the sill. He had never touched anything in this room that Keld had held for long, and he kept his hands on his own knees, where he could watch them shake.

The window gave onto the lane that ran down to the stables. A dray, backing. A boy with two buckets. A woman on a step with her apron full of something she was shelling. The farrier's door, open, and the ring of his hammer coming out of it at no steady interval.

Keld's lips were moving.

Wystan leaned in to hear it. It was a count. Keld counted in a low mutter, in ones, up to some number that was never the same, and then stopped, and after a while began again. Twelve, and a stop. Then nineteen. Then seven. Wystan watched the lane for whatever was being counted. He tried the farrier's strokes against it, and the dray's wheels, and the boy's trips to the trough, and the people passing the end of the lane. None of them fitted. He could find nothing in the street that Keld was counting.

Feet on the stair outside. Keld's head came round an inch toward the door.

"Is that them."

"It is the orderly, Master Keld."

The orderly came in with a jug, a young woman with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and a burn scar on one forearm, and set the jug on the washstand without looking at either of them. "He's eaten," she said. "Pulse porridge. He had all of it."

"Did he ask."

"Twice. Same as always." She took the old jug and went out.

Keld's head went back to the window.

"They said the sixth," Keld said.

"Yes," Wystan said.

Keld had taught him to fold a paper the druggist's way. The long sides in and then the ends under, in the back room of the old office on the river with a bundle of evidence slips and a whole afternoon, because a slip that slides out of its fold in a courtroom is a slip that was never there. Keld's big square fingers had done it without looking. There was nobody left who folded a paper the way those hands had folded one. The hands were lying on the sill.

Wystan had been the second man through the door on the landing. The first had his arms full, so it was Wystan who put his hand on the handle, and that was how he had the minute. The relief had come. It had come late, and he had been part of it.

*Six people alive know what Stage I hold.* He had counted them more than once, and the count did not change. Keld had been the first man to know it. Keld was alive. He was not one of the six.

He sat the length of time he always sat. The farrier stopped and started. Keld counted to four and stopped, and then to thirty, and then did not start again, and his hands stayed where they were on the sill.

Wystan stood, and put the stool back beside the cot.

"Master Keld," he said.

Keld was watching the street.

The day desk had its stove lit and its chalk slate filled, and the Prime Warden was standing at the lectern with the docket book open in front of her and one finger under a line.

Elspeth Lyle was a small woman, shorter than the lectern made her look, somewhere near sixty, with iron-grey hair drawn back so tight from the forehead that it lifted the brows. She wore the black gown of the office with the Division's silver-grey at the shoulder unworn and very clean. Spectacles low on the nose, on a black ribbon. A silver token on a short chain at her belt, and it swung when she turned a page. Ink on the side of the second finger of the right hand, the mark of a woman who still wrote her own minutes.

The day clerk was at the far end, very busy.

"Prime Warden."

"Warden of Duty." She did not look up. She was the only person in the building who used the title, and she used it as other people use a surname in a quarrel. Her finger moved to the margin. "Edges."

"Yes."

"That is one word. Your memoranda run to a page as a rule."

"The page is in the day book. I put in the margin what I have not proved."

"Then what is it doing in my docket book?"

"Keeping its place."

She read the memorandum again. He watched her do it, the finger going along under each line and back, as he had once watched Keld read. When she reached the foot of it she read the last sentence aloud in a level voice. "'The measurement above is not disputed.'" She looked at him over the spectacles. "You do not dispute Brack's measurement, and you have opened a file on it."

"On what it is a measurement of."

"Which you are going to stand there and tell me is not bleed, Warden."

"A bleed goes where the rock lets it. Raggedly." He kept his hands behind his back. "This stopped twenty-two paces short of a parish marker, and the ground past the marker is clean, and a forester walked it twice. Somebody put it into those animals. The Register's position is that a dose goes where it is put, and that every site will show the same margin when somebody walks it with a rule."

"A dose of what."

"I do not know yet."

"So." She took the spectacles off. "A man you cannot name has put a thing you cannot name into deer."

"Into eleven hinds and a stag."

"Into eleven hinds and a stag." She said it back to him quite flatly, and the clerk at the end of the counter wrote something very small in his ledger. "It is a curiosity, Warden. I grant it that. It is a well-made curiosity with a forester's paces in it." She closed the book on her finger. "The Register may keep a curiosity open until the quarterly inspection of the gauge-housing and not a day longer."

"And at the inspection."

"At the inspection the Board's man reads my housing and seals it, and the northern office's return goes up the same day under the same seal, and I sign the two together. I have done it that way since this room was mine." She put the spectacles in her gown. "I will not put the Night Register on that return quarrelling with the Bureau over deer."

"The Register is not quarrelling. The measurement is not..."

"You wrote a word in the margin beside Master Brack's signature." She took her finger out of the book. "Bring me the day book. The page with your position on it. I will have the position in my minute, in ink, and the term under it, and you will initial both."

She went up the stair, gown held off the treads.

The passage ran back from the day desk over the hum, the whole length of the house, to the scullery. The tin was on the sill where he had left it. His brass lay on the lid, worn smooth at the rim, the Division's device uppermost.

He picked it up first.

The Register's token was brass because the Night Register was not a full desk. Every officer of a full desk carried silver, and the porters at the Division's doors knew the difference at a glance and were a shade slower for brass. He had carried it four years and had never asked to have it changed. He put it in his waistcoat.

Then he turned the tin a quarter turn under the window and let the light rake the seal. The device stood in the wax sharp to its edges. He took the day book under his arm and went up.

The upper passage was narrow and ill-lit, with the Prime Warden's door at the end of it shut and a bench against the wall outside. A man was sitting on the bench with a Bureau folder on his knees. He stood up when he saw who it was.

"Ashmore."

Edwyn Brack was a broad man of about fifty with a short neck and a clean-shaven red face that looked scrubbed. His grey hair was cut close all over. A pair of gold-wire spectacles was pushed up onto his forehead and forgotten there. He wore the Bureau's grey worsted, good cloth, and on the pad of his right thumb a shiny white scar the size of a thumbnail, the old burn a man gets once from holding a coil wrong and never again. He held the folder against his chest in both hands. Its tape was the Bureau's green.

"Master Brack."

"I have seen your memorandum."

Wystan waited.

"I'll not pretend I liked the margin." Brack's voice was big and unhurried, and it filled the passage without his raising it. "You wrote your word next to my name. Edges. All right. I've no quarrel with a word. But I'll tell you straight, since I'm stood here. It's bleed. My man took it off a calibrated coil against the standard and it's bleed, Verdantia, tertiary, and there's no operator in it. I'd sign it again this afternoon. I'd sign it in front of you, Ashmore, and your Prime Warden."

"I have not said you were wrong."

"You've said it's a dose."

"I have said the measurement is not disputed."

"Aye, you've said that." Brack shifted the folder. "And then you've written 'edges' next to it, which is a man saying he doesn't dispute the weight and then going round the back of the scale with his little hammer." He did not seem angry. He seemed to be setting it out so that it was on the table. "Strike it, Ashmore. Go on. A solid weight rings. Mine'll ring."

"I expect it will."

"Well, then." Brack held out the folder.

Wystan did not take it at once.

"The passes," Brack said. "The field sheets off the Ferriby cut, with the coil calibration pinned to the front. Raw, as my surveyor wrote them, in his own hand, before anybody at my desk touched them. The Bureau doesn't send them out with a return. Nobody ever asks for them."

"I did not ask for them."

"No. You'd not have." He was still holding it out. "If I'm wrong, it's in there. It'll not be in there. But you'll have it, and then you'll know, and then we can..." He stopped, and started again. "I'd sooner you had the sheets than the word."

Wystan took it by the edge, two fingers and a thumb, and felt the weight of it come into his hand. It was heavier than it looked. There were a good many sheets.

"Thank you, Master Brack."

"Don't thank me. Read it." Brack sat down again on the bench and put his hands on his knees. "I'm waiting on the Prime Warden."

The door at the end of the passage said, "Warden of Duty."

The Prime Warden's room was small and very warm, with a stove in the corner and the window shut and the shutter half across it. A long table did for a desk. The minute book lay open on it beside a stick of the office's red wax, and her silver token lay on the blotter where she had unhooked it from the chain.

On the wall beside the door was the gauge-housing, brass and slate-backed, locked under the Board's seal across the hasp, and the last inspector's card tucked into the frame. He did not need to read the card. He knew the date on it.

"Shut the door."

He shut it.

She held out her hand for the day book without getting up, and he opened it at the page and laid it in front of her, turned so that she could read it, and stood back. She read *edges* and the plain sentences underneath it, and then *A dose goes where it is put*, and took up her pen, and copied the line into the minute in a small upright hand, and wrote the date and the division beside it.

He stood with Brack's folder held against his coat.

It began on the backs of his hands first, as it always did, where the stain was. A fine dry settling, with nothing there to see, as if the air in the room had given up something it had been holding since the morning and let it down on him. Then the face, over the cheekbones and along the lids, and the throat above the collar. He kept still and let it come.

It was what any shut room did by this hour, with the main running under it and people at work in it all day, and it came down on everybody in the room alike; Lyle had simply never sat a night on the Register, and had nothing on her hands to feel it with.

He did not remark it. He never did.

She was one of the six. She had had it off his file the week he was put on nights, and she had read it, and had said nothing then, and had said nothing since. He watched her write.

"The term," she said, and wrote it. He read it upside down as it came. *The Register may keep this curiosity open until the quarterly inspection of the gauge-housing, and not a day longer. E. Lyle, Prime Warden.* She turned the minute round to him and held out the pen.

He initialled the position, and then the term. His hand shook on the second initial and he did not stop to steady it. It came out legible.

She turned the book back, softened the red wax at the stove's door and dropped a thumb of it beside her name, and pressed her silver into it. She held it for a slow count of five without any help at all.

"What is that under your arm?"

"The Bureau's raw readings for Ferriby. The field sheets."

Her eyes went to the green tape and stayed there a moment. "You asked the Bureau for its raw readings."

"No. Master Brack gave them to me in the passage."

"Unasked."

"Unasked."

"Then enter them as received unasked, today, with the division, before you read a line of them." She wiped the pen. "And they go on the return with the rest of it if it comes to that."

"Did you send for him?"

"I did not send for Master Brack." She was writing again, and the pen did not stop. "He is here on the Bureau's own business."

Wystan waited, and she wrote.

"Your three lamps are on my bill, Warden," she said, without looking up. "Go and read your sheets."

Brack was on the bench with his hands on his knees and his spectacles still on his forehead. He looked up.

"Well?"

"The quarter."

Brack let out a breath through his nose. "She's generous. I'd have given you a turn." He looked at the folder under Wystan's arm, and at the day book. "And say you're right. Say your edges hold, and there's a man, and you walk him down. What do you get for it?"

Below them, toward the river, a bell began on the ninth division. Wystan counted it off and got it right.

"I will get a hand and not a purse."

Brack looked at him.

"There's not a Warden in this building would thank you for a hand," he said. "Fifteen wants the purse. It's framed over half the desks downstairs."

"I know where it is framed."

"Then what's it for?"

Wystan did not answer that. He took the brass out of his waistcoat, turned it once between two fingers so that the worn device caught the lamp at the end of the passage, and put it back.

"Master Brack," the door said.

Brack got up off the bench, and settled his coat, and pulled the spectacles down off his forehead onto his nose.

"Then I hope it's bleed," he said, "for your sake," and went in.

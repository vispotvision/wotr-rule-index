# A Number in the Room

*The Xu counting house by the river. Day 2, from the fourth division to the sixth.*

The second tranche went out of the Swale book at the fourth division, in Deming's hand, in the wastage column.

He wrote it standing. The counting house kept one high desk for the Swale contract and it was his, at the river end of the long room under the window, and the book lay open on the slope at the page he had ruled on the first day of the quarter. Receipt. Carriage. Wastage. Remaining on contract. Four columns with the red rule between them, laid down against a steel edge with a pen he kept for nothing else. He dipped, touched the nib twice to the rim of the well, and entered the draw in the third column in figures, and then again beside it in words, the way the house had always entered anything that left it without being paid for.

It took him the length of one breath. He blotted it.

Below the window the river went past brown and fast with the thaw in it. A lighter was working up against the current under two sweeps. Nobody on the sweeps was talking. On the far bank the dyers had their cloths out on the frames and the frames were steaming. Under his feet, under the boards and the cellar and the black piles the house stood on, the main ran. He could feel it in the soles of his shoes when he stood still, a low even hum that he had not listened to on purpose since he was a boy. The meter by the street door said the same thing in brass, under the Board's seal. Last month's bill hung on the spike beside it with the Xu chop in red in the corner. Paid.

He turned back a leaf and read the Swale page the way a Logistics clerk would read it, from the top.

Four years of wastage at the array, entered quarter by quarter, and every quarter of it the same. He had kept this page himself for three of those years. Each quarter he had written the loss at the housings, and each quarter it had come out within a hair of the one before. The column went down the page without a step in it anywhere.

Then this quarter. The first tranche, entered early, small enough that a tired man might carry his eye past it.

Then today.

He did not look at it long. A column looked at too long begins to look like what it is.

The letter was next.

He sat for that, at the low table by the stove, with a sheet of the house paper squared in front of him. The house wrote its covering letters to the Division on the paper it used for everything, cream laid with the chop blind-stamped in the corner, and it wrote them to the form his uncle had taught him in this room when the desk had been too high for him. To whom, in the first line. On what account, in the second. Then the matter, the figure and the cause, and the house's compliments. Nobody read a covering letter. That was its use. It went round a figure to keep the figure clean on the road, and at the other end it came off and was thrown away.

He wrote the first line without stopping.

*To the factor for the Swale supply, the Logistics and Supply Division.*

Then he held the pen over the second line until a bead of ink began to gather at the nib. He wiped it on the rim of the well and dipped again.

The second line was the account. In every other letter the house had ever sent up the river road it would have said *Swale supply, second tranche*, and nothing else, and it would have been true. Malphas had told him what it would say instead. He had told him at the coldhouse on the night he gave the work its name, while Deming stood by the door with his hat in his hands and the rain on his shoulders and argued. He had known it was the wrong moment to argue. He had argued anyway, carefully, in full sentences, with his reasons in order. A named project is a filed project. He had said that. He had given the precedent.

Malphas had heard him out, all of it, to the end.

"I would prefer the file be built off a word I chose."

That had been the whole of the answer.

Deming wrote the second line.

*On the account of Project Zombification, second tranche.*

His hand did it in the house's copying hand, upright and even, and it took the long stroke of the Z down and back without a lift, because that was how his hand had been trained to take it. He had written worse words in that hand. He could not, just then, think of one. He laid the blotter over it and pressed with the flat of his palm, and when he lifted the blotter the line was on it backwards in pale blue. He turned the blotter face down.

The rest went quickly.

*Loss at the array housings in the thaw. The second tranche of Verdantia under the Swale supply contract drawn down against it as wastage and entered this day in the house's book. Cause: the thaw.*

He looked at *the thaw* for some time. He let it stand. A cause of two words was a cause nobody argued with; a cause that ran to a sentence was an invitation to supply the next one. He wrote the house's compliments, and signed for the house, and pressed the chop beneath the signature in red paste out of the lacquer box, rocking it once each way so the corners took.

He folded the sheet in three and laid it on the corner of the Swale desk under the brass weight.

Then he took the almanac down off its nail.

It was the house's almanac, a year of turns and divisions in cheap print, with the market days in red and Auren's days marked with a small black cross for the dead. He did not need it. He opened it anyway and put his finger on today and counted forward, a square to a day, with his lips shut.

He came to the same square he had come to walking home from the coldhouse, with his hat on and the rain coming through the crown of it.

*Thirty-one.*

Thirty-one days of a man who held the contract, and the pen, and the chop, and was useful. He had done the sum at the coldhouse, in his head. Before he had finished it he had known that he was not the first man in the room to do it. Malphas had counted them already. Malphas had counted them before he ever sent for him, and had counted them again while Deming argued, and had let him argue.

He closed the almanac on his finger. After a moment he took the finger out.

The Logistics return was the other count, and it was simpler, because it was nobody's. The house kept its own copies of the Swale returns in the press by the stove, a quarter to a bundle, and years ago, on a quiet quarter, he had set the date on each copy against the date the published return came back down the river. He had done it for something to do. It came out at eleven weeks. He had done it three times since and it came out at eleven weeks each time, near enough that the roads made the difference.

Eleven weeks from the day the factor took the page away.

A bell down the river rang the fifth division. He counted it off, and on the last stroke he heard the street door.

"Still on the main?"

"Still on the main, Factor."

"Lucky you." A sniff, wet. "Half the Swale's off it."

The factor stood in the doorway with one knuckle against the glass of the meter, as he did every quarter, as though he was taking the house's pulse. He was a long narrow man somewhere past forty who had stooped under low doorways until he stooped under high ones too. He had a cold. He had had it since the autumn quarter, and it had gone into the nose, which was raw at the wings and bright at the end. Thin fair hair, rained flat to the skull, going at the crown. His face was all cheekbone and no chin, shaved close that morning and nicked twice in the doing. A coat of brown frieze to the shin, the cuffs rubbed to a shine. Under it was a knitted waistcoat buttoned wrong by one. He had grey knitted mitts with the fingers cut out, darned at the right thumb in a grey that did not match. Road mud had dried pale to both ankles. Across his chest was a satchel of waxed canvas, the strap shortened with a knot. A pencil hung round his neck on a string, sharpened down so far that he had to hold it by the tip of two fingers. In his waistcoat pocket, Deming knew, was a copper-rimmed glass that he screwed into his left eye when a figure interested him, and it would leave a red ring there all afternoon.

Deming had known his name for years and had never once used it. The house called him the factor, and he answered to that.

He did not take the chair. He never took the chair. He came down the long room to the Swale desk and put the satchel on the slope, on top of the open book, which Deming had asked him every quarter not to do.

"Right," the factor said. "Swale. Show me the bad news."

"There is no bad news."

"Always bad news. It's why they send me and not a letter." He lifted the satchel off the book and hung it on the corner of the desk. He did not look at the page. He looked at Deming. "Go on, then."

Deming turned the book a quarter round, so that it faced him.

The factor bent over it with his hands behind his back. He read the Swale page the way Deming had read it, from the top, and his lips moved on the year-ends. Halfway down, one hand came round from behind him and the mitt's bare forefinger went down the wastage column a quarter of an inch above the paper, never touching it. It went down four years without stopping.

It stopped.

The glass came out of the waistcoat and went into the eye.

"Hello," the factor said.

Deming waited.

"Four years that's lain down flat. Flat. You could've played cards on it." The glass turned up at him, the eye behind it swimming and huge. "And now it sits up."

"The thaw."

"Everybody's got a thaw."

"The Swale has had a bad one. The housings at the array are old, and when the frost comes out of the ground it..."

"Housings." The factor straightened. "Which."

"I do not keep the array, Factor. The keeper keeps the array."

"And what'll the keeper say."

"What keepers say in a thaw."

The factor sniffed and looked at the column again. He took the pencil up off his chest with the two fingertips and held it over the paper, and did not write. "See, this," he said. "This is carriage. Half of this is carriage. Road's been a river since the turn. Your carts have lost it off the tail on the Swale road and you've gone and put it on my array."

"The carriage column is clean."

"Course it's clean. You kept it."

"The carters' notes are clean. They are pinned behind, if you would like them. Every cart that went up the Swale road this quarter went up full and was entered full." That was true, and Deming heard it come out of his own mouth true and felt the relief of it in his shoulders. "Whatever happened, happened after."

"After." The factor tapped the pencil on the air over the page, over the step in the column, twice. "Carriage, I claim on your bond, the house pays, it's done by the turn. Wastage at the array, that's not done by the turn. That goes up the river in a bundle and sits in somebody's in-tray and some clerk with a cold worse than mine has to write it a reason." He let the glass drop out of his eye into his palm. "Put it in carriage, Xu. You'll pay the bond and nobody will ask you a thing."

"It was not lost in carriage."

"It's not about where it was lost."

"It is, in this book." Deming heard his own voice go careful. He let it. "The house enters what happened. If the house enters carriage on a column where the carters' notes say full, the next man who reads it reads a house that does not know what its own carts are doing. I would rather be the house with a bad array."

"You'd rather be the house with a bad array that the Division pays for."

"The contract says who pays for wastage."

"I know what the contract says. It's in my bag." The factor turned the glass over in his palm. "Rings a bit cased, all this."

"Strike it."

"I'm striking it."

"Then strike it with something." Deming stopped. That had come out shorter than he had meant it to. He took a breath through his nose and began again, in the house's voice. "Factor. The carriage column is signed by men who can be found and asked, and who will say full. The wastage column is signed by the thaw. You may ask the thaw what you like."

The factor looked at him for a while with the glass in his fist. Then he laughed, one short honk through the bad nose, and wiped it on the back of the mitt.

"All right."

"All right?"

"Wastage. On your word." He found the pocket-book in his coat, a small thing in black oilcloth bent to the curve of his hip, and opened it on the slope next to the Swale book. "Capital can have it. It's not my array." He licked the pencil stub. "Only it'll show, mind. Up the river. That column. Somebody'll look at that step and they'll want a reason off somebody, and it won't be off me."

"May I put a number in the room."

The factor looked up from the pocket-book. "You always do."

"In eleven weeks somebody in a room in the capital will look at the Swale line and see a bad spring."

"Eleven."

"Eleven. I should like it noted that I said so." Deming put one finger on the edge of the pocket-book, near the factor's hand, and took it off again. "Today. With the division. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before they see it."

The factor held the pencil still a moment. Then he wrote, slowly, the stub of it squeaking on the oilcloth-backed page, and turned the book and read it back in a flat voice with his finger under it. "'Xu says bad spring. Swale. Fifth division.'" He shut the book. "Happy?"

"Thank you."

"You're a strange man, Xu. Most of them want it not written down."

Deming watched him put the pocket-book away, and watched the interest go out of him while he did it. It went out of the long face and out of the shoulders and out of the hand that had been hovering over the step in the column. He had seen it go out of other Division men at the same place on other quarters. It was a thing he knew how to read. The man was a carriage man. The Division ate by tariff and carriage, and Deming would have wagered the house that a factor who brought carriage in off a bond was a factor his desk remembered at quarter-day. He had fought for carriage because carriage was money he could carry up the river in his own satchel. Wastage at the array was money going the other way. Once the carriage was lost to him, the Swale page was the capital's trouble and no longer his.

"Letter," the factor said, and held out a mitt.

Deming took the brass weight off it and gave it to him.

The factor unfolded it against his chest, one-handed, and screwed the glass back into his eye to read. He read the first line and grunted at it. He read the second line.

He read the second line again.

His lips did not move on it this time. The glass stayed in his eye. Deming stood with his answer ready behind his teeth. *A venture of the house, Factor. Drainage on the lower Swale. My uncle's naming, not mine.* He had it in the order he would say it, with the small apologetic lift at the end that the house used for an uncle's foibles.

The factor took the pencil stub up off his chest and made a mark in the margin beside the second line.

It was small. A short downstroke with a hook at the foot of it, turned to the left. Deming knew the factor's query, which was a cross, and he had seen it on the house's returns often enough over the years. He knew the factor's initial, a looped letter with a tail, which went at the foot of a page when it passed. This was neither of them. It was not a tick. He had never seen it on any paper of the house's in all the quarters he had kept the Swale.

The factor read the rest of the letter without stopping, the matter and the cause and the compliments, and looked at the chop, and folded it along its folds.

He asked nothing.

Deming's answer stayed behind his teeth and after a while he let it go down.

The factor opened the satchel on the corner of the desk. The quarter's returns went in at the front, the house's Swale page with them, in a bundle with a string round it that he tied off with his teeth and one hand. The letter did not go in with them. It went into the back of the satchel, into a flat folder between two boards, and the boards were pressed shut on it and the strap buckled over.

"You'll want to keep that with the return," Deming said.

"Covering letters?" The factor sniffed. "Covering letters stop on my desk and go in the stove at quarter-day. Nobody up the river reads a merchant's letter, Xu. They'd never get home." He settled the satchel on his hip.

"Then there is no need for it to go at all."

"Form wants one." He shrugged the strap higher. "Form wants a lot of things. Had a fellow up the Swale last year wrote me four pages on one short cask. Four pages. His mother's chest was in it. The weather at his sister's. I've still got a..." He stopped, and wiped his nose. "Stove had it. Warmest I've been all winter, that letter."

Deming had put his hand out for his hat while the factor was talking, from habit, because the factor was leaving and the house saw a man to the stair. He took it off the peg. It was still damp at the band from the morning.

"There was a man who wrote less than four pages," he said.

"Was there."

"Do you know the Hollow Shaft story?"

The factor tipped his head. "Should I?"

"It is twenty years old. Older." Deming turned the hat round once in his hands to find the front of it. "A cell. Some working that should not have been paid for, and a man who paid for it. They never found the hand, the one that did the work, never found him at all. The man who paid, they found. He was a careful man. Everything he kept was clean. Every column." His thumb had found the brim and was going round it, slowly, the felt soft and wet under the pad. "And he wrote one name, once, in one letter to somebody he trusted, about something else entirely. That is how they found the Hollow Shaft people. They found a name in a private correspondence."

The factor waited for more.

"They amerced him to his boots," Deming said. "To his means. Which was everything. The house, the contracts, the chop. The joke is..." He stopped. The thumb went on round the brim. "The joke is that he had been so careful about everything else."

The factor laughed. It was the same short honk, a little late, the laugh of a man who has understood that he has just been told a joke. "To his boots." He shook his head. "That's careless, that is."

"Yes."

"That's what I'm always saying. You write it in the book, you write it in the book. You don't go writing it to your mother." He clapped the satchel on his hip, twice, over the place where the boards were. "Right. That's the Swale."

"Will they wire?"

"If they want you." The factor was already at the door. "Nine words. 'Xu, Swale, explain.' That's three. They'll find six more." He put the knuckle against the meter's glass on his way past, the way he had on his way in. "Still on the main. Lucky you."

The door went, and then the stair, the wet slap of the boots going down it and the sniff at the bottom.

Deming stood with the hat in his hands.

After a while he went to the window. The factor came out below onto the river road and turned upstream along it, stooping, with the satchel against his hip and one mitt resting on the flap. He walked quickly for a man with a cold. The lighter had got some way above the house by then and the men on the sweeps had stopped to rest, and the current was taking back what they had made. The factor passed them without looking. He did not look back at the house either. The road bent at the dyers' landing and took him out of sight behind the steaming frames, and the river went on going past, brown, with the thaw in it.

Down the river the bell began on the sixth division. He counted it and got it right.

He hung the hat on its peg.

The Swale book was still turned a quarter round on the slope, the way the factor had left it. He turned it back square to the desk and read the third column once more, from the top, four years flat and then the step. It had not moved. He had not expected it to. He did not close the book. It was the house's rule that the Swale book stayed open on its page until the ink had been dry a full division, and the house's rule was older than he was.

The blotter lay face down on the low table by the stove where he had turned it.

He went and turned it over.

The second line was there, pale blue and backwards, in the house's best copying hand, which was his.

# Who Set the Marker

*Cutler Row, then the North Ferriby cut and the parish marker. Day 4, the sixth division, to Day 5, the sixth.*

The main came back at the sixth division, and Yinzhi came up the outer stair a little behind it with the Society's bundle under her arm.

He heard her on the second flight. The hum had come up through the flags and into the window glass, and out in the front room the meter had begun to tick under the Board's seal, and her boots on the iron treads were the only thing in the house not keeping time with it. She knocked once and came in without waiting for him. She had not done that before.

"The bag was on the counter when they opened." She set the bundle on the bench, well clear of the tin. "I said I would bring it up. There's one from Tally. I didn't open it."

"I did not think you had."

The Society tied its bundles with hemp twine and a knot a child could have picked. He cut it with the sill knife. The week's inserts lay on top, on the cheap rag, the ink gone furred into the fibre. Under them was a sheet of ruled paper torn from a book along the stitching, folded small and sealed with a drop of candle grease, and addressed in pencil. *Night Register. Cutler Row. By the chapter.*

He took it up by one corner and opened it on the bench with the back of the sill knife.

> *Your letter came. I am walking again, every day since, the same way as before, and writing it at the time like you said.*
>
> *The marker. I asked at the parish. It is not the parish's. It is the Ferriby estate's old survey stone, the corner of their ground, set by the estate before the parish was on the roll. The parish took it for their bound after, because it was there. Nobody knows who cut it or when. There are letters on it I cannot read.*
>
> *I am at the stone most days at dusk, writing up.*
>
> *Tally, N.*

He read it twice. The second time he read it for what was not in it.

"Well?" Yinzhi said.

"It is the estate's. An old survey stone, set before the roll."

"So nobody set it. For this, I mean. Nobody..." She stopped. Her thumb had found the outer band of the bracelet. "It was just there."

"It was there." He turned the day book back to the page where the margin said *edges*, and under it *ask Tally who set the marker*, and beside the second line he wrote *the estate. before the roll.* Then he sat and looked at it.

He had asked two things. Tally had answered one.

"I am going out to it," he said.

"To the cut."

"To the ground." He blotted the line. "It is weeks cold. I shall have to call it late, and that means standing on it, and I cannot stand on it from here."

She took a moment over that. The whole of the chapter's bench had read the field manual, looking for the part about themselves. "Then I'm coming."

"You are the assay of record. If I find anything on that ground I want the assay standing on it when I find it." He capped the ink. "Yes."

The Division's stables stood behind the medical house, and the ostler gave them two hacks on a chit to the northern office, which put the hire on the Prime Warden's bill with his three lamps. At the counter Wystan counted a wire out on the fingers of his left hand for the Timberline bench. *Assay of record with Register at Ferriby. Returns tomorrow.* Nine. The chapter would read it out to whoever was at the bench, and it did not matter in what order.

"Have you eaten?" Yinzhi said, in the yard.

"I shall eat at the cut."

The hum went out of the road at the last standpipe, iron and waist-high and capped, where the setts gave onto dirt. He felt it go through the horse and up into his spine and then not be there. After that there was the country, which ran on nothing. He rode badly, with his weight too far back and the reins in the tips of his fingers, and had not slept since the noon before, and he let the hack follow hers. She rode properly. Somebody had once made her.

The light was going when they came to the cut, at the eleventh division, and nothing went off at the eleventh, because out here nothing had been on.

The Ferriby cut was a ride through the timber, running north, wide enough for two carts and floored with old stumps and wiry grass. The birch stood across it on the east side, pale in the half-light. The ground fell a little to the west, where a burn ran along the foot of the timber, audible and not visible. Near the north end, where the cut narrowed, a squat grey stone stood on the rise, and a man sat against it on his pack, writing.

He got up when he saw them. He did it in one movement, without using his hands, and put the pencil behind his ear.

Tally was a man of middle height and no spare weight, long in the leg and short in the body, the build of a man who walks for his living and eats when he remembers. Forty or fifty; the weather had made it hard to say. His face was narrow and wind-reddened across the cheekbones, with a close grey beard he kept short with a knife, unevenly. Pale eyes, set in the permanent squint of a man who looks a long way off in bad light. His hair was cropped close under a felt hat gone the colour of bark. He wore a smock of undyed wool belted over a leather jerkin, hide gaiters laced to the knee, and boots resoled so many times the uppers were older than the soles. His hands were big for the rest of him, the knuckles swollen, the nails cut square, and there was a white seam across the back of the left one where a blade had once gone in and been pulled out. A sheath knife rode at his belt on the right. On the left, in a leather slot stitched to the seam of his breeches, a folding boxwood rule with brass joints. A notebook bound in oilcloth hung from his belt on a thong, with a second pencil tied to it on a string.

"Qiu," he said to Yinzhi, and nodded. Then he looked at Wystan, and at the black coat, and at his hands. "You'll be the Register."

Wystan took the brass out of his waistcoat and showed it on his palm before he gave the name. "Ashmore. I keep it."

Tally looked at the brass a while. "Clerk said you were a person."

"The clerk was right, as far as it goes."

Tally took the notebook off its thong and opened it. He held it for Wystan to read and did not let go of it. The pages were ruled by hand in pencil. Each day had its own head, the day and the division, in a small square hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the leaf behind, and under each head a column of places and a column of figures, and where there were no figures there was a word. *Nothing.* *North of the stone. Nothing.* Every day since the letter. The same walk, in the same order.

"Good," Wystan said.

Tally shut the book and hung it back on its thong.

"You'll want the line," he said.

He walked them the line in the last of the light. He went up the middle of the cut, where his own boots had worn the grass flat on the ninth and the tenth and every day since the letter, and Wystan and Yinzhi walked in his track behind him. The dead lay along the east side under the birch where Tally had found them. They lay down on their legs, folded, as a hind lies down to chew, and along each spine and flank the grey-green mass stood up out of the hide in soft fibrous ridges, gone pale at the crowns. The eyes were whole. Nothing had been at them. There was a smell, faint and sweetish, with no rot in it. Wystan did not count them. Tally had.

"That's her," Tally said, at the north end. "Last one."

A hind, lying toward the stone. Tally stood at her head and turned and walked north, and counted his paces aloud in a flat voice as he went. "... twenty. Twenty-one. Twenty-two." He stopped with his toe against the stone.

It stood to Wystan's hip, a squared block of grey grit leaning a little out of true toward the west, furred with yellow lichen on the north face and bare on the south. Its top had been dressed flat once and cut across with a single straight groove, the kind a surveyor sights along. Wystan put two fingers on the top edge, on the arris where the dressing met the side, and held them there. Stone, cold, grit under the lichen. It had carried nothing that he could feel, and he had not thought it would.

The letters were on the south face, low down. Tally held the lantern for him. The weather had taken most of them back into the stone, and what it had left stood proud only where the rain did not run. He read them the way he read any hand, first for the cutter and then for the words. *TERM*, and the rest of a word the chancery still used, and under it the estate's name cut shallower, by somebody else, later. Below that was a line that might once have been a year. The weather had had it.

"Before the roll," he said.

"That's what the parish said." Tally lowered the lantern. "Parish clerk had it from his father."

A trodden way came up out of the birch on the east side, narrower than the cut, bare down the middle and grassed at the shoulders, and met the stone at its south-east corner and bent away west along the foot of the rise.

"What is that?"

"Estate's round. Comes up out of their ground through the birch and goes along their bound, west." Tally lifted his chin at the far side of the stone. "That's not theirs. That's parish."

Yinzhi had her coil out already, a fresh one, the brass housing bright from the chapter's bench. She went round the stone to the north side without being asked and knelt in the grass and ran it, slowly, in the arc the Measurewright lines teach, with the gate open and her head down over it and her lips moving. She did it twice. Then she stood up and put it away.

"Nothing," she said. "Clean."

"Tally found it clean."

"I know he did." She wiped her hands on her coat. "Now I have."

Wystan turned to Tally. "You did not answer my second question."

"What they put their mouths to."

"Yes."

"Burn." Tally pointed west into the dark with the rule, which he had taken out of its slot without seeming to notice. "Runs the foot of the timber down the whole cut. Goes under the rise there and on north. Same water both sides."

"And anything set out. Troughs. Anything filled."

Tally folded the rule, joint by joint. The brass clicked at each one. "Nothing set out on my line, nor near it."

Wystan took out the day book and wrote it by the lantern. *Burn, both sides of the stone. North side clean. Nothing set out, per Tally.* So it was not the burn.

"Where do you want us?" Yinzhi said.

"The north side. Sit, and have a fire if you want one, and stay on that side of the stone until I come to you." He put the book away. "Do not walk the cut. Do not go to them. If you have to relieve yourselves, go north."

"All night?" Tally said.

"Until I come to you."

Tally unbuckled his pack and took out a dark hand-ground loaf wrapped in a cloth and a heel of hard cheese, and cut a piece of each on his palm with the sheath knife, and held them out. Wystan shook his head.

"You said at the cut," Yinzhi said.

"I said I would eat at the cut. I did not say when."

He went back down the middle of the cut in Tally's track, twenty-two paces, and stood beside the last hind with his back to the stone and his hands bare at his sides. The twelfth division began. There was no bell to count it off. He counted it anyway.

A scene more than six hours cold was a scene called late, and this one was weeks cold, and it had been rained on.

Out here nothing ran to him. In a shut room the free residue ran its gradient down to the emptiest thing present, which was always him; in the open there was no room, and what had come off the dead lay where it had come off and went on coming apart. It came apart at a rate. Every trace lost the same share of what was left of it each day, a share fixed by what it was, so that what remained of any one trace was a clock that had started when it was laid. Off the main the Aether stood thin and nothing drew at it, and the clocks ran slow and true. Wherever the ground had been broken after the dead began to shed, the break had started a later clock of its own. That was the whole of it. The rest was patience and the shape of the ground.

He built the cut first. The birch on the east side, the timber on the west, the fall of the ground toward the burn, the rise at the north end with the stone on it, and every stump. Then the weeks of weather, which had washed and diffused what the dead had shed downhill toward the water and north on the wind, and he moved it back. He did it slowly, the way a clerk rules a ledger, a line at a time. He held each piece as he placed it. Fixatio was one Wellspring of the three he carried, at the shallow depth he carried everything, and it did the one thing it did: what was held stayed held, a scene as well as a seal, so that the first thing he put back would still be where he had put it when he went looking for the next.

His Essence ran at a trickle and always had, and the working asked almost nothing of his Crystal. It asked the body instead. Under all of it he kept himself down, as he did at every hour of every day, and that cost what it always cost. The week after would send its own bill. It always did.

Then he took out what he knew.

Tally's line up the middle, the ninth and the tenth and every day since the letter: a worn strip of broken ground with a clock in it for every day he had walked it, each newer than the last. Yinzhi's, on the ninth, from the cut to one of the dead and back, and a place where she had knelt in the wet. The three of them tonight, bright and raw. He took them out one at a time and set them aside.

Across the rise the fire had caught. He could hear it crack, and their voices under it, low and not for him, and he did not attend to them and heard them anyway.

"...not doing anything," Tally said, at some point. "He's stood there."

"He is." Yinzhi. "It's... Everything that came off them goes on coming apart. At a rate. If you know the rate you know how long since. He's putting it back where it was."

"Like age on a track."

"I don't know tracks."

"Edge of a print goes soft. Rain on it, frost. A day's print and a week's, you can tell them. Anybody can."

"Then like that. Only everywhere at once."

A while after, Tally again. "Is it safe? For him."

"It's not touching. He isn't touching anything, it's not the..." Her voice went and came back lower. "It's the other kind."

The thirteenth. His feet had begun to swell in his boots, and the laces bit across the instep. The cold came up out of the ground through the soles and settled in the bones of his shins. He was hungry, and then at some point in the fourteenth he was not, which was worse; the hands started, the fine fast tremor he woke with, and he let them shake at his sides and did not look at them. His calf knotted on the left. He did not shift his weight. He kept the cut.

In the first division, with the day turned over, there was something left over.

It lay just inside the birch on the east side, on the estate's round. The round had been trodden bare long before any of this. What the dead had shed lay differently on bare ground than on grass, so the round showed in the field as a shape before anything else did. Down its middle the shed had been broken at intervals, at the length of a man's stride when he walks and is not hurrying. He followed the breaks north. They came up the round out of the birch from the south, from beyond the reach of the ground he had built, and stopped short of the stone. Well short. There they stood a while, the ground broken close and several ways at once, as ground is broken by a man standing and shifting his feet. Then they went back south down the round the way they had come.

He went over it again, and there were more of them under the first. The breaks lay over one another on the same line, at the same stride, and their clocks did not agree. The newest were weeks old. Under them lay older, and under those older again, and he could not get to the bottom of them. None went on past the place where they stopped, or followed the round where it bent west along the bound. Nothing of his came near the stone.

He held that, and went through it again from the beginning, and again. The second division. The third. His vision began to go grey at the edges when he blinked, and come back slow. The fourth, and the fifth. Across the rise the fire had sunk to embers. One of them slept.

The sixth division came, and nothing came back with it. Eight divisions. He had counted every one. There was no main out here to return. He stood in the cut with his hands shaking and heard the whole of the country be quiet around him, the burn and the birch and a wren somewhere, and nothing under any of it.

He said, "Come across."

They came round the stone, Yinzhi first, her coat buttoned to the throat and her face grey with the night. Tally stopped a pace behind her and did not come nearer the dead than he had to.

"Somebody came up from the south," Wystan said. His voice came out low and rough and he waited for it and went on. "Along the estate's round. Through the birch. He stopped short of the stone, well short, and stood a while, and went back the way he came. On foot. Not a horse, not a cart. At a walking pace, not hurried. And more than once."

"How many times?" Tally said.

"More than once." He swallowed. "I will not give you a number I cannot swear to. There are more under the ones I can count."

"Who?"

"Somebody. I have his feet. I have nothing else of him."

"The divisions," Yinzhi said. "Can you..."

"They go in the book."

"What did he do?" Tally was looking past him at the birch. "Stood there. Doing what?"

Wystan went back to it. The ground broken close, several ways at once, in the same place on the round each time, short of the stone. A man standing, shifting his feet. It did not come up in the field as anything else, and nothing he had built of the cut would make it more.

"He stood," he said. "I cannot give you what he did standing."

Tally said nothing. Yinzhi was watching Wystan's hands.

Nobody had set the marker for this. It had been there since before the roll, and a man who wanted a line did not need to cut one; he needed only to know where one was. *A boundary means somebody chose one.* He had turned short of the stone so that the dead would lie on this side of it. That was the edge. Tally had found it and checked it twice, and the far side was clean because nobody had come past the stone to make it otherwise.

He turned to look at the birch, and the ground went away from under him.

It did not go far. The grey came in from the edges of his eyes all at once and the cut tipped, and his knee was on the grass and his hand was on the stone, flat, on the cold grit, before he knew he had put it there. His heart was going fast and thin. He stayed where he was and let it come back. Yinzhi had him by the upper arm.

"Legs," Tally said. "You've stood all night. Blood goes to the feet. Put your head down."

He put his head down. After a while the grey went back to the edges and stayed there.

He took his hand off the stone. He had not held it by the edge. He looked at his palm, at the grit pressed into it and a smear of yellow lichen, and found nothing there but stone. Then he sat back against the south face, over the letters, and both calves cramped at once, and he waited that out too.

Tally cut bread and cheese on his palm again and held them out, and this time Wystan took the bread. He took it by the edge of the crust, two fingers and a thumb. It was dark and dense and tasted of the pack, and he ate it slowly, and his hands shook over it, and he did not try to stop them.

When he could, he took out the day book and opened it on his knee at the page with the margin. He wrote the sequence in the plain words he had said it in, and the divisions against each pass as near as the field would give them, and under it, *stood, short of the stone; what, not had.* Beneath *edges* and *ask Tally who set the marker* and *the estate. before the roll.* he wrote two words.

*the round.*

"Where does it go, south?"

"Round the estate. Their ground, the whole of it." Tally slid the knife into its sheath. "It's theirs. It's not my line."

He got up by stages, a hand on the stone, and stood until he was sure of it. Then he walked to where the round came out of the birch, and looked down it.

It went away south between the pale trunks, trodden bare down its middle, no wider than a man, and now that it was light he could see a long way down it.

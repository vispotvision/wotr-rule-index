# Separately

*Greyshaft Nine, the coldhouse, the ice-press room. Day 5, the third division to the eighth.*

The fourth hind had been five days off the rail and Nuvalik had her open on the slate from the point of the shoulder to the second rib, and the shoulder was the only part of her worth a lamp.

She worked with the small knife, the one she kept for seams. Under the blade the tissue came away in sheets, pale where the ice had held it and darker along the fibre where the pour had gone in, and the darker part ran inland from the pour sites in threads no thicker than a horsehair, and she followed them with the point and did not cut them.

The room had no hum in it. It had not had one for four years. What it had instead was the press at the far end going over on its cam, and the drip in the corner, and behind her the sound of Foss turning a page.

The Lord came in at the third division with his coat over his arm. Nuvalik heard the door on the slate side and did not turn round, and behind her Vesk got up off the gauge bench too fast, and a flask went over and rolled, and Vesk caught it in both flat palms against his chest and stood holding it like that.

"Sit down," Malphas said.

Vesk sat down. Malphas folded the coat over the end of the iron bench, beside him and not under him, and sat with his forearms on his knees and looked at the hind's shoulder from eight feet away.

"Nuvalik."

She wiped the blade on her apron before she spoke. "Lord."

"Four days ago you told Vesk that everything the fourth hind gave you is written down."

"I did."

"The book has the fourth hind at three entries. Rail, pour, and the cut on her second day." He said it the way a man reads a bill of lading, without raising it at the end. "Two are in your hand. The third is in Foss's, because you were at the rail."

"That is what is in the book."

"Yes. And what the shoulder did on her second day, and again on her third, is not in it."

The press went over on its cam. Nuvalik set the small knife down on the slate with the edge away from her, squared it to the stone, and left it there.

"No," she said.

Across the room Vesk made a noise that was not a word. She did not look at him. She had known for days what his face would be doing when this came, and it was not worth a lamp either.

"Say what it did," Malphas said.

"Her second day I had her open at the joint to see how far the channels had gone up. I had the scapula clear and the fascia off it in one piece. When I put the point in at the cranial border the muscle came in under my hand." She turned her palm up and closed it, once, slowly, and opened it. "Not a twitch. A draw. It came in and it held a count of two and it let go. I put the point in again at the same place to see whether it was my hand that did it, and it did it again, smaller. Then it did not do it any more that day."

"And her third."

"I came in at the second division and did it again before anybody was up. Once. Smaller than the second one."

"Twice on the second, once on the third."

"Three in all. You will want it as three and not as twice, because twice is what you said to Vesk and twice is wrong."

"Twice is what the Lord had," Vesk said from the bench. "He said contraction. Twice, he said. I have had nothing like it off any of the others and I have had all nine of them through my hands—"

"Vesk," Malphas said.

Vesk stopped.

Malphas had not moved. He had his forearms on his knees and his hands hanging off the ends of them, and from the slate Nuvalik could see the backs of them: the knuckles, the nails, the skin over the tendons. They were the colour of hands. The gauge over the door had stood at four degrees since the first division and her own fingers had gone the colour of tallow inside the first quarter of it, the way they did every day, and his were the colour of hands.

She had thought about it on the second night and been satisfied with it. He came from a warm building, and he sat a division, and he went back to a warm building, and a division was not long enough to take the colour out of a man who was not working in it. Nobody else in the room had ever sat in here without working. There was nothing to compare him to.

"Why is it not in the book," Malphas said.

"Because you would have had me pour her again."

"I would."

"Yes." She took up the knife and did not use it. "So."

"Go on."

"A thing that twitches is a thing that will walk." She said it flat, the way she would have said *the ground is shut*. "Not the same day. Not on the table with four of us round it. But a shoulder that draws on her second day and again on her third is not finished, and I have had my hands in nine of them and this is the first that was not. If it had gone in the book you would have had Vesk pour her again at another dilution, and we would have lost what she was doing in what we did to her. I wanted her let alone. I wanted to see how long she would go on not being finished."

"How long did she."

"She is finished now. I went in at her this morning and there was nothing. Three days, and smaller each time."

"You did not write it down so that I could not spend it."

"I did not write it down so that nobody could spend it. Including me."

Foss had stopped turning pages. Nuvalik heard the jeweller's glass come out of his eye and go down on the bench with a small click, and the stool scrape as he turned round.

"That is a sound decision taken by the wrong person," Foss said.

"It is the decision of the person whose hands were on it."

"Yes, and I made that argument in a room with three chairs in it and was struck off inside the turn." His voice did not go up. It never did. He explained things the way a man explains them when he expects to be explaining them again later, to somebody with the power to undo it. "I held a numbered instrument for eleven years. They did not strike me for a bad reading. They struck me for a rounding convention, which is a thing I did to the record."

"Then the Accord is a fool."

"It is," said Foss. "It is also the only thing in this country that can say what happened."

Malphas lifted his head.

"It goes in the book tonight," he said. "In your hand, dated to her second day and her third, with the divisions, in the words you have just used. Contraction at the cranial border of the scapula, on the point, twice and then once, each smaller. Write that you held it back, and write the date you wrote it. I want the holding back in the book as much as the shoulder."

Nuvalik stood with the knife in her hand. "Yes, Lord."

"And by morning I want it again."

The drip went on in the corner. Vesk had begun counting it under his breath the moment Malphas started speaking, the way he did, and had not stopped, and she could hear the shape of the numbers without the numbers in them.

"Again," she said.

"The eighth hind is on the rail and she came in behind this one. Take her down tonight. Vesk pours at the dilution that went into the fourth, at the same four places. You go in at the cranial border, and you keep going in until something comes in under your hand." He said it without any weight on it at all. "By the eighth division tomorrow I want a shoulder that draws, and I want two hands on it that are not the same two hands, and I want it in the book in both."

"That is not the same thing."

"No. It is the only part of the thing I can get twice, and a thing I can get only once did not happen."

She thought about the fourth hind's shoulder coming in under her palm in a room with nobody in it, and about doing it again on purpose with another man's hand on the same joint and the division called out.

"It will not be as clean," she said.

"It will be witnessed."

She put the knife down, squared it again, and wiped her hands on the apron below where she had wiped the blade.

"Then Foss holds it and I go in," she said. "He has the steadier hands and he has never had them in one, and if it comes in under his hand as well he cannot be told he expected it."

"Good."

"Not good. Right."

The eighth hind came down off the hook at the fourth division. Vesk came without being asked and put his flat palms under the shoulder and took the weight while the hook came out, and said nothing, and went back to the bench for the flask.

It was while he was standing the flask up that Foss put his hand flat on the thing he had been reading all afternoon and slid it along the bench.

"Lord. Before the pour. I have the paragraph."

The room changed. Nuvalik felt it go through her the way she had once felt a district come off the main under her feet, a thing arriving by not being there any more, and she did not look up from the hind's flank and heard both the other men stop moving.

It was a book with a limp grey cover, no bigger than a hand, swollen along the spine from a wet pocket. Foss had it open flat with the glass holding the leaves down.

"You said a season," Malphas said.

"I said a season and I was wrong by a season." Foss's thumb stayed on the page. "I was going to ask men in turn. I did not have to ask anybody. A whole chapter bench has been reading it since the cut, every man looking for the part about himself, and when a thing is in forty pockets in one district it stops being a thing you ask for and becomes a thing you decline. It came up the road in a carter's coat with four other books in the bundle, and the carrier could not have told you what any of them were."

"Whose is it."

"Nobody's. It is a print, not an issue. An issue is numbered and logged against a man's name and goes back when he is struck off or dies or goes to the Bench." He turned it over and showed the back, and the back was grey and had nothing on it. "This is a print somebody took off an issue, badly, with the heads set by eye. No number, no name, no chapter, no mark that says whose desk it came off. I have been through it twice for that. There is nothing in this book that leads to anybody."

"Then read me the paragraph."

Foss read it. He read the heading and the section number first, because he was a man who would have been struck off twice for leaving them out, and then he read it flat, with small pauses where the print was bad.

> *Where a working's authorship is at issue and no witness can be produced, the residue is the witness. The officer proceeds upon the principle that what has been coupled carries the signature of the thing that coupled it, and that the signature runs down and not up. An object handled by the author retains its attribution until the attribution is broken. It is broken in four ways.*
>
> *Seal the object, and the attribution stops at the seal. Interpose a hand that is not the author's, and the attribution attaches to the interposed hand. Let a season pass, and the attribution falls below the floor of the instrument. Move a wall, and the attribution is read against a room that is not the room.*

Nobody said anything. The press went over on its cam.

"Read the last one again," Malphas said.

"*Move a wall, and the attribution is read against a room that is not the room.*"

"Yes."

He did not do anything with it. He sat with his coat folded beside him and did not move, and Nuvalik, watching from the slate with her hands inside the hind, could get nothing off him at all. He had the look of a man checking a bill against a bill he had already paid. She had seen him wear it twice over Vesk's dilutions, and both times what came out the other end of it was three sentences, and every one of them a thing they now did every day.

"Two years designing against that paragraph," Malphas said, "out of what other people told me was in it. Three of the four I had right. One I had backwards. I have had you all touching flasks bare because I believed it ran the other way, up, from the object to the hand." He straightened his fingers once on his knees and let them go. "That is mine and it goes in the book too. House rules from tonight. Foss, write them where a man will see them. On the door."

"First: every flask is Fixatio-sealed when it is made up, and the seal is not broken until the flask is at the table, and it goes on again over what is left. No flask stands open on a bench to temper. If the dilution cannot be tempered inside a seal, Vesk tells me tonight what it needs and I get it."

Vesk was looking at the flask standing in front of him, out of its ice, open.

"It can be tempered sealed," he said. "It is slower. Half a division slower, thereabouts."

"Then it is slower. Second: no live dose is touched bare. Not the flask, not the tundish, not the table under it. Gloves on before the seal comes off, and the gloves stay in the room."

Vesk put both flat palms on the bench on either side of the flask and left them there. Nuvalik watched him do it. The two nails on the fingers that had taken stood up over the edge of the bench, ridged and yellow and grown out again since the fourth day, and he was looking at them the way she looked at a joint she did not like.

"Third: nothing comes from a flask to me, or from the substrate to me, without a second pair of hands between. Vesk makes it up. Nuvalik or Foss carries it. Nuvalik goes into the tissue. I do not touch a live dose and I do not touch the substrate, and if I want a thing lifted, somebody lifts it."

"You have not touched one of them yet," Nuvalik said.

"No."

"You put your hand on the ninth hind's flank on the first night. I watched you do it. She was poured."

"She was poured four divisions before, and I took the hand off her." He said it without any interest in it. "It was a bad habit and this is where it stops. Put all three on the door in a hand a man can read standing at it, Ilmar, and put under them that they are not the reason, and that the reason is in the book."

"The wall," Foss said.

"What about it."

"You have taken three of the four. The season and the wall you have left on the page."

"I have not left them anywhere. They are not house rules, because a man cannot do them on a Tuesday. A season is a thing you wait and a wall is a thing you move, and both are the end of a piece of work and not the running of it." He got up then, and took the coat over his arm, and stood looking at the west wall, where the dead standpipe housings sat in their brackets with nothing in them. "When this work is done, something will be waited and something will be moved. Write them both under the three and put a rule across, so the two do not read as instructions."

"Yes, Lord."

"And while you have the pencil." Malphas turned round. "You came here off a bench with a number on it and a coat you were not allowed to keep, and you have been Foss in this building and nothing else in any paper that leaves it. When the work is done you will be released with your own name. Not a roll-name. Not a made one. Ilmar Foss, on a bench, with hands. I will not ask what you do with it and I will not send anybody to look."

Foss had the pencil in his hand and did not write with it.

"That is not in your gift," he said.

"No. It is in mine to do and not in mine to give. There is a difference and you know it better than I do."

"I do." Foss turned the pencil over once and set it down. "I will want it in the book."

"It is going in the book."

Nuvalik stood with her hands in the eighth hind and the cold coming up through the slate into her wrists, and thought: *released with his own name, and it is the name he had before, and he will pick it up off the bench where he left it.*

Hers had been carried by three people before her. It had come to her whole, with all of them in it, and she had spent what it was worth in this room, on nine hinds and a name for the work and a book with four hands in it. There was nothing to release her to. A name was not a coat. You did not get it back with the buttons still on.

She wiped the blade on her apron.

"The dilution," she said. "If it tempers half a division slower, she is poured at the fifth division and not the fourth, and she stands a division less than the fourth hind did before I go in at her."

"Then the book says a division less," Malphas said, "and does not say they are the same. They will not be the same tomorrow either, and the day after there will be a third thing that is not the same. That is what the book is for. Pour her sealed."

Vesk got the gloves. They were the yard gloves, canvas, stiff with the cold, the only pair in the building, and he stood a long moment with his hands in them and his hands not closing all the way. Then he made the dilution up inside the flask with the seal on, and it took longer than it had ever taken, and nobody said anything about that either.

Nuvalik opened the near flank at four places and stood back. Foss carried the flask from the bench to the table in both hands, because Foss was the second pair, and set it down beside her and took his hands off it. She broke the seal at the table. It came away in a thin rind and Vesk took the rind off her in a dish before it touched the slate, and she put the tundish in and poured at the first place and gave it the count of forty.

The colour went out of the surface and away inland, faster at the two forward pours and slower at the two behind, the way it had in the ninth and in the fourth. Foss called the division. Vesk wrote it. She poured at the second place and watched the channels take it away under the hide toward the emptier end of her, and the room was as quiet as a room with no main under it gets.

Behind her Malphas stood at the door with his coat over his arm, in the part where the lamp did not reach, and stayed there through all four pours, and touched nothing.

"Eighth division," Foss said, when it was done. "And the three on the door before I sleep."

"Before you sleep."

Malphas went out. The door came to on its own weight the way it always did and did not quite shut, and Nuvalik went and shut it, and it was then that she saw that the three of them had not put a hand on one another's work all night, and that this was what he had bought.

She went to the shelf for the book.

She wrote the fourth hind first, both days, the divisions, the cranial border of the scapula, the point going in, the draw, the count of two, the letting go, twice and then once and each smaller. Then the date and the division she was writing in, and under it *held back by me, three days, so that nobody could spend it.* She read it over. It was the first entry in the book in her hand that had her in it at all, and she did not like it, and left it standing.

Behind her Foss was cutting the three rules into a board with a knife, because he said ink on a door in a coldhouse would be gone inside a turn. The small knocks went on while she wrote the eighth hind up in the ordinary way: four places, the count of forty, poured sealed, two hands.

The ninth hind was still on the table at the far end, under sacking, where she had lain since the first night. Nuvalik had written nothing about her in three days because there had been nothing to write. She went down with the lamp and turned the sacking back off the shoulder and stood and looked at the hide over the joint.

Nothing. The hide over the scapula lay the way hide lies.

She put the sacking back, squared it at the corners, and went out to the yard with her hands under her arms. Above the wall the sky had gone clear and hard, and the stars in it stood small and set in a row down toward the river, like nails in a board.

*A thing that twitches is a thing that will walk.* She had said it in a room with the Lord in it and it had cost her nothing, and by the eighth division tomorrow she had to make it true, with two hands on it and the division called out and Foss's pencil going. Her own hands had done it three times already, alone, with nobody to say so, and there was not a man in that building who could ever tell that from her say-so.

Through the wall she could hear Foss's knife on the board, and the press going over on its cam, and under both of them, in the corner where it always was, the drip.

She went back in and took the big knife off the hook to start the ninth hind's hide off her, because a hind that had done nothing in five days was a hind you learned from by opening.

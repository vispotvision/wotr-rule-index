# The Lick-Keeper

*The Ferriby estate's lick round, from the lane behind the home farm north into the birch. Day 7, the seventh division to the tenth.*

They came onto the round at the seventh division, where it left the lane behind the home farm and went north between two ditches. Wystan had wanted it from this end. Called Late had given him a man who came up from the south, and if he wanted the man's road he would walk it in the order the man had walked it.

There was no main under the estate. There had never been one. He stood at the lane's end with his boots on packed earth and nothing came up through the soles at all. At Cutler Row the main went off at the eleventh division and a man could sit and wait for it back. Out here there was nothing to wait for. His shoulders stayed half an inch high, and he let them.

His calves had not forgiven him the night at the stone. The left one still had the knot in it, low in the gastrocnemius where the muscle went over into the tendon, and it took most of the first field to walk it out. Yinzhi went ahead with the coil case on its strap and the satchel of tins knocking at her hip. At the stile she slowed without looking round. She went over when he had caught up.

The first lick stood in the corner of the second field, where the round crossed the headland.

It was an oak post, squared, knee-high, with the top cut out into a hollow and a block wedged down into the hollow. The block was grey salt with a rust-brown streak through it. It had been licked in on all four faces to a waist. Round the post the ground was trodden to mud and printed over with slots. Hinds, mostly. One stag, deeper, the toes splayed.

He did not touch it. He took the day book out of his coat and wrote *lick. oak post. headland. grey salt, brown streak*, and the division after it, and put the pencil away.

"That's what they put their mouths to," Yinzhi said.

"One of the things."

"You asked Tally. Licks, troughs, anything set out."

"I asked him two things." The streak ran down through the salt at a slant. Somebody had poured it in soft and let it set. "Leave it. We'll want the far end first."

He paced the round to the next post under his breath. It came out at half a mile, near enough that a man carrying a load would call it that and not be wrong. The same oak, the same block, licked in the same. The next was half a mile on again, at a gap in a thorn hedge. He stopped counting paces after that because there was no need.

The post after it had a man on his knees in front of it.

He had his back to the round. A carrier's frame of ash lay on the grass beside him with a sack lashed to it, and the sack stood square at the corners with whatever was in it. In his right hand he had a beetle, short in the handle, the head split once and bound round with wire. He was knocking an old block out of the post-top with the side of it, short easy knocks, and he was humming.

He heard them late. He got up in stages, one hand on the post.

He was short and thick through the chest and bowed at the knee. He wore a sheepskin jerkin with the wool turned in, and gaiters of cracked leather over boots packed at the tops with straw. Grey stubble. A knitted cap pulled down to the eyebrows. His hands were broad and split across every knuckle, and the splits were packed white with salt. Round his neck on a thong hung a length of hazel, notched down one edge and cut along its length: one half of a tally.

Wystan took the brass out of his waistcoat and showed it on his palm.

"Night Register."

The man looked at the brass for a good while. Then at Yinzhi, and the bracelet on her wrist, and the coil case. Then at Wystan's hands, which were stained to the second knuckle.

"Register," he said. "Never had the Register out here. Not in my time."

"Still on the main?"

That made him laugh, once, with his mouth shut. "Never was. Not here. Not in my father's time. You'll have come over the ground, master, you'll know there's none."

"I know."

"Josse Garretsohn." He wiped his right hand down the front of the jerkin, looked at it, and did not offer it. "I keep the licks. And you're..."

"The Register."

"Register, then." He nodded. That seemed to settle it for him.

"Tell me the round."

"The round." He turned and pointed up it with the beetle, north, where the ditch ran on toward a line of birch on the rise. "Up from the farm to the birch. A lick every half mile. I set them, and I top them when they're licked down, and at the birch I turn and come home the same road. Same as my father did it. Same posts, most of them. He put that one in." He knocked the post with the beetle's heel. "That one's older than me."

"Why the birch?"

"It's the last." He looked at Wystan as though the question had a trick in it somewhere. "Past the birch the road comes out by the old stone and goes along the bound, west, and that's walls and the bound-ditch. There's no beasts go along there worth the salt. My father turned at the birch. I turn at the birch."

Wystan wrote it down. *turns at the birch.* He underscored nothing.

The old block lay on the grass where the beetle had knocked it. It was white, worn to a stub the length of a thumb, with no streak in it.

"The old ones were white."

"Plain salt, that. These are tonic." Josse went down on one knee by the frame and turned the sacking back. The blocks inside were stacked on their ends, each the size of an assize loaf, grey, with the brown gone through them at the slant. "There's a tonic in with the salt, see, the brown. For the coat and the feet, through the back end of winter. They come out of winter with a better coat on them. New this season."

"Who paid for them?"

"Steward paid. It's on his tally." He lifted the hazel off his chest by the thong and turned it so the notches faced out. He did not take it off. "I set a lick, I cut a notch. The steward's got the other half of this and he cuts his the same, and when we lay the two together I'm paid what's on them. Tonic's a different notch." He put a split thumbnail on one. It had a second nick across it. "So it's clear whose money. Tonic's his money, not the farm's."

"Where do they come from?"

"Barn at the home farm." Josse looked down at the beetle in his hand and knocked a crumb of salt off the head against his boot. "Steward keeps them in the barn, I load up there. Veyra's day, mostly, and out."

"Who makes them?" Yinzhi said.

"Couldn't tell you, miss. They come made."

"Has anybody walked the round with you? Since the tonic."

"With me?" He gave it proper thought. His lips moved a little while he did it. "No. Who'd want to? There's me and there's the beasts. The steward's never been up past the second field in his life, he's got a bad hip, he'd..." He stopped, and grinned, and did not finish it.

The frame creaked where the lashing took the load. Up on the rise a thrush was going over the same few notes. Wystan waited.

"Heard there was beasts dead up the cut," Josse said. "The forester's side. Is it that?"

"Were any dead on your round?"

"Not on my round. I'd know." He looked at the sack. "Is there something wrong with them, master? The blocks?"

"I don't know yet."

Yinzhi looked at Wystan. He did not look back.

"I'll want to see that," he said, and meant the hazel.

Josse put his hand over it flat on his chest. "No. No, that's my half. You don't give your half over. Not to the steward, not to the Register, not to anybody, that's what a half is." He said it quite kindly. "You want to see what's owed, you go and see his."

"When were you last at the birch?"

"Up the top?" He squinted north at the line of trees. "Set a new one there back before the thaw came in. It'll not be due. I'll get up there when I get up there."

"I'm going to take it."

"The block?" He considered the Register, and the girl with the case, and the man's hands. "That's the steward's block."

"The Register will enter it."

"Then I'll set another when I'm up." He shrugged, and it cost him nothing. He knelt again at the post, took a block out of the sack in both hands, bare, and stood it on its end in the hollow he had cleared. He put the heel of his right hand flat on its top and leaned. His whole weight went down the arm into the ball of the thumb, and the block went into the cut wood and stopped. He took up the beetle and gave it two short taps, one on each shoulder of the salt. Then he sat back on his heels and looked at it, and took up the humming where he had left it off.

Wystan watched the hand come away. The thenar pad was white where it had pressed, and the white went back to red while he watched.

Every post on the round had had that hand on it last.

"Mind the ditch at the top," Josse said to their backs. "It's gone soft where the birch starts."

---

The round went up the rise in the last of the fields and in under the birch on the east side. There were more posts, half a mile apart, each with its streaked block licked in to a waist, and at each one Wystan wrote the division and did not stop. The ditch had gone soft where the birch started. He went through it to the ankle and felt the cold come in at the lace-holes.

He knew the place before he reached it.

It was just inside the birch, in a shallow hollow where the round bent a little east round a stand of thicker trunks. From the hollow the cut could not be seen. The rise stood between, and the trees. Somewhere north and west of it, twenty-two paces short of the stone, the last hind was lying where he had stood beside her for eight divisions two days back and called the ground late. Called Late had given him a man who came up this road and stopped here and stood a while. None of the passes had gone further. He had written *the round* in the margin afterwards and looked down it, and it had come here, and here was a post.

It was oak, squared, knee-high, the same as the rest. The block in its top had been licked in hard on every face, harder than any on the round below, and the brown streak showed through on all four sides. The ground round the post was trodden bare. The slots in it were old ones, rained soft at the edges.

North of the post the round went on through the last of the birch toward the stone's south-east corner. There was no post on it after this one. There had never been one. The salt stopped here. The man who carried the salt had put his frame on his back here and gone home down the same road, and the far side of the stone was clean because nobody had ever carried any salt to it.

At the stone he had decided that a walker turned short of the stone so that the dead would lie on this side. He had turned because this was the last post.

Wystan stood and let that be what it was. The dose had gone where it was put. It had been put here, in the birch, in a post a man's father had cut, and it had stopped where the posts stopped. He wrote it under *the round* in the margin, small. *the round's end. the last lick. the birch.*

The trunks round the hollow had hair on them.

He went close to see it and did not touch. On every birch that stood within a few paces of the post there was hair rubbed into the bark, red-brown, winter coat, at about the height of a hind's shoulder. It was on the north side of each trunk and not on the south. On one it had been worked in so hard the bark had gone through to the green. He looked at it for some time. He could not say what a beast did that for, at a lick, facing away from the salt.

He wrote *hair. bark. hind's shoulder. north face of each trunk.* He wrote nothing after it.

"Warden."

Yinzhi had the coil case open on her knee. She had a coil out of its paper and the paper folded back under her thumb, and she ran the coil down the near face of the block without letting it touch. The coil took. It browned from the near end down the whole of its length, and she let it finish.

"It's carrying," she said. She wrote the division on the paper and folded the coil into it and put it in the lid of the case, flat. "Or it was, and it's kept it."

"Enter it."

"It's entered." She looked at his hands. "You've got the gloves."

"I've got them."

He did not take them out. He went down on his right knee in the bare ground in front of the post, where Josse had knelt, where the man in Called Late had stood a while. He did not take the block by the edge.

He put his right hand round it. The palm went flat on the licked face and the fingers closed on the far side, and the flexors in his forearm took up the load as he began to draw it out of the hollow. The salt was tacky. It was hygroscopic and it had been pulling damp out of the birch air for weeks, and the cracks by his nails where the ink lived stung the moment it touched them.

Whatever a working leaves on an object, the last hand to close on the object lies uppermost. It is coupled Aether with the signature of whoever coupled it, a layer of residue over whatever lay under, and his Shell took nothing in of its own and so had room for what was there. The Hand took the uppermost. It had never once taken anything else.

It came.

He was kneeling in the birch with the frame off his back.

The old block came out of the post-top at the third knock, a white stub worn down to nothing, and he picked it out of the hollow with two fingers and dropped it in the sack for the hens at home. The new one was cold from the sack. He stood it on its end in the hollow, and put the heel of his hand on its top, and leaned. It went down into the cut wood and stopped. He took up the beetle and gave it two taps, one on each shoulder of the block, and felt it seat.

His knees were wet through the gaiters and he did not mind it. His back was easy with the frame off. There was dark bread in the front of his jerkin for when he was home, and a thrush up in the birch going over the same few notes, and he was humming with it and a little behind. He looked at the block standing in the post, square and new, grey with the brown gone through, and he was pleased with it. That was all there was in him. It filled him to the edges and there was room left over.

The birch closed north of him. He did not look that way. He had no call to.

He took his hand off the block and reached for the frame by its strap, and his body was already turning, south, for home.

The minute ended where the hand came off.

Wystan was on one knee in the same hollow, with the same block in his fist and a strip of it drawn out of the post, and his own knees wet. He stayed there. He went back through the minute for the edges of it, the way he went back through all of them. A voice behind. A second pair of boots in the mud. A face, a coat, a hand held out with a block in it, a word about where the block had come from or who had paid. There was the thrush, and the wet knees, and the bread. There was nobody else anywhere in it.

He had the hand. It was Josse Garretsohn's.

Garretsohn had carried the salt and set it and gone home content, and had never in his life held anything but a beetle and a block and the half of a tally. The block had come to him made. Before it came to him there had been the barn at the home farm, and a steward who paid, and back behind the steward somebody who had poured the brown into the salt soft and let it set. None of them had been in the minute. None of them would ever be in a minute off this block. The last hand on it was a lick-keeper's, and the Hand gave you the last hand.

He had told Brack he would get a hand and not a purse. He had the hand.

It was the wrong one.

A man who put his dose into salt and let other hands carry it out to the beasts was a man who never went near them. Wystan could see the whole shape of him from here. He worked at a bench somewhere a long way from any post, and he sent his work out through a barn and a steward's money and a lick-keeper's frame, and he stayed where he was. He did not go near them. He did not need to.

He drew the block the rest of the way out of the hollow and held it up in the one hand. It was lighter than a new block by what the beasts had taken off it.

"Tin," he said.

Yinzhi had it open already, the widest one in the satchel, the one the chapter used for soil. He set the block down into it on its end. It went in with a finger's width to spare on two sides and none on the others. She put the lid on and pressed it down all round with the heels of both hands until it seated. He took out the brass wax and warmed the stick in his fist, the left fist, and ran the wax round the join, and struck his brass into it while it was soft.

He opened the day book on his knee. *Register file Ferriby. Tonic block, last lick, the birch, east side of the round. Set by the lick-keeper back before the thaw. Taken bare. Assay of record: carrying.* The division. His initials.

His right palm was still white with salt. The cracks at the nails had gone from stinging to a steady burn. He did not wipe it.

"Eat something," Yinzhi said.

"When we're down."

"Now. You stood all night at the stone on nothing and you're still walking like it." She held out a heel of bread, white, array-ground, out of the satchel with the tins. "Warden."

He took it by the edge of the crust, two fingers and a thumb, with the left hand, and ate it kneeling.

When he had finished he turned to the back of the book, to the pages he kept for them. Keld's stood at the head of the column, where it always stood. Under it were forty-one more in his own hand, the newest a hind in the North Ferriby cut. He entered the forty-third beneath them. *Josse Garretsohn, lick-keeper. The last lick in the birch. Setting it.* He looked at that for a while, and then wrote one word after it, and closed the book on the pencil.

The word was *content*.

He got up off the knee. The left calf caught and let go. Then he took the gloves out of his coat and put them on, both of them, and did up the button at each wrist, and picked up the tin by its rim.

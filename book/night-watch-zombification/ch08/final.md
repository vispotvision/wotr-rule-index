# Rounding

*The Swale road inn, Day 8, the eighth division to the tenth. Greyshaft Nine, the coldhouse, Day 9, the first division. The Swale road, after.*

The Swale road inn was still on the main. Foss had it through his boot soles in the yard, a low even note coming up out of the cobbles, and it was louder in the passage where the flags lay straight on the rock. He stopped inside the door and read the meter. He read every meter he passed. He had stopped apologising for it eleven years ago, along with most things.

It was an old Board pattern with a brass face and the Board's seal whole on the lead at its side. The needle stood a hair under the half. Somebody had put the month's bill on the nail beside it for anyone who cared to look, the draught entered in a clerk's round hand with the figure under it. The landlord owed the Board what was on that paper, and the Board kept a copy of the paper. If the two ever disagreed a man came out with a gauge, and the paper was read against the meter until one of them gave. Foss liked a bill. A bill was a reading with two ends.

He took his ale at the hatch at the fixed price. The sworn taster's chalk was on the barrel head, a day old. He carried the pot to the corner by the hearth, where the carter sat over a bowl of pulse.

The carter was a long man folded onto a short settle, with his knees up near the table's edge. Fifty, or a hard forty. He wore a waxed smock gone brown at the left shoulder where a load strap had crossed it for years, and a jerkin under it with horn buttons, two missing. His hands were big and dry and split along the insides of the fingers where the reins ran. The left thumbnail was black. A whip lay coiled on the settle beside him with its thong wound round the stock. Out in the yard his cart stood tipped back on its stays with the shafts in the air, and his grey cob was in the end stall eating somebody else's hay.

Foss had never had his name. He had not asked for it, and it had not been offered.

"You're late," the carter said.

"The road's soft. Up at the top."

"Road's soft all the way." He ate. "Sit, then."

Foss sat. He set the pot down and took the bundle out of the inside of his coat, where it had pulled the lining down on the left side all the way from Greyshaft. It was rush matting, tied with a hank of the same. He laid it on the board between the bowl and the ale and kept his hand on it a moment longer than he needed to.

"Five," he said. "The last load to the ford, and the one before."

The carter did not count. He picked the tie loose with the black thumbnail and turned the matting back.

The bars lay in it side by side. Grey-white, each the length of a hand and the thickness of a thumb, broken square at the ends where they had been snapped off the sheet at the pans. There was no mark on any of them. Pan salt would not hold a stamp through a wet season, and nobody paid the extra to try.

The carter took a clasp knife out of his smock, opened it against his thigh, and shaved a corner off the nearest bar into his pulse. Then he shaved a second curl onto the flat of the blade and put it on his tongue.

"Coast," he said.

"Coast pans."

"Thought so. Swale's got more grit." He stirred the shaving into the bowl and went on eating.

Foss watched a corner of his payment go into the carter's supper. That was the virtue of the thing. A salt bar was money a man could eat. A silver mark went from hand to hand, and any hand it passed through could be asked where it had come from. A draught went on a bill, and the bill had two ends. A salt bar went into a sack, and then into a pot, and then into a man, and it was gone, and there was no bill for it at either end. Nobody had ever been asked to account for a bar of salt he had eaten.

The carter wrapped the bars again, tied them, and put them inside his smock against his belly.

"He'll want his," he said. "Him at the ford."

"He's had his. Out of those."

"Out of those he has." The carter scraped round the bowl with the spoon, looking at it and not at Foss. "He wants two more off you for the next, on account of the Register."

Foss had the pot halfway up. He put it down.

"The what?"

"Register. Man from the Register's been at the Ferriby lick-keeper. Asking about blocks."

The note under the floor was steady. The fire ticked. Foss found he had set both hands flat on the table, one each side of the pot, and he took them off again and put them in his lap.

"Which register."

"The Register." The carter looked up at him, puzzled. "I don't know which. There's the one. Man from it."

"There is not the one. There's thirty kinds of register between here and the coast. There's the Board's, there's the..." He heard himself and stopped. "What did he say he was from. Exactly. The words."

"I wasn't there, was I. He wasn't there." The spoon went at the ford, somewhere north. "Lick-keeper told him, he told me. Man from the Register. Asked about the blocks. That's what I've got, and that's what you've got."

"Asked what about them?"

"About the blocks."

"Where they came from. What was in..."

"About the blocks." The carter put the spoon down in the bowl. "I'm telling you what I was told, master. I'm not telling you any more than that because there isn't any more than that."

"What did he look like."

"Never saw him. Nor did the man at the ford. Ask me what the Board looks like."

"When?"

"Yesterday. Day before." He shrugged, one shoulder, the brown one. "He had it off the lick-keeper when he dropped the last. Told me at the ford this morning."

Foss drank. The ale was the same ale it had been, assayed and correct. He could not taste it.

"Did the lick-keeper tell him anything?"

"Don't know what he told him. I know what I'm telling you, and that's all of it."

"I want to talk to the man at the ford."

"No."

"I'll pay him for the..."

"You'll not have him off me." The carter said it without heat. "You've not had me. You don't know my name and I don't know yours, and I like it that way, and so does he. You'll have what I bring up the road and that's all anybody on this road ever had off anybody."

The note under the floor went out.

It stopped the way it always stopped, from the rock up, so that the silence arrived under the feet a count before the ears had it. The pot on the table stopped buzzing against the board. Foss had not known it was buzzing. Round the room men looked at the floor and then went back to their bowls.

"Swale," the carter said. "They've had us off at the tenth all this turn."

"It's early."

"It's the tenth." He did not look at any clock. "Two more. For him at the ford."

"Did he say two?"

"He said two."

Foss sat and looked at the bowl, where the salt had gone.

He was thinking about the road.

He had spent a season on this road asking for a book. He had asked carters and chapmen and a woman who sold horn spoons at the crossing below the inn, a little at a time and never twice to the same face, whether anybody had a grey limp-covered thing with the Division's heads in it, because a man he did work for wanted to read a paragraph. And the book had come. It had come up the road in a carter's coat with four other books in the bundle, and the carrier could not have told you what any of them were. Somebody had printed it off an issue, badly, and somebody's issue had gone missing to be printed from, and the Division would want its book back, and the Division would ask on the road. You asked a carter, and he told you what he carried.

*And what they carry up the Ferriby lane is blocks.*

He could see it on the board. The Register had come to Ferriby the way he had come to the book, carter by carter, asking, until it found a man who carried something up a lane.

"I've got two on the cob," he said.

"Get them, then."

He went out into the yard. It was full dark and the cold had come down with it. The meter by the door, as he went past, stood at rest, the needle dropped back onto its pin. He found the two bars in the near saddlebag by feel, under the spare coil and the glass in its case, and brought them in and put them on the board. The carter tucked them into the smock with the others without looking.

"Same night, next turn," Foss said.

"If there's a next."

"There'll be a next."

"You could wire, if it's a hurry. There's a wire office at the crossing."

"A wire is nine words," Foss said, "and they keep the flimsy at both ends for a turn."

The carter grunted. He had finished the pulse. He took the loaf off the board, an assize loaf, dark, and broke it, and shaved a curl off one of the bars in his smock onto the torn face of it without taking the bar out, and ate that too.

Greyshaft Nine had no note under it. The main had been capped four years, and Foss had long ago stopped expecting anything from the floor when he came in. He came in stiff from the saddle at the first division of the ninth day, and he noticed the lack of it anyway, in his soles, for the first few steps.

The gauge over the door stood at four degrees.

The board hung on its nail beneath it, where he had put it up on the fifth day. He had cut the three house rules into it with a knife, one to a line, and under the third a rule across the width of the board, and under that the other two, a season and a wall, so that nobody would read them for instructions. The knife had skipped once in the S of season. He had meant to go back and dress it.

The ice press worked along the east wall, and the air coming off its housing was warm on the right side of his face as he went down the room. Along the rail the sheets hung over what was on it. The lamp over the table was out. Somebody had left the gauge bench squared, his glass in its box, the coil in its paper, a clean cloth over the housing with the slate back off. In the far corner the drip went on into its pan.

Malphas was on the iron bench under the dead standpipes, sitting forward with his forearms on his knees and his hands hanging between them. His coat lay folded beside him and not under him. He did not look round when the door went. He looked round when Foss had come far enough into the lamplight to be seen.

Foss told it.

He told it the way he would have given a reading to a man who meant to check it. The inn, the division, five bars and two more. Then the carter's words as near as he had them, and then again, because he had got one of them wrong the first time.

"He didn't know which register," Foss said. "He didn't know what the man looked like. I asked all of it. I got nothing past the first sentence."

Malphas said nothing for a while. The drip went twice.

"I asked to talk to the man at the ford," Foss said, "and he wouldn't have it, which is..."

"Which is correct of him."

"Yes."

The drip. Foss counted it without meaning to, and made it twenty-two, and stopped.

"Ferriby stops," Malphas said. "For a season. Nothing more goes up that lane, and nobody goes up it to say that nothing more is going, and the man at the ford is not told anything, because a man who is told a thing has been told it and can say so after, where a man who simply has no crates at the ford this turn has nothing to say to anyone. What is out on the posts stays on the posts. It is licked away where it stands."

"The keeper will notice."

"The keeper will notice that the tonic did not come one turn. He will put plain salt back in his posts, which is what was in them before, and think no more of it than that."

"The one they've got," Foss said. "The Register. If they've taken one off a post..."

"Then they have a block. Blocks are what the lane carried. They will have the lane, and at the end of the lane a man who sets salt for his living and was paid to set it, and he is exactly what he says he is." Malphas turned his hands over, palms up, and looked at them, and turned them back. "The licks go to the Swale walks."

Foss had been ready for the rest of it, and not for that.

"The walks," he said. "The whole... That's not a lane. There's a keeper to every walk and a different carter at every crossing. It's six carters, it's more than six, it's..." He stopped, because Malphas was waiting, and made himself say it in order. "It's more hands. Every hand's a face that's seen a crate."

"Yes."

"It'll want three times the bars."

"Then it wants three times the bars. Salt is the one thing on the Swale nobody has ever asked the price of." Malphas sat back a little, the first time he had moved his shoulders. "One lane with one man at the top of it is a line, Ilmar, and a line can be walked from one end to the other by anybody with the patience to walk it, and the Register, it seems, has the patience. The walks are not a line. Nobody walks the whole of the Swale. I want it written that the licks go out on the walks where the keepers already set salt, one block to a keeper to begin with, and that the Ferriby lane is stopped from the ninth day for a season, and why."

"Why."

"On a carter's word. Written as the carter said it, in your hand. What he said. Nothing he did not say."

Foss went to the gauge bench and turned back the cloth. The book was under it, where it was always put, with the pencil in the spine. He opened it at the ribbon and wrote the date and the division, and then stopped with the pencil up.

The board was on the door behind him. He did not need to turn round to read it. He had cut it. *Wait a season* was under the rule across, where it had been put so that it would not be read as an instruction. It was the end of the work. Now it had come off the board and been laid on one lane, with the rest of the work going on around it. He turned it over twice. It was not a contradiction. The paragraph said to wait a season. It did not say what to wait a season on.

He wrote. *Ferriby lane stopped from the ninth day for one season. Carter's word, Swale road inn, the eighth day, the ninth division: "Man from the Register's been at the Ferriby lick-keeper. Asking about blocks." Nothing further known. Licks to the Swale walks.* He wrote *I. F.* at the foot, and then, because it was his hand and it was in the book, he could not leave it there.

"It'll have been the road," he said.

Malphas did not answer.

"I asked on that road for a season. For the book. I asked a spoon-woman. I asked every carter who'd stand still. The book came off an issue, they'll want their issue, and if they came asking on the road they'd hear everything else the road carries. That's how they got to the lane. That's me. It ought to be in the book."

"You will write what the carter said," Malphas said, "and not what you think of it. What you think of it is not a reading."

"No."

"Put the pencil in the spine."

Foss put the pencil in the spine.

His fingers had gone white at the nails in the time it had taken to write four lines. They always did in this room. He stood with the book shut under his hand and looked at them, and then, because he was a man who looked at things, he looked across at Malphas's hands, still hanging between his knees under the dead standpipes in the lamplight.

They were the colour of hands. The gauge over the door said four degrees. He had had that housing open himself and trusted it to the degree. He looked at the knuckles, where the colour goes first. It had not gone. He had no instrument for it and nothing he could lay against it, and after a while he stopped looking.

"Go home," Malphas said. "Sleep. Next turn you take the carter the walks."

He rode home by the Swale road because it was the long way, and he wanted to see it.

It was light by the third division, grey and flat, and the cob went along at a walk with her head down. On his left the ground fell away to the Swale, and past the river the forest went up in walks, each one a ride cut straight up the slope with a keeper's hut at its foot. He began to count them and stopped at nine. The count was not what mattered. Every ride had a crossing at its foot, and every crossing had a carter who came up to it and a carter who went on from it, and every one of them would want his bars.

He worked it as a tolerance. One lane, one man at the top, five bars and two. Then the walks: a block to a keeper, a carter to every two or three walks, none of them knowing the others. He had no figures for it. He put letters to it in his head and ran it, and it came out large and loose and very hard to walk from one end to the other.

The first hamlet at the Swale head had a standpipe at the corner of its green. Iron, waist-high, capped, the cap rusted to the pipe. A woman went past it to the well with a rope and a bucket and did not look at it. Nobody here had been on the main for years. Nobody here had a bill. They would take salt, though. Everyone took salt.

He found he was rehearsing.

He had not meant to. It came up out of him on the road the way it always came up, in the order it always came in, polished to the point where he could have said it asleep, and mostly had.

*The Oath's tables carry every figure to the third place and enter it at the second. A five in the third place goes to the even figure in the second. That is the Oath's convention. The instrument I was issued was ruled by its maker to carry a five up. I read the instrument I was sworn to. For eleven years every figure I entered was correct to the instrument.*

He stopped there and went back over *every*.

*For eleven years the figures I entered were correct to the instrument. As a rule.*

That was worse. He put *every* back.

He had given it to the examiners and to a clerk of the Oath and to a woman he had once thought he might marry. He had given it to Vesk, drunk. He had never yet given it to anyone who was asking. He tried it now for someone who was: somebody at a table, with a book open and a pencil, and no face, because he had none to give him. Somebody the Register sent. The Register had walked up one lane. It could walk up another.

*The Oath's tables carry every figure to the third place.*

It held. He ran it a third time and it held. The only thing that moved in it was that word, and he left the word where it was.

When the work was done he would be released with his own name. It was in the book. He had watched it written. He found, riding, that he did not know which name that would be: the one on the Oath's roll with a line through it, or the one on the bench at Greyshaft Nine, the one with hands. Either way, somebody at a table would ask him how he had come to be struck, and he would have the answer ready. It was the one thing he had ready.

Past the hamlet the road came down close to the river, and one of the walks came down to it on his own side of the water.

There was a lick at the foot of the ride. An oak post, squared, knee-high, with the top cut to a hollow and a block wedged in it. Two hinds stood at it in the grey light with their heads down. They lifted their heads and looked at him and did not run, and one of them put her head down again and went on licking.

He reined in. The cob stopped and blew.

The block in the post was plain salt, white, licked in on all four faces to a waist. He sat and looked at it until the hind had finished and gone back up the ride. Then he took out the little book he kept for readings and wrote down which walk it was.

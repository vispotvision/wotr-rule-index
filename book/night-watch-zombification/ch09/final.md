# Two Myriads

*The Arbitration Division's northern office: the archive, the wire room. Then the Timberline chapter bench, two streets east. Day 10, the eighth division to the eleventh.*

The archive had the front of the first floor, over the day desk, and the main ran under its boards the same as it ran under the scullery at the back. Wystan came up the inner stair at the eighth division with the day book under his arm. He had slept four divisions. His hands had shaken at waking and had stopped by the time he had his hair tied back, which was about usual.

The keeper sat at a table inside the door with black sleeve-guards to the elbow. The ball of his right thumb was blue from the roll. He did not look up.

"Warden."

"The schedule of metered draw. The northern one."

"Which Board?"

"Whichever has Verdantia."

The keeper put his pen in the tray and went back between the presses. He was a while. Wystan stood and listened to the hum come up through the boards, low and even, and to the keeper's feet going away, stopping, coming back.

The book was a quarto in a canvas cover, the corners gone soft and brown where hands had taken it. The keeper set it down square to the table's edge.

"That's the current schedule, Warden. Came down at the quarter."

He sat and drew the roll toward him, and put his feet back under the table, where a canvas bag with the capital's ticket on it lay on its side with the string still round the neck. He dipped his pen. "Whose name?"

"Night Register."

The keeper entered it under the last line in ink, with the press-mark and the division, and turned the roll so Wystan could see it was right. It was right. Wystan took the schedule up by the edge of its cover, two fingers and a thumb, and carried it to the reading table under the window.

He opened it at the fore-edge and turned the leaves by their corners. It ran Board by Board, and under each Board by working, and under each working by dilution, a line to a dilution, the rate at the meter in one column and the standing charge in the next. Verdantia had three leaves near the back. Third dilution was four lines down the first of them. He did not copy the rate out. He laid the side of his left forefinger under it and left it there.

The day book he opened at the back, past the minutes, on a clean leaf.

The dilution he had from the assay. The count he had from Tally, in Tally's own hand on the Society's cheap rag, and Lyle had said it back across her desk on the first day while a clerk wrote it down: eleven hinds and a stag. The weights he had to go and find. The Bureau's field sheets gave length and girth for four of the Ferriby dead and weight for none of them, so he took a spring hind and a spring stag off the forester's table at the back of the Society's almanac, which carried them on two lines, and wrote both down with the source beside them.

Under that he wrote the count.

Eleven hinds.

A stag.

He priced every carcass whole. It was too much and he knew it was too much, because nobody had told him how far into a hind the dose went and he would not guess at it. He entered nothing for what a pour lost between flask and flesh. He had no figure there either. Then he took the weight into tissue, and the tissue into measures at the dilution, and the measures to the rate, a line at a time down the leaf, in the small upright hand he kept for figures nobody else would read.

It came out at two myriads and a fraction.

He turned to a fresh leaf and did it the other way about. He priced one hind to the measure and multiplied her by eleven, and put the stag on his own line, and added. The pen caught once on a down-stroke where the salt had opened the skin at his right nails three days before. He waited until it stopped catching and went on.

The second leaf came out where the first had. The fraction was the same fraction, out to the last place he had carried it.

Two myriads.

He sat back. The hum went on under the boards. Down the room a day clerk read at a slope with her chin on her fist and had not turned a leaf since he came in.

On the first day, at the ninth division, in the upper passage by the bleed, he had told Brack he would get a hand and not a purse. The purse had had no size then. It had one now, on two leaves, in his own hand, and he had not gone looking for it.

So he went through it.

A contract ended at a meter on a wall with a Board's seal on the lead, and the meter turned, and at the month the Board sent the bill, and the bill was paid or the draw was cut. None of that was in question. It was inside the door of every house in the city with a main behind it. A man could put his dose into salt and let other hands carry the salt out to the beasts and never go near them at all, and he would still have to stand at a meter, or send somebody to stand at one for him, because there was no other way to come by it at the dilution.

*Two myriads and a fraction had been bought over a meter, at the tariff, and somebody had paid the bill when it came.*

He held that and turned it over. A man who paid a bill that size did not carry it in his head. He had a clerk. There was a copy of it somewhere, and the copy had a line above the figure with a name on it.

And the draw went up to Logistics. Every contract's draw went up to Logistics and Supply against the line it came off, and Logistics kept it by the line and published it by the quarter, and in between it sat in a room in the capital with everything else.

The map of the northern arrays hung at the far end of the archive on two rollers, the linen gone yellow, the lines inked blue and named in a copying hand. He left the day book open and went down to it. He found the Ferriby cut and held his thumb off the surface and measured out from it: a day's ride, as the Bureau's riders reckoned one, no further.

Four lines came inside the thumb. The Swale, with the road down its side. Aysgill. Wendle, where the river bent back on itself. Scarth, on the high ground south.

He had no reason to prefer any of them to any other. The Swale was a blue line with a name on it and a road beside it, and he had nothing for it that he did not have for Scarth. He copied the four into the margin of the leaf in the order they came off the cut, nearest first.

The keeper took the schedule back without opening it, entered it in, and carried it to the returns shelf.

The wire room was at the back of the ground floor where the main came into the house, and it was the warmest room in the building off the heat of the housing. The boy at the key was young enough to sit his stool with his feet hooked on the rung. He read Wystan's face at the door before he looked at the slip.

Wystan had written it on the stair. At the counter he read it over and counted it out on the fingers of his left hand, thumb first, the way he counted anything he was about to pay for.

*Draw records requested: Swale, Aysgill, Wendle, Scarth. Night Register.*

Nine.

The Register's form put the house at the head of the slip in the boy's hand, outside the count. He had never put his name on a wire. The boy chalked the Logistics address off the board behind him and sent it, his wrist going on the key in short falls. Wystan sat on the bench against the wall and listened to the housing.

It came back inside the division.

The boy wrote it out in block capitals on a fresh slip with the time at the foot and pushed it across the counter without looking up.

*Returns publish on the schedule and not before. Logistics.*

Wystan counted it on his fingers. Nine. The ninth was the desk's. Whoever had sent it had signed with the office, the same as he had.

He read it twice more. There was nothing in it to answer. The schedule was what Logistics published, which was a return to the quarter, and the wire had not offered to say which day of the quarter the Swale column would come out on. He folded the slip once and set it in the day book at the leaf with the arithmetic.

Yinzhi had pushed a note under the scullery door on a torn half-leaf of the chapter's rag, the sixth division pencilled at its foot. *Bench, after the eighth. Bring nothing.*

He had brought nothing. The Ferriby tin was on the sill under his brass with the birch block's tin beside it, and he had looked at the two of them on his way up the stair and left them where they were.

The Timberline chapter kept its bench two streets east, up an outside stair over a harness-maker's, one long room with a north light and a stone floor. It was on the main. The meter was inside the door where a meter ought to be, the dial behind glass and the Board's seal on the lead at the joint. Wystan stopped and read the dial, which was not his business, and went on in.

Yinzhi had her sleeves pinned back and a canvas apron over her coat. There was a covered pail at her feet and the chapter's book open on the bench corner, the leaves dense with her close hand.

"You're early."

"I sent a wire. There was nothing left to wait on."

"Shut the door, the draught gets into the... just shut it." She did not look up. "Pig. The shambles at the bottom of the street stuck it at the sixth and I've had the pail since. Fresh. It has to be fresh, that's the whole of the trouble with it."

"Whose pig?"

"The shambles'. They sell it for pudding." She wiped her hands down the apron. "It is on the chapter's day-book as bought and what it cost. Before you ask me."

"I was going to ask you."

"I know."

She had a stoppered flask standing in a rack at the middle of the bench, and what was in it was clear and looked like nothing at all.

"That's it?"

"That's it. From my reading in your scullery, from the coil, not from your tins. The tins are shut and I have not been near them and it is in the book that I have not." She said it flat and fast, as if she had rehearsed it on the stair. "I worked it out of my own figures. If somebody stands up at a hearing and says the assay could not say what she read, I want the answer to be that she made it twice."

"Twice."

"Yesterday and today. Yesterday it did nothing, and I had it wrong, and the wrong one is in the book as well."

"Good."

She looked up at that, and then away.

"It does nothing in the flask," she said. "It has done nothing in the flask for a day and a half. I have had it warm, I have had it cold, I have had it standing in the light. Nothing. Not a trace of it, not a..." She stopped and started again. "A weak thing does a little. This does none."

"And in the pail."

"Watch the pail."

She took the cover off. Wystan had the smell of it at once, iron and warmth and the shambles, and under it something green at the back of his tongue, which he had had before and had not written down then either.

She put a very little of the flask into the pail with the end of a glass rod, and stirred it twice, and stood back with her hands behind her.

Nothing happened for the length of a slow count. Then the surface went from red to a duller red, from the middle out, the way a skin comes over milk off the boil, and the whole of it drew in a little from the sides of the pail. A ring of wet showed round it on the tin. The colour kept going while he watched it and did not stop going.

"There," she said.

"How long does that run?"

"I don't know yet. Yesterday's did not run at all so I have one number and it is from an hour ago." She picked up her pencil and put the time in the book. "It wants blood. Not damp, not heat, not standing in the light. Blood, and it crosses over in blood, and once it has crossed it goes on in the meat with nothing left to hold it up."

Wystan stood over the pail with his hands in his coat pockets and looked at the ring of wet on the tin.

"The tin at Ferriby has gone on running under a seal," he said.

"I know what the tin has done." She had the pencil still. "I am not putting that in the chapter book off one pail of pig at one hour, and you would not let me if I tried. The pail is a pail."

"No. Write the pail."

"I am writing the pail." She wrote it. "I am not writing yours."

He let that sit. She had her head down over the book and her wrist across the page, and the regulation bracelet had come forward on it out of her cuff, the badly struck mark on the plate turned in toward the skin. She wrote four lines, and did not look at it, and did not shift her wrist.

"Nothing stood up in there," he said. "Nothing leaned."

"No. It isn't that." She kept writing. "That's the other half of it and it is not in the pail. This is only the part that starts."

"Then write only that."

"What do you think I'm doing?"

He went along the bench to the window and stood out of her light. Below in the yard the harness-maker had a hide over a trestle and was working at it with both arms. The green was still at the back of Wystan's tongue. He held it there and put nothing against it, because he had nothing to put, and it was not the room, and he had not touched the pail.

"There was a price today," he said.

"Of what?"

"Of the pour at Ferriby. All of it. At the tariff."

She set the pencil down in the spine of the book. "Say it."

"Two myriads and a fraction."

Yinzhi said nothing for a moment. Then she took her hands off the bench and put them under her apron, which he had seen her do once before, in the scullery, when he had told her what would be said about her at a hearing.

"For deer."

"For eleven hinds and a stag."

"Two myriads." She turned and looked at the meter by the door, and then at the draw-tap at the end of the bench over the stone trough, and back at him. "The chapter draws at third for a week and it is on our bill at the month and the bill comes to the secretary and the secretary complains at me about it. At the month. For a week." She stopped. "Who has a bill like that."

"Somebody does."

"Somebody paid it."

"Yes."

"Then it is on a page somewhere with his name over it." She had come up two steps toward him and stopped. "Wystan. That is the kind of thing your Division is for."

"I asked Logistics for the draw on four lines this afternoon."

"And?"

He took the slip out of the day book and gave it to her, and she read it and gave it back.

"Nine words," she said.

"Nine."

"How long is the schedule?"

"It is a return to the quarter. I don't know the day."

"So you wait until a room in the capital has had its quarter." She went back down the bench to the pail, and put the cover on it, and stood with her hand flat on the cover. "It will have gone cold by then."

"It will have published by then," he said. "That is a different thing."

The chapter's shutters were open on the north light and the light had gone the colour it went at the tenth. Wystan read her four lines in the book, holding the leaf by its corner, and did not follow the middle of them, and read them again. He signed nothing. It was her book and her hand and her reading, and that was the whole use of it.

He went back to Cutler Row at the eleventh.

The day desk was down to one clerk and the lamps were lit. He wrote the request out on the Register's form at the end of the counter, standing, because sitting down at the day desk invited a conversation.

A standing request against the Swale line of the northern arrays. The quarterly Logistics return for the line, and the draw entered against it, to be brought to the Night Register on the day of its publication. Not when it was asked for again. On the day.

He put nothing in it about Ferriby, and nothing about the other three lines, and no figure of any kind. He signed it the way the Register signed, *Night Register, Cutler Row*, and no name, and struck his brass into the wax while it was soft, with his elbow on the counter and his left hand pressed over the back of his right so that the tremor went down into the counter instead of into the seal.

"Standing," the clerk said, reading it upside down.

"Standing."

"Against the one line."

"Against the one line."

The clerk turned to the registry roll and entered it in ink: the date, the division, the office, the line, and *standing* in the last column. He wrote out the counterfoil, tore it off along the rule, and slid it across.

Under the floor the main went off.

It went the way it always went, down through a half-note and then out, and the boards under Wystan's boots stopped carrying anything, and the lamp by the clerk's hand was suddenly the loudest thing in the room. The clerk said what he said about it every night at the eleventh, and Wystan did not answer him.

He took the counterfoil down the passage to the scullery in the dark he knew by the walls. The tin was on the sill under his brass where he had left it, and the block's tin beside it, and the fold of chapter wax squared to the edge.

He set the third lamp going and opened the day book at the leaf with the two myriads on it. The wire's slip was already at that leaf. He laid the counterfoil in beside it, squared the two of them to the gutter, and wrote in the margin, in the division's figures, the day the request had been entered and the line it stood against.

Then he sat with his hands flat on the leaf and read the last column of it again, where the clerk's hand had put the one word, and the word said *standing*.

# The Ninth Hind

*Greyshaft Nine, the coldhouse. Day 12, the ninth division to the twelfth.*

Vesk made the morning's flask up under the rail with the seal already on it, the way the board on the door said, and it went as slowly as it had gone every day since the fifth. A sealed make-up could only be worked by tipping. He tipped the flask and waited for the heavy fraction to come round the shoulder of the glass, and tipped it back. In the far corner the drip came off the ceiling. He had it at twenty-two without trying.

The gauge over the door said four degrees, which it had said since the first division.

Deming had come in at the ninth division off the Swale road with river mud to his ankles and his hat already in his hands. He had stood on the slate inside the door and moved his weight from one foot to the other and back, and then again, slower. Vesk knew what that was. Men off a main stood in Greyshaft Nine feeling for the floor, because every floor they had ever stood on had carried the hum up into the soles, and this one carried nothing. There was no meter by the door. There was a square of cleaner brick where one had been.

"It is very quiet," Deming had said, to nobody, in the voice he used for things he meant to have remembered.

Nobody had answered him. Then Malphas had said, from the iron bench under the dead standpipes, that Nuvalik would read him the book.

She had read it standing at the shelf over the press, from the first night, with the lamp on the shelf beside her and her thumb in the gutter. Deming had listened with his hat held at his belt and his thumb going round the brim, slowly, the whole way round and then back the other way. She read him the pour: four places along the near flank, the count of forty between, two hands. She read him the three entries on the fourth hind and the one in her own hand that said what she had kept back and for how long. She got as far as the fifth day.

"That will do," Malphas said.

She shut it.

"I should like it written," Deming said, "that I have heard it. That I was read it, here, and heard it." He considered. "Not the... not what it said. That I heard it."

"Foss," Malphas said.

Foss wrote it at the gauge bench, in the warm air off the press, and blotted it. That was a division and more ago. Deming had not been asked to go and had not gone. He sat on the end of the gauge bench where Foss had made room, with his hat on his knee. He looked at the rail, and away, and at the rail again. He did not look at the table at all.

Vesk tipped the flask. Waited. Tipped it back.

The ninth hind lay on the slate table in the middle of the room under two lengths of sacking. Nuvalik had had the big knife to her on the fifth night and taken the hide off the near shoulder and down the flank as far as the last rib, and stopped there. She had said she could see what she wanted to see with that much off, and the rest of the hide was holding the rest of the hind together, and she was not going to take away the one thing doing any work. The sacking lay over the open side. Where it had slipped at the withers the lamp found the shoulder, bare and drawn and the colour of a plum's inside, the long muscle over the blade lying the way it had lain for a week.

Nuvalik sat on the stool by the table's head. She had the small knife and a rag and was working the rag along the flat of the blade, point to heel, the same stroke, over and over. She had sat there part of every night since the fifth. Vesk had stopped asking her why.

Across the room the Lord sat with his forearms on his knees.

His coat lay folded on the bench beside him, and not under him. He had been sitting that way since Deming came in, and in all that time Vesk had not seen him shift. The lamp on the west wall hung on a nail between the second and third housing. It lit his hands from the side where they hung between his knees.

They were the colour of hands.

Vesk looked at them for longer than he meant to. The press was on the east wall. Its warm air went along the east wall and stayed there, and Foss sat in it, and Foss's hands were pink with it to the second knuckle. The iron bench was the whole width of the room away from the press, against the brick, under the capped iron of three standpipes nobody had troubled to strip. Vesk's own hands, inside the yard gloves, had gone past aching an hour since. The two grafted fingers were only a stiffness at the ends of the gloves.

He looked at the flask instead. It wanted tipping. He tipped it.

*The drip,* he thought, and found it, and had it at twenty-two.

The sacking moved.

It was a small movement. The fold at the withers slid a finger's breadth toward the table's west edge and stopped. Nuvalik's rag stopped on the blade.

Then the near foreleg drew up.

It came up off the slate with no hurry in it, the knee folding, the hoof lifting clear by the height of a hand, and it stayed there. Under the lamp the bare shoulder changed. The long muscle over the blade went shorter from the back end forward, in bands, one band and then the next. The shoulder blade came round under it.

"She's up," Vesk said. It came out much too loud and rang off the slate. "She's... Nuvalik, she's..."

"She is not up." Nuvalik wiped the blade once more, point to heel, and set the knife down on the table's edge by the hind's ear. "She is drawing. Get the sacking off her, Foss. Don't touch her."

Foss was already off the gauge bench. He took the sacking by its far corners and drew it off toward the foot of the table and dropped it on the floor behind him, and the whole near side of her was there in the lamp. Vesk had seen her opened. He had not seen her opened and moving. The flank from the shoulder to the last rib shortened, band by band, toward her spine and toward her head both at once, and the ribs under it tipped with it, and the hind rolled.

She rolled off her side onto her breastbone, with the foreleg still drawn up, and her head came up off the slate after the rest of her. It hung. The ears did nothing.

"Get the lamp on her," Nuvalik said.

Vesk had the flask. He stood holding it in both palms with the gloves flat under it, because his hands would not close round it, and he did not set it down.

Foss unhooked the west lamp and held it out at arm's length over the slate. He had taken the glass out of his coat with the other hand. He did not use it. He held it, and watched.

"Where is it pulling from," Deming said. He had stood up. "Where is it..."

"Everywhere," Nuvalik said. "It is pulling from all of her." She had not moved off the stool. "Look at the flank. Every run of that net is getting shorter. It is shorter on the side toward the wall than on the side toward us, so she goes toward the wall. She is meat with a new net in her, and the net is shortening, and a deer that has got shorter on one side in every part at once..." She stopped, and put her hand flat on the slate by the knife, a hand's breadth off the hind. "That is what it looks like. Legs."

The hind put the drawn foreleg down on the slate. The hoof scraped. It found nothing to grip on the wet stone and slid a hand's breadth west and held. Then the off fore came under her, and the hindquarters came up in two pulls, the near side and the far side, and the weight went back over the hocks, and she was on all four feet on the table.

The ninth hind stood up off the table.

Nobody said anything. Vesk heard his own breath go out through his nose and nothing come in after it for a while.

A deer stands with its head carried and its ears going. The ninth hind stood square on four legs locked at every joint, with her head hanging below the line of her back and her tail hanging down. The drawn tissue over the scapula stood out in the lamp in a hard ridge. The unopened side of her, under the hide, had drawn up the same, and the hide was too long for it now and hung in a loose fold under the belly.

And she leaned.

Her feet stayed where they were. The rest of her went over, slowly, from the feet up, west, the whole body tipping out past the line of its own legs until Vesk thought she would come down off the table on her side. She did not come down. The near fore slid on the stone after her and caught, and she leaned further.

Vesk followed the line of it with his eyes, across the table's edge and the stretch of floor beyond, and it went to the west wall. It went to the corner, where the last of the housings stood and the iron bench stood under it.

The Lord had lifted his head. He was looking at the hind the way he looked at the book when Nuvalik read to him, with his forearms still on his knees.

He stood up.

He did it without any hurry. He picked the coat up off the bench, with one hand, and laid it over his arm, and came round the end of the bench and along the wall toward the door, keeping to the wall. His boots made almost no sound on the slate. He passed behind Foss with the lamp. He passed Deming. He did not look at the hind as he went, and he did not look away from her, and at the door he turned and stood with his back to the board, the three rules cut into it over his shoulder, and his coat over his arm.

Vesk understood it, and was glad to. The Lord was a man with manners. He had stood at the door just that way through the eighth hind's pours, with the coat over his arm, out of the hands' way. The third rule on the board behind him said nothing came from the substrate to him without a second pair of hands between, and here was the substrate coming to him across the slate by itself, with no hands between at all. *He's giving her the room,* Vesk thought. *He's giving the room to the work.* It was what a man of any breeding did, when the work was on its feet and he was sitting in its road.

She did not turn toward the door. Her line went on to the corner, and to the iron housing standing up out of the floor there, waist-high, capped, with its dead gauge behind its glass.

Her near fore went off the edge of the table.

It went down into the air and found nothing, and the whole long body came tipping over the edge after it. She came down the height of the table in a slither, the fore-legs taking it and folding and straightening, and the hind-legs coming off the slate one and then the other, and a hoof striking the table's edge with a flat knock that went round the room. Then she was on the floor.

Deming was at the door. Vesk had not seen him go. He had his hat crushed against his chest in one hand and his other hand on the frame, and he was looking at the hoof that had struck the table's edge, at the white chip it had left in the slate. His mouth was open. The Lord moved half a pace aside for him, and Deming went past him and out into the yard, and they heard him in the yard at once, being sick against the wall, and then being sick again.

Nobody went after him.

The hind came across the floor toward the corner. She did not walk it. Every part of her got shorter toward the corner and her legs went under her because they were fastened to the rest, and they caught her, and she went on, a foot at a time and then a longer way at once, and then a foot at a time. Her head swung low under her. Her tail swung. The loose hide under her belly swung the other way. The hoofs on the slate made no pattern Vesk could have counted, and he found that he was counting anyway, and that he had lost the drip.

He could not find it. He listened for it in the far corner and heard the hoofs and Foss's breathing and Deming out in the yard, and no drip.

"Eleven feet," he said. He did not know he was going to say it. "I said eleven feet, on the first..."

"Quiet," Nuvalik said.

The hind reached the housing.

She came up against it with her drawn shoulder first. The iron took her weight. The lamp was behind her now, in Foss's hand, and her shadow went up the brick and over the capped top of the housing and across the dead pipe above. She leaned there. The bands in the bare shoulder went on shortening against the iron a little way, and a little way more, and then they did not.

Her legs folded under her. She went down along the housing's side, slowly, the way she had come up, the hindquarters first and then the fore, until she lay on her breastbone on the slate with her chest against the foot of the iron and her neck laid along the brick where the housing met the wall.

She lay still.

Vesk waited for her to draw again. He stood with the flask in his palms and waited through a long count with no drip in it. Nuvalik had come off the stool at last and stood a pace behind the table's end with her hands at her sides. Foss held the lamp.

The hind did not move. The ridge over her scapula had gone down. The muscle lay over the blade the way muscle lies on a dead beast that has been dead a long time.

"She's done," Vesk said. It came out quieter. "Is she... is that her done?"

Nobody answered him, and after a while Nuvalik took the small knife off the table and wiped it, point to heel, on the rag.

"Look at the housing," Foss said.

He had brought the lamp down to it. He crouched with the lamp in one hand and his glass in the other and held the glass to the housing's face, and the lamp behind it, so that the gauge behind the housing's own glass stood out large. The needle sat against its low stop with dust along its top.

"That's where it's sat four years," Foss said. "Since the main was capped. That's the lowest reading in the building. It has been the lowest reading in the building every day any of us has been in it, and I could have told you on the first night which way she'd go if anybody had asked me which way." He did not look round. "Nobody asked me which way. I want that in the book as well."

"Asked you what," Vesk said.

"Which way the slope ran." Foss took the glass off the housing. "Anything that runs on a difference runs to the low side, and the draw does, that's the whole of a main. We put something into every flask we carry, and into our hands and the table and the rail, a little, every day, and nothing has put anything into that pipe for four years. She's the same. She had somewhere lower than where she was, and it was eleven feet off, and she went down to it." He stood up, and his knee cracked. "It's simple. I don't want it written simply. I want it written as measured."

"There's nothing in her to go anywhere," Nuvalik said. "That's what I keep... there is no Crystal in her. There is nobody in her. There's nothing in her that wants the pipe. There's only the net, and the net shortens toward the empty end, and she was the net." She wiped the blade again, though it was clean. "Now she's at the empty end and the net has nothing left to shorten into."

Vesk looked at the hind lying against the iron with her neck along the brick.

At home they put stones on the graves. It was the beck stones, the flat grey ones out of the water, one to a visitor, laid on the turf over the head. He had asked his mother once what they were for and she had said they were to keep the dead where they were put, and he had thought, even small, that that was a thing people said. Nobody in his village had ever got up. He had laid a good many stones and never known what a single one of them was doing.

*Now I know what they're for,* he thought.

He stood with that for a while. There was no stone in the building, and there was nobody to lay one, and the ninth hind was lying where the pipe had put her and not where anybody had.

Behind him Deming came back in.

He came in slowly, with one hand on the door frame, and the Lord moved aside for him again without a word. Deming's face was grey and wet at the mouth. The hat had been crushed, and he was pushing the crown back out with his thumb from the inside, going round, and he did not speak until it was the right shape. He did not look toward the corner.

"I beg the room's pardon," Deming said. "It was the ford." He put the hat's brim right with his thumb, round and round. "It is... I had eel at the inn at the ford on the way up, and I was told not to have it, the girl told me not to, and I had it. It has been coming on since the eighth division." He swallowed. "That was the eel."

Nobody said anything to that either. Foss had gone to the rail for the rule, the long one with the brass ends that hung on the last hook, and he came back with it and put one end on the slate at the table's west edge, and walked the other out along the floor, and set it down, and picked up the first end and walked it over, end over end, to where the hind's near shoulder lay against the iron. He crouched. He read it with the lamp held close.

"Eleven feet," he said. "On the rule, from the table's edge to the point of her near shoulder. Anybody who wants it again can put the rule down again."

Vesk did not say anything.

Foss went to the shelf over the press and took the book down, which Vesk had never seen anybody but Nuvalik do, and Nuvalik watched him do it and said nothing. He opened it on the gauge bench in the warm air. He turned to the last written leaf, where his own hand had put down that Deming had been read the book and heard it, and he wrote under that. Vesk came and stood at his shoulder with the flask. The pen went steadily. Foss wrote the date, and *the twelfth division*, and then a line, and then the distance, and the words *table's edge to near shoulder, measured on the rule*, and then he stopped with the pen off the paper.

"How do you want it said," he said. "That she got up."

"Stood," Nuvalik said, from across the room. "Write that she stood. She stood, and she leaned, and she went down."

Foss wrote *stood*.

The Lord had not come in from the door. He stood where he had stood, with the board over his shoulder and the coat over his arm, and he was looking at the hind against the housing, at the length of her along the iron and the foot of the pipe and the brick. The lamp was on the gauge bench now, by the book, and the door was the dark end of the room. Vesk could see the shape of him there and the coat's fold and not much else.

"Write where she went, and write it as the place and not as anything she meant by going there," Malphas said, "because the sink is the quiet, and she has found the quietest thing in this building without any of us showing it her, and that is the whole of what I wanted proven on a table."

Foss wrote for a while after that. When he had done he turned the book round on the bench so that Vesk could read it without being given it.

It said the date, and the division. It said *the ninth hind* in Foss's square small hand, and under it, on a line of its own, it said *stood*, and then what she had done, in the order she had done it, and the eleven feet on the rule, and at the head of the leaf, where every leaf in the book was headed, it said the name of the work.

Vesk read it twice. Then he looked for somewhere to put the flask, and set it down on the gauge bench beside the book, sealed, and took his hands out from under it, and they stayed open.

*It walks,* he thought.

In the far corner the drip came down off the ceiling onto the slate, and he had it again.

Over by the housing the ninth hind lay against the iron where she had put herself, and stayed down.

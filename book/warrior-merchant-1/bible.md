# Paid in Kind — book bible

*The Warrior Merchant, Book One. A novella in fourteen chapters and three movements, written straight through (review mode). Built 2026-11-07 from Isaac's questionnaire answers (`imports/drafts/merchant-novella-questionnaire/answers.json`) and the foundation research (`research/`).*

## Premise

Ned Chapman, twenty, is a chapman of the Kessen road in the Hundred Crowns of the Midmarch, 715 IC, the twenty-fifth year of the Dark Ages, with the forty-year war only just stopped. He wants three things out loud: to pay the woman warrior who pulled him out of a ditch when he was eleven and would take no payment, to become the greatest merchant of the Inner World (in the end, a Paragon of the halls), and to be a warrior too. The market laughs at him as the Warrior Merchant, because the merchant halls bar a sworn fighter from holding a trust title: a man who is both is buyer and seller of the same blood, and one has to sell to the other.

His one wild concept is **Provenance**: everything remembers who held it, and a blade he handles gives back its past owners' blows for his own shoulders to repeat. He learns to fight from the dead men's swords he buys and sells. In book one he buys his Waking Draft on nine years of debt, finds her sword in a murdered soldier's lot, learns her name from a pledge-broker's book, cries it up the road, finds her, learns that her company sold the road his father died on and that she took her share before she turned back for him, and decides the debt stands. He pays it in kind: he spends the sword's whole record, the murdered soldier's witness included, to pull a boy out of a ditch for nothing with the best blow her sword ever struck, and the emptied sword comes home to her as a stray. He ends the book woken at Stage II, a Journeyman of the Mercers, the hall rule read against his name, and answering for the first time to the name they laughed at.

## POV and narration

Close third person, past tense. Twelve chapters are Ned's. Two belong to side characters, each its own chapter (a marked break at the chapter's start): **chapter 7, Avelina Stray** (the widow's armed wake and the sale), and **chapter 9, Gisla Ravensworth** (the pinfold, the vow, the song that finds her). In those two the reader learns things Ned does not (reader_knows): what was done to Osmund, who the half-handed man is to the sword, and why the Short Measure Company left a convoy to the wolves in 706. Ned never acts on them until he learns them on the page.

Ned's narration prices things the way he does: by wear, by weight, by who held them before. It counts under its breath. It turns warm and exact when somebody needs something he can sell them. **Every chapter shows a sale that helps someone and costs Ned something** (Isaac: he gets stronger and helps people by selling them things, and that is how he connects with people). Every fight is a negotiation he is losing until it is not.

## World law (every chapter carries this; the loadouts miss most of it)

**The age.** 715 IC, the twenty-fifth year of the Dark Ages and the seventieth of the Withering Era; the forty-year war has just stopped, and discharged men come home along both roads with kit to sell. The Center holds the Turn against the Scourge from 712 to 721; free companies on Thresh contracts do most of the dying (R83-2, R83-4; `research/midmarch.md` §13). No guns in any era: a crossbow bolt, about three copper, is the cheap thing that kills a high-Stage fighter in a room (R83-5). What is built and carried is medieval; magic is done by living people and their crafts, and city lamps, heat and water are paid trades, never mains or machinery (R83-9; C-185). Aether is pulled from the ground a practitioner stands on: rich in a dark, quiet, ruined place, poor at a crowded fair (R83-9).

**The calendar** is the real one (R85-2): Thursday, March 25 (Lady Day), Thursday, June 24 (Midsummer) and Wednesday, September 29 (Michaelmas) are the quarter-days the merchants' year runs on (quarterage, the Apprentice sitting at Midsummer, the Journeyman sitting and the bond's first fifth at Michaelmas). Auren's day, the twelfth of every month, is the day the dead are sat with; 715 has no February 29. Every chapter's italic place line carries the weekday, month, day and 715 IC.

**Money.** 1 gold mark = 12 silver = 288 copper; a Nalūn salt bar passes for a silver; the money-changer's hammer says what a Tollgarth mark or a Pennycrown penny is worth this week. A loaf 3 copper, a cookshop meal 4 to 6, a pint 2, a lodging bed 3 to 4; a laborer earns 12 to 24 copper a day; a trooper's saber 6 to 10 silver; a mule 10 to 20 gold. Past the cities most of it is paid in kind (What Things Cost; `research/trade.md` §1, §8). Tolls (INVENTED rates, kept): 1 copper a pack at an unsigned chain, 2 at the Kingsbridge bridge; a token's road-writ fixes 1 copper a day on a signed stretch. Say Grade, Rarity and price aloud when the POV would (R85-1).

**Article XXX, read whole:** a member deals, makes, **carries** or treats up to his title and no further without a second signature from a token that covers it; the sale voidable and an amercement, and the second offense strikes the title. So Ned carries the Uncommon horn-marked sword only under Marion Thurlow's Master's countersign (ch 8), the T3 core stays in the Short Measure Company's salted sack under the Nether Marl clerk's chit until his silver covers it (ch 10 to 13), and the dead Master's spear is left on the gallery floor (ch 10).

**The Guild Accord, the merchant's side.** Nine titles grade trust in a trade; a merchant's title caps the Rarity he may sell: Initiate and Apprentice Common, Journeyman Uncommon, Adept and Expert Rare, Master Very Rare, Grandmaster Legendary, Archmaster Mythic, Paragon Unique ("the halls come to you"). Fees: Initiate 2 silver, Apprentice 6 silver, Journeyman 1½ gold. A title needs no woken Crystal. Selling above one's silver without a second signature makes the sale voidable (Article XXX). The token is silver, numeral and hall mark and wax, and appears on no window (RULINGS.md 2026-11-05; The Tiered Path). The Articles bind only on the signed stretch of the Midmarch; past it they are custom, though most towns keep the market court and the dusty foot (Article XXVI: a traveler's plea is heard before the market closes). The sittings, INVENTED on "a ledger that balanced": Initiate (weights stamped, the pack read, a month of tallies), Apprentice (a season's book on a view, a tray of ten pieces with frauds among them, at Midsummer), Journeyman (a book audited and quit, here a half-year short book with doubled sureties, two appraisers who dislike each other, the law asked aloud, and **the belt**: show what tokens and oaths of arms you carry) (`research/trade.md` §3). The Mercers' hall of the Midmarch sits on the height at Kingsbridge (INVENTED; the great Mercers' hall is at Waxgate in the Charter).

**The hall rule** (Isaac, 2026-11-07): the merchant halls bar a sworn fighter from holding a trust title, because a man who is both is buyer and seller of the same blood; "one has to sell to the other" is its plain-speech gloss. When the Clerk reads it against a member's name it is entered on the roll, and a fighting token found on him after strikes his merchant title on sight. Ned may pass a fighting yard and refuse its token; refusing is its own scene (`research/trade.md` §4). The halls' reasons, said by the people who hold them: a fighter-merchant makes the dead he sells; a blade sold today is drawn on its buyer tomorrow; a fighter's Pressure at the counter is a false weight.

**The fighter's side.** Fighting halls read their titles off the Stage table and confirm them with a coil and a fight in the yard (Initiate at Murmuring, Apprentice at Welling). Kemp's Yard is a fighting and hiring hall behind a green door; its yard-irons go back in the fire every quarter-day "so no candidate carries another man's luck into the yard", so they read blank to Provenance; the one thing in the yard never reforged is Bett Bodkin's own old buckler, older than the yard and hers (INVENTED custom, `research/fight.md` §6).

**Windows** (R84-1, R84-2, R84-36, R84-40). Everyone has one, the unwoken included; each line stands alone in square brackets after the event it reports; Grades, percentages, bars and levels, never EU or η. A commoner's window shows Level, a few trade skills and health, no Essence and no class; no class is offered before Glory, so **Ned has no class in this book**. Sample formats for Ned are in `research/power.md` §8 and the brief's protocol.

**Waking.** Nine souls in ten never wake; a Crystal unattended in childhood seals, and is woken rarely, by a crisis, a master's forcing, a tribulation, a Waking Draft or a blessing, always at great cost. A Waking Draft is 60 to 120 gold and often kills or warps; commoners sell themselves into guild debt for one, and the term is generally the larger bill (R84-37; The Gate Who May Learn Magic). A Trait sits unlisted before waking and goes on working; waking shows it with a level. A bought door opens and no more: the fights fill it (R84-5, R84-6).

**The Bond of the Fifth** (INVENTED instrument, `research/trade.md` §6): written by Kemp's Yard as a hiring hall, a money bond and never service (so Ned stays unsworn): a fifth part of all he takes, coin and kind, from the morning he wakes, paid each quarter-day for nine years (thirty-six quarter-days), never less than fifteen silver a quarter; it stands whether the Draft wakes, warps or kills; buyout at twice the Draft's value less what is paid. The Draft: ninety gold, Greater Wolf stock, poured at Petronel Gall's licensed house with a leech by.

**Provenance** (the kit; full account in `research/power.md` §3 to §7). Character first: he refuses an unpaid account; he survived a ditch and a hand that would take no payment; he believes nothing is ever sold clean, because everything remembers who held it. The Trait (window name **Everything Remembers Who Held It**) formed in the ditch at eleven and was weathered by nine years of other people's goods: his Primary, Level 9 when he wakes. It reads the five records a used thing really keeps (wear, the hardest load, the fatigue count, the stressed field, the count since its last fire) as the motions that made them; the glyph [Lei] Binding limits it to skin contact, one owner's strand per grip, held only while the grip holds. A Body walker gets **blows, never stories**: height, reach, handedness, the guard, the hardest blow, how a hand left the grip (handed on, dropped, or held to the end), never a name, a motive or a feeling. The borrowed shape runs at **Ned's own force**: "a beautifully placed punch from someone who cannot hurt you" until his Grades rise. A sword gives the arm, never the feet. By Stage: unwoken, a slow knack (a minute of handling: pricing, forgeries, hands that settle into a dead owner's guard, no force); Stage I, Murmuring (reads in two or three breaths, one action from the owner the blade remembers loudest, mood misfires); Stage II, Welling (chooses the owner, reads in one breath, two to four actions, reads through a bind, two owners back to back, and spills: scorched grips, wiped records). The **Working Card** for a read: he turns the thing to the light like a buyer, thumb on the ricasso, fist closed on the grip, breath held, eyes on the middle distance; faded violet afterimages of other hands bloom around his on the grip, the oldest faintest; loose iron (nails, buckles, a cloak pin) creeps toward his palm; the dry riffle of pages and a faint hum; old paper and attic dust; his signature sound word is **thriff**, every time, never retired. His colors: the dull brown of a worn penny under Murmuring's near-black at waking; dark char with faded violet on the hands after the lens (ch 10). The cost: **every past he reads stays in him** (dreams of other men's fights, aches where owners were hurt, a hand-habit or a taste every few deep reads; the window shows the gains and the physical carry and never the habits); a hard read moves the record out of the steel and into him; a fast read scorches the grip and resets the record (thermal resetting, the lens's own failure). Fair counters, as facts: new or fire-wiped steel reads blank; a padded gauntlet or no contact gives nothing; the breath a read takes is the opening; a borrowed shape is committed once driven; his force is his own; crowds and Aether-rich ground spoil the read; the Stage gap holds (one Stage crossable with a read, two only with a vow or a breakthrough). A woken owner's coat, extended into held gear, is part of what the steel recorded, so in a read that owner's hue shows through the faded violet of their afterimage (Gisla's slate-blue glimmers in ch 8 and stands at full blaze in ch 14; INVENTED, inside the Anamnesis signature). A hard read moves an owner out of the steel and into Ned and leaves the steel empty; Ned says so in ch 10 and pays it in ch 14. His named moves are the Trait's own law called aloud, not techniques of his own: his first technique, and Stage III's Catalyst, is book two's FIRSTHAND, and the window shows no Threshold for any of them. Overlap kept apart: House Scalebond's art reads places and bodies; Ned reads held objects only, never a living body, and never says "repeated by her own shoulders".

**The moves** (R82-3, R82-4, R82-25: called in Midmarch English, a chapman's cry in capitals on its own line; the art's title PROVENANCE for the big calls): **BILL OF SALE!** (the read; unnamed knack from ch 1, named ch 8), **SECONDHAND!** (one borrowed action; ch 8), **MIXED LOT!** (two owners back to back; ch 12), **THE HAGGLE!** (the read taken in a bind or clinch by a bare palm on the other fighter's own steel, a half-sword catch of the blade or a hand flat on a buckler's rim; ch 13), **NO CHARGE!** (the finisher with her sword: her one true blow, HEADLAND, the rising cut that killed the wolf in 706, spent to save someone who cannot pay; the hard read moves her out of the steel into him for life and empties the steel of everything else, Osmund's last grip and the half hand included, the widow's only witness and the dearest provenance in the Midmarch, which Ned knows when he chooses it; about 30 percent of reserve and a shoulder torn by a reach longer than his own; ch 14 only, staged as an ultimate, R82-27). At Murmuring (ch 8) SECONDHAND gives only her everyday cut; HEADLAND lies too deep for him until Welling and the hard read.

**The woken around him are loud** (R82-8, R82-12, R82-41): Amabel Breakspear, the Long Tab, RUNNING TAB! and SETTLE UP!; Wolstan Kemp, the Auctioneer, GOING ONCE! GOING TWICE! **Fights** run on the Blow Card and the Working Card (DEPTH AND EXPLANATION). Ned's body by Stage (`research/fight.md` §1.1): unwoken, Hollow output, the stats tie only against other unwoken men; Stage I, Grade cap E; Stage II, Grade cap D. Threats: chain-men and levy deserters (unwoken to Stage I; mail turns his cuts), Greater Wolves (T3; they follow armies; the pack tests, then takes the legs; stand sound, kill the alpha pair), the Nether Marl delve (masks faded violet, T2 at the Mouth to T4-T5 below; the Minotaur T5, a Natural Beast whose charge cannot turn), the herds and greywalkers of a dry August (T5: no fight at any Stage; leave the range or reach wet ground), and the cordial-men (Stage III; they never fight, they kill the sleeping, drugged and surprised; a proofed bolt beats proofed harness). Ned's kit: the measuring-staff (his best weapon, Silver's short staff), the Bauernwehr knife, the tray lid as a buckler, and whatever dead man's blade his hands are holding.

**Gear is a flex** (R85-1): a major person's first appearance describes the whole kit piece by piece; a minor person's exact stroke is often their gear; a return shows what changed; Grade, Rarity and price are said aloud by an owner, a rival, a smith or a window. Ned's whole kit is mended Common, and it tells on him.

**Small powers are paid trades** (R82-36, R83-8): the toll-sitter who cannot be lied to about a cart until his fee is paid; hedge-workers who salt a doorstep for a copper; the Whetter who sharpens a knife for a copper and a sword she respects for two silver. **Fame** (R83-7): heralds cry what the lord wants, criers sell a dozen words for a copper, minstrels get it wrong in predictable ways (the name changes for the rhyme, the numbers go up tenfold, somebody acquires a sword), bookies' boards are the most honest news in town.

**The Cordial** (R84-15): a Crystal cut from a dead practitioner and rendered makes the strongest Draft of all; it is sold as a monster-core lot at several times the price, it is the worst crime in every realm and the most profitable. Canon names the killers: **night-cutters** ("A person who dies with no one to sit up with them is what the night-cutters look for", Monster Cores), and in company nobody names them at all; Ottor calls himself a cordial-man, politely. Their victims in this book are drugged and opened alive, at inns, nowhere near a field. C-179 is open: Ned never walks a field, strips a body, or touches a battlefield stone; he buys finished gear at pay wagons, stairs, estate sales and widows' doors (`research/trade.md` §7).

**Names** (R70-122, NATALIE.md Canon Hierarchy): comic names among commons, kings and walk-ons (Hodge Toller, King Baldwin Furlong, Halloo Hal); major people keep strict names. Where the Gazetteer says comic names are "the rule among commons and kings alike", NATALIE.md governs for major people.

## Voice note

Close third on Ned, past tense, a chapman's ear: the narration prices things the way he does (by wear, by weight, by who held them before), counts aloud under its breath, and turns warm and exact when someone needs something he can sell them. Every chapter shows a sale that helps someone and costs Ned something, and every fight is a negotiation he is losing until it is not. Bright, loud Midmarch color (painted shields, ribbons, red cattle, the stormy sky over the bridge), comic names among the commons, kings who cost a penny, and the dead men's lots underneath it all.

*Exemplar:* "The spoon had been held by a left-handed man who stirred the same way for thirty years and died with his mouth full, and Ned priced it at a copper, because the widow needed the copper more than she needed to know."

*Anti-exemplar:* "The market was gray and loud and Ned was tired of all of it."

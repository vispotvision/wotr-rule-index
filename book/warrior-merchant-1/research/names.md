## 0. Three findings to build on

1. **Ned's name is the hall rule.** Old English *ċēapmann* is "dealer, merchant", and the word also meant the other side of the counter: "(obsolete) A purchaser" and, in Middle English, "(by extension) A purchaser of goods" (Wiktionary, *chapman*). The rule bars a man who "is buyer and seller of the same blood" (RUL:4169). Chapman means both. Anyone who knows the old word can tell him that his own name breaks the rule. (The reading is INVENTED; the senses are real.)
2. **The fighting-hall master's name answers it.** *Kemp* is Old English *cempa*, "warrior, fighter, champion", a "doublet of champion" (Wiktionary, *kemp*). Datamuse's sound-alikes for *chapman* return **champion** (`sl=chapman`). So the crier's pun "Chapman the Champion" is built into the language, and a hall-master named Kemp sits across the table from him: the old word for merchant against the old word for fighter (INVENTED pairing).
3. **"Copied" is already a canon insult, and it fits him exactly.** "Copied. Learned off a copied Primer. firm. Of a fraud, or of a man whose skill is somebody else's" (SWEARS:230). A man who fights with dead men's blows will hear it from the first yard he walks into. "Capped" (a sealed Crystal, grave, SWEARS:227) is what he was until the Draft.

## 1. The naming law this bank follows

- **Northern stratum.** "A small given-name pool producing constant disambiguation by trade, a frozen surname if the family is chartered and a live patronymic if it is not, and an earned byname available to anyone" (IWNA:29). Byname types: patronymic, occupational, locative, descriptive; "the byname is more real than the given name" (NGA:160). The 40-name pool at NGA:156-158 is reference only; a name "only has to sound right for its culture" (R79-7).
- **Comic names.** "Bynames and nicknames are the norm in musters, crews, taverns and markets and thin above the commons" (NAME:40). The Accord and Northern commons are "the richest home: talking and phrase surnames, rhyming pet names (Hob and Dob), soldiers' bynames, and Accord jokes in correct Latin" (R70-124). Built types: rhyming sets, Dickensian talking surnames, plausible puns from words the world owns, never a brand or celebrity (R70-120). Grown types: soldiers' nicknames, mangled names, sound comedy (most at home among goblinoids), protective ugly names (R70-121).
- **Who may carry one.** Recurring minors, villains and rivals may; "major NPCs keep strict names" (R70-122). An English pun needs a bridge, a Common mouth among them (R70-123); the Midmarch speaks Common, so the bridge is always there. The close POV may notice a joke, "the narrator outside him never remarks on it" (R70-129).
- **On the page.** A new person is a role "until they speak twice or matter" (R50-27); a walk-on may be named at once only "by a nickname in someone else's mouth" (R70-130). Two people with one name is a beat, never an error (R50-17, R50-29).
- **The Midmarch's own pattern** (canon names to match in feel, not to reuse): King Gerent Tuppence the Third (MID:14), Sella Drax (MID:16), Queen Tizz Copperwick the Fourth (MID:38), King Ambrose Fivefold (MID:48), King Hamo the Undefeated (MID:96), Nixie Sprocket (MID:110), Lord Aubrey Tancred (VAL:61). Coin and measure words in names are the house habit: Tuppence, Pennycrown, Lastpenny.

## 6. "One has to sell to the other": five versions

The canon gloss is Isaac's: "'one has to sell to the other' is the plain-speech gloss of the rule" (RUL:4169). All five versions below are INVENTED.

1. **A hall clerk** (Jasper Sealey, reading from the hall's book, Latin doublet under R50-23, correct Latin under R50-12):
   > "*Nemo sit emptor et venditor eiusdem sanguinis.* Let no man be buyer and seller of the same blood. The halls say it plainer, Master Chapman, for those who want it plain: one has to sell to the other."
   Short tag for the hall's wall: ***Alter alteri vendat***, "let the one sell to the other." Grammar: *nemo* nominative, *sit* jussive subjunctive, *emptor et venditor* predicate nominatives, *eiusdem sanguinis* genitive; *alter* nominative, *alteri* dative, *vendat* present subjunctive. Worth a second check before print.
2. **A sellsword** (Kit Quarrel, over a cup):
   > "Sword or scales, Chapman. Carry both and one of 'em sells the other the first night it's hungry."
3. **A crier** (Halloo Hal, at the bell, selling the bill for a copper, NEWS:94):
   > "O yes! O yes! By the halls' own rule, cried this morning from the steps: no token for a sworn blade! One has to sell to the other! A copper the bill, and long live whoever!"
   ("O yes" is the English spelling of the crier's *oyez* in the Chester proclamations book, Wikipedia, *Town crier*; "long live whoever" is the canon loyal toast, MID:88.)
4. **A child's skipping rhyme** (Thimble and the market children; the joke is the swear it dodges):
   > Chapman, Chapman, sword and scale,
   > which of you is up for sale?
   > One to buy and one to sell,
   > one gets rich and one gets... WELL!
5. **A minstrel's couplet** (Lettice Rimer, in fourteeners):
   > He bought the blade and sold the blade and swore to swing it too,
   > but one must sell the other, friends, when one man would be two.

Bonus, a Nalūn factor (Torgunn Saltonstall), who says it as contract law: "A man may not stand on both sides of his own instrument."

## 7. Epithets, and how they are cried

Canon: every major fighter has an epithet "that travels ahead of them, some wrong or unflattering" (R82-8; NATALIE.md:51). A crier "reads you the news off a hand-copied bill, sells you the bill for a copper, and adds whatever he thinks will sell the next one", and a bill is about a dozen words (NEWS:94). A song "gets the details wrong in a way you can predict: the name changes to make the rhyme, the numbers go up by a factor of ten, the loser becomes uglier, and somebody acquires a sword" (NEWS:96). An epithet "travels faster than its owner, and is often wrong or unflattering, and is seldom shaken off" (NEWS:102). Lesser names stick or fade; the one that sticks becomes the Third Name (R70-117), and on Isaac's characters it sticks only if he keeps it (R50-21). All cries and verses below are INVENTED.

### 1. The Warrior Merchant (canon jibe, RUL:4169)

- **Crier:** "O yes! O yes! The Warrior Merchant at Kemp's Yard, noon bell! Sells you the sword, then shows you how! A copper the bill!"
- **Minstrel** (numbers up tenfold, the loser uglier):
  > Oh, Ned the Warrior Merchant had a hundred swords to sell,
  > and he swung them all at Bushel's boy, and sold the boy as well.
- **Bookies' board** (Clarice Nock): "WARRIOR MERCHANT, evens. THE YARD, three to one."

### 2. Chapman the Champion (the crier's sound-pun; mocking first, true by the end if the book earns it)

- **Crier:** "Chapman the champion! Chapman the CHAMPION! Hear it here before the bookies do!"
- **Minstrel:**
  > They called him chapman, called him cheap, they called him worse, and then
  > they called him champion, and the song went round the fair again.

### 3. Copied Ned (unflattering; canon insult "Copied", SWEARS:230)

Who pins it: the yard, Prince Lovel Bushel, Gill Huxter when she is losing.
- **Crier:** "Copied Ned! A dead man's arm and a live man's mouth! Copied Ned at the yard, a copper!"
- **Minstrel:**
  > He never learned a single cut; he bought them by the pound
  > off dead men in a widow's yard, and swung them round and round.

### 4. Ned-in-Pledge, or the Pawned Man (unflattering; after the Waking Draft on guild debt)

Canon: "Commoners without the coin for a Waking Draft sell themselves into guild debt for it, and the term is generally the larger bill" (COST:141); "A bought Stage opens the door and no more" (VAL:69).
- **Crier:** "Ned-in-Pledge! The Mercers hold his soul to quarter day! Bill a copper!"
- **Minstrel** (the name mangled for the rhyme, the price tenfold, sixty to a hundred and twenty gold becoming a thousand marks):
  > Ted Chopman sold his soul for gold, a thousand marks, they say,
  > and bought a door with nothing through, and walked in anyway.

### Wrong versions the songs will carry

**The Peddler Knight** (he is no knight; the song gave him a title and a horse). **Dead Men's Ned** (tavern, after dark). **Chapman Twice** (a clerk's sneer: the man who is both). **Ted Chopman** (the name bent for a rhyme, spelled as sung, R50-33).

## 8. Sound words, Common English register

Law on file: a Common POV hears English sound words (LEX:12); small sounds italic and woven in, a heavy hit on its own line in capitals (LEX:10); "Never the same impact word twice in a scene unless it is a signature" (LEX:15); state words for a Common POV "come as English: a hush, a hum, a held silence, the thud of a heart, the groan of timber" (LEX:62); a technique keeps one signature sound word, "which recurs every time it fires and is never retired" (`desktop/NATALIE.md:331`). Nothing below appears in the spent list (USED:29-59) or as an italic word anywhere in `wiki/`, `lore/`, `book/` or `desktop/` (checked). All are INVENTED candidates; *dunt* and *skelp* are real Northern dialect words for a blow and a slap.

### Impacts by what struck what

- **Flesh:** *dunt* (a dull fist into a belly), *skelp* (an open hand), *whunk* (a cudgel), *thwop* (a sack-heavy body blow), *slup* (wet).
- **Bone:** *knakk* (a forearm), *krikk* (a finger bent back), *pokk* (a knuckle on a skull).
- **Steel on steel:** *tzing* (a clean parry), *klinnng* (a long ringing bind), *shrang* (blade dragged down blade), *skirl* (a squeal off a crossguard).
- **A blade through:** *thwisk* (through cloth and skin), *shuck* (a blade pulled free).
- **Wood:** *chokk* (a blade biting a stall pole), *thwonk* (a quarterstaff), *klok* (a hilt on a tabletop).
- **Shield:** *bwang*.
- **Crossbow:** *thrunk* (the release), *zzip* (the flight), *thokk* (the hit).
- **Heavy, on their own line:** *WHUNNK.* · *BRANNNG.* · *DUNNNT.*

### Market and road

The barrow wheel *creeeak-a, creeeak-a*; over cobbles *klattaklattak*; barley from a split sack *shhhhhhrrr*; a lane chain dropped *KLANNNG* and then rattling *chakka-chakka*; the crier's handbell *dang-dang-dang*; a goose *hronk*; a dropped egg *plap*; the strike-board drawn level across a measure *sssshht*; the beam's pans settling *dunk... dunk*.

### Coins

Two coins *chink*; a coin on stone *tlink*; a bright coin slapped on a counter *ching*; a purse jogging on a hip *jink-jink*; a fistful poured into a pan *shkkkkk*; coins counted onto a board *clik-clik-clik*; a coin spun on a counter *whirrrr-rr-r-tk*. **The money-changer's hammer** (MID:66): a sound coin *tiiing*, a cased one *tuk*, from the canon rule that "A solid weight rings when struck. A cased one gives a dull note" (WEIGHT:114). **The Tollgarth change-machine** (MID:38): *klunk-whirrr... tlink... tlink...* and then nothing.

### The crowd (plain state words, never italic, per LEX:10)

A hush; the hum; mutter-mutter; a groan going round; the long ooooh; a held breath. Audible single cries go italic: *HOOOO* (impressed), *haaaah* (a coin rings true), *HUZZAH*. A crowd chant from the canon greeting "Who's king today?" (MID:72): **WHO'S KING? WHO'S KING?** (INVENTED as a chant).

### Provenance: the Trait's signature word

The Anamnesis signature on file: hue "faded violet", sound "a dry whisper like old pages turning, a faint hum", smell "old paper, attic dust", touch "a magnetic tug on iron", the user's line "memories surfacing", tell "afterimages of what once happened here" (SIG:64).

1. ***shirrr*** (recommended): the dry riffle of a ledger thumbed fast, with the hum carried in the long r. Soft enough for a man who is no showman yet; it scales by length and case (*shirr* for a glance at a knife, *shirrrrrr* for a long read, ***SHIRRRRR*** when a Release reads a whole battlefield).
2. *ffssh-mmmn*: pages, then the hum, spelled out.
3. *rrriffle*: the page-riffle alone.

Whichever is kept recurs every time Provenance reads and is never retired (R81-10). Named moves under it keep their own words (two workings of one Wellspring share its smell and texture and keep their own sound words, R81-10). An INVENTED device worth trying: when Ned's shoulders repeat a dead man's blow, the impact takes the dead man's own sound, so the reader learns whose blow it was by ear.

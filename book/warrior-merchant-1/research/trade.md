## 0. Ten things not to get wrong

1. **A title is trust in a trade.** "A title says how far your guild trusts you in your trade." (TIERED:14). A merchant's title caps the Rarity he may sell (ARTICLES:110, Article XXXI; ITEMS:64; RULINGS.md:3737).
2. **Initiate and Apprentice both sell Common; Journeyman is the first Uncommon** (TIERED:22-24). What else separates the first two is "its own line under the same nine words" that each hall writes (TIERED:32), INVENTED in section 2.
3. **A title needs no woken Crystal**, "and most of the merchants' Grandmasters have never so much as warmed a kettle" (TIERED:32). Ned can climb the merchants' ladder unwoken. The waking is for the other dream.
4. **Above his silver he needs a second signature** (ARTICLES:109, Article XXX). Without it: "The sale voidable; amercement; the second offense, title struck."
5. **A forged token hangs** (ARTICLES:112, Article XXXIII; COST:159: "it is hanged in every realm").
6. **The hall rule** bars a sworn fighter from holding a merchant's trust title (RULINGS.md:4169). The reading on file: a fighting token or a sworn company; "a merchant who fights unsworn breaks no rule" (RULINGS.md:4169).
7. **At Stage II the fighting table reads Apprentice**: Grade D, Welling, T2, Green (TIERED:49). Ned can be a Journeyman merchant and, on the yard's table, an Apprentice fighter. The hall rule forbids holding both.
8. **The Waking Draft** is 60 to 120 gold and "often kills or warps the child who drinks it", and "commoners sell themselves into guild debt for one" (GATE:23; DRAFTS:134; COST:129).
9. **Stones above T4 hang.** "Most realms therefore hang people for holding an unrendered stone above T4 without a hall's chit" (CORES:187). C-179 is open (CONFLICTS.md:1967-1974): keep Ned out of battlefield rendering and battlefield stones entirely.
10. **No printing, no guns.** "No printing is established" (RULINGS.md:3569). Bills, songs and almanacs are hand-copied (COST:61: "A dozen words, hand-copied").

## 1. Coin, scale and a peddler's purse

**The rates.** "One gold mark is twelve silver marks. One silver mark is twenty-four copper. A gold mark is therefore two hundred and eighty-eight copper, and almost nobody below a shop's counting-room ever handles one." (COST:11). "A Nalūn salt bar passes for one silver mark, more in a hard winter. Imperial bronze goes by weight." (COST:13). "A laborer earns a silver mark in a good day." (DRAFTS:46). Past the last warden's round "most of this list is paid in kind: grain, salt, cloth, fuel, board, a day's labor owed and settled at year's end" (COST:15).

**Midmarch money.** "Coin is anybody's, and the money-changer at every crossroads will tell you what a Tollgarth mark or a Pennycrown penny is worth this week, which is how much metal it has in it, and he has a small hammer for finding out." (CROWNS:66). The Accord's word for a fraud is "Cased. Looks solid, rings dull when struck." (INV:46). "Most transactions involve no coin at all" (VALUE:289).

**A peddler's year (INVENTED, by ear from COST).** A good market day grosses 1 to 2 silver and nets 6 to 12 copper after stock, tolls, bed and a meal. A year nets about a laborer's year, 12 to 20 gold in equivalent, most of it in copper and kind and much of it standing in the pack as stock. Canon calibration: a T2 core is "About a laborer's month" at 12 to 18 silver and a T4 core "Most of a laborer's year" at 8 to 15 gold (COST:113, 115).

**Ned at the open (INVENTED suggestion).** A pack worth 3 to 4 gold in stock, 1 to 2 gold in coin sewn into his coat, split tallies with three suppliers, and either no token or an Initiate's. Nine years of handling dead men's gear give him an Appraisal skill that his own window shows as a Grade (everyone has a window, the unwoken included, R84-36; NATALIE.md:65), and a hall can make him swear to it (R84-41, R84-43; NATALIE.md:83).

## 2. The nine titles, as a merchant reads them

**Canon caps and fees.** Caps from TIERED:20-30 (repeated at ITEMS:66-76 and TITLE:42-52). Fees from COST:147-157: "The fee is what a hall charges to sit the examination and strike the silver token, and a hall that likes you may advance it against your term." (COST:145). The Mercers' line is INVENTED, under canon's "Every hall writes its own line under the same nine words" (TIERED:32).

| Tier | Title | May sell up to (canon) | Fee (canon) | The Mercers' own line (INVENTED) | Same numeral on the fighting table (canon, TIERED:48-56) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Initiate | Common | 2 silver | Common goods on his own account, from his own pack or stall; may hold consignments up to 1 gold | E, Murmuring, T1 |
| 2 | Apprentice | Common | 6 silver | Common goods on consignment and credit from members, sale-or-return, up to 10 gold outstanding; may keep a pitch in a hall's yard; may carry another member's goods under seal | D, Welling, T2 |
| 3 | Journeyman | Uncommon | 1½ gold | Uncommon goods on his own silver; may lend his token as the second signature on an Uncommon sale; strangers may hand him Uncommon goods on sight | C, Ascension, T3 |
| 4 | Adept | Rare | 5 gold | Rare goods up to a value the hall sets each year | B, Flourishing, T4 |
| 5 | Expert | Rare | 15 gold | Rare goods at any value; may sit as an appraiser at a hall's examinations | A band, T5 |
| 6 | Master | Very Rare | 50 gold | Very Rare goods; introduces buyer and seller (ITEMS:32) | S to SS, T6 |
| 7 | Grandmaster | Legendary | 150 gold | Legendary goods; holds pledges for crowns | SS to SSS, T7 |
| 8 | Archmaster | Mythic | 500 gold | Mythic goods | X to EX, T8 |
| 9 | Paragon | Unique | "No fee. The halls come to you" (COST:157) | The weight other markets check their own against | EX+ and beyond, T9 |

**The token.** "A title travels as a silver token at the belt, the numeral on one face and the hall's mark on the other, with a dab of the hall's wax set in a recess at its edge. It appears nowhere on your window." (TIERED:34). Silver because it takes an inscription to one person: "inscribed to that holder, verifiable on a coil, and worthless to anyone else" (VALUE:118), and "at the floor, money" (VALUE:120).

**What it opens.** "A token opens what its numeral covers: the board up to its Threat Tier, the stall up to its Rarity, the forge up to its Grade, the Long Relay's stables, the hall's bed at the hall's price." (MERIT:20). Passage "belongs to every token on the road, down to the Initiate carrying poles": "a road-writ at a fixed toll, a Long Relay stable every day's ride, a bed and a meal at any signed hall at the hall's price, and a market court that hears you before the market closes" (GATENET:12).

**Why a peddler wants one.** "Title is collateral... A commoner with a title can finance an expedition. A commoner without one cannot finance a cart." (VALUE:242). "For a great many people the Accord credential is the only portable legal identity they will ever possess" (VALUE:251).

**The second signature.** "A merchant's title caps the Rarity he may sell, and the second signature on a piece above his silver is a fee a great many people live on." (DARK:127). "Every port has a Grandmaster who lends a token to a sale for a fifth of the price and sits in the corner eating the seller's pears" (ITEMS:80). INVENTED: a Journeyman's countersign on an Uncommon sale goes for a tenth to a fifth of the price, by town and by how well the countersigner knows the seller.

**Rarity, as a buyer hears it** (ITEMS:27-35): Common, "Every market has it, and nobody remembers the maker"; Uncommon, "A good town's best shop stocks it, or will by spring"; Rare, "A city has a few, and the shop knows whose they were"; Very Rare, "A realm can name its holders"; Legendary, "Known by name across realms, with a public list of past owners". Rarity "is an argument, conducted by people with money on it, and the assay house that writes it down is mostly recording who won" (ITEMS:23), and it "moves whenever the world does" (ITEMS:25). The coil "will not read Rarity at all" (ITEMS:180).

**Freedom is not title.** A company's own membership runs Apprentice (indentured seven years), Freeman (by servitude, patrimony or redemption) and Liveryman (DARK:107-115). "Freedom and title are different things, and both show... a brilliant journey-hand who cannot afford the livery may carry a Master's and still eat at the bottom of the table." (DARK:117). "Redemption is the class question" (DARK:115). Writers: "Apprentice" names both an indenture class and the second title. Ned can hold an Apprentice's token without ever having been anybody's apprentice.

**Losing it.** "A title lost is a scandal of the same family: the hall strikes it, and the news goes down the road faster than the owner can" (TIERED:34); "by the next market nobody will hold his coat" (TITLE:58). The three things a hall or court can do: Struck, Excluded, Reserved (MERIT:75-79). Reserved is the Bench's: "not condemned and cannot trade, which is worse" (MERIT:79).

**The Mercers on file.** The Mercers' hall at Waxgate is a name to know among merchant guilds (ACCORD:36). Its Grandmaster, Dame Ottilie Marsh, held two Midmarch kings' crown jewels against their ransoms on one afternoon "because of a bit of silver at her belt" (TITLE:40). A Mercers' factor bought hide and tusks at Tollgarth "inside what his own token let him sell" (ACCORD:23). Waxgate itself is in the Sealed Charter, not the Midmarch: a river town of hives and pigment yards whose gate-toll "is a dab of hot wax on the back of your hand, in the color of your business" (CHARTER:48). A Paragon of the Mercers "has, at last count, sold one Unique piece and retired" (TIERED:157).

**INVENTED: the Mercers in the Midmarch.** The Mercers keep a lesser hall in a Midmarch city that reads Waxgate's precedents. Kingsbridge suits, because "The city on the height and the wharves on the bank belong in law to their own town council and to a bishop, and the Tuppence crown ends at the last stone of each pier" (CROWNS:34): a hall there stands outside any toll-king's reach.

## 3. How a hall examines Initiate, Apprentice and Journeyman

**Canon bones.** "The hall confers it by its own lights: a term and a masterpiece among the makers, a ledger that balanced for twenty years among the merchants" (TIERED:32; TITLE:56). Article XXIX: "Each hall confers them by its own lights and answers for what it confers" (ARTICLES:108). A hall's Court of Assistants governs "and is self-appointing" (DARK:103); the Clerk "in practice runs the company, since Masters last a year and Clerks last thirty"; the Beadle "carries the mace at a feast" and "throws you out of the hall" (DARK:105). Twenty years is the standard for the grand titles; the book needs a scaled version for the first three. What follows is INVENTED on those bones, built so that each step is "a ledger that balanced" at a smaller size.

**Getting on a roll at all (INVENTED).** A peddler who is no freeman of the Mercers is entered on the hall's **pack roll** as a road member, on one sponsor's word, paying quarterage on the four quarter-days (quarter-days are canon, VALUE:45; the hall's own "hall-fee, every quarter day" is ACCORD:58). INVENTED rate: 3 copper a quarter. He is signed, which is wax, and keeps his soul (ACCORD:66).

**The Initiate sitting (INVENTED).**
- His weights go on the hall's beam and are stamped and presented (Article II, ARTICLES:39); a cased weight is struck across with the six-point (Articles III and IV, ARTICLES:40-41).
- He swears the one weight (Article I, ARTICLES:38) in the formulary's only first person, "Ita spondeo," thus I pledge (INV:5).
- The pack read: the Clerk names ten things in his pack and he prices each against the city's cried list (COST:9 is the canon origin of that list).
- The short book: a month of split tallies, stock and foil, that fit his suppliers' halves and match his purse.
- Fee 2 silver (COST:149).

**The Apprentice sitting (INVENTED).**
- A season's book, quarter-day to quarter-day, taken through a **view**: the Clerk charges him with everything he took in (stock bought, consignments received, coin and kind) and discharges what went out (sales, tolls, stall fees, spoilage, gifts, theft sworn to). What he cannot account for is the arrears, and it must fall under a twentieth of the charge. (The method is the medieval charge-and-discharge account; section 12.)
- Two consignors as witnesses, compensated, before witnesses (Article VII, ARTICLES:49).
- The tray: ten pieces laid out, Common and Uncommon mixed, one forged stamp and one stale or cased item among them. He names each piece's Rarity and finds the frauds. Canon grounds: "A good forger beats a weak appraiser" (ITEMS:178), and "The usual frauds are a real blade with a false stamp, a low working dressed to run bright for a month, a past owner added to the list, and a Common piece aged into a Rare one in a bucket of cow urine" (ITEMS:178).
- He may be asked to swear to his Appraisal skill's Grade; a sworn lie about one's sheet "costs you, because an oath is small Fate, and only an assay can check it" (NATALIE.md:83, R84-43).
- Fee 6 silver (COST:150).

**The Journeyman sitting (INVENTED).**
- A year's book across four quarter-days, audited and quit, with no arrears carried forward.
- Sureties: two members of Apprentice standing or above, or one of Adept or above, each answering for 5 gold if he defaults on an Uncommon consignment within his first year.
- Two appraisers who dislike each other. Canon practice above Rare: "a sane buyer above Rare hires two appraisers who dislike each other and puts the piece on a coil besides" (ITEMS:180). The Mercers stage it at the Journeyman sitting as a test of nerve as much as eye.
- The law, asked aloud: forestalling, regrating and engrossing (Articles XXIII to XXV), the pledge (XXVIII), the second signature (XXX), the stamp (XXXI), the false token (XXXIII).
- **The belt.** The Clerk asks him to show his belt and to swear what tokens and oaths of arms he carries. This is the door the hall rule walks through (section 4). Canon model of a man who reads belts first: Hollis Draycott's Trait READS THE BELT (`wiki/Characters/Volume I*/Hollis Draycott.md:9`).
- A vote of the Court of Assistants. Fee 1½ gold (COST:151), which the hall "may advance against your term" if it likes him (COST:145).

**Timing (INVENTED).** A year from Initiate to Journeyman is fast for a peddler and slow for a novel. A hall may sit a man early on a **short book** (a half-year audited, sureties doubled), which lets the outline put the Journeyman sitting near the end of book one without breaking the "ledger that balanced" rule.

**Literacy (INVENTED).** The audit presumes a written book. If Ned's letters are poor, the hall takes his tallies and a scrivener's fair copy; letter-writing is a paid trade at 2 copper a letter (COST:55).

## 4. The hall rule

**Isaac's direction, as filed.** "the merchant halls of the Guild Accord bar a sworn fighter from holding a trust title, because a man who is both is buyer and seller of the same blood; "one has to sell to the other" is the plain-speech gloss of the rule, and the root of the jibe "Warrior Merchant"." (RULINGS.md:4169).

**Natalie's reading, open to overturn.** "it is a hall rule kept by every merchant hall, not a numbered Article, and it binds sworn fighting-guild members (a fighting token or a sworn company); a merchant who fights unsworn breaks no rule, and the halls may grant an exception by a hall's vote." (RULINGS.md:4169). Context: "Canon had nothing on holding two halls' tokens (What Things Cost, The Tiered Path). It is the engine of the merchant novella, whose lead means to be the first exception." (RULINGS.md:4171).

**Who counts, under the reading on file.**
- Holding a fighting guild's token: an adventurers' hall, a free company, any guild "paid to be dangerous" that reads its titles off the Stage table (TIERED:44).
- Signing into a sworn company: a free company's instrument, which "signs under seal" (MILITARY:18).
- Not covered by its letter: a merchant who fights unsworn (spars, defends his pack, kills a robber on the road); a sect disciple, since "Sect rank and Accord title are separate and both matter" (R84-38, NATALIE.md:81); a man under a personal vow, which is "small Fate, anyone's to make" (NATALIE.md:77). Each of these is where a hall will argue (INVENTED).
- A canon edge worth knowing: Dame Ysolt Varley, "a Master and an elder of the Vermilion Bell", a fighting school, sat at the Mercers' election feast with a silver token at her throat (`wiki/A Reader's Codex.md:9`). Which hall's token it was is not stated. If the book makes it a Mercers' title, she is living proof that sect rank sits outside the rule's letter.

**A losing argument (INVENTED texture).** The Accord itself splits members into signed ("wax") and sworn (the Lattice oath, "a fragment of their Soul Crystal", ACCORD:62-68). A wording-man from Waxgate's Wording Row (`wiki/The Magic System/Practice and Institution/Blessings and Curses.md:15`) can argue that "sworn" in the hall rule means Lattice-sworn only, so a signed fighting token is no bar. The reading on file rejects that: a fighting token is enough. Use it as an argument that loses, unless Isaac overturns the reading.

**What "one has to sell to the other" means at a counter (INVENTED rationale, consistent with canon).**
1. **The dead.** A man who fights and trades in gear makes the dead whose kit he sells. For Ned, who deals in dead men's swords and learns to fight from them, this is the rule's nightmare made flesh.
2. **The blade sold today is drawn on its buyer tomorrow.**
3. **The trust.** A title is "what lets a stranger hand you the goods" (RULINGS.md:3731). A man who could take the goods by force breaks the reason for the silver.
4. **Pressure is a false weight.** A woken fighter's Pressure "shows in the room first... then the bodies" (NATALIE.md:253), and the unwoken feel dread from F to D. A haggle under Pressure is a thumb on the scale, and the Accord's first Article is "One weight, one measure, one reach, through all the land" (ARTICLES:38). A hall would call a fighter's Pressure at the counter a false weight. Midmarch street slang already has the image: Pressure is "the crown", and a strong fighter entering an inn "is said to have put it on" (CROWNS:70).

**How a hall reads the rule against a man (INVENTED procedure on canon bones).** At a sitting of the Court of Assistants, before witnesses, the Clerk reads the rule from the hall's book against the member's name and enters the reading on the roll (the Bench's "power of entry" is the canon model, ARTICLES:120). Its effects:
- a fighting token found on him strikes his merchant title on sight (Struck, MERIT:75);
- until he chooses, the hall may decline to sit him for a higher title;
- any hall that sees the entry may ask him to show his belt;
- the Beadle or the Clerk says the gloss aloud, "one has to sell to the other," and the room repeats it to everyone who was not there.

**The fighting halls do not care (INVENTED inference).** Nothing on file bars a merchant from a fighting token from the fighting side. A hiring hall will take his 6 silver for an Apprentice's sitting and enjoy the joke. The bar is the merchants' to enforce.

**The exception (INVENTED detail on Natalie's reading).** A hall's vote: a majority of the Court of Assistants and the assent of the territory's Guildlord, never yet granted. Book one should not grant it; the series arc is Ned's becoming the first.

**The yard test.** Canon: for the fighting guilds "an examiner confirms it with a coil (the Accord's assay instrument) and a fight in the yard" (TIERED:44), and "Examinations read the seat before they read output" (TITLE:74). At Stage II the coil reads Welling, so the token on offer is Apprentice, Grade D, T2, Green (TIERED:49), fee 6 silver (COST:150). INVENTED: the fight is against the hall's examiner or a hired hand, to yield or first blood, in the yard in front of whoever is drinking there, and the hall strikes the token the same day if he passes and pays. Natalie's reading turns on holding, so he can pass the test and refuse the silver; refusing a token a hall has just struck for you is its own scene.

**Two design constraints for the outline.**
- If his Waking Draft is staked against a **term of fighting service** (the canon patronage road: "a hall stakes a promising hand a Draft against a signed term of service, and the term is the real product", GATE:71), he is in a sworn company in all but name and the rule bars his Journeyman token. Write the bond as money (section 6), or the ending breaks.
- Selling arms to a war is not a breach under the reading on file, but a Court voting on a Journeyman who supplies levies will hear "buyer and seller of the same blood" from somebody (deal 10).

## 5. Roads, tolls, stalls, licenses and the market court

**The Midmarch patchwork.** "The Guild Accord reaches in patches: the stretches from the Brine crossing down to the marches of Sum-gol signed the first circle at Kharven-Seat, along with several kings who have since been deposed by others who say they never agreed. Past that line the Articles are a custom a merchant may take or leave, though the market court and the dusty foot are so handy that most towns keep them anyway." (CROWNS:50). "Where a circle has signed, the Articles and the Commission framework bind as law" (ACCORD:92). INVENTED: a peddler asks at every chain, "Signed or custom?", after the canon greeting "Who's king today?" (CROWNS:72).

**Tolls.**
- "every lane has a chain across it with someone sitting beside the chain" (CROWNS:24). Ashbury to Kingsbridge: "Kessen road, and count the chains" (GAZ:52).
- Kept road: "1 to 2 silver a day for a heavy wain... set by the load and by her opinion of you" (COST:72).
- "A keeper may take a toll only where she keeps the stretch passable" (ACCORD:25); Article XXVII: "Toll forfeit; the stretch reassigned" (ARTICLES:97). The canon precedent is Queen Tizz Copperwick amerced at Tollgarth for tolling a road with "a hole in it you could lose a calf in" (ACCORD:25).
- Petty arts at the chain: "the toll-sitter who cannot be lied to about what is in a cart until his fee has been paid" (CROWNS:70).
- Nether Marl: King Hamo "charges a toll going in and a much larger one coming out" (CROWNS:96).
- Water: "1 copper the two miles by barge; 1 copper to cross at a river stair" (COST:59).
- INVENTED rates for a foot pack: on an unsigned chain, 1 copper a pack, 2 to 4 for a handcart, 2 for a mule-load; a lane of chains can take 10 to 20 copper in a day, which is a laborer's wage and the reason a peddler wants a token. On a signed stretch, a token's road-writ fixes the toll at 1 copper a day for a foot pack, paid once at the first lodge and entered on the writ. Kingsbridge bridge: 2 copper a pack.

**Stalls (INVENTED; nothing on file beyond "the stall up to its Rarity", MERIT:20).**
- A cloth on the ground at a village market: 1 copper the day to whoever holds the market.
- A trestle in a town market: 2 to 4 copper the day, 1 silver the week.
- A booth at a fair: 1 to 3 silver the fair; three times that at the Coat-Turning in Pennycrown (CROWNS:42) or Lastpenny's Noon Fair (CROWNS:106).
- A pitch in a hall's yard, for token-holders: 1 to 2 copper the day, and the stall's Rarity ceiling is the token's.
- Midmarch market days "run one town after another so nobody can miss one" (CROWNS:68), so a peddler's circuit follows them. Do not call it a "round": canon uses that word for a warden's lamp round (COST:15; INV:5, "Still on the round?").

**Licenses.**
- Canon: the street trades each need a license "and none of them can afford" it (WORKS:376). Drafts: "Licensed stock carries an excise stamp and the pourer's mark" (DRAFTS:187); "The trade is licensed, taxed at the gate and smuggled around the tax" (DRAFTS:191).
- After waking: "Taking pay for a working or a hunt without a guild license does" break the schedules (GATE:79), and "A stirred Crystal is entered on the roll of the place it was found" (GATE:85).
- INVENTED: each Midmarch crown sells its own pack-license, a tag with wax in the crown's colors, a few copper to a silver a year, and counterfeit tags are common. Inside a signed stretch the token covers passage, and the town still wants its license to sell inside the walls.
- INVENTED caution for Ned: a reading sold to someone else for a fee is "pay for a working". Keep Provenance inside his own buying and selling. Canon precedent for paid readings of the dead exists: at the Lower Reading, an Anamnesis dungeon, "Scholars go down on a rope to hear a dead witness and pay the posted price per question" (DUNGEONS:234).

**The beam and the weights (Title I, ARTICLES:38-42).** One weight; every weight stamped and presented; a false weight struck with the six-point; no cased weight, and "Any person may sound a weight at any hour without leave"; grain by level measure. The Measurewright comes "through on circuit with a fork and a bad temper" (ACCORD:58). Market insults: "Pondus falsum" (false weight), "Cased", "Short loaf" (INV:43, 45, 46).

**Forestalling, regrating and engrossing, read onto the dead-gear trade** (ARTICLES:86-88, Title V, "Of Bread, Ale and the Common Sale"):
- **Forestalling** (XXIII): "Buying goods on the road before they reach the market, to sell them dearer in it." Penalty: "Amercement; goods forfeit." A peddler who meets a company on the road home and buys its plunder before it reaches the market square has forestalled. Buy at the pay wagon in the square, or at a stair where no market is.
- **Regrating** (XXIV): "Buying in a market to resell in the same market at a higher price." Penalty: "Amercement; barred from the market." Carry the sword to the next market.
- **Engrossing** (XXV): "Buying up the whole of a commodity to control its price." Penalty: "The largest amercements the Accord has ever levied." Buying every helm in a Hem town before a levy musters.
- INVENTED reading: Title V was written for victuals, and market courts read Articles XXIII to XXV onto arms and clothing as "the common sale" whenever a plaintiff asks, which is often.

**The courts.**
- "The market court sits in the market, in the same building as the beam and the guardhouse. It hears debt, detinue and broken contract, and it absorbs whatever else the market throws at it" (ARTICLES:118). Detinue is the action to recover goods wrongly held: it is what a widow brings for her husband's sword.
- The dusty foot (Article XXVI): "A traveler's plea is heard and determined before the market closes" (ARTICLES:96), "the single most valuable procedural rule the Accord has" (ARTICLES:100). "a traveler carries his plea to the nearest market court if he has any sense" (CROWNS:78).
- Amercement "is set by the court to the offender's means" (ARTICLES:124).
- The Bench of Attribution above it: "entry, striking, attribution and reservation" (ARTICLES:120).
- Sancta Lux hears "oath, status, legitimacy, and whether a thing was done in bad faith rather than merely done" (ARTICLES:122). At Kingsbridge the Scale Minster's bishop "has the busiest court in the country" (CROWNS:34). A claim that Ned bought a widow's sword knowing it was stolen goes there.
- Canon on what the law merchant is: "not a universal code invented by merchants. It refers to a jurisdiction and to certain procedural variances permitted in specific commercial courts" (ARTICLES:26).

## 6. Credit and debt

**Tallies.** "A split stick, both halves held, is a complete and forgery-resistant record of a debt between two people who cannot read, and it is legally admissible in six kingdoms." (VALUE:295). "A ferryman who works the line between two of them keeps two tally-sticks and two faces" (ARTICLES:152). Texture: "the split tally, notched, halves that fit only each other" (INV:11). Borrowed shape (section 12): the longer half, the stock, goes to whoever advanced the goods, and the shorter foil to whoever received them; cut widths stand for sums. INVENTED Midmarch notching: a palm's width for a gold mark, a thumb for a silver, a barleycorn for a copper.

**Bills.** "a bill drawn on the crew's own hall" (ACCORD:23). A bill on Ironlink Hold was "honored in full inside the month by men who had never heard of the ship" (VALUE:17). Nalūn's Parun-bound contracts are workings: "breaking it has consequences that arrive without anyone needing to be convinced, subpoenaed, or bribed" (VALUE:160). "Nearly every crown owes the factor houses of Nalūn" (CROWNS:60), and "a Nalūn scribe with a march contract is the most frightening thing a Midmarch army ever meets" (CROWNS:120). INVENTED: a peddler rarely holds a Nalūn bill; when he does, failing to deliver is not a court matter. It lands.

**Ventures.** "A bottomry exists: a loan against the venture that is repaid only if the venture returns. It costs a great deal and it is what a crew without a patron uses." (DARK:145). A peddler's trip to the Hem towns can be raised the same way (INVENTED).

**Pledges.** Article XXVIII sets "What may be taken in pledge, and what may not: tools of a man's craft, and the last beast." Penalty: "Restitution" (ARTICLES:98). INVENTED Midmarch pawn rate: a copper in the silver for every forty days, the length of a levy (CROWNS:80), about four parts in a hundred per forty days. The real-world rates in section 12 run higher.

**The person as security.** "A young practitioner may sell a term of their future output to fund their training... and a family without a woken child may sell the child's term to a guild against a Waking Draft, on the understanding that the Draft very often kills or warps and the debt survives either way." (VALUE:246). "the modern bond has a term, a price, a court, and a theoretical buyout" (VALUE:247).

### The Waking Draft on guild debt

**Canon.** "A Waking Draft opens a Crystal with no teacher and no seven years... It is very dear, sixty to a hundred and twenty gold in a good year, and it often kills or warps the child who drinks it. Most of the rich buy one for their children anyway, generally with a physician standing by and a second child in reserve, while commoners sell themselves into guild debt for one." (GATE:23). "Once a Crystal has sealed it is woken rarely, by a crisis, a master's forcing, a tribulation, a Waking Draft or a blessing, and always at great cost." (GATE:24). "The window opens out with Essence and Stage I, and no class yet... whatever it was feeling at that moment is built into what comes out" (DRAFTS:134). "the term is generally the larger bill" (COST:141). R84-37 (NATALIE.md:81).

**Who writes the bond (INVENTED; three options, one recommended).**
- **A hiring hall (recommended).** Hiring halls are the canon patrons of the promising poor (DARK:150; GATE:71). Write it as a money bond, a fifth of his takings, not service, so he stays unsworn. The hall's interest is that a woken man is worth a fighter's retainer, 6 to 12 gold a month at Apprentice standing (COST:177), and a fifth of that beats a fifth of a pack. So his creditor wants him in its yard, and the merchants read the rule against him when he goes. Both halves of book one's ending come out of one instrument.
- The Worshipful Company of Drafters (ACCORD:38) selling its own Draft on a bond. Clean, and less pressure toward the yard.
- The Mercers. A title needs no woken Crystal, so the Mercers have no reason to stake a waking unless a factor has seen what Ned does with a blade's history. Possible later; a conflict of interest in book one.

**The instrument, on paper (INVENTED, in formulary order).** Only formulary wordings bind and "A clause improved is a clause void" (Article VI, ARTICLES:48); witnesses compensated (VII); not dated to Munahi, which is February 29 (VIII; NATALIE.md:263); the saving clause (IX); registered within one turn (X; see flag 2 in section 11).

```
BOND OF THE FIFTH, NINE YEARS
Ita spondeo. I, Ned Chapman, chapman, of the pack roll,
take of the Hall one Waking Draft, poured at a licensed house,
stamped and sealed, valued at ninety gold marks, drunk in the
Hall's presence with the Hall's leech by.
For it I owe the fifth part of all I take, coin and kind, by
trade or by hire, from the morning I wake, reckoned by my book
and my tallies and paid on each quarter-day for nine years,
which is thirty-six quarter-days; and never less in any quarter
than fifteen silver marks, whatever I take.
This bond stands whether the Draft wakes, warps or kills. If I
die, my pack, my stock, the silver of my token and all sums owed
me pass to the Hall to the balance, and my sureties answer the rest.
My book lies open to the Hall's clerk on every quarter-day.
A false book is a broken bond and a false weight.
I may buy this bond out on any quarter-day for twice the Draft's
value, less what I have paid.
Saving the right of a third party. Before witnesses.
```

**Nine years of a man's work at a fifth, two readings (INVENTED arithmetic on canon prices).**

*Reading A, a fifth of gross takings (recommended).* The trap is that a fifth of gross is not a fifth of profit. A peddler's margin runs a quarter to a third of what passes his hand, so in a lean year the fifth eats most of his profit. The floor (15 silver a quarter, about a laborer's month, COST:113) is what breaks a pack-man in winter.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Peddler, woken midyear | 40 gold | 8 gold (floor 5) | 8 | 82 short |
| 2 | Journeyman, Uncommon goods | 150 gold | 30 gold | 38 | 52 short |
| 3 | Journeyman, a stall and a mule | 220 gold | 44 gold | 82 | 8 short |

If he never rises, a fifth of a peddler's 40 gold a year for nine years is about 72 gold: the hall loses a little on the Draft and owns a fifth of a man who cannot get ahead. If he rises, the hall makes several times its outlay. That is what canon means by "the term is the real product" (GATE:71).

*Reading B, one working day in five owed to the hall.* Nine years of about three hundred working days, a fifth of them, is about 540 days. As unwoken labor at 12 to 24 copper a day (COST:167) that is 22 to 45 gold, less than the Draft. As a woken Apprentice-standing hand hired out at the retainer rate, 6 to 12 gold a month (COST:177), it is about 130 to 260 gold. So the day-in-five reading only pays the hall if he is hired out as a fighter, which is a sworn company in all but name, and the hall rule bites. Prefer reading A.

**After he wakes (canon consequences that touch the trade).**
- Registration on the roll of the place it happened (GATE:85). A bottle-woken soul is the kind that "has a very good coat and a receipt" (GATE:125).
- The counter: a Welling practitioner's power "leaks into the room", and Metering Tonic "Tightens a leaking Shell for six to nine hours" for 10 copper (DRAFTS:98). INVENTED application: a woken peddler at a counter of unwoken buyers pays for his Shell's manners.

## 7. The gear of the dead

**Canon frame.**
- "Spoils with a history (Things taken or given (a dead man's blade, a horse...) arrive with their lineage (R70-48), and the Ledger keeps who wants them back)" (R72-30, RULINGS.md:2695; its draw and firearm wording is superseded, RULINGS.md:3575).
- R70-48: a notable object is described "through who made, carried, mended and died with it".
- "Provenance is the dearest thing to fake and the hardest to catch, because no instrument has ever measured who died holding something." (ITEMS:178). Ned's concept is the instrument the trade does not have.
- "a buyer pays for the list of who died holding it as readily as for the Grade. The oldest and worst of them come off the dead battlefields, and the people who sell those generally cannot say who made them or what the maker's ceiling was" (ITEMS:190).
- R71-84: "Lots can be forged, adulterated, stale or unentered, and a scene may turn on a bad lot; the forgery is always findable by mark, assay or the Index's own failure."
- R85-1: "a poor fighter's mended Common gear and a rich fool's untested Legendary each tell on their owner" (NATALIE.md:87).

**Where a peddler lawfully buys it.**

1. **The stair.** The Stair-Sutler "Sells rope, bolts, salt, hot pies and fresh advice at the head of any dungeon that has a street on it. Buys the gear of the dead at a fair price from people too tired to argue. The only trade at the stair that has never once lost money", and pays for it by "Being asked, by someone whose sister did not come up, why that coat is on the rail" (WORKS:303). "Anything left behind by the last crew goes to whoever can carry it past the next crew." (WORKS:294). "Dungeons keep what people left in them... a dead Master's gear with its reputation still on it" (DUNGEONS:182). Crews "go down six and come up four, and the two are entered in the book before the share is struck" (WORKS:298). "Parts belong to the crew unless the instrument says otherwise, and a good instrument says otherwise at length." (ACCORD:141). The saving clause "is the clause a dead crewman's widow will bring to court" (DUNGEONS:281). INVENTED practice: the stair pays a third of new for whole pieces and a sixth for pieces that want work; a dead member's kit is entered by the captain or the hall clerk before it is sold, and his share of it is his estate's.

2. **The field, inside the law.** Midmarch war "pays the Midmarch at both ends: victuals sold to the host coming up, plunder bought from it going home" (CROWNS:56). Inside the window, lawfully or otherwise" (CROSSING:163-165). That last point is C-179, open (CONFLICTS.md:1967-1974): one page calls battlefield rendering legal, the other calls a Crystal cut from a dead practitioner the worst crime in every realm, "sourced from battlefields after dark" (CORES:199). Commanders license their sutlers: "Every commander despises them and every commander licenses them" (BATTLE:36).
   **INVENTED lawful lane, written to stay clear of C-179:** Ned buys finished gear only, from a company's plunder-master or a lord's quartermaster, at a pay wagon in a market town, after the company's clerk has entered its dead. He never walks a field, never strips a body, never buys before a field is released, and never buys, carries or touches a stone, a Crystal, bone-ash, or anything a dresser has handled.
   Supply: the Turn against the Scourge "runs North, then South, then Center, nine years each", and "The Center holds it now, from 712 to 721", with free companies on Thresh contracts doing "most of the dying" (CROWNS:108). Midmarch levies serve forty days and go home for harvest (CROWNS:80). So in 715 dead men's kit comes off the Hem every season and off the levies every autumn.

After the wake the kit is the estate's. INVENTED Midmarch order of claims on a dead man's gear:

   - the crew's or company's instrument;
   - the widow, who sells what is left to pay the rest. INVENTED price: about half of new for good pieces, because widows know prices.

4. **The estate lot.** When Arbitration divides an estate, finished goods rank fifth of six, after contracted Essence, certified persons, holding-grade material and coin (VALUE:83-90), so a bankrupt's gear sells cheap and in lots. Legendary pieces come by "An estate sale, a war, a very large auction" (ITEMS:33). INVENTED: the **dead man's lot**, a sealed chest sold as seen, on the model of the Accord's own purchase of the Ribs on "a bill of sale that reads, in full, one carcass, condition as seen" (CHARTER:40).

**Hazards.**
- **Detinue and the saving clause.** The market court hears detinue (ARTICLES:118); the saving clause "Cannot be contracted out of" (ARTICLES:51).
- **Bloodied and stolen goods (INVENTED Accord custom, borrowed from real fripper statutes, section 12).** Bloodied cloth is washed before it is sold, and stolen goods knowingly sold are forfeit and the seller is a fence. The real Cornhill problem is canon's sister on the stair: a buyer finds his own lost goods on the rail.
- **Rarity moves.** "when a kiln falls in or a war melts a family's plate, every surviving piece goes up a step overnight" (ITEMS:25). A famous death can lift a Common piece to Uncommon or Rare, past an Apprentice's silver, while it sits in his pack.
- **Concord gear.** "Weapons are never inherited. They choose successors by harmonic familiarity." (MILITARY:98). INVENTED reading: Concord-issued sworn gear (a Lawbrand, a Resonant Harness) in a dead man's lot is not merchandise; it goes back to a hall, and a peddler caught selling one meets Enforcement.
- **Relics.** True relics "read as question marks to any appraiser", and "a merchant who offers you one has found a very old sword, or has found a way to die" (ITEMS:163).
- **Monster gear carries the beast.** "A Tinder-fox-hide jerkin keeps its wearer up at night" (ITEMS:127), and it carries the beast's name and bounty (ITEMS:129).
- **Stones.** "Most realms therefore hang people for holding an unrendered stone above T4 without a hall's chit" (CORES:187). By Rarity, T1 and T2 stones are Common, T3 Uncommon, T4 Rare (CORES:71-74), and "a T6 stone needs a Master's token or a second signature, and a T7 needs a Grandmaster" (CORES:217). So a Journeyman may lawfully deal a T3 dungeon stone with a hall's chit and nothing higher. Never a battlefield stone (C-179).
- **The crime next door.** "A person who dies with no one to sit up with them is what the night-cutters look for" (CORES:201). The Dead Man's Cordial "runs several times the price of a core lot of the same grade" (CORES:195). Anyone who deals in the dead is near this trade, and some of his suppliers will be in it.

**Provenance and the trade (INVENTED application of Isaac's Q6; the powers brief governs the mechanics).**
- It authenticates a past-owner list, and "a past owner added to the list" is one of the usual frauds (ITEMS:178).
- It names who died holding a piece, and that names a claimant. The Bench refuses individual source attribution on Draft seals because it "would give every Class IV holding a standing claimant among the source's surviving family" (BENCH:74). A merchant who can name a dead owner makes the same trouble for a sword.
- It can lift a piece above his title by giving it a famous past.
- Its cost, "every past he reads stays in him" (answers.json, Q6), makes a thirty-piece lot thirty pasts. INVENTED trade habit: gloves, and a porter's hook for lots he will not read.
- Its counter, "new or wiped gear" (Q6). INVENTED: the trade's commonest wipe is a reforged or re-hilted blade sold as new, the old frippers' crime of remaking used goods to deceive. To Ned it reads blank where a past should be, which is itself a tell.

## 9. What real chapmen carried and how they sold (ideas only)

**From the research (section 12).**
- *The word.* Old English *céapmann*, a dealer; "cheap" from "a good cheap", a good deal; "chap" first meant the customer one bargains with; Cheapside and Chipping are market names. By 1600 "chapman" meant an itinerant dealer in particular. The book's joke has a second floor: Ned the chapman was once, in the old word, just "Ned the dealer", and a "chap" is the man he sells to.
- *The goods.* Petty chapmen carried haberdashery and cheap cloth: laces, ribbons, pins, needles, thread, tapes, buttons, combs, small linens; cheap lace was the clearest sign of small luxury reaching ordinary people. Long-distance peddlers sold remedies. Chapmen carried chapbooks and ballads (printed in our history; hand-copied in WOTR).
- *The selling.* Door to door, at the edges of markets and fairs, in church porches on holy days and Sundays, and out of inns as agents; they bought surplus at fairs and resold it to people who could not get to market, who paid more for the convenience. In English law a peddler traveled on foot with no beast of burden and had to keep moving when not selling.
- *Licensing.* Elizabethan dealers in grain (badgers) needed a license from three justices at quarter sessions, written by the clerk of the peace for twelve pence, with an optional bond of up to five pounds; licenses expired on the first of May, and counterfeit licenses carried by vagabonds were a constant problem.
- *Reputation.* Regulations and churchmen made the peddler a figure of suspicion, linked to petty crime.
- *Frippers.* Dealers in old clothes, furniture and household goods were confined to one London market and later barred from trading after the evening bell because of abuses; ordinances forbade selling stolen or bloodied goods and remaking used clothing to deceive. A fifteenth-century poem complains of seeing one's own stolen hood for sale there. In Montpellier the dealers in used clothes had statutes from 1322, and used clothing was valuable enough to be willed, given in dowries and handed to servants.
- *Pawnbrokers.* Lombards and Cahorsins lent on pledges at twenty to thirty in the hundred a year; the Italian public pawn banks charged about five. Interest hid inside repurchase prices. Roman law barred pledging clothing, furniture and farm tools, a close cousin of Article XXVIII.
- *Sutlers.* Traders who followed Greek armies went mainly to buy plunder cheap and resell it in the cities. The Army of Flanders regulations of 1596 allowed three sutlers to a company of two hundred, and more came. Sutlers sold food, drink, clothing and custom gear, and gave soldiers credit against payday.
- *Spoils.* English war ordinances of 1385 had a soldier pay a third of his winnings to his captain, who paid a third of that third to the king; indentures commonly gave the captain a third, sometimes half. Prisoners were bought and sold after battles.
- *Heriot.* From Old English for war-gear: a death duty of a dead man's arms and horse to his lord, later a lord's right to a dead tenant's best beast or goods. Death in battle often meant no heriot, since the winner took the horse and armor anyway.
- *The market court.* The court of piepowders, from the dusty feet of travelers, judged a fair's disputes fast, within a day and a half in twelfth-century England and Scotland; a losing defendant's property could be seized, appraised and sold. Scholars dispute whether the law merchant was ever one universal code, which canon already says (ARTICLES:26).
- *Accounts.* Medieval estate accounts ran as charge and discharge, with a midyear view and a yearly audit, and the balance left owing measured the official's debt to his lord. This is the model for section 3's "ledger that balanced".

**Ned's pack (INVENTED, every item checked against canon).**
- *Haberdashery:* pins by the paper, needles, thread, laces and points, tapes, buttons, combs, thimbles, small knives, spoons, a few small looking-glasses, cheap lace.
- *Colors:* ribbons and sashes in school colors (every Midmarch town "has a bracket ground, a sect in a loud shirt", CROWNS:68) and in the colors of three kings at once, since "Who's king today?" is the greeting (CROWNS:72) and banners run to "scarlet bulls, blue-and-yellow checks, a gold goose on purple, three fish and a ladder" (CROWNS:24).
- *Paper:* hand-copied news bills (1 copper), song sheets from the Midmarch minstrels who "get the details wrong" (CROWNS:58), Guild day-rate slips (3 copper, COST:61), almanac sheets (INV:23, keyed to the real calendar now, R85-2).
- *Small light:* tallow by the dozen, wicks, a few beeswax tapers.
- *Stamped everyday Drafts:* Willow Gray, Penny Tonic, Snowmilk, and Cutwater with a date he writes on it himself, since the trade writes none (DRAFTS:94).
- *Luck:* lead hero's badges (COST:95) and lucky teeth for the Hem cutters, "a loud, scarred, superstitious trade in gray wraps and lucky teeth" (GAZ:265).
- *Road goods:* boot-nails, bootlaces, buckles, strap leather, whetstones, salt by the bar.
- *The rail:* worn boots, coats, a saber or two, helms, the gear of the dead.
- *Barter he takes (INVENTED):* rags, feathers, old iron, eggs, a day's carrying owed, all consistent with "obligations are recorded rather than settled" (VALUE:289).

## 10. Ten deals, small to large

Prices from section 8; everything specific here is INVENTED unless cited.

**1. The chain lane.** A day through four crowns' lanes selling pins, laces and Penny Tonic. Buys a gross of pins for 6 copper and sells twelve papers at 1 copper; two dozen laces for 8 copper, sold at 1 copper each; a jug of Penny Tonic for 10 copper, sold at 1 copper a cup (DRAFTS:97). Gross about 50 copper. Out: stock 24, eight chains at 1 copper, a village cloth 1, bed 3, meal 5. Net: almost nothing. *Risk:* a chain on an unkept stretch (Article XXVII says no toll there, and arguing it costs the afternoon); a toll-sitter whose petty art reads his pack (CROWNS:70); a Tollgarth mark light on metal under the changer's hammer (CROWNS:66). The lesson that sends him for a token's road-writ (GATENET:12).

**2. Whoever's colors.** Before a valley's war he carries thirty yards each of three kings' ribbons, bought at 1 copper a yard, and sells whichever side is winning at 2 to 3 copper. About a silver a color. *Risk:* selling a deposed king's colors in his successor's market; "A king can hang you in his kingdom, and if his kingdom is small you may be able to see the safe side from the gallows" (CROWNS:78). The loyal toast is "Long live whoever" (CROWNS:88).

**3. Boots off the stair.** At a Mouth town the Stair-Sutler sells him six pairs of worn boots for 1 silver a pair; he re-nails each for 2 copper and sells them two markets on at 2 to 2½ silver. Clear 6 to 8 silver. *Risk:* "someone whose sister did not come up" (WORKS:303); the dead delver's estate claims under the saving clause (DUNGEONS:281); unwashed blood on the laces; at Nether Marl, the toll coming out (CROWNS:96). After he wakes, boots are gear too, and a dead man's last steps stay in him.

**4. The widow's saber.** In Lastpenny, after the armed wake, a Thresh cutter's widow sells his trooper's saber for 4 silver and his coat for 2. He sells the saber for 7 silver to a levy man two markets on. *Risk:* a crown's war-gear claim on the best weapon (the INVENTED heriot), perhaps from two crowns; the White Aprons' bill on the estate, so creditors may ask the market court to void a sale at an undervalue; regrating if he sells in Lastpenny itself (Article XXIV). After waking, the saber shows him how the cutter died, and may name who killed him.

**5. The forty-day pledge.** A levy sergeant pledges his officer's blade (4 to 7 gold new) for 1½ gold for the forty days, redeemable at 18 silver and 18 copper. Unredeemed, it sells for 3 to 4 gold. *Risk:* Article XXVIII: a soldier's sword is arguably a tool of his craft, and the court orders restitution (ARTICLES:98); the sergeant dies on the levy and his widow brings detinue; if the blade carries a known maker's mark it is Uncommon, and an Initiate or Apprentice cannot sell it without a second signature (Article XXX).

**6. Marble-pickers' stones.** At a dungeon's Mouth he buys three T1 stones from core-boys, "Lawful, and not on anybody's roll" (CORES:216), at 2 silver each with the hall clerk's chit, walks them in a salt sack (salt and cold "about double" the holding times, CORES:104) to the rendering house, and sells at 3 to 4 silver each, paid "by what is left" (CORES:89). Clear 3 to 6 silver. *Risk:* a T1 stone "Holds full for a day or two" and is half gone in a week (CORES:93); "In a shirt, against a living person, it leaks into you" (CORES:104); a stone without a chit; never above T4 unrendered (CORES:187); never a battlefield stone (C-179). A deal the book can use once, to show him why he stays in gear.

**7. The pay-wagon lot.** A Thresh-contract company comes in off the Hem after a bad season under the Turn. At the pay wagon in the square its plunder-master sells the kit of its dead, already entered by the company clerk: six open helms, four padded jacks, three spears, two hand-spanned crossbows, a dozen bolts, a Proofed buckler. Ned pays 6 gold for the lot on a factor's tally, under the third-of-new rule because the company wants coin tonight, and sells it across three markets for 12 to 15 gold, most of it in the buckler. *Risk:* forestalling if he meets the column on the road (Article XXIII); a dead man's share of plunder is his kin's under the company's own articles and the saving clause; the buckler may carry a loud mark and read Uncommon, above an Apprentice's silver; the camp's licensed sutlers resent a stranger at their wagon (BATTLE:36); Greater Wolves follow armies (CROWNS:94).

**8. Above his silver.** A Mercers' factor fronts him a Tempered short sword, Grade C, Uncommon, with a known maker's mark, to sell at a fair for 25 gold. As an Apprentice he needs a Journeyman's countersign at a tenth to a fifth (2½ to 5 gold), and his own commission is a tenth. *Risk:* sold on his own token, the sale is voidable, he is amerced, and the second time he is struck (Article XXX); the factor's stamp is forged, and "A forger at B has cut a Tempered mark that a Grade D appraiser's own soul will report as true" (ITEMS:178); a buyer from a fighting school wants to try the edge on the seller. A good candidate for "the second deal that goes wrong" (answers.json, Q1).

**9. The dead man's lot (the hinge).** Arbitration divides a dead free-company captain's estate, finished goods last but one (VALUE:83-90), in sealed lots sold as seen. Ned bids 9 gold on credit for a chest of thirty pieces. Inside is the sword with the mark he has looked for since he was eleven. *Risk:* pieces above his title, which he must declare to his hall and either sell under a second signature or keep (INVENTED reading of Article XXX: keeping is not dealing, and giving a sword back to its owner is not a sale, which matters for the ending); Concord-issued gear in the lot (MILITARY:98); a living claimant, since "the Ledger keeps who wants them back" (R72-30); thirty pasts if he reads them all; and her sword itself may read Rare, "the shop knows whose they were" (ITEMS:31).

**10. The levy order (the large one).** A Midmarch king commissions arms for his forty-day levy before the harvest: sixty spears, twenty helms, ten crossbows and two hundred bolts, for 40 gold, half on delivery and half by a bill on a Nalūn factor house. Ned assembles it from dead men's gear across the Hem towns on a Mercers' factor's credit and clears 10 to 15 gold. *Risk:* engrossing if he buys up a market's whole stock of arms (Article XXV, "The largest amercements the Accord has ever levied"); the king is deposed before the bill matures, "A man who has been promised something is said to have a Pennycrown deed" (CROWNS:88); the Nalūn half enforces itself if he fails to deliver (VALUE:160); and at his Journeyman sitting the Court hears that the chapman who sells the dead also arms the next war. Not a breach of the hall rule under the reading on file; the vote is another matter.

## 11. Flags for the book team (recorded here, not ruled)

1. **C-179 is open** (CONFLICTS.md:1967-1974). Keep Ned out of battlefield rendering, battlefield stones and anything a dresser has touched. The lawful lane in section 7 is written to stay clear of it.
2. **"Within one turn" against R85-2.** Articles X, XIX and XX use "one turn" (ARTICLES:52, 75, 76), and Dungeons uses it for reporting a Mouth (DUNGEONS:279). R85-2 retired "The fourteen-day turn" as a calendar (RULINGS.md:3883). No CONFLICTS row was found for it. Working choice for drafts only: clerks still say "a turn" as the formulary's term of art for fourteen days, and nobody else does. It needs a CONFLICTS row and a ruling.
3. **"Provenance" is already a trade word.** The Bench's Provenance Class runs "from Class I, taken off a Wellspring site and carrying nothing, up to Class VI, found and unlocatable" (`wiki/Materials, Alchemy & Trade/Materials/The Master Material Ledger/The Alchemical Index.md:20`), and summons carry a Crystal Provenance. A clerk who hears Ned say "provenance" hears the Bench's sense first. A pun or a confusion; the naming pass decides.
4. **Two Apprentices.** The second title and the indentured membership class share a word (TIERED:23; DARK:111).
5. **Waxgate is in the Sealed Charter** (CHARTER:48), not the Midmarch. The Midmarch Mercers' hall in section 2 is INVENTED.
6. **The hall rule's reach is Natalie's overturnable reading** (RULINGS.md:4169). If Isaac widens it (for example, to anyone who sits a yard test), section 4 changes.
7. **The Waking Draft is written for children** ("often kills or warps the child", GATE:23). An adult with a sealed Crystal is canon-possible (GATE:24), but no odds for an adult are on file.
8. **R72-30** still says "a draw allotment" and "a proofed rifle"; both are superseded (RULINGS.md:3575). Its "spoils with a history" clause stands.
9. **No printing** (RULINGS.md:3569). The archived scene The Cold has a printed chapbook (`wiki/The Scene Archive/Scenes/The Cold.md:189`); archived scenes stay as written, and new writing uses hand-copied sheets.
10. **The Accord inventory's almanac is "keyed to the sixty-count"** (INV:23), which R85-2 retired. Use the real calendar.
11. **R70-48's old clause** keeping "Tier and price kept off unless asked" is lifted by R85-1, so a counter scene says Grade, Rarity and price aloud.

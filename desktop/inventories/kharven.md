# THE STANDING INVENTORY: KHARVEN

Steppe-riders who stopped riding because the land became permafrost. Food: windmeat, bone broth, the skin (fermented milk carried in sealskin; refusing the skin is an insult), seal fat, the root cache, stonecurd. Greeting: forearm grip, diagnostic. "How's your stack?" Oaths: "By the sky that covers us." "By the fire." Insults: "wet wood," "empty saddle," "cracked bowl." Time: a fire's length, a broth, the Thin Weeks, "when the grass comes." The dead: the death-house, the Waiting, ground burial at the Seat (its own city rite) and sky burial in the clans beyond it (R54-25), "gone to the sky." Objects: the night-stone, the woodpile, the hide-coat, the belt, the blubber lamp. Exchange: meat-sharing, labour-debt, salt. Proverbs, fixed wording: "The sky does not ask whether you are ready." "A full stack speaks louder than a full mouth." "The first bowl goes to the one who cannot fill it themselves." "Wet wood burns eventually. A liar never dries." "The night-stone remembers the fire." "Don't saddle a horse you cannot feed." "The dead can wait. The cold cannot." "Under the sky, everything balances." Body: hands, breath-plume, weight, scent.

**Senses:**
- *Smells:* woodsmoke off the outer town's stacks, thick when a season's wood goes on the fire in a day; seal fat rendering in the blubber lamp: fish-oil and hot tallow; the skin: sour fermented milk and yeast when the sealskin is unstoppered; wet felt and horse, and old grease on harness; men who have slept eleven nights in mail: sweat gone sour, and iron; bone broth on the hearth: marrow, salt, scorched bone; smoked hide from the hide-coats, tannin and old smoke; air at thirty below that smells of nothing, and the inside of the nose freezing on the inhale; ammonia off the horse lines and the dung heaps; the death-house: cold stone and nothing, and at thaw a sweetness nobody names.
- *Sounds:* six hundred men breathing in the cold: a tide coming in under a pier; an axe splitting rounds for the woodpile, and the split halves knocking onto the stack; snow squeaking under boots in the deep cold; ice cracking in frozen ruts as weight goes over; frozen ground ringing under hooves, and coming up through a horse into a rider's knees; the blubber lamp spitting; a forearm grip: two mittened arms meeting with a slap of hide; the Waiting: a room full of people keeping silent over the dead; horses blowing and stamping on the lines; frost-stiff hide creaking at the elbow of a coat.
- *Colours:* breath-plume white, standing in front of a face; smoke grey over every stack in the outer town; seal-fat yellow in the lamp bowl, and the soot-black above it; hide-coat tan darkened at the cuffs with grease; the pale ends of split wood on a full stack; frostbite: a waxy white on the cheek, going purple later; blood gone black on a hide sleeve; aurora green over the Seat at night; snow gone blue in the hour after the sun; the grey of a Greymane banner, and the grey of the rock the Seat stands on.
- *Textures:* frost standing in a nap on a hide; a hide-coat gone stiff as board with cold; iron at thirty below, which takes skin off a bare palm; frozen ruts underfoot, hard as cast metal; split grain on a fresh round, and the splinters it leaves; the night-stone: smooth, and warm after the fire is out; windmeat, hard as bark, softening in the mouth; stonecurd like a pebble; seal fat greasing the fingers and never quite washing off; a belt worn soft and shiny where the hand goes to it.
- *Tastes:* the skin: sour, fizzing, faintly drunk; windmeat: salt and game, chewed a long time; bone broth with the fat still floating; seal fat: oily and fishy, coating the mouth; stonecurd: sour and chalky; root from the cache, frozen sweet; snowmelt tasting of the pot and of smoke; salt, which is money; rind-water, the honest northern name for thin broth; blood from a split lip in the cold: iron and salt.

**Gone:**
- **The grass.** The open steppe the clans rode before the ground froze through and the riding stopped. "When the grass comes" still names the spring, and the spring still brings moss.
- **The Fusi Vā.** The Queen's binding laid under the old clan oaths, so that a word given before witnesses carried a weight that could not be broken without consequence. It went with her on the night she was killed. The oath ceremony is still performed at the Seat with nothing underneath it, and a word in the north is once again only as strong as the man who says it.
- **The banner oaths to Muken.** Sworn to the First King personally, and famous for one thing: Muken always stopped. A beaten clan was governed and not humiliated. The oaths died with him, and the Banner Houses scattered because the thing they had sworn to no longer existed.
- **The old Seat.** Muken's stronghold, burned in the Tenrai seizure. The city was rebuilt on its surviving foundation stones, and the old courses sit at the base of the new walls, darker than the stone above them.

**Folk beliefs:**
- **Never meet a Moto's eye.** A clansman speaking to the Crown looks at the chest, the hands or the floor, because the Moto read through the eyes and a man who meets them has opened his door. Children are taught it before they are taught the forearm grip.
- **The cold keeps the dead quiet.** The death-house is never heated, and a corpse that lies in the cold through the winter will not walk. Families who cannot bring their dead to a death-house pack them in snow.
- **Wet wood burns eventually.** A worker who has run dry will burn again if he is fed seal fat and kept by the fire, and the first bowl goes to him the way it goes to a child.
- **Nothing binds on the nameless day.** Scores are settled on it, because a killing on a day when no oath holds carries no blood-debt. The death-house takes what it takes, and the Waiting begins on the first named morning after.
- **The night-stone remembers the fire.** A chip off the hearth-stone of a house where a worker lived is laid under a sick child's bedding, because the stone kept some of what was done beside it.

**Literacy:** Almost none among the clans. A man signs with his mark before witnesses, the woodpile is counted and never written, and the Waiting keeps names in mouths rather than on paper. The readers are the Moto house, which writes its own hand and reads the Concord hand as a foreign script, and the Hallenfeld quartermasters and courier captains, who read Concord because the roads require it.

**Swears, oaths and insults** (canon; the full page is *Swears, Oaths and Insults*):

The clans swear by the Sky and the fire, curse with the cold, and insult by scarcity: bad wood, empty saddles, cracked bowls, short stacks. The Sky is never named beyond the Sky.
| Swear | Literal meaning | Strength | Who says it |
|---|---|---|---|
| By the sky that covers us. | An oath taken under the whole Sky. | firm (solemn) | Anyone swearing a thing true; elders at a death-house; never said lightly |
| By the fire. | An oath on the hearth that keeps the household alive. | mild | Everyday emphasis in any household; "by the fire, I told him twice" |
| Sky take it. | Let the Sky have it, since the Sky takes the dead. | mild | The everyday "damn it" in any mouth, riders, wives, children who think nobody is listening |
| Wet wood. | Wood that smokes and will not catch. | hard | Said of a liar or a man who cannot be relied on; the whole proverb, "wet wood burns eventually, a liar never dries," is the formal version |
| Empty saddle. | A saddle with nobody in it. | hard | a man who is not there when the ride goes out; riders, veterans, the banner houses' men |
| Cracked bowl. | A bowl that cannot hold its share. | hard | one who takes the first bowl from the one who cannot fill it (breaks the proverb); women at the pot, elders |
| Short stack. | A woodpile too small for the winter. | firm | Said of a lazy or improvident man; neighbours, the answer to "how's your stack?" given about someone else |
| Full mouth. | All talk, since the full stack speaks louder. | firm | Said of a boaster; young men to young men, old men to young men |
| Thin Weeks on you. | May the late-winter hunger find your house. | hard | A curse between households in a feud; not said in a lean year, when it is too close to true |
| Qatuq take you. | Qatuq: the cold that takes the unready. | hard | Hunters and sealers; a curse that wishes a man dead the northern way, slowly and by his own mistake |
| Kiilut. | One of the dead left without the Waiting; a corpse nobody sat with. | grave | The worst thing one Kharven can call another; old women say it and men go quiet; never shouted |
| Hulluk. | The native word for wet wood. | hard | The same insult as "wet wood," in the older mouth; carried-name households, the Keld, sealers |
| Numra. | An empty bowl held out; a mouth that eats and brings nothing. | firm | Said of a guest who never hunts, a relative who only arrives at meat-sharing |
| Skin-refuser. | One who refused the skin (the fermented milk offered at the door). | grave | An accusation of contempt for a host; said once and then there is a fight |
| Frozen piss. | Exactly that. | mild | The body-swear in its winter version; riders, soldiers on the wall |

**Signature items** (texture to reach for, no minimum, R79-3): the woodpile and "how's your stack"; the night-stone; "wet wood"; the Thin Weeks; the death-house and the Waiting. Pending entry: "the third bowl" (Brida's shorthand for Class III haemorrhage).

**Release words** (R78-10):

The clans stopped riding when the ground froze through, and they still talk like riders. A release is the horse given its head.

| Release | Word | Literal meaning | Who says it |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | Letting the horse run | Giving a held horse its head after a long walk on a short rein. | Anyone; riders and the banner houses' men first. "He's let the horse run." |
| Second | The grass comes | The open steppe before the freeze, which "when the grass comes" still names. The ground round him turns his, as the steppe once was everyone's. | Elders, and the men who have seen one; said quietly, because the grass is gone. |
| Third | The Sky on the reins | The Sky takes the reins beside the rider. | Said low and rarely; the old, at the death-house, in the Waiting's stories. Never shouted. |
| Held past the limit | A horse you cannot feed | From the proverb "Don't saddle a horse you cannot feed": a release forced past its last tenth. | Veterans, of a man who broke himself; also a warning to a young one. |
| Released (adj.) | Loose-reined | Riding with the rein let out. | Riders, soldiers on the wall. |

Carried-name households (the Keld, the sealers) say **the name stands**: the dead person whose name the bearer carries rises up in him and fights. No native word is on record for it yet.

In a clansman's mouth: "He's let the horse run, Sky take it. Don't stand in front of a running horse, boy."

**Old forms (R23-8, ruled 2026-09-12):** airag is the skin, borts is windmeat, aaruul is stonecurd, the deel is the hide-coat, and the sky-name Tengri is simply the Sky, capitalised and unnamed. The old forms are stale in new prose.

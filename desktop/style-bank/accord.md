The Accord is the law's own example of the West tilt ("an Accord counting-house further West", R70-1-BLEND_MODEL), so money, the institution and the city press hardest here, while narration, interiority and structure keep the Eastern lead (R70-3-STRAND_LEADS_LAYER). ## 1. A fight or a working

It was the low hours, three to five by the Guild clock, when the Board ran its maintenance and the hum under the city thinned to a thing a sleeper could forget. Hester Gallard had never been able to forget it. She had been born in a room against a meter wall, like half the girls of her street, and the hum had been in her teeth since before she had teeth, so that in the low hours she woke without knowing why and lay listening to the absence, the way a miller's child is said to wake when the wheel stops. Tonight she had not slept at all. She came down the meter-house stair with her lamp held low and set it on the bottom step, and the flame stood up straight in the still air and gave her the cellar.

It smelled of damp iron and of something sharper under the iron, the ozone a main gives off where it has been opened, which she knew from the training yard and had smelled outside it only twice. The district main ran along the far wall at the height of her hip, sealed conduit as thick as a man's thigh, its brass bands gone green at the edges. Three reaches upstream of the meter somebody had taken a spur. The new pipe was wiped bright and wrapped at the joint with tarred cord, and it ran off low along the wall toward the tenements on Cutler's Row, where she had walked that afternoon past forty doors with cut plates in their boxes and heard, through one of them, a child coughing in a cold room. A man knelt at the joint with a fitter's bar across his knees, finishing the wrap with a care she recognised, because her father had wrapped joints that way, thumb flat, the cord laid so close that no light would get between the turns.

"Enforcement." She held up the silver token, and it caught the lamp. "Josiah Pell, you are taken for an unlicensed spur on the Board's main. Sine sigillo."

The words came out whole, as the academy had put them into her, and she heard how thin they sounded in a cellar. Pell did not get up at once. He tied off the cord and tested it with his thumb, and only then did he rise, slowly, with the bar hanging from one hand, and she took him in the way the school taught, hands first. Grease worked into the knuckles past any washing. A stopcock burn across the left palm, old and shiny, the kind a man got turning a cock that somebody else had left live. Fitter's hands, then, a tradesman's before they were anything else. The face above them was ordinary, long and tired, with three days' beard and a nick on the chin where he had shaved in the dark. *A hedge-speaker,* she thought, *a man who learned his Latin off a stolen primer and a dead uncle,* and she let the guess comfort her more than it should have, because she was alone in a cellar at the fourth hour and the comfort was what she had.

"You wired the yard before you come down," he said. His voice was Cutler's Row, every aitch left on the floor where it fell. "'Course you did. They teach you that first. Wire the yard, show the token, say the Latin." He looked at the spur and not at her. "There's an 'undred an' twelve souls on the end o' that pipe, officer. There's a lyin'-in on the third floor. Board cut 'em Veyra's day for nine silver they ain't got."

"Then they can stand before the Assize and say so."

"An' freeze while they're stood there." He shrugged, and the shrug was tired, and she believed in it. "Go on, then. Write it down. Write 'oo said it."

She had sat the Vox paper at seventeen, in a hall that smelled of wax and frightened sweat, and she still had the grammar by heart the way some girls had psalms. Nomen, mensura, iussum: a thing named, a bound set, an order given, and nobody in the sentence to hang for it, which was the whole reason the chancery had chosen that tongue for workings and the whole reason Enforcement existed. She let him walk in. She had decided it on the stair, that she would let him come until one more step put his bar within reach of her, because a man walking was a man committed and a man committed could be cut. He came two paces, three, the bar loose, and on the third pace he spoke, low, almost kindly, the way one speaks to a horse.

"Pondus. Per axem. Descende."

He stopped hiding himself as he said it. Until then he had kept his aura folded small and dark inside his coat, the way a careful man keeps a lamp under a bucket, and now he let it out, and it came off him in a slow verdigris glow, the green of old brass left out in rain, crawling up his sleeves and standing off his shoulders like heat off a summer road. The thin hum of the main bent half a tone flat. Her lamp's flame drew down into a short hard spike as if a thumb had pressed it, and a rat that had been sitting on the conduit ran the length of the main and was gone into the dark. The pressure reached her chest a breath later. Her stomach turned over, and sweat broke at her hairline and under her arms all at once, cold as well water, and the count she kept in her head slipped a place so that she had to go back for it. She understood then, too late to be useful, that he had stood well above her the whole time with all of it folded away, and that she had looked at his hands and his chin and his tiredness and told herself a story about a hedge-speaker because the story let her come down the stair.

Then her own weight came down her spine.

It came all at once, the whole of her, as if she had stepped off a roof and the ground had arrived without the fall. Her knees buckled and her heels drove into the flags. Her neck shortened; she felt its bones crowd together and heard a wet click high in her back, and her sword arm went heavy as a sack of shot. She knew what he had done because the grammar told her, even while her body was busy failing. He had named her weight, *pondus*, bounded it to the axis of her standing body from the crown to the heels, *per axem*, and ordered it down; and the working took the pull the earth had always had on her and multiplied it along that one upright axis, so that every pound of her pressed on the pound beneath it as if the whole of her had been stacked on herself again and again. The bound was the axis, and an axis was a place.

She threw herself sideways off it.

It was the ugliest thing she had ever done with her body, a sprawling roll across a cellar floor with the weight still in her as she went, so that she landed hard on her left hip and felt the bruise go down to the bone. But she was out of the bound, and the bound stayed where it had been set; behind her the lamp, which had been standing inside it, flattened on the step with a crack of glass and a gout of burning oil that went out at once in the damp. Her weight went back to being her own. She came up on her feet with her heart going like a fist on a door, her breath sobbing, and the relief of being light was so enormous that she almost laughed aloud, and then she was within reach of him.

She cut down from the right shoulder with everything her hips could give, the hard falling stroke the school drilled until girls wept, her back foot driving and the edge traveling down and across for the join of his neck and collarbone. He took it on the bar. Iron rang on steel with a flat ugly note, and with the blades crossed he rolled the bar over hers and pressed it down and away, and the moment her point dropped he brought the bar down at her head. She got her left forearm up because nothing else would come. The bar broke the long bone of the forearm across the middle with the noise a schoolroom ruler makes when a master brings it down on a desk, and the arm folded where no arm folds. She did not feel it. She saw it, the sleeve kinking, and some cold clerk in her head entered it and set it aside for later, and she kept her sword.

His bar was low now, carried past her by its own weight, and his right arm had gone up with it, and for the length of a breath his armpit stood open to her like a door left ajar.

The meter hung on the wall between them, brass-cased, the Board's seal on its face. Its draw-lit dial was the last light in the cellar now that her lamp was out, and the glow lay on the brass the way an evening lies on a canal, thin and gold and slow to leave, and in it she saw the green of his aura gutter as he spent and hauled the bar back up. She thought, very clearly, of the child coughing on Cutler's Row. She thought that she was going to kill a man for nine silver and a lying-in, and that he was going to kill her for the same, and that the Board would enter whichever of them was left by morning.

She drove her point up under his raised arm into the hollow of the armpit, her whole body behind it from the back heel, and opened the great artery there.

The blood came out of him in a spout that struck the meter's glass and the joist above it, black in the gold light, a hot rope of it that kept coming for as long as his heart kept working. He made a small surprised sound. The bar fell out of his hand and rang on the flags. He sat down against the standpipe with his legs out in front of him like a man at the end of a long shift, and his aura went out in patches, the way frost goes off a window, and his blood ran down over the Board's seal and filled the letters.

"There's a lyin'-in," he said. "Third floor." He did not say anything else.

At the fifth hour the hum came back up through the flags, full and steady, into her teeth and into the broken arm, which woke all at once and screamed. She sat on the bottom step among the glass and held the arm against her chest and wept with the pain of it and with something much larger than the pain that she would have no name for until morning, loud and ugly, the way she had not wept since she was nine. Upstairs the landlady was already laying a copper on the sill, for the smell of it.

## 2. Court and talk

Quarter-day, and the Court of Assistants of the Fitters and Gaugers sat in the long room over the hall while the rents went unpaid in the street below. Ambrose Sealy had kept its roll for thirty years. Masters lasted one. He sat at the Clerk's desk below the board with the roll open at a clean page and his nib wiped, and he watched the room the way he had watched every room since he was articled at fourteen, for the thing that would have to be written down. The long room smelled of hot wax from the board on the end wall, where the company's ranks hung in green and blue and gold, of the coal fire nobody had told the porter to bank, and of the Assistants themselves, broadcloth and snuff and the pomade Prime Wardens favored in their year. Through the tall windows the quarter-day crowd moved along Fitters' Lane with its bills in its hands, and now and then a voice came up out of it, a woman's, arguing a sum.

Overdraught, Prime Warden that year, laid his silver token on the table before he rose, as custom wanted, so that nobody at the board could lie across it. He laid it face up and squared it to the edge of the baize, and Sealy, who had watched thirty years of Prime Wardens lay that token, saw this one look at it a moment after he had set it down, as a man looks at a dog he is not sure of. The token was supposed to darken near a lie. Sealy had never seen one do it and had never met anyone willing to find out.

"That a man who came to the freedom of this company by purchase, and not by the seven years; who has sat, so far as this Court is informed, no examination this company sets; and whom the Assistants, at the last sitting, by a margin I need not recall, elected, should now, by our silence, sit at this table as one of us," he said, and paused, and arrived at the verb, "I cannot, salvo iure tertii, commend."

It was well built, and Sealy admired it professionally, the way a joiner admires the dovetails on a coffin he would not care to lie in. The whole weight of the sentence hung on its last word, so that the room had to carry the man's purchase, his missing examination and his election all the way across the period before it was told what to think of them, and by then it was tired enough to agree. Overdraught had set the advocate's dry phrase in the middle, saving the right of a third party, to tell anyone with Latin that he was leaving himself a door.

Old Lumley did not look up from his blotter. He had been an Assistant since before Sealy was articled, had been Prime Warden twice and refused it a third time, and at every sitting he drew small careful gallows in the margin of his blotter, which the younger Assistants believed was a joke and Sealy had long suspected was a tally. "Where coin fades, Merit endures."

"A clause improved is a clause void, Master Lumley."

"Then don't improve it, Prime Warden." Lumley added a rope to the gallows. "Vote it."

Somebody at the far end laughed into his hand and turned it into a cough. Overdraught's ears went red above the collar, a slow flush that climbed into the pomade, and he did not sit.

Thomas Hand sat at the foot of the table in a good black coat cut for somebody else. It was too wide in the shoulder and too short at the cuff, and Sealy knew without asking that it had come from a dead master's widow, because that was where a man with money and no company found a coat fit for a Court. He was from the eastern quarter, where the conduit-fitters had come in from the river provinces two generations back and kept their own chapels and their own customs. His hands lay folded on the green baize. They were fitter's hands, scarred white across the backs where a live main had kissed them, and very still. When Overdraught turned to him for an answer he gave none. He looked at the Prime Warden steadily and kept his hands folded, and the room waited for him, and then the room went on waiting.

Sealy had drawn contracts for eastern houses since he was articled, and he knew a lith when one was held in front of him. In the eastern quarter a man accused before his betters did not argue, because to argue was to grant that the question was open; he folded his hands and held his silence, and the silence said that he had answered this already with his life and would not answer it again with his mouth. Hand had answered. Sealy could hear it as plainly as a bell. The westerners at the board heard a man with nothing to say, and Sealy watched them hear it, watched Overdraught's flush settle into something more comfortable as he took the silence for confession, and felt the old sick weariness of a translator nobody has thought to ask.

*He takes me for a dumb ox,* Thomas Hand thought, looking at the red ears. *Good. Let him go on taking me for one a little longer.*

The silence ran long enough for the wire-boy to come up the stair, touch his cap to the board and lay a slip at Sealy's elbow. The boy smelled of rain and of the wire office's lamp oil. Nine words, in the wire office's capitals:

> GUILD COMMENDS FREEMAN HAND NUMBER FOUR ARRAY HELD ALONE.

Sealy read it, and his stomach dropped as though a stair had gone from under him. The Number Four array fed the river wards. It had gone wild three nights since, in the storm, and the papers had said that the night crew fled and the wards went dark for an hour; they had not said who stayed. Sealy looked down the table at the white scars on the backs of the folded hands, which he had taken for old, and saw now that the pink at their edges was new.

Overdraught broke first. Westerners always did. He came down out of the High the way a man takes off a coat in a hot room, and said in the Plain of any yard, "Well? Bought your freedom, didn't you?"

The Beadle shifted his mace from one arm to the other. Lumley put his pen down. Every company had that word and kept it for the street, and in Sealy's time nobody had said it in this room before witnesses. It went down the long room like a draught under a door. A chair creaked. Down in Fitters' Lane the arguing woman had stopped, or the windows had grown thicker, and Sealy felt the blood come up into his own face, hot and furious and ashamed, as if he had been the one to say it, as if thirty years at this desk had made him partner to every word spoken over it. He wanted, quite badly, to stand up and read the wire aloud into the Prime Warden's red face. His hand shook on the slip.

Thomas Hand unfolded his hands, laid them flat on the baize, and looked at the token.

It lay between them, bright as it had been. It had not darkened. The thing said had been true, and that was the worst of it, that the Prime Warden had told no lie and the silver would bear him out, and that Hand had bought his freedom with money made on the river barges and then held a wild array shut with his own hands while men who had served their seven years ran down the stair.

Sealy read the wire twice. By morning the Guild would have the commendation entered on the man's registration and its mark struck on his token, and no Court in the Accord could take either off; Hand would sit at this table with a Guild mark on his silver that no other Assistant here had earned, and Overdraught would have to look at it every sitting for the rest of his year. Sealy let himself feel that pleasure fully, for the space of one breath. Then he folded the slip once and slid it under the roll, because the question had been put before the wire came, and what had been said stood said, and a Clerk who entered things out of their order was a Clerk who had decided to be a judge. He wet his nib.

"The question is on the freeman's seat," he said. "As it stands."

## 3. Quiet and travel

From the wire-office steps at the top of the hill the whole city could be watched taking its evening draught. Kit stopped there whenever he had a late wire, because the office windows could not see the steps and because nothing in his life had ever been as beautiful as this, and he was fourteen and had not yet learned to be embarrassed about that. The river lay at the bottom under its own haze, gold where the foundries along the bank were drawing, the barges lit like strings of beads. Above it rose the warehouses with their hoists and the tram wires strung between them, singing faintly in the wind, then the terraces in their rows, roof over roof up the hill, and at lamp-time the gauge-houses threw the street lamps on district by district, so that the light climbed the hill in slow bands, street over street, and the hum under each band deepened as it came on, until the hill was humming under his boots and he could feel it in his shins. The Nail had come out at the pole, white and fixed. One square of roofs below the tannery stayed dark. The commons called it the Deafs. Every city had one and every city moved it; last quarter it had been the streets behind the rope-walk, and three years ago it had been his own.

He had the wire in his cap band. At the office the other boys called him Doorstep, because nothing ever showed on his face at a door, and it was the one thing about him they admired. They thought he had been born that way. He had built it, in a draper's window at the bottom of Pellow Street, the month after a wire came to his mother's door behind the rope-walk and he watched her read the boy who brought it, read his held face in one look and go gray before she had broken the seal. He had sworn then, a boy in a cut street, that nobody would ever read a wire off his face, and he had practiced in shop glass until his face could be shut like a door.

It was cutting week, and every street on the way down had a man in it with a key. Kit knew the cutters by their walk, the slow stop and start of a man reading door numbers off a list, and by the keys, long iron things worn bright at the bit and hung from a belt chain that clinked as they moved. Meters glowed by the doors, each under the Board's seal, the little dials lit gold and the needles shivering; in a street that had paid they made a row of small warm lamps at knee height, and children sat by them in the cold to warm their hands. A girl at a stall was still getting three copper for the day's assize loaves, the white gone and only the dark left, and she called them fresh as morning and nobody believed her. Kit stopped at the lit draper's window, the same one, looked at his face in the glass, made it into nothing, and went on.

The hum stopped at a curb.

He had it first in the soles of his boots, a going-out like the tide drawing off a step, then in his ears, which rang with the want of it, a high thin whine that was only his own blood. The Deafs smelled of tallow and cold grates and of too many people in rooms with the windows shut. No lamps had come on. There was candlelight in some windows and none in most, and the dark between them was thick and brown, and his eyes, raised under lamps, took a long time to give him anything at all. Somewhere a bell was ringing the notch, the old seven stations, because the Guild hour came down the wire and the wire stopped at the edge of a cut district, so this street kept one clock and the hill another. Through a window he saw a cradle pushed against the wall where the main came in, for luck, though nothing had come through that wall since the cutter called. The baby in it was asleep. Children raised on a main slept badly off it, everyone said so, and this one slept anyway with its fists up by its ears, and Kit felt a tenderness for it so sudden and absurd that his throat closed.

The door had its cut plate in the junction box beside the step, the iron disc dropped in so cleanly it looked like part of the house.

He stood on the step and settled his face. He knew this door. He had not let himself think about it all the way down the hill, and now he could think of nothing else, and he made his face into the shut door one more time, the one he had built in glass, and felt it hold.

A woman opened before he knocked. She was thin, in a shawl over her nightdress, her hair down; she had been listening for steps in a street that had nothing to listen to but steps. She looked at his face for a long time.

He had practiced. She read it anyway. He watched her do it, the way he had watched his mother three years before, and understood at last that the door he had built had never once been shut, that women like this read a wire-boy's face by what was held still in it, and that a boy holding his face that still, at this hour, in cutting week, could carry only one kind of news. Her mouth opened and closed. She took the wire and did not open it. She found a copper in her apron for him, because that was owed and she would not be in debt to the Guild's boy on top of everything else, and she held the folded slip flat against her apron with her other hand as though it were warm.

"Thank you, love," she said. "Go on 'ome now."

Kit went down the step with the copper in his fist, and three doors along his face came apart all at once, the whole careful thing, and he cried with his mouth open in the dark of the Deafs where nobody could see him, hot and furious and fourteen, for her and for his mother and for the boy at the draper's glass.

Behind him, down the dark street, children were skipping to the rhyme he had skipped to:

> *Cutter, cutter, drop your plate,*
> *Mother's late and the bill is late.*
> *Nine words come and the hum goes,*
> *Who's to be next, nobody knows.*

## 4. A system moment

> FORM VII · OF THE PUBLIC RE-ASSAY OF A REGISTERED PRACTITIONER
> I. The candidate stands within the holding coil, bare-handed, before not fewer than three witnesses.
> II. The Measurewright closes the circuit and keeps silence while the coil reads.
> III. The tape is read aloud by the Clerk, title before name, and entered on the Reckoner's roll.
> IV. Witnesses keep silence until the entry is made.
> Quod dictum est, dictum stat.

The holding coil of the Fitters and Gaugers stood on the hall floor, a hoop of wound copper taller than a man, with four lenses of Clearbeam beryl set at its quarters like the hours on a clock-face. Clemency Ferrule had walked past it every working day for six years on her way to the gaugers' bench and never once stood inside it, because a gauger at Welling had no business with the hall coil and the Guild did not re-assay girls on hope. It looked larger from inside. The copper was warm, wound so close and so many times that the hoop had the dull sheen of a ship's cable, and through the nearest lens the benches went pale green and swam, faces and hats and the gallery rail running together like a street seen through the bottom of a bottle. She stepped in bare-handed, as the form wanted. Both her palms were burned across, nine days pink, the new skin shiny and tight so that she could not close her fists all the way, from the night the Pellow Street main burst and she held the junction shut with them, in a way no school had taught her, until the cutters came. Nobody had asked to see them. The form did not ask.

Plumb, the Measurewright, wore her loupe on a black ribbon and closed the circuit without looking at anyone. She was a small dry woman with ink in the creases of her knuckles and a name in the companies for never having been seen surprised, and Clemency, who had wanted her approval for six years with the hopeless steadiness of a dog at a butcher's door, could not tell from her face whether she had been brought here to be raised or to be made a fool of.

The hum of the hall's main came up through the boards and climbed. It went into Clemency's back teeth and set them aching. The hair stood on her forearms. Her palms burned again, fresh, as though the junction were under them, and the whole of Pellow Street came back into her hands at once: the roar of the burst main, the white fizzing light, the stink of her own sleeves scorching, the certainty that she was about to die and the second, stranger certainty that she would not let go. Under her breastbone something answered the coil the way a sound gauge-glass answers a flick of the nail, cleanly, and held the note. Her aura came up with it before she could stop it, a thin clean white like wire at the moment it starts to glow, fizzing at her knuckles, and somebody on the front bench said "Oh," quite loudly, and was hushed.

She knew what the coil would find. She had read it nine days ago on Pellow Street, with the main roaring through her hands, behind her breastbone in no voice at all:

[Stage III, Ascension. Journeyman.]
[Grade ceiling C.]

There had been no line for her hands. The Crystal knew its own and nothing of the flesh round it; it had told her what she was and not what she had paid for it. The rest had been hers alone, and no clerk in the Accord would have entered a word of it on her say-so. For nine days she had carried it like a coin sewn into a hem, feeling for it in the night to be sure it was still there, half certain it was a fever-dream from the burns and half certain that if she said it aloud someone would laugh, and the laugh would be entered too.

The ticker in the stand chattered and put out a white tongue of tape. Plumb read it, initialled it, wrote in the margin and passed it to the Clerk. Sealy read it aloud and entire.

> HOLDING COIL III · HALL OF THE FITTERS AND GAUGERS · FORM VII
> FERRULE, CLEMENCY. ENTERED STAGE II, WELLING.
> READ STAGE III, ASCENSION. BAND I, MORTAL FOUNDATION.
> TIER OF STANDING: JOURNEYMAN. GRADE CEILING C.
> PROB EST. H.P., MEASUREWRIGHT, HEXAGONAL OATH.

The hall let its breath go before the nib had touched the roll. Clemency felt it go out of them like warm air out of an oven door, and with it something in her own chest broke loose that she had been holding for nine days, and for six years, and for the whole of her life since the gaugers' master told her at fourteen that she had good eyes and no draw and should thank the Archons for the eyes. She had meant to stand like a journeyman. She stood like a girl, with her burned hands open at her sides and her mouth trembling, and her eyes filled and ran over, and she let them, because the coil still held her and she could not have wiped them without leaving it. Her name went back along the benches in the new order, title first. Journeyman Ferrule. Journeyman. Ferrule's girl, from the Gaugers. Fifteen gold the month, at the least, a man's voice said, doing the sum aloud the way the Accord did every sum. At the end of the front bench old Lumley said, "Cheap at it," and that was the verdict.

Then Sealy read the margin too, in the Latin it was written in. "Candidata bis rogavit num spira proba esset. Proba est."

The clerks' bench laughed. Clemency went scarlet to the ears, because she had asked twice, in a whisper she had thought nobody heard, while Plumb was setting the lenses; she had asked because it was too much to be true unless the coil was broken. The benches behind the clerks waited, and Sealy gave it again in Common, dry as chalk. "Candidate asked twice whether the coil was sound. It is."

The whole hall laughed then, which the form did not provide for, a warm rolling laugh with no cruelty in it, and Clemency laughed too, wet-faced and helpless, her hands burning, and Plumb did not look up, but the corner of her mouth moved once, and Clemency would remember that longer than the tape. Sealy wet his nib and made the entry.

*Twice,* Plumb thought, winding the loupe ribbon round one finger. *I asked three times at her age, and nobody thought it worth writing down.*

At the door waited a factor's man in a tall hat, come for the price the coil had just set. He had a card ready and a smile ready, and he had been standing there, Clemency realised, since before the circuit closed. "Pellow and Sons, Journeyman. Fifteen gold the month, the first quarter in advance."

Clemency looked at her palms.

## 5. A comic beat

Veyra's day, and the corn market was packed to the beam-house steps. The stalls ran in four rows under the iron roof, the sacks open at the mouth and rolled down like stockings, oats and barley and the red southern wheat, and the dust of them hung in the light from the roof lamps so that the whole market seemed to stand in a fine gold smoke. Pigeons sat in a gray row along the beam-house gutter and watched the oats with the patience of creditors. Below them the town beam showed through the open doors, black oak and brass pans and the Assize seal burned into the crossbar, and a queue of farm wives waited to have their butter weighed against it with the faces of women who had been short-weighed by somebody at some time and had not forgotten who.

Hob and Dob had both been entered on the parish roll as Robert, in the same ward, in the same month, and the Assize had sent them out as a pair for eleven years so that nobody need ever work out which Robert was meant. Hob did the talking. Dob carried the hammer. It suited them both. Hob had a voice like a chapel organ and loved the use of it, and Dob had learned early that a man who said nothing was assumed to know something, and had lived comfortably on the assumption ever since.

"Mr. Heapwell," said Hob, to the grain merchant and the whole market, the way a curate gives out a hymn number. "Nomen est omen."

"Means 'is name's a warnin'," a porter told the woman beside him, who had not asked.

Heapwell smiled with every tooth he had. He was a large soft man in a dove-gray waistcoat, a watch-chain across it heavy enough to moor a barge, and he had heard the Latin before, from better inspectors, and outlasted them in trade. "Inspector. Inspector." He bowed to each Robert in turn, which was a small joke of its own, and the market enjoyed it. "You will find, if you will be so good as to watch, that every measure upon this stall is, by the formulary, level."

His strike man stepped up to the bushel, where the oats stood heaped over the rim in a fat gold dome. "Go on, Treacle," somebody called from behind the oat sacks, "we ain't got all day, some of us 'as work." The strike man laid the strike-board across the rim and drew it toward himself so slowly that a pigeon came down off the gutter, walked once round the heap with its head on one side, took a grain, considered the board, and went back up to the gutter before the board had reached the middle.

"Level," said Heapwell.

"Glacial," said Hob.

The market laughed, a big easy laugh, and Heapwell laughed louder than anyone and slapped his waistcoat, and Dob, who had heard that laugh at forty stalls, marked that it stopped a moment before everyone else's did.

Dob said nothing. He was thinking of a story he had told once, years ago, to a new man in the Third Party, which had SALVO IURE TERTII painted over its door in letters a foot high and a landlord who believed it meant the third pint was on the house. An inspector in the south weighed a merchant's pound on the beam before he tapped it. It came out true, and he stamped it. The case unscrewed. The merchant pared the lead inside it a grain at a time and sold short under that stamp for a year, and when they hanged him at last he carried the stamped weight to the gallows in his pocket and held it up to the crowd as proof of his honesty. The new man had asked what the moral was. "Tap first, weigh after," Dob had said, and gone back to his pint, which had cost two copper and was short.

He lifted Heapwell's pound weight off the scale by its ring. It was a handsome thing, bright brass with the PROB EST mark crisp on its shoulder, and it sat in his palm with exactly the heft a pound should have, which proved nothing at all. He tapped it with the small hammer.

Thock.

It was a soft note, smothered, the sound a knuckle makes on a coffin lid, and every market person in earshot knew it, because nothing else in the world sounds like it. A solid weight rang. This one had not. The market stopped. A woman with a basket of eggs set it down on the flags with great care, as if the floor might be about to move. The strike-board halted halfway across the oats. Hob drew breath to give out the verdict, the big organ breath, and Heapwell's smile stayed exactly where it was while his eyes went past both Roberts to the back of his stall.

Then the pigeons went up off the gutter all at once, every one, in a clap of wings that made half the market duck. The hum under the stalls went flat, a sour half-tone down, the roof lamps dipping as though the whole market's draw had been leaned on. The oats in the bushel began to run off their heap on their own, grain by grain, slithering down the gold dome and pattering onto the floor in a thin dry rain, and the dust in the lamplight stopped drifting and sank. Behind Heapwell a big quiet man in a drover's coat, who had leaned on a sack all morning with his hat over his eyes, stood up off it. A dull iron-gray glow came off him as he rose, low and heavy, like the light in a forge gone cold, and it pressed outward through the stalls until Dob felt it on the front of his body as a weight, a broad flat hand laid on his chest and leaning.

Dob's knees understood it before he did, and were for sitting down. He found that he wanted very badly to be in the Third Party with a short pint and a new man to lecture, that the hammer in his hand was a very small hammer, and that a working life of saying nothing had not prepared him in the least for having nothing to say.

"Pondus falsum," said Hob, his organ voice gone up an octave and cracking at the top like a choirboy's. "Before witnesses."

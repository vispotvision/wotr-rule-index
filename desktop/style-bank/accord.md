# Style Sample Bank: Accord

Worked examples of the R70 style law for an Accord or Concord POV, to be read before writing in that culture. The people and events are illustrations, not canon, unless Isaac adopts them. The Accord is the law's own example of the West tilt ("an Accord counting-house further West", R70-1-BLEND_MODEL), so money, the institution and the city press hardest here. Narration, interiority and structure still take the Eastern lead (R70-3-STRAND_LEADS_LAYER). Cadence is the house baseline that R5-C3-NARRATION_REGISTER_EXTENSION names for a Concord human POV, worked as a clerk works: clause entered after clause, short closed words in the street, and in hall and chancery the verb held late, as the Common page says High Common holds it (R70-21-CADENCE_CULTURE). The dial is period, the Victorian imperial of R53-28-VISUAL_REFERENCE, until the Inventory sets its own (R70-27-ANTIQUE_NARRATION_RUNS). The eye takes the seal first, then the hum, then the price, and misreads anything unsealed or unentered as worth nothing (R70-44-CULTURE_KEYED_NOTICING). There are no honorific words. Title goes before name at a formal door, in speech only, and narration keeps plain surnames; level lives in Common's Plain, High and Cant, and a drop from High to Plain is an event the room hears (R70-107-SPEECH_LEVELS_ADDRESS_KOREAN, R70-118-HONORIFICS_NARRATION). The learned tongue is chancery Latin, quoted from the formulary and never improved (R70-26-LEARNED_TONGUES_BESIDE_LATIN). Comic names have their richest home here: talking surnames a notch past believable, puns on the Draw Age's own words, rhyming pet names, market bynames, signboards and jokes in correct Latin (R70-124-PEOPLES_WEST_NORTH, R70-120-BUILT_COMIC_NAMES, R70-128-PLACES_INNS_SHIPS_GEAR). Common is the page, so an English pun always has its bridge (R70-123-WHOSE_EAR_HEARS_JOKE). Units are reaches, Guild hours and draughts (R70-29-UNITS_MEASURE). Texture comes from the Inventory first (R51-31-SENSE_BANK), and every passage carries at least two of its five recurrence items (R70-51-REPETITION_AGAINST_NOVELTY).

## 1. A fight or a working

It was the low hours, three to five by the Guild clock, when the Board ran its maintenance and the hum under the city thinned to a thing a sleeper could forget. Hester Gallard came down the meter-house stair and set her lamp on the bottom step.

The cellar smelled of damp iron. Three reaches along the district main, upstream of the meter, somebody had taken a spur: new pipe, wiped bright, run off toward the tenements. A man knelt at the joint with a fitter's bar.

"Enforcement." She showed the silver token. "Josiah Pell, you are taken for an unlicensed spur on the Board's main. Sine sigillo."

She read him the way the school taught, hands first. Grease in the knuckles. A stopcock burn across one palm. Welling, she judged, a mouth at Grade D on a good night. She let the measure close.

She had sat the Vox paper at seventeen. Nomen, mensura, iussum: a thing named, a bound set, an order given, and nobody in the grammar to hang for it.

"Write it down, then," Pell said. "Write who said it." Then, softly: "Pondus. Per axem. Descende."

He stopped suppressing as he spoke. The thin hum bent half a tone. Her lamp's flame drew down into a short hard spike, and a rat ran the main and was gone. The count in her head slipped a place, and she had to go back for it. He had been a Stage above her all along, folded small.

Then her own weight came down her spine, all of it at once.

The bound was the axis. She threw herself sideways off it, and behind her the lamp flattened on the step with a crack of glass.

She came up in measure and cut a Zornhau from the right shoulder. He took it on the bar. In the bind he rolled the iron over her blade and brought it down, and she got her left forearm up because nothing else would come, and the ulna broke across its shaft with the noise a schoolroom ruler makes over a desk.

Indes. His bar was low.

The meter hung on the wall between them, brass-cased, the Board's seal on its face. Its draw-lit dial was the last light in the cellar. The glow lay on the brass the way an evening lies on a canal, thin and gold and slow to leave.

Her point went up under his raised arm into the axilla and opened the axillary artery.

He sat down against the standpipe. His blood ran over the seal.

At the fifth hour the hum came back into her teeth and the broken arm. Upstairs the landlady was already laying a copper on the sill.

## 2. Court and talk

Quarter-day, and the Court of Assistants of the Fitters and Gaugers sat in the long room over the hall while the rents went unpaid in the street below. Ambrose Sealy had kept its roll for thirty years. Masters lasted one.

Overdraught, Prime Warden that year, laid his silver token on the table before he rose, as custom wanted, so that nobody at the board could lie across it.

"That a man who came to the freedom of this company by purchase, and not by the seven years; who has sat, so far as this Court is informed, no examination this company sets; and whom the Assistants, at the last sitting, by a margin I need not recall, elected, should now, by our silence, sit at this table as one of us," he said, and paused, and arrived at the verb, "I cannot, salvo iure tertii, commend."

Old Lumley did not look up from his blotter. "Where coin fades, Merit endures."

"A clause improved is a clause void, Master Lumley."

"Then don't improve it, Prime Warden. Vote it."

Thomas Hand sat at the foot of the table in a good black coat cut for somebody else. He was from the eastern quarter. When Overdraught turned to him for an answer he gave none. He folded his fitter's hands on the green baize and the room waited.

Sealy had drawn contracts for eastern houses since he was articled, and he knew a lith when one was held in front of him. Hand had answered. The westerners at the board heard a man with nothing to say.

The silence ran long enough for the wire-boy to come up the stair, touch his cap and lay a slip at Sealy's elbow. Nine words, in the wire office's capitals:

> GUILD COMMENDS FREEMAN HAND NUMBER FOUR ARRAY HELD ALONE.

Overdraught broke first. Westerners always did. He came down out of the High the way a man takes off a coat in a hot room, and said in the Plain of any yard, "Well? Bought your freedom, didn't you?"

The Beadle shifted his mace from one arm to the other. Lumley put his pen down. Every company had that word and kept it for the street, and in Sealy's time nobody had said it in this room before witnesses.

The token lay on the baize between them, bright as it had been. The thing said had been true.

Sealy read the wire twice. By morning the Guild would have the commendation entered on the man's registration and its mark struck on his token, and no Court in the Accord could take either off. Then he folded the slip once and slid it under the roll, because the question had been put before it came, and what had been said stood said. He wet his nib.

"The question is on the freeman's seat," he said. "As it stands."

## 3. Quiet and travel

From the wire-office steps at the top of the hill the whole city could be watched taking its evening draught. The river lay at the bottom under its own haze. Above it rose the warehouses and the tram wires, then the terraces in their rows, and at lamp-time the gauge-houses threw the street lamps on district by district, so that the light climbed the hill in slow bands, street over street, and the hum under each band deepened as it came on. The Nail had come out at the pole. One square of roofs below the tannery stayed dark. The commons called it the Deafs.

Kit had the wire in his cap band. At the office the other boys called him Doorstep, because nothing ever showed on his face at a door, and it was the one thing about him they admired.

It was cutting week, and every street on the way down had a man in it with a key. Meters glowed by the doors, each under the Board's seal. A girl at a stall was still getting three copper for the day's assize loaves. Kit stopped at a lit draper's window, looked at his face in the glass, made it into nothing, and went on.

The hum stopped at a kerb.

He had it first in the soles of his boots, then in his ears, which rang with the want of it. The Deafs smelled of tallow and cold grates. Somewhere a bell was ringing the notch, so this street kept one clock and the hill another. Through a window he saw a cradle pushed against the wall where the main came in, for luck, though nothing had come through that wall since the cutter called.

The door had its cut plate in the junction box beside the step, the iron disc dropped in so cleanly it looked like part of the house.

A woman opened before he knocked. She looked at his face for a long time.

He had practised. She read it anyway. She took the wire and did not open it, and found a copper in her apron for him, and held the folded slip flat against her apron with the other hand.

Behind him, down the dark street, children were skipping to the rhyme he had skipped to:

> *Cutter, cutter, drop your plate,*
> *Mother's late and the bill is late.*
> *Nine words come and the hum goes,*
> *Who's to be next, nobody knows.*

## 4. A system moment

> FORM VII · OF THE PUBLIC RE-ASSAY OF A REGISTERED PRACTITIONER
> I. The candidate stands within the holding coil, bare-handed, before not fewer than three witnesses.
> II. The Measurewright closes the circuit and keeps silence while the coil reads.
> III. The tape is read aloud by the Clerk, title before name, and entered on the Reckoner's roll.
> IV. Witnesses keep silence until the entry is made.
> Quod dictum est, dictum stat.

The holding coil of the Fitters and Gaugers stood on the hall floor, a hoop of wound copper taller than a man, with four lenses of Clearbeam beryl set at its quarters like the hours on a clock-face. Clemency Ferrule stepped inside it bare-handed. Both her palms were burned across, nine days pink, from the night the Pellow Street main burst and she held the junction shut with them, in a way no school had taught her, until the cutters came. Nobody had asked to see them. The form did not ask.

Plumb, the Measurewright, wore her loupe on a black ribbon and closed the circuit without looking at anyone.

The hum of the hall's main came up through the boards and climbed. It went into Clemency's back teeth. The hair stood on her forearms. Her palms burned again, fresh, and under her breastbone something answered the coil the way a sound gauge-glass answers a flick of the nail, cleanly, and held it.

She knew what the coil would find. She had read it nine days ago on Pellow Street, with the main roaring through her hands, behind her breastbone in no voice at all:

[Stage III, Ascension. Journeyman.]
[Grade ceiling C.]

There had been no line for her hands. The rest had been hers alone, and no clerk in the Accord would have entered a word of it on her say-so.

The ticker in the stand chattered and put out a white tongue of tape. Plumb read it, initialled it, wrote in the margin and passed it to the Clerk. Sealy read it aloud and entire.

> HOLDING COIL III · HALL OF THE FITTERS AND GAUGERS · FORM VII
> FERRULE, CLEMENCY. ENTERED STAGE II, WELLING.
> READ STAGE III, ASCENSION. BAND I, MORTAL FOUNDATION.
> TIER OF STANDING: JOURNEYMAN. GRADE CEILING C.
> PROB EST. H.P., MEASUREWRIGHT, HEXAGONAL OATH.

The hall let its breath go before the nib had touched the roll. Her name went back along the benches in the new order, title first. Journeyman Ferrule. Journeyman. Ferrule's girl, from the Gaugers. Fifteen gold the month, at the least. At the end of the front bench old Lumley said, "Cheap at it," and that was the verdict.

Then Sealy read the margin too, in the Latin it was written in. "Candidata bis rogavit num spira proba esset. Proba est."

The clerks' bench laughed. The benches behind them waited, and Sealy gave it again in Common. "Candidate asked twice whether the coil was sound. It is."

The whole hall laughed then, which the form did not provide for, and Plumb did not look up. Sealy wet his nib and made the entry.

At the door waited a factor's man in a tall hat, come for the price the coil had just set. "Pellow and Sons, Journeyman. Fifteen gold the month, the first quarter in advance."

Clemency looked at her palms.

## 5. A comic beat

Veyra's day, and the corn market was packed to the beam-house steps. Pigeons sat in a grey row along the beam-house gutter and watched the oats.

Hob and Dob had both been entered on the parish roll as Robert, in the same ward, in the same month, and the Assize had sent them out as a pair for eleven years so that nobody need ever work out which Robert was meant. Hob did the talking. Dob carried the hammer.

"Mr. Heapwell," said Hob, to the grain merchant and the whole market. "Nomen est omen."

Heapwell smiled with every tooth he had. He was a large soft man in a dove-grey waistcoat, and he had heard the Latin before. "Inspector. You will find, if you will be so good as to watch, that every measure upon this stall is, by the formulary, level."

His strike man stepped up to the measure. "Go on, Treacle," somebody called from behind the oat sacks, "we've all day." The strike man laid the strike-board across the rim and drew it toward himself so slowly that a pigeon came down, inspected the heap, and went back up to the gutter before the board had reached the middle.

"Level," said Heapwell.

"Glacial," said Hob.

Dob said nothing. He had told the story once, years ago, to a new man in the Third Party, which had SALVO IURE TERTII painted over its door. An inspector in the south weighed a merchant's pound on the beam before he tapped it. It came out true, and he stamped it. The case unscrewed. The merchant pared the lead inside it a grain at a time and sold short under that stamp for a year. The new man had asked what the moral was. "Tap first, weigh after," Dob had said, and gone back to his pint, which had cost two copper and was short.

He lifted Heapwell's pound weight off the scale by its ring and tapped it.

Thock.

The market stopped. A woman with a basket of eggs set it down with great care. The strike-board halted halfway across the oats.

Then the pigeons went up off the gutter all at once, every one. The hum under the stalls went flat. The oats in the measure began to run off their heap on their own, grain by grain, toward the floor. Behind Heapwell a big quiet man in a drover's coat, who had leaned on a sack all morning, stood up off it.

Dob's knees understood it before he did, and were for sitting down.

"Pondus falsum," said Hob, a little higher than before. "Before witnesses."

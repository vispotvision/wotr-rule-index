# Court, council and negotiation exemplars (provisional)

Few-shot bank for talk-heavy scenes where power moves through words: wants that conflict, lies that serve them, rank spoken in forms of address, and culture doing work (what can and cannot be said). Method after The Weak Grade: perception into memory into inference into action, the speaker's habits making their sentences, consequences given room.

All people are invented for the example. Take the discipline, not the images.

<examples>

<example name="a-factor-and-a-lord" teaches="rank in forms of address, a lie held by exact wording, contempt as the joke that is also a threat">
Factor Ellis Pennick bowed from the waist, which was one inch lower than a Guild factor owed a lord of the Kōkan Line, and one inch higher than the lord's steward expected, and both men noted it and neither said so. That was how the negotiation began, before anyone had spoken, in the arithmetic of a bow.

"Lord Sodan," Pennick said. "The Guild thanks you for the hour."

"The Guild is welcome to an hour," said the lord. He sat on the low dais with his hands on his knees and the winter light behind him, so that his face was hard to read and the factor's was not. "I have given it a great many. I notice they never come back with change." The steward behind him did not smile. The room smiled for him, in the way a room does when a man of rank makes a joke at someone else's cost.

Pennick had prepared for the joke. He had prepared for it in the hired carriage, with the account-book on his knees, and he had decided that he would not answer it, because answering a lord's joke told the lord it had landed. "The Guild has never yet charged for a lord's time," he said. "We charge only for the ink." It was true in the narrowest way a thing can be true, since the Guild charged the tenants for the ink and the lord for nothing at all, and Pennick, who knew the lord knew this, watched the lord's eyes for the moment he would decide whether to say so. The lord did not say so. He turned his hand over on his knee, palm up, which in the Kōkan houses meant *proceed, and I will remember you did.*
</example>

<example name="a-hearing-with-a-lie" teaches="a lie caught when someone acts on the truth; speech that carries a want; what a room does while it listens">
They had given the old woman a stool, which meant they expected her to be long, and she sat on it with her hands round her stick and her feet not quite touching the floor.

"You say you did not see the cart," said the magistrate.

"I say I saw the horse," said Marta Voll. "The horse is a different animal."

Somebody in the gallery laughed and was hushed, and the magistrate waited for the hush with the patience of a man who had once been paid by the hour. Beside him the clerk's pen scratched. In the front bench the carter's brother sat very straight and looked at the wall. He had brought a purse. He had not taken it out. He was waiting, Marta knew, as she had waited for forty years of market days, to see whether the old woman would sell.

"The horse was brown," she said. "A brown gelding with a white off-hind and a notch out of the left ear, and the notch is old. I know the notch. My son put it there with a gate."

She watched the carter's brother's hands. They had been resting on his knees. They went flat.

"The cart behind it was blue," she said, and it had not been blue, it had been green, and she let the lie lie there on the floor of the court like a dropped glove and waited for someone to pick it up.

It took the clerk eleven seconds. "The record shows a green cart," he murmured to the magistrate, and the magistrate's eyes went to the carter's brother, who was no longer looking at the wall.
</example>

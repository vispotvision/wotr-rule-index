# Dialogue and NPC voice exemplars (provisional)

Few-shot bank written 2026-10-30 from Isaac's dialogue answers: a major voice is built culture and rank first, then want, then a verbal fingerprint; subtext follows culture (court voices layered, soldiers and commons blunt); lore comes out through an NPC's pride in their subject; a room takes the audible shape that room would have. A line two characters could swap has failed.

All people are invented for the example. Take the method, not the images or the lines.

<examples>

<example name="a-moto-room" teaches="layered subtext and forms of address: the meaning is in what is offered and what is refused">
"The tea is cold," said Lord Sodan, without looking at the cup.

Hatsu, who had been steward in that house for twenty-two years and had poured the tea himself at the second bell, bowed from the knees and took the cup away, and did not say that it had been scalding when he set it down. A cold cup was not a statement about tea. It was a statement about how long a guest had been permitted to stay, made by the host, in the one voice a host could use without being the first to say *go.*

"My lord is gracious to have kept it warm so long," said the guest. He was a Guild assessor in a traveling coat, and he understood perfectly well what had been said, and he understood also that there was a second thing under it, because the steward had taken the cup with his left hand, which was the hand a house used for taking away a thing it did not intend to replace. The assessor drew breath. He had a number in his coat for the grain levy, and a smaller number that he had meant to offer if the first was refused, and he saw that he would not now be asked for either.

"If the Guild should send a man in the spring," said Lord Sodan, "the roof will be mended by then."
</example>

<example name="a-dawi-grammar" teaches="culture as grammar: what the language will not let a speaker say, and what that does to a lie">
"The seeing was done," said the smith.

It was how a Dawi witness spoke, and the magistrate had been among them long enough not to ask who had done the seeing. In the hold's tongue a sentence about a deed had a doer at its head and a mark of the Tally at its tail, and a woman who wanted to put a thing on the record without putting a name beside it could leave the doer out, and everyone in the hall would hear the gap and know how big it was. Nobody ever called it lying. It was a courtesy to the dead, who could not be asked, and to the living, who would be.

"The seeing was done at what hour?"

"At the closing of the forge. When the door was barred."

The magistrate waited. The smith's hands, which were the size of loaves and shiny to the wrist with old burns, lay open on her knees, and she did not look at the Common speaker in the third row, the one in the good coat, whom she had known since he could not lift a hammer. In Common she would have had to say *I.* She had been three days deciding to say the whole thing in the one tongue where she did not have to.
</example>

<example name="soldiers-talking" teaches="blunt voices with their own oaths and jokes: swearing as texture, banter as bond, the joke that is also a warning">
"Wet again," said Corporal Dunn, to the sky generally, and spat. "I'll have you know I was promised a summer posting. Highest sort of promise. Sworn on the Colonel's mother."

"The Colonel hasn't got a mother," said Pike.

"Course he has. He was hatched from the same bloody cabbage as the rest of us." Dunn turned a boot over, looked into it with the despair of a man looking into a grave, and poured out a thin brown tablespoon of the Ridge. "Right. Listen. When the horns go, you stay on my left, you keep your head down, and you don't try to be clever. You're not clever. You're lovely, but you're not clever."

"Cheers, Corp."

"I mean it kindly. Clever lads die first. They stop to look at things." He rammed the boot back on and stamped it down. Behind them the line was beginning to shuffle, one man's cough setting off the next, and somewhere ahead a signal-lamp flickered twice and went dark. Dunn's face did not change, but his voice dropped half a note, and Pike, who had been listening to that voice for two years, set his hand on his sword. "Stay on my left," Dunn said again, and this time it was not a joke.
</example>

<example name="a-man-proud-of-his-subject" teaches="exposition carried by pride: the NPC over-explains, the narration lets him, and the explaining reveals him">
"Now, the thing about a Stannvaard lock," said Old Brix, "is that it has no key."

He had laid it on the bench between them, a plain gray box of riveted plate no bigger than a loaf, and he stood over it with both hands flat on the bench as a man stands over a new calf. "You'll think that's daft. Everyone does. A lock with no key, you'll say, how's that a lock, it's a wall with a handle. And I'll say to you what my father said to me, which is: you've never seen a *proper* lock, lad. A key is a confession. It says to every thief in the world, *there's a way in, and it's this shape.* Now look."

He tapped the plate with a knuckle. Inside the box something shifted a very long way down, a slow dry slide of weights.

"Eleven tumblers. Each one hangs off the last, and you don't lift them, you *breathe* on them. You wait for the weather. A Stannvaard lock opens when the damp in the room agrees with it, and not before, and it's never opened for a thief in four hundred years because no thief has ever had the patience to stand in a cold cellar for a week." He sniffed. "I'll sell you one for nine marks. It's worth thirty. I'd sell it for nothing to a man who understood it, and I've not met him yet."
</example>

<example name="a-council-talking-over-itself" teaches="a room's audible shape: cross-talk with a few clear voices, the POV catching half, the rest as a living murmur">
By the second hour the Chamber had stopped taking turns.

"If the northern wardens would simply"-"the grain, I keep saying the grain"-"not a matter for the Chamber, Aldous, and you know"-"the *precedent,* gentlemen, the precedent"-and under it all the long grinding bass of Speaker Voll, who had not yet raised his voice and would not, because he had learned in forty years of sessions that the man who waits is the man who is heard last, and last is how a room remembers.

Hettrun, at the clerk's bench with her pen going, caught only a half of it. *...cannot hold the pass without...* said somebody to her left, and *...ask the widow, ask the widow herself...* said somebody else, and a laugh from the back with no joy in it at all. A bench scraped. A door opened and shut. She wrote *general discussion* in the margin, and under it, because she could not stop herself, *the Speaker has not spoken, and every man in the room has noticed.*
</example>

# Fight exemplars (provisional)

Loaded before any fight or set piece, as the few-shot bank. They teach density and method: how many blows, how much tissue, how a technique rides a blow, how a kill is slowed. They never supply images. Every fight invents its own signature image; the devices already spent are listed in `used-devices.md`.

Status: written by Claude from Isaac's questionnaire answers (2026-10-30); magic-on-the-blow rewritten 2026-11-03 to show the full Working Card (R81-2, R81-3, R81-5, R81-7, R81-17, R81-18). Isaac has not signed off on them. Replace or add passages from fights he loves; his pasted passages outrank these.

Canon note: the Wellspring names, aura colors and technique details inside these passages are illustrative and are not canon (for example, a blue cold-edge has no Family of its own). Real fights take the color from the character's Family hue (The Color of Essence) and the working's signature from `desktop/style-bank/wellspring-signatures.md`.

How to use: read all of them, then write the fight with different bodies, different tools, different ground. Copy nothing but the discipline.

<example name="blows-and-gore" teaches="the Blow Card on a duel: every blow landed or missed, tissue named, damage carried, the arm taken">
*The Brass Lantern, Corvane Quarter. Third bell.*

Dagan's first punch missed by a hair and told Ketsu everything.

It was a right hook thrown from the hip. The back heel ground a quarter-turn into the boards, the hip rolled after it, the shoulder came through behind the hip, and the fist arrived last with all the big man's weight stacked behind it like cordwood. Ketsu saw the heel turn and was already dropping. He folded at the knees with his chin buried against his collarbone, and the knuckles went over his head close enough to lift the hair from his scalp. The wind of it cooled the sweat on his neck.

The fist kept going. It met the brick wall behind him and *KRAAAK*, a piece of the wall the size of a loaf blew out in a cloud of plaster and red dust, and when Dagan pulled his hand free the skin over his first two knuckles hung off in a wet flap with the white of the bone shining through. He looked at it with interest. He did not stop smiling.

"Everything I've ever lit in this room is mine, friend!" he roared. "Every ember! Every spark! Come and take it from me!"

Ketsu came up out of the crouch driving through his right leg, the whole line of his body unspooling upward from heel to shoulder, and put his left elbow into the soft gap under Dagan's ribs. The point of the olecranon found the eleventh rib, the floating one, the one no muscle covers, and bent it inward until it broke. *Crack*, small and wet, like a green stick. Dagan's breath left him in a pink spray that speckled Ketsu's shoulder and the side of his neck, warm as spit. The big man did not fold. He grunted, a sound of faint surprise, and Ketsu understood that the rib was broken and that the man had decided it did not matter.

Dagan's knee came up. It was a plain, short, ugly blow, thrown without any art, and it struck the outside of Ketsu's thigh and drove the great muscle into the nerve beneath it. White light went up the leg from the point of impact. Then the leg went dead from hip to ankle, a post of wood nailed to the bottom of his body, and he staggered sideways and caught his weight on the bar with his hand.

The open palm came round and cracked across his cheek. It caught the side of his face from the jaw to the temple, rocked his head on his neck like a flag in wind, and split his lip against his own teeth. Blood came hot down his chin and a tooth, a molar, came loose and ticked across the boards and rolled under a bench. The room tilted. He tasted iron, and under the iron he tasted the copper of fear, which had a much older flavor.

*Left leg gone. Twenty seconds before the blood comes back to it. Don't plant it. Don't pivot on it.*

He drew.

The saber came out of the scabbard in a rising cut from the right hip, a long diagonal across the body that opened at the knee and climbed to the left shoulder. Dagan, who had been expecting another fist, leaned back from it a half-second late. The point caught the plum coat at the belt line and tore it open to the collar, then went on and opened the skin across his chest from the belt to the right collarbone in a long clean line. The cold came off the steel in a breath of mist. For one heartbeat the cut was white and bloodless, the lips of it curled back from the fat and the muscle underneath, and then the blood came all at once in a bright curtain, running down into the ruin of the coat.

"AH!" Dagan shouted, delighted, and looked down at himself as at a present. "Oh, that's *good!*"

He threw the left hook before the shout had finished. Ketsu saw it come, a whole slow arc of fist and shoulder, and his instinct told him to step back on the bad leg. He put his weight on the right instead and turned the saber in his hand, and the hook met the flat of the blade with a ringing *TANG*. The cold bit into the knuckles. The fist glanced off and kept going into the lamp-bracket on the wall, tore it out of the brick, and hurled the whole fitting, lamp and bracket and burning oil, across the room, where it burst on the floor in a bloom of fire.

Ketsu was already inside the swing. The left hook had pulled Dagan's whole body around, and his left side lay open: ribs, armpit, the long underside of the forearm. The saber was in Ketsu's right hand. He put his weight on the good leg, set his heel, and cut the left arm at the elbow.

The edge went in on the inside of the joint, between the two long bones, where the tendons lie in a cord. It parted the brachial artery on the way through and found the other side, and the forearm, hand still clenched in its fist, fell away and hit the floor with a heavy wet *thump* and rolled. For the length of one breath nothing came from the stump but a puff of vapor, because the steel had frozen the cut face white. Then the pressure behind it broke the ice, and the blood leapt out of the arm in three hard arcs, and where the cold air met it the beads froze and hung and rattled down onto the boards like red hail.

Dagan looked at his arm. It lay on the floor with the fingers slowly opening. Ketsu watched the color leave the big man's face from the forehead down, saw the pupils spread, and thought, with horrible clarity, *he's going to scream.*

He laughed instead.

It started low in the chest, shaking the ruined rib, and came up through his teeth in a high happy howl, and he stood in the freezing ruin of his coat with the red hail ticking at his feet and slapped the stump against his own thigh, again, and again, to see if it would hurt. "The *edge*," he said. "Oh, the *edge!* Friend, nobody has ever, in all my life, ever, ever, ever *hurt me before!*"

His right hand came up and closed on Ketsu's throat.

The fingers met around the windpipe and the thumb dug in under the angle of the jaw, and Ketsu's feet left the floor. The room went dark at the edges, a tunnel closing, and the roaring in his ears was his own blood. He hit the big man's wrist with the pommel, once, twice, a flat dull crunch like a boot on a frozen puddle, and felt the small bones go and the grip slacken and tighten again, like a living thing deciding. He put his left thumb into the wound across Dagan's chest and dug.

Dagan screamed then, a real scream, and the fingers opened, and Ketsu fell, and the saber was in his hand.

<example name="magic-on-the-blow" teaches="the full Working Card at first display, interleaved with the Blow Card; an ordinary repeat written by what changed; the aura coat once, then as body tells; a held card shown by effect and explained at the reveal; η as a proportion; a clash settled by real physics named on the page">
*The Salt Stair, Corvane harbor. Sixth bell, rain lifting.*

The two debt-collectors came down the stair together, and Ketsu knew from the way they came that they had done this before.

The taller one, a lean man with a brass-handled cane, set his feet three stairs above and spoke without hurry. "You'll want to know what the cane does, so I'll tell you. It does nothing. It's a cane. My partner does the work." The partner was a short wide woman on the landing below, her aura a muddy ochre that lay close to her skin like wet clay and thickened around her left fist. *A bluff, or the truth told so flatly it sounds like one.* Ketsu filed both and drew.

The cold came up in him the way it always did, from the breastbone out. He breathed in through his nose until his ribs creaked, held it, and let the pull take it: Aether hauled in off the wet harbor stone and refined in the Soul Crystal under his sternum, the hard bright knot that every practitioner carries where a weaker man carries only a heartbeat, and Essence pushed out through the Shell, the body's own skin of worked Essence, into his arms. A practitioner's Shell is a leaky pipe. Of every measure he pulled, something like two parts in three reached the steel and the third came out of him as heat, which was the joke of his art: the cold-edge ran him hot. Steam lifted off the back of his neck into the rain. His ears burned red.

The aura answered on the outside. Blue climbed his forearms like breath fogging cold glass, pale and clean as a winter morning, and as he pulled it sharpened at the wrists into two bright cuffs and ran on down the hilt and along the saber's spine in a thin line of light. The rain changed where it touched him. Drops that hit his shoulders ticked and stayed, beaded white; drops that crossed the blade turned to grit and fell, *tsiiin*, the high thin singing sound the cold-edge always made, like a wet finger round the rim of a glass. The air within an arm of the steel smelled of snow and struck iron. His fingers ached on the grip the way they ache on a pump handle in the dead of winter.

What it did was simple to say and hard to do. Heat runs from hot to cold, always, faster the steeper the difference and the better the two things touch; the old natural philosophers called it conduction, and every cook who has burned a hand on a skillet handle has felt it. The Wellspring under his art did not make cold, because cold is only the absence of heat. It made the edge a sink: the glyph set its boundary at the steel's surface and allowed heat to cross that boundary in one direction only, inward, so whatever the edge touched poured its warmth into the blade and the blade drank it and never gave it back. The pull paid for the drinking. Skin, cloth, mortar, it did not matter. The edge took heat from all of it.

The cane came down at his head, a plain downward chop, the lean man's whole forearm behind it and his front knee bending to put his weight on the stair below. Ketsu saw the knee go and stepped in under the arc instead of away from it, turning his left shoulder to meet the cane. The brass handle struck the point of the shoulder, *TONK*, and the blue there thickened into a coat as hard as lacquer, rippled once from the point of contact to the elbow like a struck bell, and thinned again. The force went down through his collarbone into his planted back foot and the stone took it. His collarbone ached. A smaller man would have lost the arm.

He was inside the lean man's guard now, a forearm's length from the coat. His back heel drove, the hip turned, and the saber came up from his right hip in a rising diagonal, the edge leading. It met the lean man's coat at the belt and opened it toward the left shoulder, and the cold went in with it. The wool went white and stiff along the cut as the water in it froze. The skin went white after it, the fat beneath, the thin sheet of muscle over the ribs, all of it frost-burned in the same instant it was opened, so that for a breath the wound was a pale clean seam and nothing came out of it. Frozen blood cannot run. Then the man's own body heat beat the steel's last touch, and the blood came in a bright stiff curtain, slow, cold to the eye, steaming where the rain hit it. The lean man looked down at his chest with professional distaste and took one step back up the stair to get out of reach.

"Now," he said.

The partner's fist hit the landing.

That was the held card, and Ketsu understood it only when it landed. The stone under his boots heaved like the deck of a boat in a cross-swell, an ochre ripple running up the flight from her knuckles, and the rain-slick steps tilted outward toward the harbor. It was a wave in the ground, a real one: her strike put its force into the stone in a single pulse, and the pulse ran along the surface the way a wave runs along a shaken rope, lifting each step and dropping it in turn. Engineers call it a surface wave, and it is the part of an earthquake that knocks houses down. Hers was small, only a stair's width, but it did not need to be large. It only needed to arrive while the cane held him in place.

He had no footing and no time. He pushed the blue into his heels instead, a burst of pale light out of the soles of his boots that left a streak of afterimage hanging in the rain where he had stood, and he was three stairs up and past the lean man with his back to the wall. The coat on his shoulders had gone thin as tissue. *Half gone already. Breathe.* The cuffs at his wrists had dulled from white to a gray blue.

*She moves the ground. He pins me while she does it. They drilled this.* The thought came with a small cold pleasure, the pleasure of seeing a machine whole. *Then the machine has a joint.*

He laid his off hand flat on the wall and felt the next pulse coming up through the stone. It had a note, a low thrum like a drum heard through a floor, and it changed as it passed the stair's turn, where old mortar had been patched with lime. Lime mortar is porous. It holds water the way bread does. The wave slowed in the patch, and the patch was where the whole flight hinged.

"The edge takes heat," Ketsu said, loudly, to give her something to hear. "That's all it is. Whatever it touches."

He cut the patch. *Tsiiin*, and the frost ran from the edge into the lime along the line of the cut and down through the joint to the landing, white crystals feathering out across the gray in the shape of ferns. Water swells by about a tenth when it freezes, so every soaked pore in the mortar burst its walls from inside, the same way a forgotten bottle of milk splits on a winter step, and frozen mortar that has burst its own pores has no grip left on the block. The stair shrugged, shuddered, and the whole turn slumped with a long grinding *KRRRRRUMBLE*, dropping the short woman's footing a hand's breadth. Her next strike came down on a block that was no longer joined to anything. The pulse went into it and stopped, with nowhere to run, and the ochre on her fist flashed once and stuttered out. She swore in the language of the northern docks.

The cost arrived as she swore. Ketsu's hands shook on the grip. The second cut had taken less out of him than the first, but it had come out of a smaller purse, and the blue on his arms had drawn back to the wrists, thin and flickering at its edges like a lamp low on oil.

The lean man looked at the broken stair, then at Ketsu, with something very like respect, and swung the cane once more. Ketsu met it with the flat of the saber. *TANG.* The brass handle came away in his hand, and the cane, which was a cane, rolled down the steps and into the harbor.

"Told you it did nothing," the lean man said, and bled.

Dagan did not wait for him to stand. He crossed the floor in four long strides, faster than a man that size had any right to be, and came in low this time, the left shoulder down and leading, meaning to put Ketsu through the bar with the whole moving mass of him. Ketsu read the dropped shoulder and side-stepped off his right foot, and the big man went past him close enough that the plum coat brushed his sleeve and hit the bar instead. *GADONG.* The brass rail folded in the middle like a wet reed and a rack of bottles went over behind it in a long jangling slide of glass. He's pouring it up from the floor.*

There was a crowd, of a kind. Up in the rafters, where she had gone the moment the first lamp turned, Marit hung from a beam with one arm hooked over it and the other pressed to her ribs. "He isn't aiming!" she shouted down, thin and furious. "He's pouring! Watch the floor, not the man!"

"Thank you, I had noticed!" Ketsu snapped, and tested it, because a theory is only a theory until it has cost you something. He hooked a splinter of table-leg with his good foot and flicked it into the smoke. The threads left Dagan's boots for a heartbeat and swarmed the wood, and the leg was ash before it hit the wall. *It takes whatever is nearest, and it takes it greedily.* He filed that and rolled onto his knees, and as he rose he saw the other thing.

Dagan had not once stepped toward the far end of the room. He had walked the long way round the bar. He had put out a boot, begun a stride toward the kitchen door, and changed it into a swagger, quick as a cough. Behind that door, bolted into the wall and fat as a bell, stood the Lantern's cistern: eight hundred liters of parish water, black iron with a welded seam. A man who is afraid of nothing does not walk around a water tank. Ketsu filed that with the rest and let his face stay stupid.

<example name="the-turn-and-the-kill" teaches="a planted clue paid off, the slowed perfect hit, then the Kill Card: the wound named, the dying lingered on, last words">
She had been watching his feet. She had been watching his feet since the first minute. The cork soles. The brass plate. The ward-plate by the stall and the ward-plate by the parish stone and the one he'd stepped on to sit on the barrel, and the brass cap of the corner post in the middle of the aisle, and every time the tall man moved he had put his foot on a plate, and every time he put a foot on a plate the lanterns dimmed. *He isn't just standing on the plates. He's never left them. He's been walking from plate to plate across the whole of the Market, and between the plates, where it's bare stone, he's nothing. A man with a bag of stones and no tap.*

The last plate was eleven paces behind him, down the aisle, in front of the baker's stall, and he was walking backward toward it with his eyes on her and his heels feeling for the brass.

She counted the banked force in her arm. She counted it as she'd counted coins, in the old days. It was enough to kill a horse. It was enough to knock a bridge-pier out of true.

*He has the stones. He has fifteen, twenty minutes of stones. It doesn't matter.* She breathed once, hard, and her bad eye, the white one, seemed to look back at her. *The plate is the tap, and the tap is a worked Core under the Market with the whole district's hours in it. He's tied himself to it. If it breaks while he's standing on it, everything in that pool goes through him.*

"Valdric," said Tessaly.

"What?"

"You're on your last one."

He glanced down. His heel had found the brass, the baker's plate, and for one half-second his weight went onto it and his whole body said *safe*, and the lash in his hand went slack. Tessaly broke into a run.

She went down the aisle in a streak of gold, the cobbles cracking under her heels. Behind her Pim was sobbing and calling her name, and the dark Market rang with her feet, and the tall man in the gray coat was backing away with his hands up and his face white, and she did the thing she had never done in her life, which was to let the fist go with everything she had, banked and bought and borrowed and sworn.

The room slowed.

It came on her the way it comes once or twice in a life, all at once and without being asked for. The long lines of the lantern-strings hung in the air like drawn thread. A saffron lantern lay in the middle of the aisle with its flame bent flat, caught in a falling curl. Valdric Oake's mouth was open, the pale blue eyes huge, a bead of blood from the corner of his lip stretched into a ruby thread. She saw every hair of the clipped mustache. She saw her own gold fist, a hand's breadth from the tall man's chest, with a halo of sparks around the knuckles, drawing out and out, and she thought of her father's yard and the drunken fists, and her first week at the drill hall, and Pim's dull voice counting links.

She did not hit the man. She hit the plate.

*KRAKA-DOOM.*

The fist went into the brass ward-plate under his heel, and the plate went up like the lid of a pot. A black spark leapt off it and hung in the air like a crack in a window, and every soul in the Market saw it. The cobbles for six feet in every direction lifted in a single shuddering wave. The licensed Core under the plate, a pool as wide as the aisle, split along its lip and spat a column of white-gold fire into the air, and Valdric Oake, who was standing on top of it, went with the plate.

He went up with his arms flung wide and the gray coat opening around him like the wings of a moth, and the sulfur filaments in the wool sparked from collar to hem. The stones on his back went in a spray of brass shards and a burst of blue-white flame. Then he came down.

He came down on the bare cobbles with a flat wet crunch, spread on his back, and stayed there.

---

Tessaly walked to him.

She walked slowly. Her bad eye saw a white fog and her good eye saw the shape of him in the dark, and the dark was not quite dark anymore, because a string of green lanterns had caught from the fire and a tongue of gold from the broken Core was licking up along the gantry. The smell was burned wool and burned hair and the sweet, awful smell of scorched fat.

He lay with his head turned toward her. The coat had burned away down the front. Under it the chest was a wreck. Her last punch, the one she had never landed, had landed after all, because the force she had thrown into the plate had gone through him as it went through the world: the sternum had split lengthwise like a log under a wedge, and the ribs on both sides had sprung outward from the breastbone like the staves of a broken barrel. The heart lay in the cage of them, bluish and dark and still, shining wetly. Blood welled in the hollow of him in a slow dark sheet and ran across the cobbles into the cracks. One of the stones lay a foot from his hand, still hissing.

"Tessaly," he said.

He had said it correctly, which was the only thing that surprised her.

"Yes."

"They did say you'd stand still for it." His voice came out in a whisper, the pale eyes looking at the lantern-lit sky. A long breath rattled in the broken chest and did not seem to find its way home. "It's a very good vow. I wish I'd..." He stopped. The corner of his mouth moved. "You know who sent me?"

"No."

"I'd rather not say." A thin, bright laugh, and a bubble of red at the lip. "They'll pay. They always do. Oh, but it's cold..."

The pale eyes went out slowly, the way a lamp goes when the oil is gone, and the light went out of them at last and left them blue and clear and wide. She knelt and closed the lids with her thumb. The bone of her face hurt like fire. She did not weep, because there wasn't time.

"Pim," she said.

# Style Sample Bank: Kharven

Worked examples of the prose law for a Kharven POV, as it stands after the 2026-10-17 directions (rules/doc-directions-2026-10-17.yaml, R79-3 to R79-6) on top of the R70 style law (rules/doc-style-law-2026-10-03.yaml). Read it before writing in this culture, beside *The Weak Grade* (references/the-weak-grade-isaac-reference.md), which is the primary model for how a passage develops. The people are illustrations; a style sample is not an archived scene, so nothing here stands as canon until it is used in one (R79-4).

Kharven tilts West, toward the saga and the soldier: the count, the blunt body, debts paid in wood and labor. The Eastern strand still leads narration, interiority and structure here as it does for every culture (R70-1, R70-3), so the season word opens the scene, the road runs as kishotenketsu and a fight can hold still at its turn. LitRPG leads where a man's Stage moves, and in a kingdom that cannot read, the tape is read aloud. The prose carries no statistics (R79-3). Paragraphs are developed and follow the mind as it moves, from what the POV sees into what he remembers, infers and feels; a short line stands where a revelation earns it, and the consequence that follows gets room. The north's flavor lives in the words, short hard English for the clansmen, and in what the eye goes to first: the body's ledger of hands, breath-plume, weight and scent, then the stack, because the north judges a man by what he has put by (R70-44). Emotion plays anime-big by default, felt and thought through; the held image is an occasional device (R79-4). The POV may interpret and draw meaning in narration, and NPCs may have italic thoughts wherever a thought reveals them (R79-4). Kharven speech runs short by habit, and that is the culture's flavor and each card's, never a cap: the card sets how a person speaks, accents are spelled in full phonetic where a card would set them, and each person keeps his own humor, in danger as well (R79-5). Fights are body, path, target, result and cost, the full mechanism allowed on the page, figures in mouths and never in narration, wounds plain and anime, shrugged off and felt after (R79-6). Measures run in bowshots, a pot's boil, a fire's length and the notch (R70-29). Kharven has no honorifics of its own: level lives in English grammar, and a shift of it is an event the room hears (R70-107); a Kharven POV keeps plain names in narration even in a Moto room (R70-118), the Shieldwarden is addressed by title alone (The Banner Houses of Kharven), and a clansman speaking to the Crown looks at the chest and never the eye (the Inventory's folk beliefs). Comic names grow from the scarcity insults, from protective cheap names and from roll-names an Accord clerk assigned, and never from the recently dead, whose name waits unspoken until it is given on (R70-124, R70-127, R23-11-NAME_AVOIDANCE_WAITING). The Standing Inventory (desktop/inventories/kharven.md) is reference: draw from it what the scene uses (R79-3).

## 1. A fight or a working

The nameless day came round that year in hard frost, and the law slept through it.

Ketil Rafnsson came out behind the death-house with his ridge sword bare and his left mitten off, because a hand on the grip must feel the grip, and the ridge had taught him that with the skin of better men than him. The hairs in his nose froze on the first breath and cracked on the second. He had lain awake most of the night listening to the outer town's dogs and turning over the Stack-Count two winters gone, when he had walked the lanes behind the headman with the witnesses and said aloud, before every door, that Sverri's pile came up short. He had been right. He had watched Sverri's wife look at the ground while he said it, and being right had cost him nothing at the time. He had learned since that in the north a true word can sit in a man like a splinter under a nail and work its way in until it festers, and that on the one day of the year when no oath holds, a festered thing comes out of the house with an axe.

Sverri the sealer waited by the woodpile with a hand-axe in his right fist and a seax in his left, the seax laid back along his forearm the way sealers carry a flensing knife out onto the ice, edge outward, so the blade hides behind the arm until it is already in you. He was a broad man gone thick through the middle with seal fat and winters, his beard frozen into a single gray plank, his eyes red and wet at the rims from the cold or from something the cold was covering for. Ketil looked at his hands first, as anyone born under that sky would: the right loose and easy on the haft, the left far too still.

"How's yer stack, ridgeman."

Ketil did not answer him. He took the sword up into the Roof, the high guard of the ridge, hilt beside his right ear and the point standing up behind his head, and settled his weight over the rear foot where the frozen rut would hold it. Eleven winters on the Ridgewall had given him one law for a man who carried two blades: the high one is the one he shows you, so watch the low one, because the low one is the one he means.

Sverri came in three running steps with the axe high, the snow squeaking under his boots, and Ketil struck first, because a Greymane man always struck first; the ridge called it taking the Before, and held that the man who struck second spent the whole fight answering. The Wrath-Cut came down off his right shoulder with his hips turning behind it and all his weight dropping through the blade onto the axe-arm. It should have opened the forearm to the bone. Sverri's hide sleeve had frozen stiff as board, though, and the edge met it at a slant and skidded, and skidded on into the axe's beard, the hooked jaw of steel that hangs below the bit, and caught there. Sverri turned the haft a quarter. The beard took Ketil's blade with it, dragging the point down and wide until the two weapons locked and ground against each other, and Ketil felt the seax move beneath the bind before he saw it, the way a sealer feels the ice shift under his knees before it cracks. It came in low for the belly.

He let the bind go and stepped off to his left, half a heartbeat late. The seax opened his coat, the felt beneath it and the skin over his lowest rib in one cold stroke, and the cold was the worst of it, a strip of ice laid along his side and then, a breath behind, fire poured along the ice. He did not look down. His bare palm had frozen to the pommel while he stood in guard, and when he shifted his grip the iron kept a strip of his skin, and he left it there and felt that no more than the rest. The ridge said a man felt it after. A man who felt it now was a man about to lie down.

Far up, the aurora hung green over the Seat. A bowshot down the lane the outer town's smoke stood straight up from every roof, gray into green, in air too cold to move. At Ketil's shoulder ran the death-house wall, unheated, keeping the winter's dead quiet in their rows, and with the cold clarity that came to him only in a fight he thought that he and Sverri had both carried men in through that door, and that whichever of them lay down here would lie in there with them until the thaw. Their two breaths stood between them like a pair of ghosts that had stepped outside to watch. He kept his eyes on Sverri's left hand. The sealer had felt the seax go in and wanted to feel it again; it had already slid back along his arm, waiting.

Ketil threw the Crooked Cut.

It came with his hands crossed on the grip, right over left, the blade swinging down across Sverri's body from the outside and turning edge-first onto the inside of the wrist that held the seax, a finger above the heel of the hand, where the ridge's old bonesetters said a man kept the cords that close his fingers and the vessel that feeds them. It went through the tendon sheaths and the radial artery and stopped on bone. Sverri's fingers sprang open all together, the way a sealer's net falls open when the drawstring parts, and the seax dropped point first into the drift. Then the blood came, a bright jet that leapt an arm's length on the pulse and smoked in the air and pattered down onto the snow, and leapt again, and again, and where it landed on the hide sleeve it went black almost before it had finished being red.

Sverri sat down against the woodpile. He looked at his hand as if it belonged to a man he had known once and could not place, and his mouth worked, and what came out of it was a laugh, short and astonished, the laugh of a man who has gone down on the ice and found himself sitting in it.

"Above the elbow," said old Gunna, who had watched it all from the death-house step with her arms folded inside her coat. "Belt it there and tight, ridgeman, and he keeps the arm. Leave it a pot's boil and you can carry him in the door behind me."

Ketil knew the rest from the ridge. A pot's boil of that, and Sverri would be in the third bowl, where men stopped asking for water and started asking for their mothers. The fury that had walked him out behind the death-house drained out of him all at once, like broth from a cracked bowl, and what came in behind it was worse: two winters of a true word, and here was where it had carried them both, a sealer's blood smoking on the snow and his own going cold in his felt. He knelt in the red snow and pulled his belt free, and his hands shook so hard he dropped the end once and had to scrabble for it. Tomorrow was a named day. Every debt in the outer town would wake with it, and this one would wake in his name. He cinched the belt above the sealer's elbow with the hand the iron had skinned, and now, at last, he felt it.

## 2. Court and talk

Stack-Count came in with the first frost that would not lift, and by dusk every house in the outer town had squared its pile against the wall.

Eyvind the headman had walked the lanes at dark with two witnesses and counted every woodpile aloud, house by house. Nobody wrote it down. By his fire the count lived in thirty mouths at once, and Ragna's stack had come up twenty rounds short of what she had split. She had known it by the shape of the pile before he reached the end of her wall, the way a mother knows a child is missing from a sleeping room without counting heads. She had split every one of those rounds herself since Torolf went to the sky, through the dark of the autumn with her shoulders burning and the axe-haft rubbing her palms raw under the mittens, and the pile against her wall was the only thing in the outer town that still said his name without anyone having to say it. Twenty rounds gone out of it in the dark. Her anger had been hot for three days. Standing at Eyvind's fire, she found it had nowhere left to sit.

The boy stood at the hearth beside his grandmother. Arni, fifteen, Yrsa's last. Ragna looked at his hands first. The knuckles were split, splinters had gone black under the skin of both palms, and his forearm was so thin the hide-coat stood off it like a tent off its pole. He had been splitting, then, and splitting hard, and his own house's stack was still short. So he had split for his mother until his hands bled and come up short anyway, and then walked down the lane to the widow's wall. Ragna understood it all at once and wished she did not, because a thief she could have hated cleanly.

"Twenty rounds," Eyvind said. "From a widow's pile, in the dark."

"Wet wood burns eventually," said Yrsa.

"A liar never dries."

"He told you it was true. Where's your liar?" Yrsa did not wait for him. "The first bowl goes to the one who cannot fill it themselves. His mother's stack was shorter than Ragna's ever was."

"Then she comes to my fire and says so, and the town splits for her."

Arni's chin came up. His face had gone red to the ears, the color climbing out of his collar as if somebody had lit a fire under it, and when he spoke it came out far too loud for the longhouse, and his voice cracked on the last word and went up like a girl's. "The sky does not ask whether you are ready."

Nobody answered him. Somebody set a bowl down on the hearth-stone, and it rang, and the ring went on longer than a bowl should, and Arni stood in it with his fists shut and his eyes swimming and his mouth pressed flat to keep the rest from coming out.

"That's an old man's saying," Eyvind said. "Grow a beard under it first."

At the far bench the Hallenfeld quartermaster sat with his ledger open, because the Seat now wanted labor-debt written down, in the Concord hand that nobody else in the room could read. "The Seat would take salt for it," he offered. "A bar passes for a silver mark, more in a hard winter."

"Salt buys at the Seat," said Eyvind. "It doesn't burn. A day's splitting for every round, owed to the widow, settled when the grass comes."

The quartermaster wrote it, and worked out under his breath what twenty days came to at the low day rate, which was ten silver, ten bars that Arni's house had never seen. The room did not care what it came to. A debt in wood was a debt in wood, and the north had been paying those since before the Concord had letters.

Yrsa took her hand off the boy's arm and turned to Ragna. "My mother knew a woman on the north road, Saali, who burned nothing she could do without. They found her at her hearth in the Thin Weeks, frozen, with her stack to the eaves outside the door. Ash-cold, stack-full." She let it sit. Then, to the fire: "Headman."

She had called him Eyvind across that hearth for sixty winters. Every head in the longhouse turned. Ragna felt it land in her own chest like a stone dropped into a well, because she knew what it cost: an old woman who had wiped Eyvind's nose when he was smaller than Arni had just given him the seat, out loud, and put her pride on the hearth-stone beside the rung bowl so that her grandson would not have to put his there.

*Sixty winters,* Eyvind thought, *and the first time she says it, it is to beg.*

"Twenty days," Ragna said. "He splits the first ten for his mother."

## 3. Quiet and travel

Moss-coming came late to the north road that year, and the snow lay on in the hollows.

Far out, the Polar Reach lay white to the edge of sight, and over it the Nail stood in a green-washed sky where it always stood. Nearer, the treeline gave out in black spruce no taller than a rider, and the thaw had opened the ground between them in brown patches the size of a horse-blanket, moss and nothing else, which was what the old people meant when they called the season what they called it. Nearer again, a Hallenfeld signal post stood on its knoll, mast unlit, woodpile to the eaves. Nearest came Sigga on a gray mare, a chip of night-stone wrapped in hide against her breastbone for her sister's sick child in the Keld camps. It had come off the hearth-stone of the house where their mother died, and it had been warm when she wrapped it. She could not tell any more whether it was still warm or whether it was only her own heat in it, and she had stopped asking, because the answer was no use to her on the road.

She reached the post at the fifth notch of dark. The keeper came out before she knocked. He was a long, stooped man with a Hallenfeld courier's badge gone green on his coat and a face weathered to the color of old harness, and he looked at her mare's legs before he looked at her, which told her he had ridden for the Hallenfeld himself once and buried horses on this road.

"One rider north," he said, and wrote it in a book with a pencil the length of his thumb. "Toll's a round for the stack."

She paid it from the split wood lashed behind her saddle and set it on his pile, which took it without showing it. He had stacked the post in the old courier way, bark up and ends squared, every course chocked, so that a man coming in half-dead at night could pull from the bottom without the whole pile coming down on him. It was the best stack she had seen since the Seat. She stood a moment with her hand on it, the way you rest a hand on a good horse.

Inside, the stove was cold.

The hearth-stone had a skin of frost on it. The keeper sat on his cot in his coat with the book on his knee and his breath going up white, and outside his door stood enough wood to see three houses through the Thin Weeks. Sigga looked from the stove to the old man and back again and felt the anger come up in her first, hot and quick, at the waste of it, and then felt it go out of her just as fast.

"Riders come in froze," he said, when he saw where she was looking. "They need it more."

So that was the shape of it. He had made himself into the stack. Every round he did not burn was a round some rider coming in off the Reach with white cheeks and dead fingers would have, and he had sat in the cold so long keeping it for them that he had forgotten he was a rider too, once, and that the cold did not care which side of the book a man was written on. Ash-cold, stack-full, her mother would have called it. Sigga did not argue with him; there was no arguing with a man who had built himself a reason that good. She went out, took three rounds off the bottom of his stack, and stood the first on his block. Frost had checked the end grain fine as hairs. She set the bit in the widest of the cracks and let the axe's own weight finish what the cold had started. *Kaluut*, went the halves onto the frozen ground. She quartered them, and quartered the quarters, until the pieces were thin enough to catch from a spark, white inside and sharp with resin, and she laid the fire small to large on the frosted stone and lit it from her own flint.

The keeper watched her do it and said nothing at all. When the flame took he put his hands out to it, and his face did something she had not expected, crumpling in on itself around the mouth, and he wiped his nose hard on his sleeve and kept his eyes on the fire.

*Small to large,* he thought, *the way Ingrith laid it, every night for thirty years.*

She left at first light. Behind her the post-stack stood to the eaves under its new snow, every round on it but three.

## 4. A system moment

Cull-week at the Seat, and the horse lines stood with gaps in them where the mares had been.

Orri came into the lower hall with his mittens in his belt. He had crossed the yard bare-handed at thirty below, and his hands were pink. Frost would not sit on his eyebrows any more, and his collar had gone wet where it touched his neck, as though he carried a small summer about with him under the hide-coat and it was leaking. He had drunk snowmelt the whole way up and was thirsty still. Three days of it had frightened him worse than the river had. A man who did not feel the cold in the north was a man the cold was already killing, everybody knew that, and he had lain awake each night waiting for the sleepiness that came before the end, and it had not come, and he had begun, very carefully, to hope.

The dogs under the benches got up as he passed and went to the far wall. The blubber lamps spat at his shoulder, one after another, their flames leaning away from him. Then the benches turned, heads coming round before anybody had decided to look, the way heads come round to a door opened in winter, and Orri felt sixty pairs of eyes settle on him like hands and wanted, with his whole body, to be back on the horse lines with a muck-fork.

Three nights back he had led a culled mare out on the river ice to water her at the hole before the knife, because it seemed a mean thing to kill a horse thirsty, and the ice had gone under them both. The cold had shut his chest like a fist. Then something in the black water had taken hold of all of him at once, from the soles of his feet to the roots of his hair, and held him the way you hold a thing to look at it, and let go. He came up. The mare did not. He had heard her under the ice for a long breath, the hooves, and then not. On the bank, shaking so hard his teeth cut his tongue, he had found the words already standing behind his breastbone in no voice at all: Stage II, Welling. He had read it the way he read a woodpile, the whole count at once, with no letters in it, and then he had put his face in the snow and wept for the mare until he could not breathe.

The coil sat on the Reckoner's table in its brass housing. The Hallenfeld clerk set Orri's bare palm on the plate and turned the crank. As the coil wound up its hum, a light came up out of Orri's skin, faint as breath on glass, the color of river-water under ice, green going to black at the edges; it ran over the backs of his hands and up his wrists and lay along the plate, and the frost on the brass housing went to beads and ran. Sixty people breathed out together, one long white cloud over the benches, and a tape came up out of the slot in Concord type.

> ASSAY · KHARVEN-SEAT · THE LOWER HALL · RE-ASSAY BEFORE WITNESSES
> BEARER: ORRI, THE HORSE LINES. ROLL-NAME HALFSADDLE.
> ON THE ROLL AT MUSTER: STAGE I, MURMURING.
> READ THIS DAY: STAGE II, WELLING. TIER OF STANDING: APPRENTICE.
> GRADE CEILING D. LEVEL CEILING 100.
> CURRENT AT CONTACT: NOT IDENTIFIED AT THIS COIL.

Beside the last line the clerk pencilled his note: *Bearer asks whether the coil also counts his woodpile. Told no. Bearer disappointed.*

Orri could not read a word of it. The Reckoner could. He stood, because a reading before witnesses was stood for, and read it to the hall line by line in a voice dry as a cracked board, and entered it on the roll in front of them with the day and the river.

"Welled," somebody said at the back, trying the Reckoner's word in his own mouth like a new tooth.

Then it went along the benches, quick and loud as sparks up a flue. "Orri off the horse lines." "The river gave him back." "In Cull-week, and it kept the mare." "Apprentice, he said. Apprentice." "Halfsaddle's got a whole one now, Sky take it!" and the laugh that went up after that last one was so big the dogs barked at it.

Old Bodil, who had sat the Waiting for three husbands in the death-house, came off her bench and took his forearm in the grip, her mitten on his bare wrist, and held it longer than a greeting needed. She was reading him the old way, through the arm, and he saw her feel the heat in it and saw her eyes go wide and then wet. "Under the sky, everything balances," she said. Orri thought of the mare under the ice and of this old woman's three husbands, and of what the river had taken in trade for him, and his face broke open, and he stood in the middle of the lower hall with the tape still curling out of the coil behind him and cried like a boy, and nobody on any bench looked away.

Then they put an axe in his hands. A man the coil had read split his first round for the poorest stack in the outer town, and that winter the poorest stack was Bodil's. He went out to it bare-handed.

## 5. A comic beat

In the Thin Weeks the Wetwood twins slept against the one night-stone and fought for it.

Their mother had named them Hulluk and Numra, wet wood and the empty bowl, so the cold would pass over two boys nobody could want. It had passed over them for sixty-one winters. The Accord clerk who came north to count heads for the grain had asked Hulluk, the elder by a breath, what his name meant, been told, and entered the meaning as the family's surname, so the roll at the Seat carried them both as Wetwood. Hulluk thought of it as a promotion. Down the lane they were the Bowl and the Wood, and the Bowl was always first to the pot, and the Wood had been complaining about it since before either of them had teeth.

"Yer turn," said Numra, from the stone.

"I went at the broth."

"Ye went at the *end* of the broth. That's a different broth."

"Same pot."

"Full mouth."

"Short stack."

"Cracked bowl, if we're at it," said Numra comfortably. "Ye've been one since Ma weaned ye. I'm on the stone," he added, with the deep peace of a man who has won a forty-year argument on a technicality, "and the night-stone remembers the fire."

"Then it'll remember me putting ye off it."

They went out together in the end, the way they always did, because neither would let the other freeze alone over something that stupid, and because each of them privately believed the other would get lost between the door and the woodpile without him. They took the Widow, the hand-sledge that complained of every load. "Qatuq take ye," Numra said at the door, which was as near as he came to tender, and Hulluk said "And you, ye great bowl," which was as near as he did.

It was thirty below. *Huqaq*, went the snow under four boots, and the Widow groaned along behind them like a mourner paid by the hour. Hulluk got his arms round a frozen round at the woodpile and straightened with it, grunting, and turned to say something about his back that he had been saving since supper.

The death-house stood a bowshot off at the lane's end. A thread of smoke was going straight up from its roof-hole into the green of the sky.

The round fell out of Hulluk's arms and landed on his foot, and he never made a sound. Every hair under his hat had stood up at once, he could feel them lifting the felt, and the cold that had been outside him all his life was suddenly inside, in his belly, in the hinges of his knees. Beside him Numra had gone the color of old snow. His mouth was open. Numra, for the first time in sixty-one years, had nothing to say.

*Not tonight,* Numra thought, very clearly, the way a man thinks one thing when everything else in him has stopped. *Sky, not with only the two of us out here to see it.*

Nobody heated the death-house. A corpse kept cold the whole winter through would not walk, and five lay in there that winter waiting for the thaw, and every child in the outer town knew why the stone was never warmed. The twins had carried two of those five in themselves, at the start of the Thin Weeks, and laid them on the shelf, and sat the Waiting.

The door stood open a hand's width. The light in the gap was yellow, and it moved, the way firelight moves when someone has just put another round on.

## Texture this bank coins

Everything below was invented for these samples and is marked pending: a style sample is not an archived scene, so each item stands as canon once it is used in one, and may be struck (R79-4). The season words, words, idioms and images can seed Kharven's Inventory fields (R70-45).

**Season words** (R70-16, R70-61)
- **Stack-Count** (pending). The evening in late autumn, at the first frost that will not lift, when the headman walks the outer town with two witnesses and counts every household's woodpile aloud. Nothing is written. Mood: being weighed.
- **Cull-week** (pending). The week a house kills the horses it cannot feed through the winter, after the proverb about saddling. Mood: hard mercy, done early so that it need not be done late.
- **Moss-coming** (pending). The old people's wry word for the spring, built on the Inventory's Gone entry for the grass: "when the grass comes" promises grass, and the thaw brings moss. Mood: a small, sour hope.

**Words** (R70-22; Far-Northern sound rules, R23-11-PHONOTACTICS, R51-20-KHARVEN_WORDS)
- **huqaq** (pending). Sound word. Snow squeaking under boots in the deep cold.
- **kaluut** (pending). Sound word for a split half knocking down onto frozen ground or onto the stack.
- **ridgeman** (pending). A Greymane-trained veteran of the Ridgewall, home in the outer town. His **ridge sword** (pending) is the Greymane-school blade he carried home, long in the grip and used in two hands.
- Not coined here: **the third bowl** is Brida's pending Inventory entry. Her card says the ridge learned it from her, so the first passage gives it to a ridgeman, inline, as the wound read of R70-85.

**Idiom**
- **Ash-cold, stack-full** (pending). Saali of the north road burned nothing she could do without and was found frozen at her hearth with her stack to the eaves. Said of saving that keeps nobody warm. Yrsa tells the story in the second passage; after that it goes bare, as in Sigga's close narration in the third.

**Folk words** (R70-79, R53-14-FOLK_KNOWLEDGE)
- **The river gave him back** (pending). The benches' own phrase for a man who Welled at water, said beside the Reckoner's word, which they borrow.

**Customs** (R70-45, R53-11)
- **Labor-debt for wood** (pending). A day's splitting for every round taken. It is owed to the one robbed and settled when the grass comes.
- **Written labor-debt** (pending). The Seat now wants a hearing's labor-debt entered in Concord, so a Hallenfeld quartermaster sits at the headman's far bench with a ledger nobody else in the room can read.
- **The post toll** (pending). One round for the stack, paid in kind. The keeper of a Hallenfeld signal post also enters every passage in a book, by notch and direction.
- **The courier stack** (pending). Bark up, ends squared, every course chocked. A half-frozen rider pulls from the bottom and the pile stays up.
- **The muster reading** (pending). A levy man is read on the coil at muster and his Stage goes on the Seat's roll.
- **A reading is stood for** (pending). The Seat's Reckoner rises to read a re-assay aloud. Then it goes on the roll with the day and the place of the contact.
- **The first split** (pending). A man the coil has read splits his first round for the poorest stack in the outer town.

**Apparatus and places** (R70-77)
- **The Seat's crank coil** (pending). A coil in a brass housing on the Reckoner's table, with a palm plate; a Hallenfeld clerk turns a crank and it prints a tape in Concord type, with the clerk's pencil in the margin. The tape's layout is the fourth passage's. The crank keeps it off any wire, since none has reached the Seat (The Sky, the Hour and the Year).
- **The lower hall** at the Seat, where re-assays are read (pending).
- **The river under the horse lines** (pending). Its current is not identified, and no Wellspring is claimed for it. The aura it woke in Orri shows river-water green going to black (pending, illustration only).

**Comic names** (R70-128, R70-131)
- **Wetwood** (pending). The roll-name an Accord clerk made by entering what Hulluk means, a talking surname by accident (R70-120), and English in a Common hand, which is its bridge (R70-123).
- **Halfsaddle** (pending). Another clerk's roll-name, good for a joke the day its bearer is ranked.
- **The Bowl and the Wood** (pending). The lane's byname for the twins.
- **The Widow** (pending). A hand-sledge that complains of every load.
- **Hulluk and Numra Wetwood** (pending), given the canon swear-words as protective cheap names, and who play as a double act under R78-8, in a light phonetic north-lane speech.

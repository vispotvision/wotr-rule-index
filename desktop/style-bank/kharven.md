# Style Sample Bank: Kharven

Worked examples of the R70 style law (rules/doc-style-law-2026-10-03.yaml) for a Kharven POV, to read before writing in this culture. The people are illustrations, and none is canon until Isaac adopts it. Kharven tilts West, toward the saga and the soldier: short speech, the count, the blunt body, debts paid in wood and labour. The Eastern strand still leads narration, interiority and structure here as it does for every culture (R70-1, R70-3), so the season word opens the scene, the road runs as kishotenketsu, a fight holds still at its turn, one image carries what nobody names, and a sound word sits in the close POV. LitRPG leads only where a man's Stage moves, and in a kingdom that cannot read, the tape is read aloud. The cadence is short declaratives that stack like a woodpile, with the count doing the work, the habit Bram Greymane's register already keeps, and speech stays short even at a headman's fire (R70-21, R70-111). The dial is plain; R70-27 names a Kharven POV in its own sample. The eye goes first to the body's ledger, hands and breath-plume and weight and scent (the Inventory's Body field), then to the stack, because the north judges a man by what he has put by, and "Did you store enough firewood?" is the kingdom page's first test of honour (R70-44, R70-55). Measures run in bowshots, a pot's boil, a fire's length and the notch (R70-29). Kharven has no honorifics of its own. Level lives in English grammar, and a shift of it is an event the room hears (R70-107); a Kharven POV keeps plain names in narration even in a Moto room (R70-118), the Shieldwarden is addressed by title alone (The Banner Houses of Kharven), and a clansman speaking to the Crown looks at the chest and never the eye (the Inventory's folk beliefs). Comic names grow from the scarcity insults, from protective cheap names and from roll-names an Accord clerk assigned, and never from the recently dead, whose name waits unspoken until it is given on (R70-124, R70-127, R23-11-NAME_AVOIDANCE_WAITING). Each passage carries two recurrence items at least (R49-54-KHARVEN).

## 1. A fight or a working

The nameless day came round that year in hard frost, and the law slept through it.

Ketil Rafnsson came out behind the death-house with his ridge sword bare and his left mitten off. A hand on the grip must feel the grip. The hairs in his nose froze on the first breath and cracked on the second.

Sverri the sealer waited by the woodpile with a hand-axe in his right fist and a seax in his left, the seax laid back along his forearm the way sealers carry a flensing knife onto the ice. Two winters ago, at the Stack-Count, Ketil had called Sverri's stack short before the whole outer town. Today no word held.

"How's your stack, ridgeman."

Ketil took the sword up into Vom Tag. Eleven winters on the Ridgewall had taught him to watch the low blade in a man who carried two.

Sverri closed the measure in three running steps, axe high. Ketil went first, in the Vor, and the Zornhau came down off his right shoulder onto the axe-arm. The hide at Sverri's cuff was frozen stiff as board. The edge skidded. It caught in the axe's beard, and Sverri turned the haft and dragged the blade down into a bind. The seax came in under it for the belly.

Ketil let the bind go and stepped off the line. The seax opened his coat, the felt, and the skin over his lowest rib in one cold line. His bare palm had frozen to the pommel, and the iron kept a strip of it when he shifted his grip.

Far up, the aurora hung green over the Seat. A bowshot down the lane the outer town's smoke stood straight up from every roof, grey into green. At Ketil's shoulder ran the death-house wall, unheated, keeping the winter's dead quiet in their rows. Their two breaths stood between them at thirty below.

Ketil cut the Krumphau.

It came crooked, his hands crossed on the grip, down across the line onto the inside of the wrist that held the seax, a finger above the heel of the hand, through the tendon sheaths and the radial artery. The fingers opened. The seax dropped point first into the drift. Blood came in a pulse, steamed on the snow, and went black on the hide sleeve.

Sverri sat down against the woodpile and looked at his hand.

"Above the elbow," said old Gunna, who had watched it all from the death-house step with her arms folded. "Belt it there and he keeps the arm."

Ketil knew the rest from the ridge. A pot's boil of that, and Sverri would be in the third bowl, where men stopped asking for water. He knelt in the red snow and pulled his belt free. Tomorrow was a named day. Every debt in the outer town would wake with it. He cinched the belt above the sealer's elbow with the hand the iron had skinned.

## 2. Court and talk

Stack-Count came in with the first frost that would not lift, and by dusk every house in the outer town had squared its pile against the wall.

Eyvind the headman had walked the lanes at dark with two witnesses and counted every woodpile aloud, house by house. Nobody wrote it down. By his fire the count lived in thirty mouths at once, and Ragna's stack had come up twenty rounds short of what she had split. She had known it by the shape of the pile before he reached the end.

The boy stood at the hearth beside his grandmother. Arni, fifteen, Yrsa's last. Ragna looked at his hands first. Split knuckles, splinters gone black under the skin, a forearm so thin the hide-coat stood off it. He had been splitting, then, and his own house's stack was still short.

"Twenty rounds," Eyvind said. "From a widow's pile, in the dark."

"Wet wood burns eventually," said Yrsa.

"A liar never dries."

"He told you it was true. Where's your liar?" Yrsa did not wait. "The first bowl goes to the one who cannot fill it themselves. His mother's stack was shorter than Ragna's ever was."

"Then she comes to my fire and says so, and the town splits for her."

Arni lifted his chin. "The sky does not ask whether you are ready."

Nobody answered him. Somebody set a bowl down on the hearth-stone, and it rang, and the ring went on longer than a bowl should.

"That's an old man's saying," Eyvind said. "Grow a beard under it first."

At the far bench the Hallenfeld quartermaster sat with his ledger open, because the Seat wanted labour-debt written now, in the Concord hand nobody else in the room could read. "The Seat would take salt for it," he offered. "A bar passes for a silver mark, more in a hard winter."

"Salt buys at the Seat," said Eyvind. "It doesn't burn. A day's splitting for every round, owed to the widow, settled when the grass comes."

The quartermaster wrote it, and worked out under his breath what twenty days came to at the low day rate, which was ten silver, ten bars that Arni's house had never seen. The room did not care what it came to.

Yrsa took her hand off the boy's arm and spoke to Ragna. "My mother knew a woman on the north road, Saali, who burned nothing she could do without. They found her at her hearth in the Thin Weeks, frozen, with her stack to the eaves outside the door. Ash-cold, stack-full." She let it sit. Then, to the fire: "Headman."

She had called him Eyvind across that hearth for sixty winters. Every head in the longhouse turned.

"Twenty days," Ragna said. "He splits the first ten for his mother."

## 3. Quiet and travel

Moss-coming came late to the north road that year, and the snow lay on in the hollows.

Far out, the Polar Reach lay white to the edge of sight, and over it the Nail stood in a green-washed sky where it always stood. Nearer, the treeline gave out in black spruce no taller than a rider, and the thaw had opened the ground between them in brown patches the size of a horse-blanket, moss and nothing else. Nearer again, a Hallenfeld signal post stood on its knoll, mast unlit, woodpile to the eaves. Nearest came Sigga on a grey mare, a chip of night-stone wrapped in hide against her breastbone for her sister's sick child in the Keld camps.

She reached the post at the fifth notch of dark. The keeper came out before she knocked. He was a long, stooped man with a Hallenfeld courier's badge gone green on his coat, and he looked at her mare's legs before he looked at her.

"One rider north," he said, and wrote it in a book with a pencil the length of his thumb. "Toll's a round for the stack."

She paid it from the split wood lashed behind her saddle and set it on his pile, which took it without showing it. He had stacked the post in the old courier way, bark up and ends squared, every course chocked, so a man coming in half-dead at night could pull from the bottom without the pile coming down on him. It was the best stack she had seen since the Seat.

Inside, the stove was cold.

The hearth-stone had a skin of frost on it. The keeper sat on his cot in his coat with the book on his knee and his breath going up white. Outside stood enough wood to see three houses through the Thin Weeks.

"Riders come in froze," he said, when she looked at the stove. "They need it more."

Ash-cold, stack-full, her mother would have called it. Sigga did not argue with him. She went out, took three rounds off the bottom of his stack, and stood the first on his block. Frost had checked the end grain fine as hairs. She set the bit in the widest of them and let the axe's own weight finish what the cold had started. Kaluut, went the halves onto the frozen ground. She quartered them, and quartered the quarters, until the pieces were thin enough to catch from a spark, white inside and sharp with resin. She laid the fire small to large and lit it from her own flint. The keeper watched her do it and said nothing at all, and after a while he put his hands out to it.

She left at first light. Behind her the post-stack stood to the eaves under its new snow, every round on it but three.

## 4. A system moment

Cull-week at the Seat, and the horse lines stood with gaps in them where the mares had been.

Orri came into the lower hall with his mittens in his belt. He had crossed the yard bare-handed at thirty below, and his hands were pink. Frost would not sit on his eyebrows any more, and his collar had gone wet where it touched his neck. He had drunk snowmelt the whole way up and was thirsty still.

The dogs under the benches got up as he passed and went to the far wall. The blubber lamps spat at his shoulder, one after another. Then the benches turned, heads coming round before anybody had decided to look, the way heads come round to a door opened in winter.

Three nights back he had led a culled mare out on the river ice to water her at the hole before the knife, and the ice had gone under them both. Something in the black water had taken hold of all of him and let go. He came up. The mare did not. On the bank, shaking, he had found the line already standing behind his breastbone in no voice at all: Stage II, Welling. He had read it the way he read a woodpile, the whole count at once, with no letters in it.

The coil sat on the Reckoner's table in its brass housing. The Hallenfeld clerk set Orri's bare palm on the plate and turned the crank. Sixty people breathed out together, one long white cloud over the benches, and a tape came up out of the slot in Concord type.

> ASSAY · KHARVEN-SEAT · THE LOWER HALL · RE-ASSAY BEFORE WITNESSES
> BEARER: ORRI, THE HORSE LINES. ROLL-NAME HALFSADDLE.
> ON THE ROLL AT MUSTER: STAGE I, MURMURING.
> READ THIS DAY: STAGE II, WELLING. TIER OF STANDING: APPRENTICE.
> GRADE CEILING D. LEVEL CEILING 100.
> CURRENT AT CONTACT: NOT IDENTIFIED AT THIS COIL.

Beside the last line the clerk pencilled his note: *Bearer asks whether the coil also counts his woodpile. Told no. Bearer disappointed.*

Orri could not read a word of it. The Reckoner could. He stood, because a reading before witnesses was stood for, and read it to the hall line by line, and entered it on the roll in front of them with the day and the river.

"Welled," somebody said at the back, trying the Reckoner's word in his own mouth.

Then it went along the benches. "Orri off the horse lines." "The river gave him back." "In Cull-week, and it kept the mare." "Apprentice, he said." "Halfsaddle's got a whole one now."

Old Bodil, who had sat the Waiting for three husbands in the death-house, came off her bench and took his forearm in the grip, her mitten on his bare wrist, and held it longer than a greeting needed. "Under the sky, everything balances," she said.

Then they put an axe in his hands. A man the coil had read split his first round for the poorest stack in the outer town, and that winter the poorest stack was Bodil's. He went out to it bare-handed.

## 5. A comic beat

In the Thin Weeks the Wetwood twins slept against the one night-stone and fought for it.

Their mother had named them Hulluk and Numra, wet wood and the empty bowl, so the cold would pass over two boys nobody could want. It had passed over them for sixty-one winters. The Accord clerk who came north to count heads for the grain had asked Hulluk, the elder by a breath, what his name meant, been told, and entered the meaning as the family's surname, so the roll at the Seat carried them both as Wetwood. Hulluk thought of it as a promotion. Down the lane they were the Bowl and the Wood, and the Bowl was always first to the pot.

"Your turn," said Numra, from the stone.

"I went at the broth."

"You went at the end of the broth. That's a different broth."

"Same pot."

"Full mouth."

"Short stack."

"I'm on the stone," said Numra, with the deep peace of a man who has won a forty-year argument on a technicality, "and the night-stone remembers the fire."

"Then it'll remember me putting you off it."

They went out together in the end, the way they always did, because neither would let the other freeze alone over something that stupid. They took the Widow, the hand-sledge that complained of every load. "Qatuq take you," Numra said at the door, which was as near as he came to tender.

It was thirty below. Huqaq, went the snow under four boots, and the Widow groaned behind them. Hulluk got his arms round a frozen round at the woodpile and straightened with it.

The death-house stood a bowshot off at the lane's end. A thread of smoke was going straight up from its roof-hole into the green of the sky.

Numra, for the first time in sixty-one years, had nothing to say.

Nobody heated the death-house. A corpse kept cold the whole winter through would not walk, and five lay in there that winter waiting for the thaw, and every child in the outer town knew why the stone was never warmed.

The door stood open a hand's width. The light in the gap was yellow, and it moved, the way firelight moves when someone has just put another round on.

## Texture this bank coins

Everything below was invented for these samples. All of it is pending, and none of it is canon until Isaac rules; the season words, words, idioms and images can seed Kharven's new Inventory fields (R70-45, R70-59 to R70-65, R70-33).

**Season words** (R70-16, R70-61)
- **Stack-Count** (pending). The evening in late autumn, at the first frost that will not lift, when the headman walks the outer town with two witnesses and counts every household's woodpile aloud. Nothing is written. Mood: being weighed.
- **Cull-week** (pending). The week a house kills the horses it cannot feed through the winter, after the proverb about saddling. Mood: hard mercy, done early so that it need not be done late.
- **Moss-coming** (pending). The old people's wry word for the spring, built on the Inventory's Gone entry for the grass: "when the grass comes" promises grass, and the thaw brings moss. Mood: a small, sour hope.

**Words** (R70-22, R70-33; Far-Northern sound rules, R23-11-PHONOTACTICS, R51-20-KHARVEN_WORDS)
- **huqaq** (pending). Sound word. Snow squeaking under boots in the deep cold.
- **kaluut** (pending). Sound word for a split half knocking down onto frozen ground or onto the stack.
- **ridgeman** (pending). A Greymane-trained veteran of the Ridgewall, home in the outer town. His **ridge sword** (pending) is the Greymane-school blade he carried home, long in the grip and used in two hands.
- Not coined here: **the third bowl** is Brida's pending Inventory entry. Her card says the ridge learned it from her, so the first passage gives it to a ridgeman, inline, as the wound read of R70-85.

**Idiom** (R70-65)
- **Ash-cold, stack-full** (pending). Saali of the north road burned nothing she could do without and was found frozen at her hearth with her stack to the eaves. Said of saving that keeps nobody warm. Yrsa tells the story once, in the second passage; after that it goes bare, as in Sigga's close narration in the third.

**Folk words** (R70-79, R53-14-FOLK_KNOWLEDGE)
- **The river gave him back** (pending). The benches' own phrase for a man who Welled at water, said beside the Reckoner's word, which they borrow.

**Customs** (R70-45, R53-11)
- **Labour-debt for wood** (pending). A day's splitting for every round taken. It is owed to the one robbed and settled when the grass comes.
- **Written labour-debt** (pending). The Seat now wants a hearing's labour-debt entered in Concord, so a Hallenfeld quartermaster sits at the headman's far bench with a ledger nobody else in the room can read.
- **The post toll** (pending). One round for the stack, paid in kind. The keeper of a Hallenfeld signal post also enters every passage in a book, by notch and direction.
- **The courier stack** (pending). Bark up, ends squared, every course chocked. A half-frozen rider pulls from the bottom and the pile stays up.
- **The muster reading** (pending). A levy man is read on the coil at muster and his Stage goes on the Seat's roll.
- **A reading is stood for** (pending). The Seat's Reckoner rises to read a re-assay aloud. Then it goes on the roll with the day and the place of the contact.
- **The first split** (pending). A man the coil has read splits his first round for the poorest stack in the outer town.

**Apparatus and places** (R70-77)
- **The Seat's crank coil** (pending). A coil in a brass housing on the Reckoner's table, with a palm plate; a Hallenfeld clerk turns a crank and it prints a tape in Concord type, with the clerk's pencil in the margin. The tape's layout is the fourth passage's. The crank keeps it off any wire, since none has reached the Seat (The Sky, the Hour and the Year).
- **The lower hall** at the Seat, where re-assays are read (pending).
- **The river under the horse lines** (pending). Its current is not identified, and no Wellspring is claimed for it.

**Comic names** (R70-128, R70-131)

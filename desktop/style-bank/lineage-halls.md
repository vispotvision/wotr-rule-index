# Style Sample Bank: The Lineage Halls

These are worked examples for the Chinese-register lineage halls, to be read before writing in them, under the page law of 2026-10-17 (R79-3 to R79-6) and the prose of *The Weak Grade* (style-bank/references/the-weak-grade-isaac-reference.md). Every hall, person and event here is an illustration; any of it stands as canon once it appears in an archived scene (R79-4). The halls are the houses whose continuity is a record (scholar-physicians, Measurewright families, alchemical houses, trading lineages), and the book is the house (R23-2-CHINESE_STRATUM_SUMMARY; Inner World Naming Amendment §X). What the culture does on the page is make every beat answerable to the record: a killing is entered before the blade is wiped, a re-assay goes into the book line for line off the tape, a refusal is an omission kept true, and people think in entries, gaps and seals because their lives are kept that way. Let that shape what a hall POV notices, admits and misreads, and let each paragraph follow the mind through it, perception into memory, memory into inference, inference into feeling, with no rhythm or count to meet (R79-3). Emotion plays big and is thought through; an NPC may have an italic thought wherever it reveals something; the POV may interpret and draw meaning (R79-4). The halls' speech likes a matched pair, a clause answered by one of like length and grammar, as flavour in the mouths that have it (R70-21). The antique dial is period, for "a bureaucracy with a very long memory" (§X, Phonotactics; R70-27). Wenyan is the learned layer, real and romanised (R70-26); narration does not gloss it, and its meaning comes in on a mouth or on the scene (R70-25). The eye notices the record first (R70-44): a seal, a hand, an entry or its gap, and the generation syllable in a stranger's name, which settles seniority before anyone speaks (§X, Generation character). Address is native and plain: shifu, shibo, shixiong, shijie, qianbei (R70-107). Shifu rides into a disciple's close narration (R70-118), and titles stack at the step (R70-117). Adults go by the courtesy name, and the given name belongs to parents, teachers and the very senior (§X), so a slide to it is a level shift the room hears; translated forms come out only at court, ceremony and insult (R70-108). Fights run as body, path, target, result and cost, with the mechanism on the page, one clear causal line at a working's first display, auras in their own colour, wounds plain and big, and figures only in mouths (R79-6). Each person speaks as their card has them, or, with no card, as their want and work shape them; banter and each character's own humour run wherever they are earned (R79-5, R78-8). Comic names take the four forms R70-125 gives the halls: mocked studio names, insulting posthumous names, cheap child-names and spoken homophone puns, and an English rendering of one rides an in-world bridge, a mouth or a byname carried in English (R70-123). The halls have no Standing Inventory, so they are written from the wiki and the cards (R79-4): the Inner World Naming Amendment, the Lu Sanjing, Qiu Yinzhi and Xu Deming cards, The Night Watch, What Things Cost and FOW's Stage table. The Night Watch gives the coil at the left wrist, the wire and the calibration against the standard. The rest is listed at the end.

## 1. A fight or a working

The Tan godown stood on the river with its loading door open to the night, and Tan Huaishan sat on the hall's chest with the jian across his knees, as his uncle had sat on it through the flood year and his great-grandfather, by the tally book's account, through a siege. The chest held eleven years of draw contracts bought forward under the Tan seal, the district's supply off the Lower Ferry main until a son of his, if he ever had one, would be old enough to be capped. A man in the city wanted them badly enough to send word of the hour he would come for them. That was a courtesy, and Huaishan had spent the evening turning the courtesy over in his hands like a coin of doubtful weight and finding nothing kind underneath it. A man who names his hour has already counted what he will meet.

Pressure came in before he did. The lamp over the tally desk leaned away from the door, its flame lying down sideways in the glass as if a draught had found it, though the night outside was dead calm and the river flat as oil. In the tea chests the rats stopped scratching all at once. The hum in the conduit along the back wall dropped a tone and stayed there, low and sullen, and only then did Huaishan's eye begin to slide off the doorway the way an eye slides off a page in a script it was never taught. He knew the feeling from the manual and had never once had it in his body, and it was worse than the manual said. His bowels went loose. Sweat came out cold along his hairline. Some animal part of him that had never read anything wanted very badly to get up off the chest and let the man have it, and he sat there and listened to that part of himself with a kind of horrified interest, as he might have listened to a stranger in the next room arguing for his death.

A man stood in the door in a good grey coat, with a cane, and the air round him had a colour. It came off his shoulders like heat off a summer road, a dull pewter shimmer, and where it touched the lamplight the light went thin and tired. Flourishing, Huaishan judged, or near enough that the difference would not matter to a corpse: one Stage over his own Ascension, sitting in the godown like a second roof.

"Ascension," the grey man said, looking him over with mild interest. His voice was city-bred and pleasant, the voice of a man who chaired committees. "And a C at the ceiling, by the look of that light. They've left a C to sit on a chest." He turned the cane in his gloved fingers. "I was at B in my second year at Flourishing, boy. You know what a letter is worth. Get up and walk to the river and I'll forget your face."

Huaishan did know what a letter was worth. The manual gave the gap of a single grade a whole page, and the page said, in the hand of Shifu's grandfather, that a man one letter down wins only by seeing the other man's habit before the other man sees his, and that he is usually dead before he has finished looking. He glanced at his own aura where it lay along his forearms, a thin jade green, and thought it looked like pond water beside the other man's metal. He thought of his uncle on this chest with the river coming in under the door. He thought, with a clarity that surprised him, that he would rather be entered dead in his own book than walk to the river and live to be entered as the Tan who stood up.

He stood up, and stepped toward the door.

The cane came apart in the grey man's hands and was a blade, thin and straight, a Western smallsword with a cup guard that took the lamp and threw it.

He came in off a Western stance, front foot pointed, rear heel turned out, and the first ci came at Huaishan's eyes so straight it seemed to arrive without having travelled. Huaishan dropped the jian point-down across his face and let the thrust run off the flat past his left ear, the gua the Second Form teaches before anything else, and the smallsword hissed along his steel and left a cold seam of sparks hanging in the air. He stepped through on the right foot into gong bu, hips turning behind the step, and drew a mo across the grey man's sword wrist with the last hand-span of the blade. The sleeve took it. Under the parted wool the jian bit on rings and skated. There was mail under the good coat, and the grey man smiled at him for finding it, the way a tutor smiles at a slow boy who has finally got the sum.

The second ci came faster and from lower, because the man had seen where Huaishan's eyes went, and Huaishan was late by the width of a breath. The point went in at his hairline above the left brow, scraped along the bone and came out again, and blood sheeted down warm into his left eye and filled it, so that half the godown went red and then went away entirely. He felt it only as a hot slap, as if someone had struck him with a wet rag. Later it would burn like a brand. For now it was a shutter closing on one side of his face, and he shook his head once, hard, and blood flew off his brow in a fan across the chests.

"There," the grey man said. "Now you're fighting with one eye. Walk to the river."

Huaishan wiped nothing. He had watched the two thrusts the way Shifu had made him watch the carp in the hall pool for a whole summer, until he could say where a fish would turn before it turned, and the two thrusts had told him the same thing twice. Each time the smallsword came back from the lunge, the grey man brought his elbow up high and wide to recover it, a fencing master's flourish learned on a polished floor among gentlemen who did not kill each other, and each time, for the length of that flourish, the collar of his coat sat open over the right collarbone, where a mail shirt's neck stopped and a man was only skin. The other man knew more, and was stronger, and was faster, and he had a habit. Huaishan held onto the habit the way a drowning man holds a spar.

The third came at his body, and he gave the bind.

"Liu Shui Bu Fu," he said through his teeth, because Shifu said a form spoken was a form owned, "the Fourth." Flowing Water Does Not Rot. The Fourth Form is for a man already hurt, and it asks him to do the thing every nerve in a hurt body refuses: let the other blade come, keep the arm soft, and go in through the place the other arm has just left. He let the green run. That was the working inside the form, and all there was to it: he kept his aura moving down the arm and out along the steel in one unbroken stream, the way water runs through a mill-race, so that the heavier pewter weight bearing on his blade found nothing standing still to push against and slid along it as a hand slides on wet stone. Green light ran off his knuckles like rain off a roof-edge and pattered away into nothing on the boards. The cost went up his arm in a sour ache to the shoulder, and his breath came shorter, and he felt the reserve under his breastbone go down a notch, like water in a cistern when someone below has opened the tap.

For the length of that bind the godown held still. Outside, the river ran black under the moon, and the far quay's lamps lay on the water in a line that broke and mended and broke again where the current took it. Tar smoked in the caulkers' pot on the quay. The wet tea in the opened chests smelled of cut hay, and of something sweetish under the hay that was going over, and Huaishan smelled all of it very clearly and thought that if he died tonight the last thing he would ever know was that the Ma family had sold his uncle damp tea.

Then the grey man's blade slid off the bind, as running water lets go of anything it was never holding, and the elbow went up high and wide to bring it back, and Huaishan went in under it. He drove off the back foot with his whole weight behind the turning hip, and the jian went in above the coat collar, over the right collarbone where the mail stopped, angled down behind the bone and through the great vessel that runs beneath it.

Blood came out of the grey man in a rope. It leapt from the wound the height of a man's head, bright as new paint, with every beat of his heart, and it spattered the lamp glass and the tally desk and the face of a chest stencilled with the Tan seal, and the pewter light round him went patchy and thin and blew out like a candle in an opened door. He sat down among the chests with a look of enormous surprise. The smallsword rang on the boards and rolled.

The manual gave a wound there less than a cup of tea's time. Huaishan knelt and pressed it anyway with the heel of his hand, hard, down against the first rib behind it, the way the hall's physician had shown the boys on a slaughtered pig, his left eye shut on its own blood and his right full of the other man's, and the blood came up hot through his fingers and would not be stopped by anything a hand could do. He found he was weeping. He did not know when it had started. It came out of him in great ugly heaves, for the man, or for himself, or for the plain obscenity of how fast a whole person went out through a hole the width of a finger, and his hand went on pressing all the while, steady, because that was what the hand had been taught to do.

"Your name," he said, and his voice cracked in half on it. "For the book."

The man laughed once, wetly, and it put a red bubble on his lip. "Write C," he said, "since you're so fond of letters," and gave him no name, and then there was nothing left in him to give.

Huaishan stayed on his knees until the shaking let go of his hands. Then, before he wiped the blade, he took the tally book off the desk and entered the dead in his own hand, the brush wobbling and then steadying on the paper: one man, nameless, of no hall, by the Fourth Form, on the night of the contracts. Shifu said a man who kills and leaves it unentered has killed twice. He read the entry over, and found that it was true and that it did not help at all, and that both of those could stand in the same column. Then he cleaned the jian on his own sleeve and left the grey coat where it lay. The coat was evidence.

## 2. Court and talk

It was the Airing, and the Pei hall's book lay open in the courtyard sun, every volume propped on trestles so the worm would find nothing dark to live in. Three hundred years of the hall's births and cappings and cures and deaths lay turned up to the sky, and the courtyard smelled of old paper warming, a dry sweet smell like bread crust and dust, and of the camphor that came off the bindings when the sun got into them. Pei Wenlan sat at the end of the trestles with the brush and the day-record. She loved the Airing more than any feast. It was the one day the hall let the light look at what it had written, and on every other day of the year she thought of the book as a thing kept in the dark, like a heart.

The Banner House steward had the guest's chair and a cup of tea he had not touched, and beside him stood the man he had brought, announced at the gate as Pei Jie.

Her tongue found the gap before her mind did. Pei, then Jie, and between them, where it ought to have been, nothing: the place where the syllable of her own generation should have sat, as it sat in her name and in her cousins' names and in every name the poem had given her year. Her tongue went to the place the way a tongue goes to a lost tooth and keeps going back. Then the rest of him came in, in the order a Pei daughter is taught to take a stranger. The hall's long jaw first, unmistakable, the jaw on half the portraits in the ancestral room, under a Northern crop that showed the white of his scalp. A Banner coat of undyed wool, heavy and good and cut for a colder country, darned at one cuff by a hand that was no tailor's. A signet on his left hand, the wrong hand for a man raised in a hall, where the seal goes on the right so the brush hand can press it. He stood like a man who had been told to wait at the back of a room and had waited there so long it had become his shape.

She knew who he was then, and her heart went hard and fast against her ribs as if she had come round a corner on a ghost. She had been four when it happened. What she knew of it she knew from the book and from the kitchens, so that she knew the entry to the stroke and the man not at all: a cousin of the Wen generation who had done a thing nobody would name, and had been sent North to be fostered out of the hall, and whose name the Elder had then written out of the poem with his own brush. The kitchens said he had been seventeen. The kitchens said a girl had died. The book said only that he was omitted, which was the cleanest and most terrible thing the book could say of anyone, and she had read that line every Airing of her life and never once thought of it as a person who might come back through the gate in a Northern coat.

The porter gave the Elder all his titles at the step: Elder of the Book, Senior Brother of the Shou generation, first disciple of the late Hall Master. The Elder let every one of them settle in the courtyard before he sat.

"The House asks that this man's son be entered," the steward said. He was a broad grizzled Northerner with frost-scarred cheeks, and he spoke Common the way the North speaks it, every word set down like a stone on a cairn. "In th' book. In the poem, wi' his generation. The House'll carry the hall's bill for a year at trade supply. Five gold the month. Sixty, paid at the counter." He put his big hands flat on his knees. "It's what the House has."

The Elder set his cup down without a sound. He was very old and very small, and his summer robe hung on him like a robe on a peg, and when he spoke it was in the measured pairs of the old chancery, each clause answered by its twin.

"The Banner House is generous, and this unworthy hall is grateful. Yet the masters left us a line older than the House. Ming bu zheng, ze yan bu shun. Where the name is wrong, speech will not run true, and where speech will not run true, nothing done under it will stand. Twenty years ago this hall entered an omission in that book, in my own hand, on a morning like this one, with the volumes open to the sun. The omission is true. A true entry is the one thing in this courtyard that cannot be bought, and the House has come with sixty gold to buy it."

*Sixty,* the Elder thought, and the old sickness of that morning came up in him like water in a well. *Sixty gold, for a line I would have given my eyes to unwrite.*

"Don't saddle a horse you cannot feed," the steward said. "The House can't feed the lad through another winter, Elder. Not the way the House is now. He's eleven, an' he coughs."

A saying answered by a saying was courtesy in a hall. The Elder answered in verse, which was more than courtesy: it was a door opened a crack, and the guest was meant to put a couplet through it.

> Ink that has left the brush does not return to it;
> the hand that let it go may lift the brush again.

The steward looked at the old man for a long moment, honestly, the way a man looks over a horse he has been told is sound. "I don't follow," he said. "I'm sorry for it. I'm a plain man, an' we talk plain up home."

Two copyists at the near trestle bent over their leaves as if the leaves had moved, and one of them, a boy with ink on his nose, made a small strangled noise into his sleeve. Wenlan knew the kitchens would have the whole of it by dark, couplet and all, and that by morning the kitchens would have improved the couplet.

She was not watching the copyists, though. She was watching the Elder's hands, which had gone still on his knees, and with a jolt that went through her whole body she understood what the couplet said and how badly the wrong man had heard it. The ink that has left the brush does not return: the omission stands, and the book never unwrites. The hand that let it go may lift the brush again: the hand that wrote it is still living, and may write a new line under the old. The Elder could not take the omission back and would not. He was offering to enter the son beneath it, and he wanted someone in the courtyard to say so aloud, so that the thing would be asked for and not merely given, because a hall entry made unasked is a gift and a gift can be refused by the dead. The steward had missed it entirely. The fostered man, somewhere at the back of his own body, had not lifted his eyes from the flagstones.

The Elder looked past the steward at him. "Wenjie," he said.

The courtyard heard it. A copyist at the far trestle stopped with a leaf half turned and held it up in the air, forgotten, while the sun shone through the writing on it. It was the given name with the syllable back in it, Wen, her own syllable, said the way an elder kinsman says it across a dinner table, a privilege twenty years unused, and the man in the Banner coat went red to the roots of his cropped hair. His jaw worked. His eyes filled all at once, like a child's, and he did not wipe them, and two tears ran down into the Northern stubble while he stood there and let the whole Pei courtyard watch them go. Wenlan's own eyes stung so fiercely she had to look down at the day-record, and found the brush trembling in her fingers and the date blotted.

"Shibo," the man said. His voice came out rusted, as if it had not been used in that tongue since he was seventeen. "Shibo, I," and he stopped there and swallowed, and the steward put one big hand on his arm and left it.

Wenlan wrote nothing under the date. She could not have written a stroke if the Hall Master had stood over her. Beside her the book lay open to the sun with the omission in it, one clear line in the Elder's hand, and under the line a hand's breadth of clean paper.

## 3. Quiet and travel

Book-Close had come. Far up, the ridge carried its snow to the edge of the sky, white against a hard bright blue that rang like a struck bowl. Below the ridge the district's standpipes came down the slope in their row, each in a cap of white, to the river town and the black water that had not yet frozen. Nearer, the rail ran along the river on its embankment, and nearer still, in a third-class carriage that smelled of wet wool and coal smoke and somebody's oranges, Gu Yongmo sat with the coil case on his knees and watched the standpipes go by and knew the last calibration date of every one. His granddaughter slept against the window, her hair in a child's two tails, years short of the double coil, and her breath fogged a circle on the glass that shrank each time she drew it back. The fare was four silver for the hundred miles. He had paid it from his own purse, because the Guild had stopped paying it in autumn, when it was settled in a short letter under a long seal that this would be his last circuit.

He did not mind the four silver. He minded the letter more than he had let anyone see. Forty-one years he had carried the coil out along the river and back, and the end of it had come through the post folded in three, and the clerk who wrote it had got his generation syllable wrong. He had sat looking at that wrong syllable at the kitchen table for most of an evening. Then he had gone and woken Xinyi, who was six and furious to be woken, and asked her whether she would like to see the river.

The assay house at Lower Ferry kept its standard in a cedar box under the counter: a block of bronze worked long ago to read one value and no other, worn smooth as a river stone, its stamps rubbed blind, honest. The house clerk set the box on the counter for him the way a man sets out the good cups for a guest he likes, and stood back, and did not chatter, which Yongmo had always held to the man's credit. He set the coil beside the bronze and opened the gate, and felt the faint cold of the standard come into the coil and through it into his wrist, a cold like the inside of a well. The wire came over. It settled a hair heavy. He took the trim key from his waistcoat, turned the screw an eighth, and watched the wire come true and stand there trembling, and then stand still. Then wax over the screw-head, his seal pressed into it, the date. Good work in his trade looked like nothing at all: a wire standing where the bronze said it should, and a seal nobody would have cause to break for a year. He had spent his life making nothing happen, carefully, along a hundred miles of river, and somewhere on the train that morning he had caught himself thinking that a man might do a good deal worse.

Outside, a rope was going round. Xinyi had woken on the platform with the cross-eyed rage of a child who has slept on a train, had found the ferryman's children inside a quarter hour, and had taught them a skipping rhyme, and the rhyme was the Gu line's generation poem, thirty characters fixed before the Gu had a roof over their heads. Through the frosted window he could hear them chanting it, badly and joyfully, in the high flat voices of children who did not know what the words meant and did not care. They jumped on Yong, his syllable, and on her father's, and on Xin, hers. The rope came round on the next one, which belonged to nobody yet, and the smallest girl tripped on it and sat down hard in the snow and laughed until she hiccupped, and the rest of them laughed at her laughing, and Yongmo stood at the counter with the trim key in his hand and found that he had to set the key down.

The syllable nobody had yet. He thought about it for longer than he meant to: the child who would be called by it, whom he would not live to see, and that child's own grandfather, who would be Xinyi's son or Xinyi's brother's son or some boy not yet thought of, standing at some counter in some river town calling a child in out of the snow. The poem ran on past all of them. That was what it was for. He had always known it the way a man knows the date, and it had never before made his eyes sting so that the counter swam.

He called Xinyi in before the light went. She came in red-cheeked and snowed to the knees, still chanting under her breath, and he turned the bronze over in its box for her and tipped the box toward the lamp. On the underside, among the scratched marks of calibrators back past anyone's memory, stood his father's mark in the Gu hand, and under it his own, forty-one times, each a little different from the last, the early ones cut deep and proud and the late ones small and sure. Under his, the bare metal.

She looked at it a long time without chanting anything. Then she looked up at him, and he saw her understand it, and saw her face begin to crumple, and he spoke before it could.

"Small," he said. "Under mine. The way I showed you on the slate."

He gave her the scribe. She held it in both hands like something alive, with her tongue out at the corner of her mouth, and cut her mark small under his, a little crooked, and blew the filings off, and then threw both arms round his neck and held on so hard that his spectacles went askew.

## 4. A system moment

The Lin hall's courtyard was full. The street had come in through the gate to watch, since a re-assay was a rite and a rite cost nothing, and the street had brought its children and its dumplings and its opinions. Salt-sellers stood on the well-curb for the height. A tinker had climbed the plum tree and was being told to come down by three people at once.

The coil went on at Shuyao's left wrist, against the pulse, and her teeth began to ache before the Measurewright had the clasp shut: the old cold of contact, behind the back teeth, like biting snow. With it the three burned fingertips of her right hand woke under their linen and throbbed in time with her heart. The still-room had taken that skin nine nights ago, when the reagent would not settle and she had settled it anyway with a working no page had taught her. She had laid her own aura over the flask like a palm laid on a frightened animal's flank, and held the boil down by main force while the glass went too hot to touch, and had gone on touching it, because letting go meant the batch, and the batch was a month of the hall's salts.

The housing ticked. Tape came out of its slot in a narrow white tongue, stamped as it came, and the Measurewright tore it at the brass edge and held it to the light:

> RE-ASSAY OF RECORD · LIN HALL · BEFORE WITNESSES
> BEARER: LIN SHUYAO. PRIOR ENTRY: STAGE II, WELLING.
> STAGE III, ASCENSION. TIER OF STANDING: JOURNEYMAN.
> GRADE CEILING C. LEVEL CEILING 100.
> CATALYST: FIRST TECHNIQUE DEVELOPED INDEPENDENTLY FROM INSTRUCTION.
> *Margin, in the Measurewright's pencil:* Hall states the technique is in no manual. Instrument holds no view on manuals.

She had read the same Stage nine nights ago at the bench, a line standing up behind her breastbone in no voice at all, with her burned fingers in the cold-water basin and the reagent clear as rainwater in its flask. Afterwards she had sat on the still-room floor and laughed and cried at once until her nose ran, alone, with nobody to tell and the whole hall asleep above her. The tape was for everyone else. Knowing that did nothing to stop her heart going like a festival drum while she watched the Measurewright's eyes travel down it.

The Measurewright read it aloud, every line, in the flat voice the Guild taught for it, and the Reckoner's clerk entered it on the roll.

The courtyard took it in waves. "Ascension," said the dumpling woman from the corner, to nobody, and her neighbour said, "At nineteen?" and a Lin cousin from the junior wing said, "First of the Shu generation, the very first," far too loudly, and went scarlet to the ears. "Off no page at all, they're saying," said the tinker in the plum tree, and leaned out so far to say it that he nearly came down after all. Then old Granny Hao of the east wing, who had ground salts for the hall since before it had glass in its windows, and who had once chased Shuyao twice round the well with a pestle for stealing candied ginger, took her pipe out of her mouth and said, "Qing chu yu lan," and put the pipe back, and nobody in the courtyard could think of anything to add.

Shuyao's face went hot, and then her eyes went too. She had promised herself she would not, not with the whole street in the courtyard and the Wei hall watching, and the tears came up anyway, hot and enormous, and she stood there with them running down her face and her burned hand held against her chest, grinning like a fool through all of it. Granny Hao had said it. Granny Hao had never in Shuyao's life said a kind word to anyone she was not also hitting, and she had said that, in front of the street, about her.

Shifu came down the step with the book under his arm. Over the book-room door behind him four characters stood in fresh black, shu er bu zuo. He had them repainted every Airing. He did not look at her yet, and she knew him well enough to know that this meant he could not.

A trading man from the Wei hall was already at her elbow, talking past her to Shifu as though she were a bolt of silk he had come to price. He had a soft clever face going comfortably to fat and an amber bead on his cap. "A Journeyman's retainer runs fifteen to thirty gold the month," he said. "Call it eighteen, and the Wei hall carries her bill from tomorrow." He smiled up at the characters over the door. "Your door says transmit and do not make, Elder. She made. We can use a girl who makes. You, forgive me, have built a house that cannot."

*He is right,* Shifu thought, and the thought tasted of iron. *Three hundred years we have kept what we were given and added nothing, and I have been proud of it, and the first child in a century to make a new thing did it under my roof, at night, alone, with no one to hold the flask for her.*

Shifu did not look at the Wei man. He opened the book on the trestle and entered the re-assay in the hall's hand, line for line off the tape, while the courtyard watched his brush and the Wei man's smile stayed on his face a little too long. Then he turned to the last written page of the manual, wet the brush again, and began to set down what she had done in the still-room as the next form, from the first reagent, so that from today it would be transmitted. Halfway down the page he stopped and held the brush out to her without a word, and the whole street watched the hall's newest Journeyman cross the flagstones with her face still wet to take it.

## 5. A comic beat

It was the Sweeping, and the Pei hall's dead wanted their names read.

Erniu had the broom and Daniu had the lamp, which was the wrong way round, since Daniu could not read and the lamp was for reading. Their mother had buried three sons under good names before them, scholars' names, names with brushes in them. She named the next two for the ox in the yard, so that death would look in at the door, see two oxen, and go on down the lane. It had. Daniu was the size of his name and very nearly the shape of it. Erniu had come second at everything but letters, and at letters he had come first by a distance, which he did not let his brother forget and his brother did not let him enjoy.

"Layman's sick," Daniu said. "Fan Tong says."

"Fan Tong says the rice is cooked."

"Fan Tong." Daniu grinned all over his big face. "Say it quick."

"Rice bucket," said Erniu. "Everybody says it quick. His own mother says it quick. Hold the lamp up, you great lump, I'm reading by smell down here."

The tablets climbed the wall of the dark room in their rows, black lacquer and gold names, the eldest at the top under the smoke of three hundred Sweepings, and the gold woke as the lamp came near and slept again when it passed, so that the dead seemed to open their eyes one by one to look the brothers over and close them again, unimpressed. The room smelled of cold incense and mouse and the vinegar the women had washed the floor with. The old scholar who should have been reading them styled himself the Layman Who Listens to the Rain, and in Erniu's opinion he had listened to it all his life from under the best roof in the hall. He would draw his fee for tonight in bed. The brothers would draw a labourer's day for it, twelve copper each, and the burnt cakes off the offering table, which Daniu had already started on.

"Them's for the dead," Erniu said.

"Dead don't mind burnt," said Daniu, with his mouth full. "Dead don't mind nothing. That's the good of 'em."

Erniu swept and read. He read the way he had learned it, standing behind the Layman since he was tall enough to hold the lamp: the posthumous name first, then the given name, in the reading voice, nose up. Jing the Reverent. Ding the Settled. Then a short one high up in old lacquer, the gold gone nearly black on black, so that he had to stand on the broom-end and squint.

"Miu," said Erniu.

Daniu brightened. "Niu? One of ours?" He swung the lamp up hopefully. "There's an ox on the Pei wall?"

"Miu. Lips shut. Mmm." Erniu put his nose up, the way the Layman put his up, and did the Layman's voice, high and dry and down the nose, which he did very well and had been beaten for twice. "Ming yu shi shuang yue miu. When the name and the man part company, he's Miu. Mistaken." He dropped the voice. "Up on the wall for good, and everybody that sweeps reads it out. Worst thing they can do to you once you're dead, short of leaving you off."

"What'd he do?"

"Wrote something down wrong in the book, probably. That's what gets you, in this house. Lamp."

Daniu held it up, still chewing.

Tamade. The last tablet was new. The lacquer had not had time to darken, and the gold stood out on it sharp as a fresh cut, and it smelled faintly of size and lamp-black. It carried the same short name, Miu, in a clerk's careful hand, and under it, where the given name goes, the Hall Master's, a name nobody in the hall had said aloud since his capping. The Hall Master was in the courtyard behind them, alive, shouting at the cook about the price of pork.

Erniu put out one finger and touched the gold, and it came away tacky. Somebody had made this tonight. Somebody in this house had sat up with size and gold leaf and a fine brush and written the Hall Master dead and mistaken, and had set him on the wall among his ancestors for the Sweeping, when the reader stands before each tablet and reads it aloud, and the reader tonight was Erniu. All the blood went out of his face at once and down into his feet. He laid the broom on the swept floor without a sound, very carefully, as though it might go off.

"Er," Daniu whispered behind him, in a voice gone small as a child's. "Er. Who's got the key to this room?"

His brother's lamp began to shake, and all up the wall the shadows of three hundred years of dead began to shake with it.

## Texture this bank coins

Everything below is illustration, offered toward the halls' first Standing Inventory (R70-45). Each item stands as canon once it appears in an archived scene, and may be struck (R79-4). Where a word or a line is real Chinese, it was supplied rather than coined.

**Fields proposed for the Inventory**

- **Cadence:** the matched pair, as flavour in the mouths that have it (R70-21). **Dial:** period (R70-27).

- **Notices first:** the record. A seal, a hand, an entry or its gap, the generation syllable (R70-44).

- **Touchstones:** the book and the generation syllable.

- **Verse form:** the matched couplet traded at court, where a plain answer to one costs face (R70-110). A couplet offered is a door opened a crack; the guest is meant to put one back through it.

**Season words (R70-61)**

- **the Airing.** Late summer. Every volume of the hall's book goes out on trestles in the courtyard sun, against the worm. Mood: exposure, the private record open to any eye. After the real shaishu, the airing of books.

- **Book-Close.** Year's end, when a hall shuts its book and enters nothing until the year turns. Mood: the last of a thing, held over. After the real year-end sealing of the office seals. (Named so as not to collide with the Moto bank's Ink-freeze or Alchemetrica's canon Sealing.)

- **the Sweeping.** The night the tablet room is swept and the posthumous names are read aloud. Mood: accounts with the dead. After the real grave-sweeping.

**Words and learned tags (supplied, real Chinese; R70-26)**

- Jian moves in a hall POV's narration: ci (thrust), gua (hanging parry), mo (wiping cut); the stance gong bu (bow stance).

- tamade: damn it, the hall commons' swear, close narration and speech (R70-34).

- jushi: lay recluse, the tail of a scholar's studio name.

- fantong: rice bucket, a good-for-nothing. The pun under the name Fan Tong.

- shibo: a master's senior brother, and in a hall the address for an elder kinsman of the teaching line.

- Ming bu zheng, ze yan bu shun: where the name is wrong, speech will not run true (Analects).

- Shu er bu zuo: transmit, and do not make (Analects), painted over a book-room door; its meaning reaches the page in a trader's mouth.

- Qing chu yu lan: blue comes out of the indigo. The pupil past the master; the full line, after Xunzi, adds that the blue is bluer than the plant. Used bare.

- Ming yu shi shuang yue miu: when name and substance part, call it Miu (the old rules for posthumous names).

**Images and units (R70-29)**

- Pressure felt as an eye sliding off a page in a script it was never taught, and a lamp flame lying down sideways in still air.

- The gap in a name where a generation's syllable should sit, found the way a tongue finds a lost tooth.

- The book as a thing kept in the dark, like a heart, and the Airing as the one day the light looks at it.

- A cup of tea's time: a short span. The idiom is real Chinese.

- Good work in a Measurewright's trade: a wire standing where the bronze says it should; a life spent making nothing happen, carefully.

- Under an entry, a hand's breadth of clean paper: room for what may yet be written.

**Workings and auras**

- The Fourth Form of Liu Shui Bu Fu, for a man already hurt: give the bind, keep the arm soft, go in where the other arm has just left. The working inside it keeps the bearer's aura running down the arm and along the steel in one stream, like water through a mill-race, so a heavier aura bearing on the blade finds nothing still to push against and slides off. Its cost is an ache up the arm to the shoulder, a shortened breath and a notch of reserve; its tell is the light running off the knuckles like rain off a roof-edge; it answers pressure on a bind and nothing else.

- Auras in this bank: Tan Huaishan's thin jade green; the grey man's dull pewter, which thins the lamplight it touches and blows out like a candle when he bleeds out.

**Sayings and customs**

- The killer's entry. The dead go in the book in the killer's own hand before the blade is wiped; a man who gives no name is entered nameless, of no hall. Shifu's saying: a man who kills and leaves it unentered has killed twice.

- A form spoken is a form owned: a hall fighter names the form aloud as he gives it.

- A man who names his hour has already counted what he will meet.

- Draw contracts bought forward and kept under a trading hall's seal.

- An Elder's titles are given in full at the step on the Airing: Elder of the Book, a coined title, then Senior Brother of his generation, then first disciple.

- An elder kinsman may say a fostered man's given name with the syllable back in it, as speech, while the book keeps the omission; the omission is never unwritten, and a new line may go under it, asked for rather than given.

- A couplet coined for court. Ink that has left the brush does not return to it; the hand that let it go may lift the brush again.

- An assay house keeps a bronze standard worked to read one value, and calibrators scratch their marks on its underside, a family's marks in one line. (Calibration against the standard is canon, The Night Watch; the bronze and the marks are new.)

- A Measurewright line's child wears two tails until she is old enough for the double coil. (The double coil is canon, Qiu Yinzhi; the two tails are new.)

- Children skip rope to the hall's generation poem.

- A re-assay is held in the courtyard, gate open, and the street comes in.

- A pupil's new working is copied into the manual as the next form, so that it is transmitted from that day, and the master may hand the pupil the brush to finish it.

- Tablets carry the posthumous name above the given name, and both are read in that order at the Sweeping. The reader draws a labourer's day (What Things Cost).

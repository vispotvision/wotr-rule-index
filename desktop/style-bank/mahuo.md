# Style Sample Bank: Mahuo

These are worked examples of the R70 style law (rules/doc-style-law-2026-10-03.yaml) for the Mahuo, to be read before writing in the culture; the people and events are illustrations, not canon, unless Isaac adopts them. They are set in the present, 715 IC, and keep canon's one fact about the valley: the Mahuo house went out of Sum-gol by attrition, and what remains is sixty roofs and a teaching house, with the province run under the Mahuo elders (the Ketsuen page; the Alftian Codex, Volume the First; Aurelian's card). Write the house as a remnant, a table laid for twelve with five at it; han is the ground note. Mahou is the line as an institution and Mahuo the surname a person bears (The Mahuo Family), so a factor prices the Mahou line. No Mahuo Standing Inventory exists yet. Ketsuen is not Korean-stratum: the Yukari lead it, and verify files it under the Japanese school. Its Inventory still governs Sum-gol, the Mahuo province, so the ward-post and "who keeps your posts", the weeping cedar, "on the record", the mallet and the pressure at the temples come from it, with its recurrence minimum of two a scene. The pressure is felt by a visitor and by anyone in his first month home, never by a resident (Ketsuen Inventory, Body; Codex I). The Mahuo layer is built from R37-1-MAHUO_ELEMENT_INVENTORY, R23-2-KOREAN_STRATUM and R23-10-TELLING_APART_KOREAN (a hyphen means Mahuo), the Cozbi, Ara Min, Kwon Mu-jin and Aurelian cards, Kwon Mu-jin's Book of Summons, the Alftian Codex's Sum-gol chapters, Obrenkael, the Zettari-Mahou Accord and the Ketsuen page; every price is quoted from What Things Cost. Cadence (R70-21) follows pansori (R70-5), tempo matched to feeling: long laid clauses at court and in grief, short ones in a fight. A sentence may turn in its last clause, as a sijo's third line does. Under strain or among kin, the valley's speech drops the copula in short assertions, puts the qualifier last and ends on a rising note that does not come down (R52-20, with Aurelian's and Kwon Mu-jin's voice lines), and every Mahuo slips into "we" for himself (R52-23). The antique dial (R70-27) is period in narration, archaic in day-records and verse, plain in the commons' mouths. The eye (R70-44) notices breath first and debt second, after the element inventory's breath and ledger strata, the pause between breaths the Mahuo working runs on, and Cozbi's "blood and debt". Speech (R70-107, R70-118) carries -ssi, -nim, sunbae, eoreusin for a house elder, and the vocative -ya or -a (Ara-ya, in the Codex). A drop into the plain form is an event; "this house" comes out only at ceremony (R70-108). Comic names (R70-125, R70-121) chime through the generation syllable onto a real Korean word, and a sickly child keeps an ugly milk-name until it is safe to stop. The summoner's law is canon (the Book of Summons, the Codex): a Draft-circle asks for one verb, and the summoner is recognised or is not. The dial, cadence and noticing lines are supplied, not sourced.

## 1. A fight or a working

It was the Stubble-Burn, and the terraces above the river road had smoked since first light.

Ye-won walked behind the last cart and counted breath, which the valley taught a child before it taught coin. The ox breathed slow. Gaettong, at its head with the hwando across his shoulders, breathed slower. His mother had named him Dog-dung against the fevers, so that death would pass over a child nobody prized, and it had worked too well for anyone to stop using it. Twelve sacks of rice rode in the carts, the valley's share for the warded track, paid in kind.

At the ford three men rose out of the reeds. The river went jol-jol over the stones.

She held her attention over them as the school taught, a palm over a pot, and nothing rose to meet it from any of the three. They were hollow men, thin at the cheek, one with an old armoury musket bound in wire and two with billhooks, and the one with the musket kept the heel of his free hand at his temple, as a man does who has not been a month in the valley. The whole of the gap was hers. It would not turn a ball.

"Who keeps your posts?" Gaettong asked them in Common.

Nobody answered. A man from warded ground had a carver's name ready on any road, and these had none to give, so they had come down out of the Marches, where nobody kept anything.

"Rice stays," Gaettong said.

Ye-won knelt and poured the salt across the ruts, a Draft-circle in three paces, its four layers laid down and closed. A summoner cannot compel. She had learned it at six and taken it for manners. A summoner asks for one thing, and is recognised, or is not. She asked for Guard, as what she was, a Journeyman on her knees in cart-dung, and the valley's ground took the call and turned it over.

The musket came up. Jenjang.

Smoke lay along the terraces in long grey bands. Below the ford a heron stood with one foot lifted and did not set it down. On the far bank the ward-post stood white on its windward face, harvest straw tied round it.

The pan flashed.

What answered rose out of the ford, shaped to the verb: a wall. The river stood man-high between muzzle and carts, took the ball, and fell back as rain. Heat went through her Shell like a swallowed coal. A headache opened behind her left eye.

Gaettong went through the falling water, and a billhook came down at his head. He met it with a rising makgi that turned the hook off the flat of his blade, stepped inside the arm, and put the point up into the armpit in a short jjireugi, where the axillary artery runs out from under the clavicle. Blood ran from the man's cuff before he knew he was touched. He sat down in the ford.

The other two ran.

The man in the water was breathing quick and shallow now, the third breath, as Sum-gol's healers call it, when a man has lost a third of what he carries. Gaettong knelt beside him and turned his face downhill, so that the breath would go out into the valley and stay there, spread thin with the rest.

Ye-won's nose bled onto the salt. She counted the ox again.

## 2. Court and talk

> DAY-RECORD OF THE HOUSE AT SUM-GOL · KEPT AT THE WALL OF THE LOWER HALL · NOT READ BY THE EOREUSIN
> The Water-Letting, the fourth day. The factor of the Coldharbour Line came up under seal and named fifteen gold the month for Chae-won of this house, Journeyman, the floor of the rate. His reason was in writing.

The table in the lower hall was laid for twelve, as it had been laid every day of Kye-rok's life, and five sat at it. She sat at the wall with the brush, which was the recorder's place. The factor had rubbed his temple since the first bend of the river, as visitors to Sum-gol did and residents forgot to.

"I'll not dress it up," he said. "The Line pays twenty for a Journeyman out of Halcrest. The difference is the name. My principals have read your Accord. They read the first article twice."

The eoreusin let the silence sit, and then answered at length, as the house answered anything that came up the valley under seal.

"This house has heard the Line's figure. This house has taught its art in this valley since the valley had a name, and has let go of more of its own than the Line has hulls, and the name your principals discount was given to us by one man's work, and we did not choose it. Yet this house does not refuse an honest price for an honest reason. Say it on the record, then: that the Mahou line is worth five gold the month less than a Nalūn line. Say it, and this house will consider it."

"Eumsu sawon," said Seol-jin, who was sixteen, and looked round the table to see it land.

"When you have a spring of your own, you may quote it," said the eoreusin, without turning his head, and the boy looked at his bowl.

Then the old man turned in his chair. "Chae-won-a. Come and sit."

It was the plain form, the one for a child at the rice bowl, and the hall heard it go by. The factor did not.

Chae-won stayed by the door. She breathed slow, from low down, a breath a cup could be set on. She answered in the high form, and in verse, which in that hall was a door closed politely.

> Water let down from the terraces does not climb back up the hill,
> and what goes down the river road is paid for at the ford.
> Eoreusin, send me down at twenty, or keep me here for nothing.

The factor looked from face to face, a man who knew a price had been named and could not find it.

A verse was owed a verse. Kye-rok held the brush over the record and waited for the old man's three lines.

"Gyeolja haeji," he said, and then, in Common, for the factor: "The hand that tied the knot unties it. Twenty, or nothing."

> The eoreusin answered her in plain speech. The factor wrote twenty in his book and did not initial it.

## 3. Quiet and travel

It was the Sap-Weep when Ju-won came over the last ridge, and the valley lay open under him in the long light.

The mountain first, eleven miles off. Then the terraces, stepping down both flanks in shelf after flooded shelf, each holding a square of sky. Then the river between its dikes. Then the sixty roofs and the long roof of the school among them, and the orchard behind it, and the gate of the house where he was born, and beside the gate a ward-post, and on the step a child.

He went down by the dikes, stiff from a four-copper bed at the last station. The rice stood knee-high in the water. Women were coming up out of the paddies with their skirts kilted and their calves grey to the knee, and one of them straightened, pressed both fists into the small of her back, and nodded to him as to anyone. The ground had begun to press at his temples at the first bend of the river. It would go on a month. He let it.

The house was a potter's house now. Racks of green bowls stood drying where the brine crocks had stood, and a kiln smoked in the bank behind. The child on the step was perhaps seven, with clay to the elbows.

"Who keeps your posts?" she asked him, very correct.

"The school's carver."

"Halmeoni keeps ours. She lives in the post." She went to the wall, knocked with her clay knuckles, and laid her ear to the boards. "Listen."

He bent and listened. The house hummed, ung-ung, low in the timber.

The post was his own Halmeoni's work. The grain had gone silver, and on the windward face the cuts had weathered to the colour of the wood, and in the third line one stroke landed a hair late, as her third stroke always had. The carvers at Senri's Gate called that an apprentice's fault. The house had called it her hand. The late stroke was worn deeper than the rest, where children had put a finger in it going past, and there was clay in it now.

He told the child nothing. He laid his palm on the cedar of the gate beam, which was weeping, and the sap took his skin and held it.

When he went on the light had gone. The Nail stood over the mountain where it always stood. Around him Shiro lay in every flooded shelf of the terraces at once, a hundred moons, and the frogs had begun in all of them, and somewhere among the sixty roofs a door closed on a child being called in. He walked to the school with his palm held a little away from his coat.

## 4. A system moment

It was the Ward-Lighting, and the school yard was full of lamps and of people who had not come for the lamps. Down at the gate the valley's new carver, out of the Academy that summer, was cutting his first station, and his mallet went on under everything, the third stroke always a hair behind.

The Measurewright had set his coil on a tripod by the well: a brass drum the size of a rice measure, a glass dial, a spool of paper tape and a stamping head that ticked as it warmed. He was an Accord man, still breathing through his mouth after the climb from the station. His fee, in kind, was two sacks of rice.

Eun-sun stood at the front, as she had at everything in that yard for fifty years. Her granddaughter stood at the rail. The girl was fourteen and had carried her milk-name, Soettong-i, since the winter she would not take the breast, and the name had kept her.

"What does it mean?" the Measurewright asked, pencil up.

"Cow-dung," said the roll-keeper, and the Accord man wrote it down.

The girl had read her own line in the spring, behind the breastbone, on the night the top sluice gave and she held the terrace wall with a working nobody had taught her, and she had told nobody but Eun-sun. In Sum-gol a thing was not so until the roll said it. She laid her wrist on the plate. Eun-sun watched her breath, because the valley watched breath before anything. It went in and stopped, high in the chest, and stayed stopped. Her ears went red to the rims. The needle swung and stood, and the head stamped out its tape.

> ASSAY · GUILD OF MEASUREWRIGHTS · SUM-GOL, THE SCHOOL YARD · THE WARD-LIGHTING
> BEARER, AS GIVEN AT THE RAIL: SOETTONG-I, OF THE SCHOOL AT SUM-GOL
> PRIOR ENTRY: STAGE II, WELLING. TIER OF STANDING: APPRENTICE
> READING: STAGE III, ASCENSION. TIER OF STANDING: JOURNEYMAN
> GRADE CEILING C. SUB-STAT CEILING 175. LEVEL CEILING 100
> MOVED. READ BEFORE WITNESSES. TO BE ENTERED
> *Clerk, in the margin:* Bearer's name as given by the yard. The coil read her the same under either.

The Measurewright read it aloud in Common. The roll-keeper read it again in the valley's tongue and entered it on the roll, on the record.

The yard took it in waves. First the breath, all at once. Then the word, back through the lamps: "Third step." "Soettong-i." "The cow-dung girl, a Journeyman." Then old Jeong-bo from the well, who had been a Journeyman forty years: "Harmonics first. A Journeyman who can't draw is a lamp with no oil."

"Tempering," said the Measurewright, "or she'll draw what she can't hold. One seventy-five is a long road for either."

A man in a Halcrest coat was already at the rail with his card. Fifteen gold the month was the floor for a Journeyman, and the school had not held that much coin in a year. Eun-sun did the sum in rice and lost it.

"The name for the roll," said the roll-keeper.

The girl let her breath go. "Sae-bit."

It was the first time anyone had said it aloud in that yard. Eun-sun, who had not wept for two cousins' fevers or for her son's going, sat down on the well-curb in front of the whole valley and wept with her mouth open, aigo, aigo, while the stamping head ticked and went still.

## 5. A comic beat

It was the last week before the Thread-Reading, when Sum-gol settled its quarrels and paid its debts, because the Crow read every thread at midwinter and nobody cared to be read owing.

Jang-dae had been asked to witness, being the one cousin who had never lent the others anything and could see over both their heads. They were the Dae generation of their line, and the generation syllable had been kind to none of them. Ban-dae's father had wanted greatness reflected and got an objection. Jang-dae himself had been meant as a great chapter and was a pole for knocking down persimmons, and was used for it every autumn.

Sun-dae had brought the debt in a basket: seven links of sundae, blood-sausage steamed that morning, which was exactly the kind of thing Sun-dae did.

"One silver mark," said Ban-dae. "Lent at the Ward-Lighting twenty-two years ago, for a coat to court the potter's widow in."

"I got her."

"You lost her. The coat I never saw again. A silver mark is twenty-four copper."

"Seven links is seven meals," said Sun-dae, "and a meal at the station cookshop is four to six copper. Call it five. Thirty-five copper. You're up eleven."

"I object."

"To what?"

"A cookshop meal is broth and bread and whatever was cheap at dawn. This is sausage." Ban-dae lifted the cloth and regarded the links. "I object to the interest."

"There's no interest."

"I object to that as well."

"My mother named me for purity and greatness," Sun-dae told the rafters, "and this valley has eaten me every winter since I could walk. Take the sausage, Ban-dae-ya."

The paper in the door drew in against its lattice, as if someone outside had leaned his breath on it.

The house stopped humming. Frost came up the side of the brine crock in a white line. Under the maru the dog stopped scratching. Then the valley's ground, which none of them had felt since their first month of life, came down on their temples like two thumbs, and sweat stood out on Sun-dae's lip in the cold, and Ban-dae shut his mouth.

Something went by on the river road in the dark, at a walking man's pace. None of them looked.

"Paid, we," Sun-dae said to the door, very low, and the end of it went up and stayed up, unfinished, the way their grandmother had talked when she was frightened.

Ban-dae did not object.

A long while after, the paper eased out on its lattice with a small dry tick. The house took up its hum, ung-ung, low in the wood. Under the maru the dog began to scratch again.

Ban-dae put out one finger and counted the links. "Seven," he said. "Agreed."

In forty years Jang-dae had never heard him say it. "On the record," said Sun-dae, and Jang-dae, the witness, said it after them, and nobody spoke of the road. Ban-dae ate the first link standing, cold, and his hand shook.

## Texture this bank coins

Every item below is pending. None of it is canon until Isaac rules, and each is a candidate for the first Mahuo Standing Inventory (R70-45, R70-59 to R70-65, R70-33). Real Korean words are real; their use here is the coinage.

- **Stubble-Burn.** A Sum-gol season word for the turn after the terrace harvest, when the stubble is burned and the valley's share goes down the road in kind. Its mood is the account called in. Pending.

- **Water-Letting.** Spring, when the sluices are opened and the terraces flood from the top shelf down. Its mood: what goes down the river does not come back up. Pending.

- **Sap-Weep.** High summer, when the old cedar of the school and the houses weeps (the weeping is canon, Codex I; the season word is new). Its mood is the house remembering its dead. Pending.

- **The Ward-Lighting's mood.** The word is canon (Ketsuen Inventory, Time: the autumn equinox, when the Academy's graduates cut their first permanent station); the mood is new: the first cut that has to hold. The lamps in the school yard that night are new. Pending.

- **Words.** Jol-jol, water over stones; ung-ung, the hum of a warded house; aigo, grief said aloud; jenjang, a mild swear in close POV (R70-34); eoreusin, a house elder addressed; maru, the raised wooden floor of a house, with the dog under it; the vocative -ya or -a used between cousins and in council. Eumsu sawon (drinking water, think of its source) and gyeolja haeji (the one who tied the knot unties it) are real hanmun idioms, chosen here for the Words entry (R70-26). Pending.

- **Milk-names.** Gaettong (dog-dung), kept by a grown escort because it worked; Soettong-i (cow-dung), a sickly girl's. The milk-name is put down when the child is confirmed on the roll, and the true name is said aloud there for the first time. Pending.

- **The third breath.** Sum-gol's healers' word for the quick, shallow breath of a man who has lost a third of his blood, the ATLS third class read by breath. It is the Mahuo sister of the Kharven "third bowl". Pending.

- **The breath rite.** A Mahuo killer turns the dying man's face downhill, so his breath goes out into the valley (R70-105). It is built on the grandmothers' breath sentence in the Codex, the breath that goes out of a person into the valley and stays there, spread thin. Pending.

- **The salt circle.** A Draft-circle poured in salt where chalk will not take, on a cart track or in mud; the four layers and the one verb are canon (the Book of Summons), the salt is new. Pending.

- **The greeting as a challenge.** "Who keeps your posts?" is canon; asked of armed strangers, it sorts warded ground from the Marches by whether a carver's name comes back. Pending.

- **The day-record.** Kept at the wall of the lower hall by a recorder, and never read by the eoreusin (R70-67), so the recorder enters what his plain answer cost the elder. In that hall a verse is owed a verse, and a plain answer costs standing (R70-110). Pending.

- **Notices first.** Breath, then debt. **Prized in age:** the record of use worn into a thing, as a late stroke on a post wears deeper where children put a finger in it. **Despised:** a thing nobody put a name to. Pending fields (R70-44, R70-63).

- **Dial and cadence.** Period narration, archaic day-records and verse, plain commons (R70-27); pansori tempo with the sijo's turn in the last clause (R70-21). Supplied, not sourced. Pending.

- **Images.** A breath a cup could be set on; a palm held over a pot, for feeling rank; verse as a door closed politely; a hundred moons in the flooded shelves; door paper drawn in as if someone outside leaned his breath on it. Pending (R70-59).

- **Folk belief.** A potter's child holds that the dead carver of her post lives in it and keeps the house humming. Left uncorrected. Pending.

- **Craft and road.** The valley's sword named in Korean (hwando, makgi, jjireugi) as the escort's school; harvest straw tied round a ford's ward-post; brine crocks at a house's side; paper-latticed doors; a persimmon pole; the Measurewright's fee paid in rice. Pending.

- **People and names.** The Won cohort: Ye-won and Chae-won, summoners at Journeyman, and Ju-won. The Dae cohort, a comic set on the shared syllable: Ban-dae (bandae, objection), Sun-dae (sundae, blood-sausage) and Jang-dae (jangdae, a long pole), with Ban-dae and Sun-dae carded as a double act if Isaac adopts them (R70-113). Kye-rok the recorder, Seol-jin, Eun-sun and her granddaughter Sae-bit, old Jeong-bo, an unnamed eoreusin, an unnamed roll-keeper, the valley's new carver, and the Coldharbour Line, an Accord shipping house. Every given name keeps the element inventory's syllables (R37-1). Pending.

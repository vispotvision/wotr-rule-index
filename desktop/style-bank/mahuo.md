# Style Sample Bank: Mahuo

These are worked examples of the live prose law for the Mahuo, to be read before writing in the culture. They model the 2026-10-17 directions (R79-3 to R79-6) over the Style Law (rules/doc-style-law-2026-10-03.yaml), with The Weak Grade (references/the-weak-grade-isaac-reference.md) as the prose reference; the people and events are illustrations, not canon, unless Isaac adopts them. They are set in the present, 715 IC, and keep canon's one fact about the valley: the Mahuo house went out of Sum-gol by attrition, and what remains is sixty roofs and a teaching house, with the province run under the Mahuo elders (the Ketsuen page; the Alftian Codex, Volume the First; Aurelian's card). Write the house as a remnant, a table laid for twelve with five at it; han is the ground note. Mahou names the house as an institution and Mahuo is the surname a person bears (The Mahuo Family), so a factor prices the Mahou name. No Mahuo Standing Inventory exists yet. Ketsuen is not Korean-stratum: the Yukari lead it, and verify files it under the Japanese school. Its Inventory still covers Sum-gol, the Mahuo province, as reference with no draw order or minimum (R79-3), so the ward-post and "who keeps your posts", the weeping cedar, "on the record", the mallet and the pressure at the temples come from it where a scene wants them. The pressure is felt by a visitor and by anyone in his first month home, never by a resident (Ketsuen Inventory, Body; Codex I). The Mahuo layer is built from R37-1-MAHUO_ELEMENT_INVENTORY, R23-2-KOREAN_STRATUM and R23-10-TELLING_APART_KOREAN (a hyphen means Mahuo), the Cozbi, Ara Min, Kwon Mu-jin and Aurelian cards, Kwon Mu-jin's Book of Summons, the Alftian Codex's Sum-gol chapters, Obrenkael, the Zettari-Mahou Accord and the Ketsuen page; every price is quoted from What Things Cost.

The page carries no rhythm or count rule: paragraphs follow the mind as The Weak Grade shows, perception running into memory, inference and feeling, and a short line stands only where the movement around it earns it (R79-3). Narration sounds like the POV's own reach, close, with the valley as flavor: period in narration, archaic in day-records and verse, plain in the commons' mouths (R79-4). Cadence borrows from pansori (R70-5): clauses laid long at court and in grief, a fight told as bodies doing things, and a sentence may turn in its last clause as a sijo's third line does. Emotion plays anime-big, felt and thought through, and the held image is an occasional device; the POV may interpret and draw meaning; NPCs think in italics wherever it reveals them (R79-4). A fight is body, path, target, result and cost, its working explained in one clear causal line at first display, auras glowing, wounds plain and anime, figures only in mouths (R79-6). Speech follows each person's card (R79-5): under strain or among kin the valley drops the copula in short assertions, puts the qualifier last and ends on a rising note that does not come down (R52-20, with Aurelian's and Kwon Mu-jin's voice lines), and every Mahuo slips into "we" for himself (R52-23); a stranger's accent goes in full phonetic spelling, as heavy as his speech would be. The eye (R70-44) notices breath first and debt second, after the element inventory's breath and ledger strata, the pause between breaths the Mahuo working runs on, and Cozbi's "blood and debt". Speech (R70-107, R70-118) carries -ssi, -nim, sunbae, eoreusin for a house elder, and the vocative -ya or -a (Ara-ya, in the Codex). A drop into the plain form is an event; "this house" comes out only at ceremony (R70-108). Comic names (R70-125, R70-121) chime through the generation syllable onto a real Korean word, and a sickly child keeps an ugly milk-name until it is safe to stop. The summoner's law is canon (the Book of Summons, the Codex): a Draft-circle asks for one verb, and the summoner is recognised or is not. The cadence and noticing lines are supplied, not sourced.

## 1. A fight or a working

It was the Stubble-Burn, and the terraces above the river road had smoked since first light, shelf above shelf of cut stalks smouldering in long gray bands, so that the whole valley behind the carts looked like a hearth somebody had banked and walked away from.

Ye-won walked behind the last cart and counted breath, which the valley taught a child before it taught coin. The ox breathed slow, a great wet bellows that took four of her steps to fill. Gaettong, at its head with the hwando across his shoulders and both wrists hooked over it like a man carrying a yoke, breathed slower than the ox, and in three years of escort she had never once caught him breathing fast, on the climb out of Ko-ri or on the night the cart went into the ditch with him under it. His mother had named him Dog-dung against the fevers, so that death would pass over a child nobody prized, and it had worked too well for anyone to stop using it. He was forty now and broad as a door, and the potters' children still shouted it after him up the river road, and he still turned round and barked at them, which was the only joke Ye-won had ever known him make on purpose and the one he made most. Twelve sacks of rice rode in the carts behind him, the valley's share for the warded track, paid in kind, and every one of them had been counted onto the cart by a widow who would count them again in her head each night until the receipt came back up the road.

At the ford three men rose out of the reeds. The river went jol-jol over the stones behind them, and a heron below the ford lifted one foot and held it there.

She did what the school taught before she had decided to do it, holding her attention out over them the way you hold a palm over a pot to know whether it has come to the boil, and waiting for the heat. Nothing came up to meet it from any of the three. They were hollow men, thin at the cheek and the wrist, one with an old armory musket bound round the stock in wire and two with billhooks gone brown along the edge, and the one with the musket kept the heel of his free hand pressed to his temple, as a man does who has not been a month in the valley and feels the ground leaning on his skull from inside. So they were from outside, and lately, and frightened of a thing they could not see, which made them dangerous in the way a cornered dog is dangerous. The whole gap between her and them was hers, a full Stage and more. *And not one grain of it will turn a ball,* she thought, and her stomach went to water, because she had read them right and the musket did not care.

"Who keeps your posts?" Gaettong asked them in Common, pleasantly, as if they had met on the road to market.

Nobody answered at first. A man from warded ground had a carver's name ready on any road, the way he had his own, and these had none to give. They had come down out of the Marches, then, where nobody kept anything and was proud of it.

"Posts," said the one with the musket, and spat into the ford. His Common came from somewhere far off and flat, dragged through the nose. "Ah'll keep thi rice, an' tha keeps thi 'ead, an' we all on us goes 'ome. That's posts, that is."

Gaettong laughed. It was a big laugh from low in the belly, and the heron went up off the water on it. "Rice stays," he said, and then to Ye-won, in the valley's tongue, without turning his head: "Ye-won-a. Do the wall, we. I do the rest."

She dropped to her knees in the cart-dung and tore the salt bag open with her teeth. Chalk would not take on a wet track, and the school's first lesson for the road had been salt, poured in a ring three paces round, the four layers laid down in the order the Book gives them and closed with the thumb. Her hands shook so badly that the third layer went on wavy, and she closed it anyway. A summoner cannot compel. She had learned that at six and taken it for manners, the way a child takes the bow at the door for manners, and only on the road had she understood that it was the whole of the art and the whole of its danger: a summoner asks for one thing, as what she is, and is recognised or is not, and if she is not, nothing comes, and she is a girl kneeling in salt in front of a gun.

The musket came up to the man's shoulder. *Jenjang,* she thought, and asked.

"Jikyeo juseyo," she said into the ground, in the high form, the form for an elder at the head of a table, because you did not give orders to the valley that had buried your grandmothers. Guard, please.

Light came off her then, the color of paddy water at first light, gray-green and cold, rising off her hands and forearms like steam off a horse, and it ran down into the salt and lit the four rings one after another so that the salt hissed. She felt the call go out of her and down, through the dung and the gravel and the old riverbed under the track, and she felt the valley's ground take it and turn it over slowly, the way the eoreusin turned over a petition he had not yet decided to hear. On the far bank the ward-post stood white on its windward face, harvest straw tied round its waist for the season. Smoke lay along the terraces. The heron was gone and had left a ring on the water.

The pan flashed.

What answered came up out of the ford, shaped to the verb she had given it. The circle carried her one word down into ground so soaked with the valley's Aether that it answered like a living thing, and the ground lent the word the nearest body it had, which was the river, so the ford stood up off its stones in a wall man-high across the track between muzzle and carts, water held upright by the whole weight of the valley pressing on it from below. The ball went into it with a sound like a slap on a wet back and was gone. The wall held a breath longer and fell back as rain. The price came the moment the water stood: heat went through her Shell like a swallowed coal, down the throat and out along the ribs, and a headache split open behind her left eye as if someone had set a chisel there and leaned on it.

Gaettong went through the falling water with a roar that had no words in it. The nearer billhook came down overhead at his skull with the man's whole weight behind it off a bent front knee. Gaettong's back foot drove into the gravel and his hips came round, and the hwando rose off his shoulder in a two-handed makgi that took the hook on the flat of the blade and sent it skating down and off past his head; the hook's point opened his scalp above the ear on the way by, and blood came down over his jaw in a sheet, and he did not notice it. He was already stepping in, left foot past the man's right, inside the arm that still held the hook up and out, and he drove the point upward in a short jjireugi into the hollow of the armpit, where the big artery comes out from under the collarbone to feed the arm. The steel went in a hand deep. He turned his wrists a quarter and drew it out.

The blood came after the blade in a red jet as long as the man's arm, hissing into the falling rain, and spattered the ox's flank all the way down to the hock. The man looked at his own sleeve, which was filling, with an expression of enormous surprise. Then his knees went and he sat down in the ford with the water round his waist, and the river went on round him, jol-jol, and carried him away from himself in a red ribbon toward the place where the heron had been.

The other two ran. Gaettong took one step after them and roared again for the pleasure of it, and then stopped and put a hand to his head and looked at the hand and said "Huh," mildly, as if he had found a coin in the road.

He knelt in the water by the man he had killed. The man was breathing quick and shallow and high in the chest, a dog's breath on a hot day.

"Third breath," Gaettong said. "A third of him gone in the river already. Won't be long, we." He took the man's face in both hands by the jaw, gently, and turned it downhill, so that the last of the breath would go out over the water into the valley and stay there, spread thin with everyone else's, as the grandmothers said a breath did. "There. Go on down. No posts here either, friend. Nobody keeping them for you."

Ye-won had not got up. She knelt in her ring of melted salt with her hands on her thighs and the light going out of her forearms in patches, like a lamp running dry, and watched the stranger's breath go downhill and could not stop watching it. She had asked the river to stand and it had stood, and because it stood the ball had gone into water instead of into her, and because the ball had gone into water Gaettong had gone through it, and now there was a man sitting in the ford breathing out the last of himself, and every link of that chain was hers as surely as if she had held the hwando herself. The valley had recognised her. She had wanted that since she was six. She had not known it would feel like being seen naked by someone who loves you, and she began to cry, hard and ugly with her mouth open, the way she had not cried since she was small, and her nose bled onto the salt and the blood and the tears ran together off her chin.

Gaettong came and squatted in front of her, dripping, his scalp still pouring, and did not tell her to stop.

"Good wall," he said. "Big. Very wet." He wiped the blade along his trouser leg, and only then winced, and touched the cut over his ear, and swore at it fondly in the valley's tongue as if it were a dog that had bitten him. "You count the ox, Ye-won-a. Ox still breathing slow, everything fine."

She counted the ox. It took four of her breaths to fill, and by the second count she was laughing through the tears, and the headache sat behind her eye like a driven nail, and up on the terraces the smoke went on rising.

## 2. Court and talk

> DAY-RECORD OF THE HOUSE AT SUM-GOL · KEPT AT THE WALL OF THE LOWER HALL · NOT READ BY THE EOREUSIN
> The Water-Letting, the fourth day. The factor of the Coldharbour Line came up under seal and named fifteen gold the month for Chae-won of this house, Journeyman, the floor of the rate. His reason was in writing.

The table in the lower hall was laid for twelve, as it had been laid every day of Kye-rok's life, and five sat at it. She knew the empty places by name the way other girls knew the stalls at market: the eoreusin's dead brother at his left hand, the two cousins taken in the coughing winter, the aunt who had gone down the river road to Halcrest at twenty gold the month and written twice. Every morning the bowls went down in front of them and every evening they were taken up full, and the rice went to the dogs, and nobody in the house thought it strange except visitors, who were too polite to ask. Kye-rok sat at the wall with the brush, which was the recorder's place, below the table and outside it, so that whatever was said would be said in front of her and never to her. The factor had rubbed his temple since the first bend of the river, as visitors to Sum-gol did and residents forgot to, and he rubbed it now with two fingers in small circles while he looked at the empty bowls and decided not to see them.

He was a thick, clean man in a good gray coat with the Coldharbour anchor worked on its buttons, and he breathed through his nose, evenly, a man who had sat at many tables and won at most of them. She counted his breaths out of habit and found them steady. She counted the eoreusin's and found them slower. Breath first, her grandmother had taught her, and then debt, and the debt was always in the breath if you waited long enough.

"I'll not dress it up," the factor said. "The Line pays twenty for a Journeyman out of Halcrest, twenty gold the month, found and berthed. For yours it pays fifteen, and the difference is the name. My principals have read your Accord. They read the first article twice."

Kye-rok wrote it. Her brush did not falter on the number, which pleased her, because she had felt it go into her like a splinter. Fifteen was the floor of the rate. It was what the Line paid a Journeyman nobody had heard of from a village nobody could find on a chart.

The eoreusin let the silence sit. He let it sit so long that the factor shifted his weight, and the lamp's flame leaned with the draught from the door and came upright again, and somewhere in the kitchen a pot lid lifted on the steam and fell. Then he answered at length, as the house answered anything that came up the valley under seal, and Kye-rok laid the brush to the paper and took it down as it came, every clause, because what an elder said under seal was owed to the record whole.

"This house has heard the Line's figure. This house has taught its art in this valley since the valley had a name, and has let go of more of its own than the Line has hulls, and the name your principals discount was given to us by one man's work, and we did not choose it, and we have carried it through every market in four quarters with our faces up. Yet this house does not refuse an honest price for an honest reason. Say it on the record, then. Say it in front of the recorder, who will write it in a hand your grandchildren can read: that the Mahou name is worth five gold the month less than a Nalūn one. Say it, and this house will consider it."

The factor opened his mouth and closed it. *Five gold,* he thought, *and the old devil wants it in ink. I've bought whole crews for less trouble.* He looked at Kye-rok's brush as though someone had laid a pistol on the table.

"Eumsu sawon," said Seol-jin, who was sixteen, from the foot of the table, and looked round it to see the old idiom land. Drink the water and remember the spring: it was a thing old men said to young ones, and he had been waiting all morning to turn it on a foreigner.

"When you have a spring of your own, you may quote it," said the eoreusin, without turning his head, and the boy went red to the collar and looked into his bowl as if something had died in it.

Kye-rok's mouth twitched, and she wrote that down too, because it had been said at the table and the record was the record, and she knew Seol-jin would find it there in twenty years and either laugh or burn the page.

Then the old man turned in his chair, all the way round, which he did for nobody. "Chae-won-a," he said. "Come and sit."

It was the plain form, the one for a child at the rice bowl, the one he had not used to Chae-won since she was proven a Journeyman at fifteen, and the hall heard it go by like a dropped cup. Kye-rok's brush stopped. Her chest went tight and hot, because she understood exactly what he had done and what it would cost him. He had taken the bargaining out of the house's mouth and put it back into a family's, in front of a foreigner and a seal, and an eoreusin who spoke plain at a sealed table gave away standing he would never quite get back. He had spent it on her cousin the way a grandfather spends his last coin on sweets at a fair. The factor heard none of it. He heard an old man call a girl to the table, and he smiled at her kindly, as at a niece.

Chae-won stayed by the door. She was twenty, narrow and very straight, in a teaching-house coat with ink on the cuff, and she breathed slow, from low in the belly, a breath a cup could be set on. Kye-rok had seen her breathe like that once before, on the day she knelt at the rail for her reading, and knew it for the breath Chae-won took when she was most afraid. When she answered, she answered in the high form, and in verse, which in that hall was a door closed politely in a guest's face.

> Water let down from the terraces does not climb back up the hill,
> and what goes down the river road is paid for at the ford.
> Eoreusin, send me down at twenty, or keep me here for nothing.

The factor looked from face to face, a man who knew perfectly well that a price had been named somewhere in the room and could not for the life of him find it. Kye-rok watched his breath go short and high for the first time all morning, and thought, with a mean warm pleasure she would never record, *There. That is the debt.*

A verse was owed a verse. That was the oldest law of the lower hall, older than the Accord and older than the seal on the factor's letter. Kye-rok held the brush over the record and waited for the old man's three lines, and the whole table waited with her, Seol-jin with his mouth a little open, and the lamp burned straight up in the still air.

"Gyeolja haeji," the old man said, plainly, to Chae-won, and then in Common, for the factor: "The hand that tied the knot unties it. You heard her. Twenty, or nothing."

To answer a verse in plain speech was to pay for it in the only coin the hall still kept, and every Mahuo at the table knew he had paid twice in one morning. Chae-won's chin came up. Her eyes filled and spilled all at once, both cheeks wet in a breath, and she did not wipe them or take her eyes off the old man, and Kye-rok, who had sworn at fourteen never to weep at the wall, found she could no longer see the paper. She wrote the closing line blind, by the feel of the brush.

> The eoreusin answered her in plain speech. The factor wrote twenty in his book and did not initial it.

## 3. Quiet and travel

It was the Sap-Weep when Ju-won came over the last ridge, and the valley lay open under him in the long light, and he stood on the ridge with his pack still on and for some time could not go on.

The mountain first, eleven miles off, with snow still in its creases in high summer. Then the terraces, stepping down both flanks in shelf after flooded shelf, each holding a square of sky, so that the valley seemed to be made of windows laid flat. Then the river between its dikes, brown and quick. Then the sixty roofs and the long roof of the school among them, and the orchard behind it, and the gate of the house where he was born, and beside the gate a ward-post, and on the step a child he did not know. He had carried the shape of that view for nine years in Halcrest, in the counting-house and in a rented room over a cooper's where the wall smelled of wet oak, and he found now that he had carried it wrong. He had kept the sixty roofs and lost the windows. He had kept the house and forgotten how small it was from up here, how one thumb held out at arm's length covered it and the gate and the post and the child together.

He went down by the dikes, stiff in the hips from a four-copper bed at the last station, a bed with a ridge down its middle like the spine of a fish. The rice stood knee-high in the water and very green. Women were coming up out of the paddies with their skirts kilted and their calves gray to the knee with silt, and one of them straightened and pressed both fists into the small of her back until something in it cracked, and nodded to him as to anyone at all, and he understood with a jolt that she did not know him, and then that he did not know her either, and that nine years was long enough for a girl to grow into a woman whose back cracked. The ground had begun to press at his temples at the first bend of the river, two broad thumbs leaning in from either side. He had forgotten that too. It would go on a month, his grandmother used to say, and then the valley would decide you were back and let go. He let it lean.

The house was a potter's house now. He had known it from the letter, and knowing had been a different thing from seeing. Racks of green bowls stood drying along the south wall where the brine crocks had stood in his mother's time, rows of them unfired and the color of celadon gone wrong, and a little kiln smoked in the bank behind where the pickle store had been. Somebody had planted squash along the drain. Somebody had mended the roof with tiles that did not match. The child on the step was perhaps seven, with clay to the elbows and a smear of it across one eyebrow, and she stood up when she saw a stranger at the gate and wiped her hands on her front, which made them no cleaner.

"Who keeps your posts?" she asked him, very correct, in the form for a grown man of no known rank, the way her mother must have drilled her.

"The school's carver."

She considered this and found it sufficient. "Halmeoni keeps ours. She lives in the post." She went to the wall of the house, knocked twice with her clay knuckles, laid her ear to the boards with enormous seriousness, and looked back at him with one eye. "Listen. You have to be quiet or she won't."

He bent and listened. The house hummed, ung-ung, low in the timber, the sound he had gone to sleep to every night of his childhood and had not heard once in Halcrest, where the walls were only walls. It went in through his ear and his cheekbone and down into his chest, and he had to put a hand flat on the boards to stay standing where he was.

The post was his own Halmeoni's work. He would have known it from the ridge if his eyes had reached that far. The grain had gone silver with weather, and on the windward face the cuts had worn to the color of the wood itself, as a Ketsuen ward does, showing nothing to anyone who was not looking, and in the third row of cuts one stroke landed a hair late, as her third stroke always had, on every post she ever carved down the whole river road. The carvers at Senri's Gate called that an apprentice's fault, and had told her so to her face when she was sixty, and she had laughed at them with every tooth she had. The house had called it her hand. He saw now that the late stroke was worn deeper than the rest, polished dark and smooth, because children had put a finger in it going past, year after year, children who were not his family and had never heard her laugh, and there was fresh clay in the groove, packed in where a small fingertip had been that morning.

Something came loose in him then that had been tied tight since the letter that told him she was dead and he too far off to sit with her. He laughed out loud, a cracked bark that made the child jump, and in the same breath he was weeping, both at once, his forehead against the post and his shoulders going, nine years of it coming up through him like water through a sluice when the boards are pulled. She was in the house. She was in the house, and strangers' children touched her every day, and she kept them warm and humming, and the potters did not know her name, and it did not matter in the least, and he was so glad and so grieved that he could not have said which he was.

The child watched him with her clay hands held away from her sides. "You can touch it," she said generously. "She doesn't mind."

He told the child nothing. There was nothing he could tell her that she did not already know better than he did. He wiped his face on his sleeve and laid his palm instead on the cedar of the gate beam above him, which was weeping in the heat as the old cedar does at Sap-Weep, and the sap took his skin and held it, warm and sticky, a small soft grip that did not want to let go.

When he went on the light had gone. The Nail stood over the mountain where it always stood. Around him Shiro lay in every flooded shelf of the terraces at once, a hundred moons laid down the valley's two flanks, and the frogs had begun in all of them, and somewhere among the sixty roofs a door slid shut on a child being called in to eat. He walked to the school with his palm held a little away from his coat, so as not to wipe it off.

## 4. A system moment

It was the Ward-Lighting, and the school yard was full of lamps and of people who had not come for the lamps. Down at the gate the valley's new carver, out of the Academy that summer and still thin with it, was cutting his first permanent station by lantern, and his mallet went on under everything, tok, tok, tok, the third stroke always a hair behind, so that half the old women in the yard were smiling into their collars and none of them would tell him why.

The Measurewright had set his coil on a tripod by the well: a brass drum the size of a rice measure, a glass dial with a needle like a heron's leg, a spool of paper tape and a stamping head that ticked to itself as it warmed, like a beetle in a wall. He was an Accord man, pink and damp, still breathing through his mouth an hour after the climb from the station, and he kept touching his temple with the end of his pencil as though the valley's ground were a fly he could brush away. His fee, in kind, sat on the cart behind him in two sacks of rice with the school's mark chalked on them, and Eun-sun had counted the cost of those two sacks in suppers since the spring.

She stood at the front, as she had stood at the front of everything in that yard for fifty years: weddings and readings, the funerals of both her cousins in the fever winter, the morning her son went down the river road with a Halcrest contract in his coat and did not look back because she had told him not to. Her knees hurt. She did not sit. Her granddaughter stood at the rail by the coil, fourteen and too tall for her skirt, and Eun-sun watched the back of the girl's neck and thought that the hair there was exactly the color her son's had been at that age, and then made herself stop thinking it.

The girl had carried her milk-name since the winter she would not take the breast. Soettong-i, they had called her, the ugliest name the midwife could think of, so that whatever walked the valley in the cold months looking for children worth taking would look at her and wrinkle its nose and go by. It had gone by. She had come through two fevers and a fall from the persimmon tree, and the name had kept her.

"What does it mean?" the Measurewright asked, pencil up, the bearer's name being required in full.

"Cow-dung," said the roll-keeper, without expression, and the Accord man wrote it down in a careful round hand and underlined it, and somebody at the back of the yard made a noise into a sleeve.

Nobody in the yard knew what Eun-sun knew. In the spring, on the night the top sluice gave in the rain and the whole upper terrace began to slide, the girl had run out barefoot and put her two hands flat on the wall and held it, the whole sodden shelf of mud and rice and water, with a working nobody had ever taught her, until the men came with timbers; and afterwards, shaking on the kitchen floor in a blanket, she had told her grandmother that she had seen her own line come up behind her breastbone, bright as writing on the inside of an eyelid, with a new word in it. She had told nobody else. In Sum-gol a thing was not so until the roll said it, and a girl who said it before the roll did was a girl putting on airs, and Eun-sun had carried the secret since the spring like an egg held in her mouth.

The girl laid her wrist on the brass plate.

Eun-sun watched her breath, because the valley watched breath before anything. It went in and stopped high in the chest and stayed stopped. Her ears went red to the rims, as they had gone red at four when she was caught in the honey. Under the skin of her wrist where it lay on the plate something showed for a moment, a faint wash of color like the green at the heart of a rice shoot, and was gone. *Please,* the girl thought, *let it have been real, let me not have dreamed the wall.* The needle swung, hesitated, swung on, and stood, and the stamping head gathered itself and stamped out its tape in a rattle like hail on a roof.

> ASSAY · GUILD OF MEASUREWRIGHTS · SUM-GOL, THE SCHOOL YARD · THE WARD-LIGHTING
> BEARER, AS GIVEN AT THE RAIL: SOETTONG-I, OF THE SCHOOL AT SUM-GOL
> PRIOR ENTRY: STAGE II, WELLING. TIER OF STANDING: APPRENTICE
> READING: STAGE III, ASCENSION. TIER OF STANDING: JOURNEYMAN
> GRADE CEILING C. SUB-STAT CEILING 175. LEVEL CEILING 100

> *Clerk, in the margin:* Bearer's name as given by the yard. The coil read her the same under either.

The Measurewright read it aloud in Common, peering, and got the Stage name right and the girl's name wrong. The roll-keeper read it again in the valley's tongue, slowly, every line, and entered it on the roll in the school's ink, on the record, and blew on the ink, and only then did the yard believe it.

They took it in waves, as the valley takes anything. First the breath, the whole yard drawing in at once so that the lamp flames leaned toward the well. Then the word, running back through the lamps from mouth to mouth like a spark along a fuse: "Third step." "Soettong-i." "The cow-dung girl, a Journeyman, at fourteen, the cow-dung girl." Then old Jeong-bo from the well, who had been a Journeyman forty years and never climbed a step past it and had opinions about everyone who did, pointing with his pipe: "Harmonics first. Harmonics before anything. A Journeyman who can't draw is a lamp with no oil, girl, you hear me?"

"Tempering," said the Measurewright mildly, without looking up from his tape, "or she'll draw what she can't hold and crack her Shell doing it. One seventy-five is a long road whichever end she starts from."

"Harmonics," said Jeong-bo.

"Tempering," said the Measurewright, and the two of them looked at each other over the girl's head with the deep contentment of men who have found an argument that will last them the night.

Eun-sun barely heard them. A man in a Halcrest coat was already moving through the crowd toward the rail with a card between his fingers, a lean courteous man with a factor's quick eyes, and she knew him for what he was before he had said a word, because she had seen that cut of coat on her son's shoulders. Fifteen gold the month was the floor for a Journeyman, the man would say, and the school had not held fifteen gold in its strongbox in a year. She did the sum in rice, standing there, sacks and suppers and seed for the upper terrace, and lost it halfway, and did it again and lost it again, and understood that no sum she could make would come out to anything that kept the girl in the valley.

"The name for the roll," said the roll-keeper, brush lifted, because a Journeyman was entered under her true name or not at all.

The girl let her breath go at last, long and shaking. "Sae-bit," she said. New light.

It was the first time anyone had said it aloud in that yard. Eun-sun had chosen it herself fourteen years before, sitting up with a feverish baby who would not feed, and had told it to nobody, the midwife or the girl's mother either, and had whispered it into the baby's ear exactly once and then kept it folded away under the ugly name the way you keep a good coat under a sack. She had not wept for two cousins' fevers. She had not wept when her son went down the road. She sat down now on the well-curb in front of the whole valley with her knees gone out from under her, and wept with her mouth wide open like a child, aigo, aigo, aigo, rocking, both hands over her face and the tears running out between her fingers, while her granddaughter knelt in front of her with red ears saying Halmeoni, Halmeoni, and the Halcrest man stood at the rail with his card and waited, and the stamping head ticked once more and went still.

## 5. A comic beat

It was the last week before the Thread-Reading, when Sum-gol settled its quarrels and paid its debts, because the Crow read every thread at midwinter and nobody cared to be read owing. All week the valley had been full of people knocking on doors they had not knocked on in years, with baskets over their arms and their faces set, and of other people opening those doors with faces set to match.

Jang-dae had been asked to witness, being the one cousin who had never lent the others anything and could see over both their heads. They were the Dae generation of their house, and the generation syllable had been kind to none of them. Ban-dae's father had wanted greatness reflected and got an objection, which the boy had grown into as other boys grow into a coat. Sun-dae's mother had wanted purity and greatness and got blood-sausage. Jang-dae himself had been meant as a great chapter, a long and honorable account, and was instead a pole for knocking down persimmons, and every autumn without fail some aunt sent a child to fetch him to the orchard, and every autumn he went, because the persimmons were good and he was, after all, very tall.

Sun-dae had brought the debt in a basket: seven links of sundae, blood-sausage steamed that morning and still warm under its cloth, which was exactly the kind of thing Sun-dae did, and the reason nobody in the family had managed to stay angry with him for forty years.

They sat on the maru with the paper doors shut against the cold and a brazier between them. Under the floor the dog scratched at its fleas with a steady thump, and the house hummed, ung-ung, low in the wood, as a warded house does.

"One silver mark," said Ban-dae, who had come with a written account and laid it on the boards between them like a summons. "Lent at the Ward-Lighting twenty-two years ago, for a coat to court the potter's widow in."

"I got her," said Sun-dae.

"You lost her. And the coat I never saw again. A silver mark is twenty-four copper."

"Seven links is seven meals," said Sun-dae, counting them off on fingers as plump as the sausages, "and a meal at the station cookshop is four to six copper, depending who's cooking and whether the dog got in. Call it five. Thirty-five copper. You're up eleven, cousin. You ought to be paying me."

"I object."

"To what?"

"A cookshop meal is broth and bread and whatever was cheap at dawn." Ban-dae lifted the cloth with two fingers and regarded the links as a magistrate regards a confession he does not believe. "This is sausage."

"It's very good sausage."

"I object to the interest."

"There's no interest."

"I object to that as well."

Jang-dae put his face in his hands. *Forty years of this,* he thought, *and they will bury me between them, and Ban-dae will object to the hole.*

"My mother named me for purity and greatness," Sun-dae told the rafters, arms spread, a man appealing to a higher court, "and this valley has eaten me every winter since I could walk. Do you know what it is, cousin, to hear your own name shouted down the street on a feast day by a man with a cart? Take the sausage, Ban-dae-ya. Take it and choke on it and be paid."

Ban-dae opened his mouth to object, and the paper in the door drew in against its lattice, slowly, as if someone outside had leaned his breath on it.

The house stopped humming. It stopped all at once, and the silence it left was so large that Jang-dae heard the brazier's coals tick as they cooled. Frost came up the side of the brine crock by the door in a white line, crawling and faintly crackling, though the brazier burned an arm's length from it. Under the maru the dog stopped scratching and made no sound at all, which was worse than any sound it could have made. Then the valley's ground, which none of them had felt since their first month of life, came down on their temples like two great thumbs and pressed, and kept pressing, and the edges of Jang-dae's sight went gray, and sweat broke out on Sun-dae's upper lip in the cold and stood there in beads, and Ban-dae shut his mouth so hard his teeth clicked.

Something went by on the river road in the dark, at a walking man's pace. They heard no footsteps. They heard the frogs stop in the nearest terrace, and then in the next, and in the one past that, a silence walking down the valley at the speed of a man. None of them looked toward the door. Jang-dae found that he had taken hold of Sun-dae's sleeve with one hand and Ban-dae's with the other, as he had held them both by the collar when they were small and the river was high, and that neither of them had shaken him off, and that Sun-dae's whole arm was trembling under his fingers like a plucked string. *Don't look,* he thought, *don't let it hear you thinking, oh grandmother, oh all the grandmothers, we're paying, look, we're settling up, we're good men, all three.*

"Paid, we," Sun-dae said to the door, very low, and the end of it went up and stayed up, unfinished, hanging in the cold, the way their grandmother had talked when she was frightened, the way none of them had heard a living voice talk in thirty years.

Ban-dae did not object.

A long while after, the paper eased out on its lattice with a small dry tick, like a held breath let go. The frogs began again in the far terrace and came back up the valley shelf by shelf toward the house. The house took up its hum, ung-ung, low in the wood, and Jang-dae felt it come up through the floor into his seat bones and nearly sobbed at the plain goodness of it. Under the maru the dog began, tentatively, to scratch.

Nobody said anything for some time. Sun-dae wiped his lip with the back of his wrist and looked at the wet on it as though it belonged to someone else.

Ban-dae put out one finger, which was not steady, and counted the links on the cloth, touching each one. "Seven," he said. "Agreed."

In forty years Jang-dae had never heard him say it. Sun-dae stared at him with his mouth open, and then his whole face crumpled and went red and he began to giggle, high and helpless, trying to hold it in and failing, tears squeezing out at the corners of his eyes, and that set Jang-dae off, and the two of them rocked against each other on the maru wheezing like old bellows while Ban-dae looked at them both with great dignity and a white face.

"On the record," Sun-dae managed at last, hiccupping.

"On the record," said Jang-dae, the witness, after him, and wiped his eyes, and nobody spoke of the road.

Ban-dae picked up the first link and ate it standing, cold, in three bites, without objecting to anything, and his hand shook all the way to his mouth.

- **Stubble-Burn.** A Sum-gol season word for the turn after the terrace harvest, when the stubble is burned and the valley's share goes down the road in kind. Its mood is the account called in. Pending.

- **Water-Letting.** Spring, when the sluices are opened and the terraces flood from the top shelf down. Its mood: what goes down the river does not come back up. Pending.

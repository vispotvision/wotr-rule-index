# Style Sample Bank: Moto

Worked examples of the page law as of 2026-10-17 (R79-3 to R79-6) for a Moto room or a Moto head, to be read before writing in one. The people and events are illustrations, and none of them is canon until it is adopted or entered in an archived scene (R79-4). The prose follows The Weak Grade (references/the-weak-grade-isaac-reference.md): perception running on into memory, inference and feeling, paragraphs that follow the mind's movement, and no rhythm, length or count rules (R79-3). A Moto room leans furthest East (R70-1). Narration sounds like the POV's own reach, close by default, with the culture as flavour (R79-4): a Moto head thinks in full forms with no contractions, lets its clauses run side by side on "and," and leaves a silence for the other man to fill, as the Inventory's Sounds and Swears have it and The Moto Narration Register sets out (R51-22, R70-21): cold before smell and sound, and sight after them; the act before its reason; a judgement closed by bounding it; no question asked in narration or in a Moto mouth. A Kharven voice in the same room keeps its own plainer English. The eye notices what a thing is, what it owes and what it will become, then the hand it is written in, since a document in the wrong hand is void, then a man's eyes (R70-44; Inventory, Body; Sōhai, IV). Retainers read little, know the fourteen hands by their shapes, and take a Kharven chore for one of the fourteen labours, and the narration lets that stand. Feeling plays big and is thought through on the page; the held image is used as a device in the quiet beat and nowhere else here (R79-4). An NPC may carry an italic thought where it shows what the POV cannot see (R79-4). The fight runs as body, path, target, result and cost, with the working's mechanism in one clear causal line at its first display, the aura in its colour and sound, wounds plain, figures only in mouths, and no readout until the breakthrough lands (R79-6). Address in speech is Japanese and feudal (R51-21), and a Moto or retainer head carries the honorific into close narration, Shiroma-sama, where a Kharven head says the plain name (R70-118). Daily talk is plain English; the translated forms come out at court, oath and insult, and a drop from full form to a bare order is an event the room hears (R70-107, R70-108). Each speaker talks as his card or his role would, and a Northern rider's or a clanswoman's accent is spelled in full (R79-5). Loanwords (kehai, ma, shiin) stand plain with no appositive gloss (R51-15); a retainer's calling-name is glossed once by a character (R70-125), and cadets get bynames behind their backs. A given name need only sound right for the house, with the element bank as reference (R79-7). No carded Moto appears.

## 1. A fight or a working

It was the Ink-freeze. The register's ink had frozen in its well on the gallery two lines into the morning, and nothing done in the yard would be entered until the thaw, so that whatever happened below the gallery would happen first and be written down later by people who had watched it, each of whom would remember it in his own way. Setsuki Shirogane Moto had lain awake turning that over and had decided, somewhere near the hinge of the day, that it favoured him. A thing seen and not yet entered could still be made correct.

He stood at the centre of the yard in his grey under-robe with the sword at his left hip, and the cold came up through the soles of his boots into the small bones of his feet and stayed there. At first the air smelled of nothing. Then, as he filled his chest to begin, it smelled of horse from the lines behind the stable, of wet felt, of the oil in his own hair, which his mother's woman had combed back and cut level at the nape that morning as if for a wedding. On the gallery the elders of the house sat in the order of their standing, and among them sat Shiroma-sama, his grandmother's sister, who had assayed him at seven and again at ten and had said "correct" on neither occasion. The Thornwall rider had called him poet at table, before a Kharven headman, for the way he had cut a pear, and Setsuki had asked the correction in form within the hour. The rider had laughed and accepted, and the house had set the morning.

Eskil Thornwall stood twelve paces off in mail under a hide-coat, a short-hafted axe hanging easy in his right fist and his left mitten tucked through his belt. He was a big man gone to beef across the shoulders, with a yellow beard frozen white at the lip where his breath had wetted it and a nose broken flat at some old muster, and his eyes rested on Setsuki's chest, where every clansman is taught to look when he faces a Moto.

Setsuki began the declaration as the house teaches it, blood before man. His voice came out higher than he meant, and he hated it, and it steadied on the second clause. "I am Setsuki, of the Shirogane, of the Moto, whose mothers assayed the white shelf for four thousand years, whose grandmother..."

"Ye'll be at it while t'thaw, poet," Eskil said, and came through the maai.

He came low and fast over the frozen ruts with the axe rising off his hip in a swing that had begun before his feet moved, and there was nothing in his face to give it away, because his face stayed turned to Setsuki's breastbone the whole way. Setsuki had been taught all his life that a clansman's lowered eyes were deference, and in the half breath before the axe fell he understood that they were also a shut door with a man behind it. His left arm went up before the blade had cleared the saya, because the body had been drilled to put something between the skull and a falling weight, and the haft came down across the forearm a hand's breadth below the elbow with all of Eskil's bulk behind it, the rider's hips slewing round into the blow and his back foot driving a furrow through the snow. The forearm broke in the middle. Setsuki heard it inside his own head, a wet green crack like a frozen branch, and the hand below the break opened on nothing and the fingers hung.

There was no pain in it yet. There was outrage, enormous and scalding, that came up through his chest into his face until his eyes stung with it. The declaration had not been finished. A Thornwall had walked through a Moto's name in the middle of the saying of it, before the whole gallery, and every elder up there had seen, and the ink was frozen, so none of them could write it down and all of them would carry it. His cheeks burned. He thought, with perfect clarity, that he would rather the axe had taken the arm off at the shoulder.

He finished the draw with the right hand alone, a nukitsuke with no left hand at the saya to throw the scabbard back, and the blade came out dragging and late and graceless while Eskil was already turning the axe for the second cut. It came in from the outside at his head. He got the flat under it more by luck than by teaching; the bit screamed off the shinogi and went past his ear so close that it sheared a lock out of his hair tie, and the hair fell across his eyes, white in the white air. Then the pain arrived all at once, from the elbow to the fingertips, a hot iron wrapped round the bone, and his knees told him plainly to sit down in the snow.

He did not sit. He circled, and the rider let him, and he thought it through while his breath smoked. Every kamae the house had given him, from the first morning in the cold yard when he was five years old and could barely lift the practice blade, was built for two hands. Chudan wanted the left hand at the pommel to steer the point. Jodan wanted it to drive the cut down. Eskil knew that. He had broken the arm first because a man who is taught two-handed is half a man with one, and he had done it during the declaration because he had heard, in some Kharven barracks, over some fire, that a Moto will always finish his name. It had been planned, and planned well, by a man who had never been inside the house and had read it anyway. Setsuki felt the hatred go through him for that, and under the hatred, worse, a respect he would have given the good arm not to feel.

He felt for the rider's Stage and found nothing there but the plain heat of a big man working hard in the cold. A commoner. A commoner with an axe had broken a Moto's arm in front of the gallery and would break the other in a moment, and nothing the house had taught him would stop it.

So he opened the eye.

It came up from behind his breastbone like a lamp lifted into a dark room, and its light went out of him silver. His aura stood off his skin a finger deep, pale as hoarfrost with a cold blue core, the snow round his boots lit faintly from beneath, and in his ears rose the high thin ringing of a wet finger drawn round the rim of a bronze bowl. The eye does what the house's eye has always done: it sees the Aether that settles into a made thing over the years of its handling, and so it sees where old work ends and new work begins, the way a man at a window sees fresh snow lying over old ice.

The yard held still. A hawk-owl on the gatepost had turned its head toward him and kept it turned. On the gallery Shiroma-sama's hand had gone to her beads, where nobody was meant to look, and the Kharven headman by the stair dropped his gaze so fast that his chin struck his chest. "Sky take it," the headman said into his collar, "the lad's lit." Eskil's mail came up in the eye ring by ring, every ring dulled grey with years of the man's sweat and the man's own Aether, and at the left hip, below the hem of the hide-coat, a patch of rings had been rebound with new lacing. That lacing shone like a cut in a dark cloth. It had been on him a week at most. It was stiffer than the old and stood off the rings by the width of a thread, and when he raised the axe it gaped.

Nobody had taught him a thrust for that. The thought came to him in Shiroma-sama's own voice, dry and complete, and he found he did not mind it.

He let the rider come. The axe went up a third time, Eskil's weight rocking onto his front foot and his left hip opening as both arms rose, and Setsuki stepped in under the swing off the right foot and drove tsuki one-handed, the whole of his body behind the hilt from the planted heel through the turning hip to the locked shoulder, the kissaki laid on the new lacing and run along the seam. The point went through the gap above the hip bone and into the thick meat of the flank. He stopped it at a finger's depth by the feel of the steel in his palm, and blood jetted out round the blade in a hot red spout that steamed in the cold and spattered the snow as far as the rider's boots.

The axe hung at the top of its swing. Eskil stood with the steel in him and looked down at the red on the snow as if someone had spilled a thing of his. "Well, bugger me," he said, quietly, nearly admiring, and let the axe drop behind him.

"That was not taught," said Shiroma-sama from the gallery. "Correct."

The word went through Setsuki like heat through a frozen hand, and for one long breath the joy of it was so large that he thought he would weep before the whole house and did not care who saw. He shook the blade out in chiburi, and the blood left it in a single red line across the snow. With no left hand at the scabbard mouth he found it with the point by feel, and the guard settled home with a kachin, and in Kharven that meant the verdict was complete. Then the shaking came up out of the broken arm. Something warm ran from the corner of his left eye down to his lip, iron and salt, and the silver went out of the air round him, and his breastbone ached as if a man were kneeling on it. The joy went flat in his chest like a lamp turned down to a blue bead. The house would tell him afterward that the flatness was the eye's price. At the time it felt like nothing at all, and the nothing frightened him more than the axe had.

It was written behind his breastbone in no hand at all, which in his house would have voided any other document, and it was the most exact thing he had ever been given to read. It said nothing of the arm. He understood, reading it, that the thrust had been the Catalyst, the first thing he had ever made that nobody had handed him, and that the arm was what it had cost, and that the writing did not care what it had cost. The rider had gone down on one knee in the ruts with a mitten clamped to his side and the blood coming dark between the fingers. He kept his eyes on Setsuki's chest, as he had been taught, and Setsuki, flat and tired and strangely grateful to him, could not find anywhere in himself to put the anger.

## 2. Court and talk

Ice-out. Cold in the flags of the lower hall, cold enough to come up through two pairs of stockings, and through the shutters the winter's dead being read out one at a time in the order they had died, by a man with a list doing his best, while the opened ground took them all together. Tadanori had stood at that reading for nineteen springs. His mother was on the list this year, fourth from the end. He had heard her name go by an hour ago, in the cold, said correctly, and he had kept his face, because the household scale was to be assayed when the reading finished and a steward does not stand before the Shirogane with wet eyes.

The scale stood on its trestle by the hearth, its beam lacquered a red so deep that it read black until a flame leaned toward it, and one leaned now as the door opened and the draught came in. Tadanori knew its voice: the soft tock of the beam as it settled, the thin chime of the brass pan taking a weight. He had weighed the ration on it every morning of nineteen winters, rye and pork and salt by the grain, for the retainers and the kitchen and the dog, and he loved it the way a groom loves a horse he has never once had to strike.

Shiroma-sama, of the Shirogane, came down the stair to assay it, as her house did every Ice-out, for two salt bars paid at the door and entered. He took the price first, as a steward does, and then the rest of her. She was small and narrow and very upright, all of it held as if she were carrying a full bowl on her head, in a dark field coat with its fur collar turned up and bright silver at throat and belt, a lynx's face snarling at the clasp. Her hair was white and cut level at the nape. The bones of her face stood close under the skin, and at the brow and the line of the collarbone the metallic birth-sheen of the house caught the lamps like a thin wash of pewter. He did not look at her eyes. Her clerk came behind her with a book under his arm and an ink-stone in a quilted box, a long sallow young man who walked as if the floor might give.

She had the beam off its pin and tried against the Shirogane bronze inside a quarter-hour, with the headman of the outer town brought in from the door to witness for the clans. Then she straightened and spoke in the full register, and the hall went still to hear it.

"This house finds," she said, "that the beam of the Kōkan ration scale, tried before these witnesses against the Shirogane bronze, settles light by the breadth of one grain of rye; that every ration weighed on it since the last Ice-out went out short by that grain; and that a house which feeds its retainers short has made an Empty Bow of the Weighing, since the offence is inaccuracy."

On the last clause she let a little of herself into the room. A cold blue light came up at her edges like the halo round a winter moon. The smoke from the hearth lay down along the floor as if a hand pressed it there. Frost skinned the water on her clerk's ink-stone with a faint tick. The headman by the door kept his eyes on the flags, as his people keep them before the house, and sweated in the cold, and said "Sky take it" into his collar in the voice of a man who had not meant to say it aloud.

Tadanori had it as kehai on the back of his neck, a weight like a wet hand, and his shoulders came up under it. A Guild man would have called it Pressure. The finding hit him harder than the weight did. A grain of rye a ration, every ration, every day of the year: he did the sum without wanting to, the way a steward cannot help doing sums, and it came out at the kitchen boy's thin wrists and the old cook's cough through the Thin Weeks and the retainers who had gone out to the wall on short bread because he had trusted a beam he loved. The shame came up hot from his belly into his face and his ears rang with it, and for a moment the flags seemed to tilt under him toward the hearth, as if the whole hall had been built out of true and he had only now been told. He bowed to the right depth and took the certificate in both hands.

No retainer of the house is taught to read, and Tadanori could not, and he knew the fourteen hands all the same by their shapes, the way a groom knows horses by their gait, from nineteen winters of watching men who could. He looked down at the paper expecting nothing. The strokes on it were long and leaning and closed at the bottom like a shut fist, and they belonged to Maelor, and the Weighing belonged to Urion, and the hope that went through him then was so fierce that his hands shook on the paper and he had to wait until they stopped.

"Shiroma-sama. The Weighing is Urion's labour, and this is written in Maelor's hand. A document in the wrong hand is void."

"A clerk's hand is his house's hand," she said. "The Shirogane answer for their own."

"The old men of this house have a story about that." He left a ma after it, and nobody filled it, and he heard his own heart in it. "When the white shelf went, the Kōkan stood on it and bought a window with themselves. Six lines crossed in it. Nobody sailed back. Window bought, nobody sailed."

He meant it as she would take it: that a debt is owed by the house that ran it up, and that the Shirogane, of all the lines, had crossed in a window the Kōkan paid for and had never been asked to pay it back. He watched it go into her, and he watched her understand that a man who could not read had read her.

"Fold it," Shiroma-sama said to her clerk, and the hall heard her come down to the bare order. The headman looked up from the flags before he remembered himself and looked down again.

*He cannot read a stroke of it, and he is right,* Shiroma thought, *and before the day is out my clerk will pay for his hand, and this man for my face.*

Then, in full form: "I will write it again in Urion's hand by the next hinge of the day, steward, and you will hear it twice." Under the silver cuff her thumb found her beads. The clerk folded the certificate, and before he closed his book he wrote something small in its margin and looked once at Tadanori while he did it, a long flat look, as a man looks at a debt.

When the hall had emptied and the reading outside had reached the last name, Tadanori set two fingers on the beam and felt it settle, slow, as it had since the first thaw. The grain was still owed, by somebody, to the boy and the cook and the men on the wall. He stood there with his fingers on the lacquer until the cold came through them, thinking that his mother had been read in the right order and that he would weigh tomorrow's ration on the Shirogane bronze, and that he would add the grain himself, from his own share, until the house told him to stop.

## 3. Quiet and travel

Cold through the soles on the ridge road, and the inside of the nose freezing on each breath, so that there was no smell at all and the air went into the chest like a blade going into a sheath. Then the whole of it lay open below them. Far off, the forest stood black along the west, and above it the sky had gone the grey of the snow it was waiting to drop. Nearer, the Seat stood on its grey rock with smoke rising over every stack of the outer town, each column leaning the same way like men listening for the same sound. Nearer again the boundary road ran down from the ridge, white, its markers set a chō apart, and far down it, small enough to cover with a thumb, somebody was dragging a sledge up from the tannery.

It was the Walking, Zhaeren's labour, and Tsunemori had done it at dusk every Ink-freeze since he was old enough to carry the salt. He had been the child's moriyaku since the cradle, since the night the child's mother had put the white-headed scrap of it into his arms and said nothing at all, because she was a Moto and had already said it with the hands. Now the child did the righting and Tsunemori carried the salt. Yukiren-sama knocked the rime off each stone with a mitten and leaned on the crooked ones until they stood, and counted them aloud in the order of the doorpost scratches, the small voice carrying a long way in the cold.

Between the markers they kept the ma, and neither of them spoke into it. Tsunemori liked the ma better than any talk. He was old enough to know that the child would not walk this road with him many more winters, because the white hair meant the eye and the eye meant the Shirogane and the assay-room and a life he could carry the salt for but not into, and he walked behind the small back in its too-large coat and filled himself with the sight of it as a man fills himself with bread before a long march.

At the ninth marker the rime was already off the stone. Somebody had knocked it clear that morning, all but one patch on the windward face, where a mitten had pressed and left its print in the frost: the thumb, and the broad blunt rest of the hand. The stone stood true. Yukiren-sama stopped and looked at the print a long while and then at Tsunemori, and the look had the whole of a question in it, and the child did not ask it, because a Moto does not.

The woman with the sledge had come up as far as the eleventh, hauling split rounds from the woodpile behind the tannery. Outer town, Kharven, her hide-coat dark with grease at the cuffs and her breath standing in front of her face like a second, whiter face. She looked at Tsunemori's hands, as her people look at anyone from the house, and at the salt on his belt.

"It were leanin'," she said, of the ninth, and jerked her chin back down the road. "How's yer stack?"

"Full enough." He took the salt bar from his belt, laid it on the marker, broke it into four with the butt of his knife, and held out a quarter.

She looked at it, and he saw her weigh whether to refuse it and decide that a refusal would cost him face in front of the child. "Under the sky, everything balances," she said, and put it inside her mitten, and went on up the road toward the stacks with the sledge runners squealing on the packed snow.

He entered it in his head as Zhaeren's labour, and the greeting as Thalen's, both done by a woman who would have called them fixing a stone and asking after the wood. It moved him more than he could have told anyone. The house had come over the water with its fourteen labours and its register and its scratched doorposts, and it had spent eleven hundred years believing that the labours were the house's to do, and here was a woman with grease on her cuffs doing two of them before supper for nobody, without a name for either, because a stone had been leaning. He thought that if the Archons kept any count at all, she stood higher in it than he did.

The aurora came up green over the Seat. Tsunemori laid his hand on the white head, which the household watches first and feeds best, and said nothing, because the nothing was what the hand meant and he had been taught that it was enough. Under the palm the skull was warm through the hair. He kept his hand there until the last light went off the road, and for all that time he wanted very badly to say something, and did not, and the child leaned back against his hand as if it had heard.

## 4. A system moment

The coil came up from the Guild house in the outer town under a felt hood, carried between two porters like a reliquary. When the coil-clerk drew the hood off in the lower hall the brass caught every lamp at once, and the lacquered walls behind it went darker for the comparison, as a room goes darker when a lit window opens on snow.

Shiroma read the instrument first, as a Shirogane reads anything that claims to weigh. The calibration seal on the housing was whole, the wax unbroken and the Guild's mark sharp in it. The Reading Oil stood clear in its sight-glass, without the faint milky bloom of a spoiled batch. The tape waited on its spool, white as a new register page and a great deal cheaper. She had sent back two coils in her life for oil that bloomed, and both times the Guild had sworn the batch was sound, and both times she had been correct.

Then she read the boy.

Setsuki stood at the rail in his court robe with the left sleeve slit and pinned over the splints. Eleven days had taken the swelling down, and the bruise had gone from black to the yellow of old lacquer at the wrist. He carried the arm across his body like a thing lent him by someone strict, and he held his chin a little too high, and she knew that chin. His grandmother had held hers exactly so at fourteen when she had been told she would not be sent to the white shelf's successors, and had been sent anyway, and had died there, correct to the last line of her last assay. Shiroma had felt it at the door, the moment the boy came in: he stood heavier by a Stage than he had stood in the yard, the Crystal in his chest settled lower and denser, the way a full cask stands differently from an empty one. Pride went through her so hard and so hot that she had to put her hand on the rail. Feeling is not entering. The house had witnesses for that, ranged along the wall in the order of their standing, and she would not shame him before them by being early.

The clerk set the boy's right palm to the contact plate. The draw-hum in the housing climbed half a tone as the oil took his Aether and held there, and nobody spoke into it. Then the type-hammers began to strike, a dry chatter like hail on a shutter. The tape came out of the slot in short jerks, and the clerk tore it at the toothed bar and handed it to the Reckoner, who read it to the hall in the Concord manner, every word given the same weight.

> PUBLIC RE-ASSAY · LOWER HALL, KHARVEN-SEAT · BEFORE WITNESSES
> SUBJECT: SETSUKI SHIROGANE MOTO. PRESENTED BY HIS LINE.
> PRIOR ENTRY: STAGE II, WELLING. TIER OF STANDING: APPRENTICE.
> READING: STAGE III, ASCENSION. TIER OF STANDING: JOURNEYMAN.
> GRADE CEILING C. LEVEL CEILING 175.
> CATALYST: OUTSIDE THE COIL'S READING. REFER TO WITNESS.
> In the clerk's pencil, beside it: *Arm not assayed. Coil does not do arms.*

The Reckoner entered it on the roll in the Concord hand and blotted it.

"Enter it in Urion's hand also," Shiroma said.

"The roll takes one hand, my lady."

"Then the roll takes it in one hand, and the house takes it in Urion's, and both readings are correct." She took the brush from her own case and wrote beneath the pencil, slowly, in Urion's hand, which the Shirogane had closed their assays in since the white shelf: meijitsu itchi. The ink went on wet and black and stayed wet. The Ink-freeze was over, and she found that she was glad of it in a way that had nothing to do with ink.

Setsuki did not look at the tape. He looked at the wall a little above the Reckoner's head, and his jaw worked once.

*I read it already,* he thought. *In the yard, in the snow, with the arm broken, and nobody wrote it down.*

She saw the jaw work and knew roughly what was behind it, because she had been fourteen in that hall herself, and she let him have it. A thing read in the Crystal belongs to the man who reads it. A thing read in the hall belongs to the house. He would learn that both were his, and that the second was heavier, and that it was the second the house would hold him to.

"Correct," said Shiroma, and held the paper to the brazier until the strokes were dry.

## 5. A comic beat

Bead-cold. The beads were too cold to hold bare until the stove had been lit an hour, and on the third such morning Ukkari carried Shiroma-sama her water before her beads.

Chakkari knew it because the beads were not on their cushion by the stair. He knew the rest by looking at Ukkari, who was weighing out the morning rye with tremendous care and wearing the face he always wore at such times, the open, untroubled face of a man with nothing whatever on his mind, which Chakkari had learned over ten winters to fear more than fire.

The old cook had named the pair of them in their first winter at the Seat and glossed it once, for the whole kitchen, banging her ladle on the pot for silence. "Ukkari," she said, "is the one as puts the salt in the tea. Chakkari is the one as sells the tea after and keeps the salt." Both names had stuck. In that house the offence was inaccuracy, and the names were accurate.

"The beads," Chakkari said.

"On the tray, with the water."

"The tray came down with a dry cup on it and nothing else."

"Then Shiroma-sama has them," Ukkari said to the rye. Chakkari, who had seen her bare hands on the stair rail at first light with nothing in them, felt his stomach drop through the flags. He began to search the kitchen by method, in the order of the labours: the scale, the woodpile, the knife-block, the scraps for the dog. Ukkari searched behind him with great goodwill and put everything back in the wrong place, so that the knife-block ended up in the woodpile and the dog ended up with the knives and had to be talked out of them. The kitchen boy came in for Ginbae's rye.

"The silver fly wants feeding, does he," the cook said, since the young lord with the splinted arm had stood at the assay-room door since dawn like a fly at a window, and nobody answered the boy.

*She'll never know,* Ukkari thought serenely, stirring the dog's scraps with a ladle. *Near enough is near enough.*

Then Chakkari looked at the scale properly. The kitchen's bronze weight had gone down to the lower hall with the scale at Ice-out and was being held there, entered, until the finding was answered; only the scale had come back up. In the far pan, under a fold of cloth, lay a string of black beads worn smooth by a dead woman's thumb.

He looked at them for a long moment while the whole of his life went past behind his eyes: every bowl of dripping he had sold the outer town at a thumb of salt the bowl, every short measure, every time he had kept the salt.

"They weigh what the bronze weighed," Ukkari said helpfully. "Near enough."

The third stair creaked. The kitchen went shiin all at once, the dog included, the dog most of all, flattening itself under the table with its ears gone back like a pair of folded hands.

Shiroma-sama stood in the doorway without her beads. The ring-lines stood clear in her irises, three of them, fine as the grain in good lacquer, and the outer ring flared once, cold blue, as she looked from the boy to Ukkari to Chakkari, and Chakkari felt it go over him like a draught under a door and knew past any doubt that it had found the dripping. The folk said the beads were what held the eye shut. He had always half believed it and now believed it entirely, and his knees, which had carried him through every winter of the house, informed him that they would not be carrying him any further today.

She crossed to the scale without a sound and lifted the beads from the pan. The beam swung up empty with a tock. She weighed the string a moment in her palm, as her blood weighs anything, her face showing nothing at all, and the whole kitchen watched the palm.

"Lighter than the bronze," she said, "by a grain of rye."

Ukkari beamed as if he had been praised. Chakkari went down onto one knee on the stone before he knew he had decided to, and stayed there, and heard behind him the dog's tail begin, very cautiously, to thump.

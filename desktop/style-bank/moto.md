# Style Sample Bank: Moto

Worked examples of the R70 style law for a Moto room or a Moto head, to be read before writing in one. The people and events are illustrations, not canon, unless Isaac adopts them. A Moto room leans furthest East (R70-1). Cadence (R70-21): the house speaks and thinks in full forms with no contractions, lays its clauses side by side on "and," cuts a long period with a one-word verdict, and leaves a silence for the other man to fill, as the Inventory's Sounds and Swears have it and The Moto Narration Register sets out (R51-22; approved to publish 2026-09-26, on the wiki): cold first, then smell and sound, sight last; the act before its reason; everything counted; no question asked in narration or in a Moto mouth; a judgement closed by bounding it. A Kharven head in the same room keeps its own short beat. The dial is archaic (R70-27, whose own sample is a Moto-court steward; R51-01), carried by sentence shape and the old register more than by old words, and the Inventory has no dial field yet. The eye notices first what a thing is, owes and will become, then the hand it is written in, since a document in the wrong hand is void, then a man's eyes (R70-44; Inventory, Body; Sōhai, IV). Retainers read little (Inventory, Literacy). A retainer knows the fourteen hands by their shapes and never by their words. It reads a Kharven chore as one of the fourteen labours. The narration lets that stand. Its light is lacquer in the dark (R70-66, R34-1). Address in speech is Japanese and feudal (R51-21), and a Moto or retainer head carries the honorific into close narration, Shiroma-sama, where a Kharven head says the plain name (R70-118). Daily talk is plain English. The translated forms come out at court, oath and insult (R70-108), and a drop from full form to a bare order is an event the room hears (R70-107). Loanwords (kehai, ma, shiin) stand plain with no appositive gloss (R51-15, R4-13); the one gloss the law asks for is a retainer's calling-name, given once by a character (R70-125), and cadets get bynames behind their backs. Given names stay on the element bank (R40-1). No carded Moto appears. Every passage carries two of the Inventory's recurrence items at least.

## 1. A fight or a working

It was the Ink-freeze. The register's ink had frozen in its well on the gallery two lines into the morning, and nothing done in the yard would be entered until the thaw.

Setsuki Shirogane Moto stood in the snow below and asked the correction in form. The Thornwall rider had called him poet.

He began the declaration as the line teaches it, blood before man. "I am Setsuki, of the Shirogane, of the Moto, whose mothers assayed the white shelf, whose grandmother..."

Eskil Thornwall came through the maai.

He came low over the ruts with the short axe rising, looking at Setsuki's chest the whole way, as every clansman was taught to look at a Moto, so there was nothing in his face to read. The boy's left arm went up before the blade had cleared the saya. The haft came down across it. The ulna broke at the middle of its shaft, the break a man takes when he puts a forearm against a club, and the hand below it opened on nothing.

He finished the draw with the right hand alone, a nukitsuke with no left hand on the saya to throw it back, and it was ugly. The axe came again and glanced off the shinogi past his ear. Every kamae the house had given him, from the first morning, was built for two hands.

He felt for the rider's Stage and found none. A commoner with an axe had broken a Moto's arm in front of the gallery. Setsuki opened the eye.

The yard held still. A hawk-owl on the gatepost had turned its head and kept it turned. On the gallery Shiroma-sama's hand had gone to her beads, where nobody was meant to look. The rider's mail came up in the eye ring by ring. At the left hip it had been rebound, and the new lacing was stiffer than the old and stood off the rings by the width of a thread.

Nobody had taught him a thrust for that.

He came back on the backswing with tsuki, one hand, the kissaki laid on the lacing and driven along the seam. The point went through above the hip bone into the muscle of the flank. He stopped it at a finger's depth.

The rider stood still with the steel in him and looked at the snow.

"That was not taught," said Shiroma-sama from the gallery. "Correct."

He shook the blade out in chiburi, and the blood left it in one red line across the snow. With no left hand at the scabbard mouth, he found it with the point by feel. The guard settled home with a kachin, and in Kharven that meant the verdict was complete. Then the shaking came up out of the broken arm. Something warm ran from the corner of his left eye down to his lip, iron and salt.

## 2. Court and talk

Ice-out. Cold in the flags, and through the shutters the winter's dead read out one at a time, in the order they had died, while the opened ground took them together.

The household scale stood on its trestle, its beam lacquered a red so deep that it read black until a flame leaned toward it. Tadanori had kept that scale for nineteen winters. He knew its voice.

Shiroma-sama, of the Shirogane, had come down to assay it, as her line did every Ice-out, for two salt bars paid at the door and entered. Bright silver at throat and belt. A lynx's face at the clasp.

"This house finds," she said, "that the beam of the Kōkan ration scale, tried before these witnesses against the Shirogane bronze, settles light by the breadth of one grain of rye; that every ration weighed on it since the last Ice-out went out short by that grain; and that a house which feeds its retainers short has made an Empty Bow of the Weighing, since the offence is inaccuracy."

On the last clause she let a little of herself into the room. The smoke lay down along the floor. Frost skinned the water on her clerk's ink-stone. The headman of the outer town, by the door, kept his eyes on the flags as his people kept them before the line, said "Sky take it" into his collar, and sweated.

Tadanori had it as kehai on the back of his neck, and his shoulders came up under it. A Guild man would have called it Pressure. He bowed to the right depth and took the certificate. No retainer of the house is taught to read it, and Tadanori could not, and he knew the fourteen hands all the same by their shapes, the way a groom knows horses by their gait, from nineteen winters of watching men who could.

"Shiroma-sama. The Weighing is Urion's labour, and this is written in Maelor's hand. A document in the wrong hand is void."

"A clerk's hand is his house's hand," she said. "The Shirogane answer for their own."

"The old men of this house have a story about that." He left a ma after it, and nobody filled it. "When the white shelf went, the Kōkan stood on it and bought a window with themselves. Six lines crossed in it. Nobody sailed back. Window bought, nobody sailed."

"Fold it," Shiroma-sama said to her clerk, and the hall heard her come down to the bare order. The headman looked up from the flags before he remembered himself.

Then, in full form: "I will write it again in Urion's hand by the next hinge of the day, steward, and you will hear it twice." Under the silver cuff her thumb found her beads. The clerk folded the certificate, and before he closed his book he wrote something small in its margin and looked once at Tadanori while he did it.

When the hall had emptied, Tadanori set two fingers on the beam and felt it settle slow, as it had since the first thaw.

## 3. Quiet and travel

Cold through the soles on the ridge road, and the inside of the nose freezing on each breath. No smell at all, at that cold. Then the whole of it lay open below them. Far off, the forest stood black along the west, and above it the sky had gone the grey of the snow. Nearer, the Seat stood on its grey rock with smoke over every stack of the outer town. Nearer again the boundary road ran down from the ridge, white, its markers set a chō apart, and far down it, small enough to cover with a thumb, somebody was dragging a sledge up from the tannery.

It was the Walking, Zhaeren's labour, and Tsunemori had done it at dusk every Ink-freeze since he was old enough to carry the salt. He had been the child's moriyaku since the cradle. The child did the righting. Yukiren-sama knocked the rime off each stone with a mitten and leaned on the crooked ones until they stood, and counted them aloud in the order of the doorpost scratches.

Between the markers they kept the ma, and neither of them spoke into it.

At the ninth the rime was already off the stone. Somebody had knocked it clear that morning, all but one patch on the windward face, where a mitten had pressed and left its print: the thumb, and the broad blunt rest of the hand. The stone stood true.

The woman with the sledge had come up as far as the eleventh, with a load of split rounds from the woodpile behind the tannery. Outer town, Kharven, her hide-coat dark with grease at the cuffs. She looked at Tsunemori's hands, as her people looked at anyone from the house.

"It was leaning," she said, of the ninth. "How's your stack?"

"Full enough." He took the salt bar from his belt, laid it on the marker, broke it into four with the butt of his knife, and held out a quarter.

"Under the sky, everything balances," she said. She put it inside her mitten. Then she went on up the road toward the stacks.

He entered it in his head as Zhaeren's labour, and the greeting as Thalen's, both done by a woman who would have called them fixing a stone and asking after the wood.

The aurora came up green over the Seat. Tsunemori laid his hand on the white head, which the household watched first and fed best, and said nothing. Under the palm the skull was warm through the hair. He kept his hand there until the last light went off the road.

## 4. A system moment

The coil came up from the Guild house in the outer town under a felt hood. When the coil-clerk drew the hood off in the lower hall the brass caught every lamp at once, and the lacquered walls behind it went darker for the comparison.

Shiroma read the instrument first, as a Shirogane reads anything that claims to weigh. The calibration seal on the housing was whole. The Reading Oil stood clear in its sight-glass, without the faint bloom of a spoiled batch. The tape waited on its spool, white as a new register page and a great deal cheaper.

Then she read the boy.

Setsuki stood at the rail in his court robe with the left sleeve slit and pinned over the splints. Eleven days had taken the swelling down. The bruise had gone yellow at the wrist. He carried the arm across his body like a thing lent him by someone strict. She had felt it at the door: he stood heavier by a Stage than he had stood in the yard. Feeling is not entering. The house had witnesses for that, ranged along the wall in the order of their standing.

The clerk set the boy's right palm to the contact plate. The draw-hum in the housing climbed half a tone and held, and nobody spoke into it. Then the type-hammers began to strike. The tape came out of the slot in short jerks, and the clerk tore it at the toothed bar and handed it to the Reckoner, who read it to the hall in the Concord manner, every word given the same weight.

> PUBLIC RE-ASSAY · LOWER HALL, KHARVEN-SEAT · BEFORE WITNESSES
> SUBJECT: SETSUKI SHIROGANE MOTO. PRESENTED BY HIS LINE.
> PRIOR ENTRY: STAGE II, WELLING. TIER OF STANDING: APPRENTICE.
> READING: STAGE III, ASCENSION. TIER OF STANDING: JOURNEYMAN.
> GRADE CEILING C. LEVEL CEILING 175.
> CATALYST: OUTSIDE THE COIL'S READING. REFER TO WITNESS.
> In the clerk's pencil, beside it: *Arm not assayed. Coil does not do arms.*

The Reckoner entered it on the roll in the Concord hand and blotted it.

"Enter it in Urion's hand also," Shiroma said.

"The roll takes one hand, my lady."

"Then the roll takes it in one hand, and the house takes it in Urion's, and both readings are correct." She took the brush from her own case and wrote beneath the pencil, slowly, in Urion's hand, which the Shirogane had closed their assays in since the white shelf: meijitsu itchi. The ink went on wet. The Ink-freeze was over.

Setsuki did not look at the tape. Eleven days ago, in the yard, he would have read the same lines behind his own breastbone.

"Correct," said Shiroma, and held the paper to the brazier until the strokes were dry.

## 5. A comic beat

Bead-cold. The beads were too cold to hold bare until the stove had been lit an hour, and on the third such morning Ukkari carried Shiroma-sama her water before her beads.

Chakkari knew it because the beads were not on their cushion by the stair. He knew the rest by looking at Ukkari, who was weighing out the morning rye with great care and the face he always had, the face of a man with nothing on his mind.

The old cook had named the pair of them in their first winter at the Seat and glossed it once, for the whole kitchen. Ukkari, she said, is the man who puts the salt in the tea. Chakkari is the man who sells the tea afterward and keeps the salt. Both names had stuck. In that house the offence was inaccuracy, and they were accurate.

"The beads," Chakkari said.

"On the tray, with the water."

"The tray came down with a dry cup on it and nothing else."

"Then Shiroma-sama has them," Ukkari said to the rye, and Chakkari, who had seen her bare hands on the stair rail at first light, began to search the kitchen by method, in the order of the labours: the scale, the woodpile, the knife-block, the scraps for the dog. Ukkari searched behind him and put everything back wrong. The kitchen boy came in for Ginbae's rye. "The silver fly wants feeding," the cook said, since the young lord with the splinted arm had stood at the assay-room door since dawn, and nobody answered the boy.

Then Chakkari looked at the scale properly. The kitchen's bronze weight had gone down to the lower hall with the scale at Ice-out, and only the scale had come back up. In the far pan, under a fold of cloth, lay a string of black beads worn smooth by a dead woman's thumb.

"They weigh what the bronze weighed," Ukkari said. "Near enough."

The third stair creaked. The kitchen went shiin all at once, the dog included.

Shiroma-sama stood in the doorway without her beads. The ring-lines stood clear in her irises, and the outer ring flared once as she looked from the boy to Ukkari to Chakkari. Chakkari thought of every bowl of dripping he had sold the outer town at a thumb of salt the bowl, and of the beads being the thing that kept the eye shut, and his knees would not do anything at all.

She crossed to the scale and lifted the beads from the pan. The beam swung up empty. She weighed the string a moment in her palm, as her line weighs anything.

"Lighter than the bronze," she said, "by a grain of rye."

Chakkari went down onto one knee on the stone before he knew he had decided to.

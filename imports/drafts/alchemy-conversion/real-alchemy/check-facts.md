# The Real Alchemy: fact check

Checked against: Lemery 1686 (archive.org full text), Boerhaave *Experiments* (Gutenberg 63318), Darling 1911 (Gutenberg 56353), Wood and Bache 1870 (archive.org full text), Lavoisier 1777 (chemteam), Earles 1964 (Cambridge PDF), Philalethes *Open Entrance* (alchemywebsite.com), Babcock 1882 (todayinsci), Conniff 2014 (Smithsonian), the Antiquity Oberstockstall gallery, Crossref for the journal citations, PubMed abstracts (Andersen 1993, Wedeen 1989, Ditchburn 1980), UKPID 37 and 43, IPCS PIM G042, ATSDR, the Forensic Toxicology 2022 case (Europe PMC), and Wikipedia ("Emerald Tablet", "Mary the Jewess", "Red heat", "Borosilicate glass", "Johann Rudolf Glauber", "Jean Beguin", "Calomel", "Blue mass", "Later life of Isaac Newton", "Ground glass joint", "1858 Bradford sweets poisoning").

Confirmed as written (sample): Lemery's tin calcination (36 h pan / 4 days crucible), aqua fortis charge and the 15 to 16 hour hold, three days for oil of vitriol, the four degrees of fire, all three Lemery lutes, the reverberatory lute and dimensions; Boerhaave's 500 / 448 distillations, the fulling mill from 1 March to 13 November 1732, the 180 °F months, the pitch-rosin-tallow-brimstone cement; Lavoisier's 4 oz, 12 days, second-day specks, about 45 grains, about one sixth; Howe's table and Darling's 50 °C and 20 °C; Wedgwood 1782, Seger 1886, Le Chatelier 1886; every 1870 dose for calomel, corrosive sublimate, arsenious acid, Fowler's (formula, strength, 10 drops, lavender, flint-glass decay) and tartar emetic, the 10-grain fatal figure and the half-ounce survival of 1853; the Earles figures (587 men, about 200, three weeks, 14 mg/m³ = 140 × 0.1, 16 to 27 days); Andersen 1993 (77 men, 12.3 years, 18.2 % vs 7.5 %); Wedeen (1860, 1912); the Bradford figures; Oberstockstall 300 / about 40 / about 60, 1980 and 1993-94, sand-grog-graphite fabric; Hessian wares above 1,300 °C; the glass 40 / 170 °C shock figures; the ATSDR NOx 3-to-30-hour interval; the arsenic 70-180 / 50-300 mg ranges; corrosive sublimate 1-4 g; Bernhoft's half-lives and the mercury-vapour arithmetic; the *Open Entrance* regimen times; the Holmyard and Steele and Singer citations; Rush's 10 gr calomel pills and the heroic 20 gr.

## Findings, most serious first

### 1. The Trismegistus line is in the Arabic, not "only in the Latin line of descent"
Page, "From and like": "the Trismegistus line exists only in the Latin line of descent." Wrong. The *Sirr al-Khalīqa* version (in Ruska's German, via Marshall's Alchemy Web Site, which the page itself cites) ends with Hermes "threefold graced with wisdom." Only Holmyard's Jabirian version lacks it. Correction: "Holmyard's Jabirian version names no Trismegistus; the *Sirr al-Khalīqa* version names Hermes as threefold in wisdom, and the Latin gives it as Hermes Trismegistus with three parts of the wisdom." The Codex split still holds, because Venur recites Holmyard's version, but the page's general claim has to go. Sources: Marshall, alchemywebsite.com/emerald.html; Wikipedia, "Emerald Tablet".

### 2. The Newton poisoning dispute is credited to the wrong paper
Page, "Named cases": the doubts (no classic tremor or gum signs, the break with Fatio) are cited to Johnson and Wolbarsht 1979. Their paper's title is "Mercury poisoning: a probable cause of Isaac Newton's physical and mental ills". They argued for poisoning, as Spargo and Pounds 1979 did. Correction: cite the case against to R. W. Ditchburn, "Newton's illness of 1692-3," *Notes and Records* 35 (1980): 1-16 (a depressive illness with no mouth signs and no tremor in the handwriting), and Milo Keynes, *Nature* 292 (1981): 197-198. Keep Johnson and Wolbarsht on the poisoning side. Also note that "about fifteen times the normal mercury" is the Wikipedia and Lesney figure; other reports give up to about 40 times. Present it as one reported figure.

### 3. Rasori's dose row is Ginielle's practice
Tartar emetic table, row "Rasori's practice, Milan, from 1800: raised from 4 to 16 or 20 gr a day over 8 to 16 days". Wood and Bache 1870 (p. 1020) say Rasori's contrastimulant dose was "from a grain to two grains or more, every two hours". The increase "from four grains to sixteen or twenty daily" is M. Ginielle's, in twenty-eight cases of articular dropsy. The "eight to sixteen days" was the time the effusion took to absorb, and was never the length of a course. Correction: Rasori, 1 to 2 gr or more every two hours (65 to 130 mg a dose). Put Ginielle separately: 4 rising to 16 to 20 gr a day (0.26 to 1.3 g), with the effusion gone in 8 to 16 days.

### 4. The pyrometer history leaves out three 19th-century instruments, and that feeds a canon-conflict row
"Instruments": "Above red heat there was nothing until Wedgwood's clay-shrinkage pyrometer of 1782 ... Seger's cones followed in 1886." The fault paragraph adds "The furnace heats that mattered most went unmeasured until the 1880s." Darling 1911, the page's own source (pp. 2-4), records three more:
- Daniell's expansion pyrometer of 1822, the first continuous reading;
- Pouillet's air pyrometer and his colour table of 1836;
- Siemens's resistance pyrometer of 1871.

Darling also puts Le Chatelier's optical pyrometer at 1892. All of these fall inside the Imperial Age span (1800 to 1900), so notes.md conflict row 1 is understated. Correction: add them, and change "unmeasured until the 1880s" to "measured crudely from the 1820s and reliably from 1886."

### 5. Conniff 2014 does not say what the page cites it for
- "Principe, working from Starkey's notebooks, watched an amalgam ... swell like leavened dough (Conniff 2014)." None of "dough", "swell", "leavened" or "Starkey" appears in the Smithsonian article.
- "by morning a metallic tree had grown in it (Conniff 2014) *(single source for the overnight timing)*", and in the regimen fault, "The one modern replication found grew its tree overnight." Conniff says only that "one morning" Principe found the flask full of a gold tree. No elapsed time is given.

Correction: drop "overnight" and "by morning" and say that Conniff gives no duration. The swelling-dough detail and the Starkey attribution need their real source (probably Principe 2013, *The Secrets of Alchemy*). If it cannot be found, cut them. The Wedding and regimen "In WOTR" lines that rest on the overnight timing need the same fix.

### 6. The bain-marie credit to Maria through Zosimos is stated as settled but is contested
"The water bath": "Zosimos, about 300 CE, credited the bath to Maria the Jewess." Sella 2009 says so, but Wikipedia ("Mary the Jewess") says Zosimos credits her with the tribikos (and discusses the kerotakis), and that the name *balneum Mariae* was introduced by Arnold of Villanova in the 14th century. Wikipedia calls her contributions disputed. Correction: "The bath is traditionally credited to Maria. Zosimos credits her with the tribikos and describes the kerotakis. The name *balneum Mariae* is medieval (Arnold of Villanova, 14th century), and whether Zosimos himself gave her the bath is disputed." Then reword the WOTR line "the same shape of problem as Maria's credit through a single later writer" to say the credit comes through a name coined a thousand years later.

### 7. Arsenic "for ague it was one twentieth of a grain" is not in the source
Wood and Bache 1870 give 1/20 gr three times a day as Henry Hunt's dose for ulcerated cancer of the uterus and menorrhagia. That dose is not for ague. For intermittents they give Fowler's at up to 15 to 20 drops three times a day (Mitchell). Correction: delete the ague figure, or replace it with "Hunt's 1/20 gr (3.2 mg) for uterine cancer and menorrhagia."

### 8. Glauber's disability from 1666 was a fall
"Named cases" and "The whole career" put his whole disablement down to poisoning. Wikipedia says he became seriously ill in 1660 (poisoning is the usual inference) and was "crippled by a wagon fall in 1666" and confined to bed after that. Correction: "ill from 1660; bedridden after a fall from a wagon in 1666." Keep the poisoning inference for the 1660 illness only.

### 9. The date for Glauber's salt is contested and stated as settled
"about 1658 (Ahonen, DSB)". Wikipedia dates his discovery of *sal mirabilis* to 1625, and his published account of making it from salt and oil of vitriol is *De natura salium* (1658). Correction: "found about 1625, published with the spirit-of-salt process in 1658". Mark it as disputed between the two dates.

### 10. The date of the *Tyrocinium Chymicum* is unsupported
"Jean Beguin's *Tyrocinium Chymicum* (Paris, from 1608)". The cited Wikipedia "Jean Beguin" gives 1610 and does not mention calomel. The 1608 date on Wikipedia's "Calomel" belongs to a garbled sentence that credits the *Tyrocinium* to Oswald Croll. Correction: "Paris, 1610". Source the claim that it describes calomel to a historian (Multhauf or Debus), or mark it single-sourced.

### 11. Calomel: the recorded death was from nitromuriatic acid, not "that combination"
The calomel fault paragraph lists salt, sal ammoniac, bitter almonds and strong acids, then says "The 1870 authors record a death from that combination". Wood and Bache (p. 1207) report, at second hand ("One of the authors has been informed of a case"), a death after calomel given with nitromuriatic acid (aqua regia). The salt-meat point is Bauwens's, as Wood and Bache relay it: physicians near brackish water seldom gave calomel, and naval surgeons stopped giving it to sailors on salt meat. Correction: "a death reported at second hand after calomel with nitromuriatic acid", with the salt-meat point credited to Bauwens.

### 12. Goldsmith's dose is antimony from a mixed powder, and the 130 mg death is not in UKPID
UKPID 37 gives Goldsmith's dose as an estimated 132 to 198 mg of *antimony*, taken as a mixture of antimony trioxide and tartar emetic (James's Powder). The page reads as tartar emetic alone. UKPID gives 0.2 g as fatal (Miller 1982). The 130 mg death comes from a separate 1967 case, found through a ScienceDirect extract (research-paracelsus.md), and UKPID does not carry it. Correction: "132 to 198 mg of antimony, from James's Powder (antimony oxide with tartar emetic)". Cite the 130 mg case to its own source, marked single-sourced.

### 13. Stirling 1905 is misread, and the gap between the tables is understated
The Stirling table (Wikipedia, "Red heat") reads: just visible 525; dull red 699; dull cherry 800; full cherry 900; clear cherry 1,000; deep orange 1,100; clear orange 1,200; white 1,300 °C. The page sets Howe's "light red" (850) against Stirling's "orange" (1,100-1,200), which are different colour names. The same-name gaps also run to 300 °C (cherry: Howe 700 against Stirling 800-1,000), not "100 to 150 °C". Correction: add Stirling's 525 °C first visible red. Give Howe's light red as unmatched and Stirling's orange separately. Change the fault to "the same colour name sits 75 to 300 °C apart". The WOTR line ("about equal to the gap between the tables") should say that the gap is often wider than Alchemetrica's hundred degrees.

### 14. Lemery's "five days and nights" is a hypothetical in the aqua fortis chapter
The strong-waters bullet says Lemery "mentions runs of five days and nights" of oil of vitriol, and the long-runs table says "3 days and nights, up to 5". Lemery's line (p. 305) is in his aqua fortis remarks: if you kept the fire on five days and nights, the receiver would still be full of clouds. It is a conditional, and no run is recorded. Correction: "three days and three nights (a run he did); he says five would still yield fume". Take "up to 5" out of the table.

### 15. The dates of Newton's experiments contradict each other
The long-runs table has "about 400 experiments, 1678 to 1695 (Spargo 2005)". The fault paragraph has his dated experiments stopping "in 1696" (Newman 2019, February 1696). Correction: use one end date, 1696 by Newman, or give Spargo's range as his own.

### 16. Oberstockstall's patron
"Oberstockstall ran under a Fugger patron." The laboratory sat on the Trenbach estate: Christoph von Trenbach, cathedral canon of Passau, redeveloped it in 1548, and Urban von Trenbach followed him. The Fugger connection is Victor August and Sigmund Friedrich Fugger, who were among the pastors of Kirchberg (local history society, hf-kirchberg.at; von Osten 1998). Correction: "run by the Trenbach canons of Passau, with Fugger clergy among its circle".

### 17. HMS *Triumph*: the midshipmen were not the least affected overall
Earles 1964 says the men under the fo'c'sle got off with a slight affection of the gums, and the main and upper decks were lightly affected. The midshipmen were the least affected *among those quartered on the lower deck and orlop*. Correction: "Among those who berthed below, the midshipmen, whose duties kept them on deck, came off lightest." Also, "coins and watches came up silvered" is "overlaid with an amalgam" in Earles. That is acceptable as a paraphrase.

### 18. Boerhaave's red-hot hours unfixed the powder
"Coagulation, congelation and fixation". Boerhaave did name "Fire passing thro' the Glass" as the fixing cause (Corollary 6, Exp. VIII). But the three to four hours at or near red heat are the experiments in which the "fixed" powder went back to running mercury, leaving 15 grains fixed out of 17 ounces (Corollaries 3 and 5: "The acquired Fixedness does not bear a great Fire"). Correction: the entry should say the red-hot hours revived the mercury. That matches the page's own fault line.

### 19. Ground joints: the page contradicts itself
"Wax, cement and stoppers" says ground stoppers existed by the 1690s and still joints were made with "custom-ground glass" before standardisation. The WOTR line then says "Alchemetrica's statement that there are no ground joints holds for the real still train before the twentieth century." Custom ground joints existed; only standard, interchangeable ones date from about 1930 (Wikipedia, "Ground glass joint"). Rubber bungs are also left out of the pre-standard list. Correction: "no *interchangeable* ground joints before about 1930; custom-ground joints and stoppers existed."

### 20. Lemery's Tree of Diana: "cold" is not in the text, and the entry is in the wrong table
Lemery says to let the matrass stand "in some convenient place for forty days", and he says nothing about cold. It is a cold metallic precipitation and was never a putrefaction or digestion. Correction: drop "cold". Move it out of the putrefaction table, or label it as a still standing that runs for the same forty days.

### 21. Accident table (Babcock 1882)
Babcock gives Bunsen's cacodyl explosion as 1841. Wikipedia and DSB give 1843; the page uses 1843, which is fine, but should say the two disagree. Babcock says Faraday held "a few grains", not seven. The page's "seven grains" needs its own source or should be dropped. Babcock names Mansfield's still as holding naphtha; "benzol" is a gloss.

### 22. Boerhaave's little furnace "about 85 °F by his reckoning"
ARTECHNE gives about 85 °F as the "summer-heat" at which Boerhaave distilled rosemary. It is a modern approximation, and ARTECHNE does not give it as the furnace's own heat by his reckoning. Correction: "used for 'summer-heat' distillations, about 85 °F (ARTECHNE's conversion)".

### 23. Minor citation fixes
- Soda-lime glass "softens at about 730 °C" is cited to Wikipedia "Borosilicate glass", which gives no soda-lime softening point. Cite "Soda-lime glass" (726 °C).
- Calomel "rat oral LD50 210 mg/kg" has no citation. Wikipedia "Calomel" does not give it; cite "Mercury(I) chloride".
- Ruska and Wiedemann, "Alchemistische Decknamen", *Sitzungsberichte der Physikalisch-medizinischen Sozietät zu Erlangen* 56: 17-36, is dated 1924, not 1923.
- "Steele and Singer tentatively named Plato of Tivoli": Wikipedia ("Emerald Tablet") says only that the translator has been "tentatively identified" as Plato of Tivoli. Whether Steele and Singer made that identification is unconfirmed.
- The spagyria fault paragraph cites "Boyle's objection in *The Sceptical Chymist* (1661)" to "Boyle 1675", which is a different work. Cite the 1661 text or change the attribution.

### 24. Stated as settled, one source only (should carry *(single source)*)
- Glauber's *Furni novi* as "the first book to give full furnace designs with their dimensions" (Mineralogical Record only).
- Hutchinson's "five of his six cases being psoriasis patients" (NRC 1977 extract only).
- "Electroplating after 1840 ended fire-gilding" (Schwarcz only). Fire-gilding went on after 1840, for example at St Isaac's Cathedral, which notes.md mentions. Better: "began to replace".
- "Principe tested readings of several Keys in the laboratory" (through Wikipedia only).

## Nothing else found wrong
The dose arithmetic, the metric conversions, the Fowler's arithmetic (8.8 mg/ml, 8 to 34 ml), the vapour-concentration arithmetic and the "60 to 250 times" margin all check. The single-source markers on the page are otherwise honest.

# The Real Alchemy: research lane BODY

The body at the bench: what years of alchemy and iatrochemistry did to the practitioner, as real toxicology, plus the accident record and the poison tests of the 1800s.

Conventions. Every claim carries its source tags [S#] (list at the end). **[single]** means I could confirm it from one source only. **[contested]** means reputable sources disagree, and both readings are given. All summaries are in my own words. Figures are given as the sources give them. Conversions are mine and marked (conv.). One apothecaries' grain = 64.8 mg (conv.).

Each section ends with **Checks in WOTR**, which points only at canon that already exists: Alchemetrica ("What it costs the body", "Luting", "The Menstrua"), *wiki/Materials, Alchemy & Trade/Alchemetrica.md* lines 197-252, and the live render */tmp/wotr-drafts/alchemetrica-live.md* at the same lines; the Alftian Codex vol. 1 lines 45, 145-147 and 289, and vol. 2 line 175.

## 1. Mercury: the bench disease

### 1.1 How it gets in
- **The route is the lungs.** Metallic mercury vapour is absorbed through the lungs at roughly 80%. Liquid mercury swallowed is barely absorbed at all. Calomel (mercurous chloride) is poorly absorbed too [S1]. **[single for the 80% figure]**
- **The vapour has no smell.** A room can be saturated and nobody knows. This is the standard point in the Charles II literature [S16, S17].
- **Saturation at room heat.** At 20 °C, air over open mercury saturates at about 14 mg/m³. That is 140 times the 1964 permissible level of 0.1 mg/m³ [S5, Earles 1964].
- **Heat multiplies it.** Wikipedia's mercury vapour-pressure table gives 1 Pa at about 42 °C, 10 Pa at about 77 °C, 100 Pa at about 120 °C and 100 kPa at the 356.7 °C boiling point [S18]. Worked through the ideal gas law (conv.), the saturated air concentration is about 77 mg/m³ at 42 °C, about 690 mg/m³ at 77 °C and about 6,100 mg/m³ at 120 °C. A warm sand bath, a gilder's pan or a "fixing" of mercury over a fire puts out vapour by the gram per cubic metre.
- **Half-lives.** In blood it is 3 to 5 days. In the central nervous system it is longer than several years [S1]. **[single]** This is why the tremor outlasts the exposure.

### 1.2 The chronic triad and the order it comes in
Classical mercurialism is a triad: tremor, erethism and gingivitis with salivation [S2, S3, S1].

- **Tremor.** A fine tremor starts in the hands. It then spreads to the eyelids, lips and tongue, and every few minutes coarse jerks break through it. At its worst the man can hardly cross the floor. The trade had names for it: "hatter's shakes", "gilder's palsy" [S2, S3, S19]. Ramazzini (1700) wrote of gilders who drive mercury off amalgam by fire and get vertigo, asthma, paralysis and a palsy of the neck [S20]. **[single, via secondary summary of the translation]**
- **Erethism.** This is the personality change. It brings irritability, timidity and pathological shyness, low self-confidence, depression, memory loss and insomnia [S2, S1]. In 1810 the ship's surgeon's reading added indecision and poor judgement of distance [S5]. Shyness came late to the medical literature. New Jersey hatters' tremor was described in 1860 (Freeman), but hatters' pathological shyness was not recorded there until 1912 [S4, Wedeen 1989].
- **Mouth.** Gingivitis and copious salivation come first, then loosened teeth and ulcers. At high dose the tongue and face swell, gangrene sets into the cheeks, teeth are lost and the jaw necroses [S1, S5].
- **Other signs.** These include red fingers and toes, red cheeks, sweating and hearing loss [S2]. **[single]** Since Atkinson (1943), a brown reflex on the front capsule of the lens ("mercurialentis") has been described in mercury-vapour workers [S21]. **[single; the claim that it appears in all workers exposed over a year is from a single secondary summary and is not relied on]**

### 1.3 Onset: dose, not years
Real mercurialism is set by the **air concentration**, not by a fixed length of service.

- **Measured thresholds.** The US Public Health Service felt-hat study (Neal et al., Public Health Bulletin 263, 1941) found no case of mercurialism below 0.1 mg/m³. At under 0.08 mg/m³, 3 of 66 men were borderline. At 0.08 to 0.15 mg/m³, 15 of 127 were borderline or first-stage [S6]. WHO: above 0.08 mg/m³ (about 100 µg Hg per g creatinine in urine), tremor and erethism are highly probable. At 0.025 to 0.08 mg/m³ come subtler effects: measurable tremor, slowed nerve conduction, fatigue and irritability [S7].
- **Time to onset.** At low occupational levels, symptoms come on over months to years. Some people show personality change within 6 to 12 months [S3]. **[single, lay-medical source]** At high levels it takes weeks. On HMS Triumph (1810), cases appeared within three weeks of the spill, and in mid-April about 200 of 587 men (nearly a third) had symptoms [S5].
- **Distance and ventilation decide it.** On the Triumph, poisoning followed where a man slept. The men under the forecastle had sore gums. Those on the lower deck and orlop, next to the store, were worst. The midshipmen, whose work kept them on deck, got off lightest. Sheep, pigs, goats, poultry, cats, a dog, a canary, rats, mice and cockroaches all died. Copper, brass, gold watches and coins came up silvered with amalgam [S5]. The sloop *Phipps*, a smaller and more open ship carrying the same cargo, had far fewer cases [S5].
- **Fixing mercury in a closed room.** Charles II kept a private laboratory and worked on "fixing" mercury. That involved distilling it in quantity. His death in 1685 (convulsions, then fits) has been put down to mercury vapour [S16, S17]. **[contested: stroke and kidney failure are the other readings]**

### 1.4 Does it go away?
- **Partly.** In milder cases, tremor and erethism fade slowly over years once exposure stops [S2, S3]. On the Triumph, most men with salivation (ptyalism) were back aboard after 16 to 27 days ashore. Four needed eight weeks. Two men sent ashore for "debility", probably erethism, recovered in a fortnight. Of three admitted with phthisis and ptyalism, two died [S5].
- **Not fully.** Andersen et al. (1993) examined 77 former chloralkali workers an average of 12.3 years after exposure ended. Compared with 53 referents they had more postural tremor (18.2% against 7.5%), more loss of distal sensation (13.0% against 1.9%) and more impaired coordination (10.4% against 1.9%) [S8]. Letz et al. (2000) studied men exposed more than 30 years earlier, with peak urine mercury above 600 µg/L. Peripheral-nerve damage still tracked cumulative dose, but there was no link to dementia [S9]. Bernhoft (2012): symptoms may fade after exposure stops, but in many cases they do not, and lasting neurological signs are common [S1].
- **Fault in the real record.** The tremor is not specific to mercury. It has to be read against the exposure history. Newton's case shows how hard that is (§1.6).

### 1.5 Mercury as medicine: the doses a physician handled
- **Calomel.** In the 1793 yellow fever, Benjamin Rush gave 10 grains of calomel (about 648 mg, conv.) with 10 to 15 grains of jalap as a purge, repeated daily [S22].
- **Blue mass (pilula hydrargyri).** A recreated recipe is about 33% metallic mercury, roughly 65 mg of mercury per pill, taken two or three pills a day. Hirschhorn, Feldman and Greaves (2001) put Lincoln's intake at nearly 9,000 times the modern EPA limit [S23]. **[single for the multiplier]**
- **Corrosive sublimate (mercuric chloride).** The estimated fatal dose is 1 to 4 g [S24]. In 1825 Thénard swallowed some by mistake during a lecture and survived on egg white [S15]. **[single]**
- **The salivation cure.** For syphilis, mercury was given by ointment rubbed into the skin, by fumigation over heated crucibles, or by mouth, until the patient salivated heavily. An 18th-century text calls for 5 or 6 ounces of ointment per rubbing, and precipitate by mouth at 12 to 15 grains for 6 to 12 days. It lists the costs as ulcerated mouths, loose teeth and fetid drool [S25]. The rule of thumb was about three pints of saliva a day [S26]. **[contested attribution]** Kang and Pedersen (2018) credit the three-pint standard to Paracelsus [S26]. Wong et al. (2016) say instead that Paracelsus preferred ointment because he saw how toxic mercury elixirs were [S27]. Other reviews say his syphilis tracts urged milder mercury use than the practice of his day, and that his 1529 tract describes chronic mercury poisoning [S28].
- **Pink disease, as a dose-to-harm figure.** This is outside the 1800-1900 span, 20th century. About 1 in 500 infants given calomel teething powders developed acrodynia, and about 20% of those died (Warkany and Hubbard 1951) [S29]. It shows how unevenly the same dose lands on different people.

### 1.6 Named alchemists and chemists
- **Isaac Newton (1642-1727).** He had furnaces from 1669 and did heavy mercury and antimony work. In 1693 he broke down with insomnia, poor digestion, memory loss and paranoid letters. In 1979 hair said to be his was analysed. It showed high mercury: about fifteen times normal, and about four times normal lead, arsenic and antimony [S10, S11]. Spargo and Pounds (1979) and Johnson and Wolbarsht (1979) proposed poisoning [S10, S12]. **[contested]** Against it: the classic tremor and gum signs are not recorded for Newton, and the hair's provenance and dating are open [S12]. Some writers put the breakdown down to the break with Fatio de Duillier [S11]. It is said that Newton blamed his early grey hair on quicksilver [S12]. **[single; not confirmed]**
- **Johann Rudolf Glauber (1604-1670).** His health failed from about 1660. From 1666 he was bedridden and paralysed, sold books and apparatus to support his family, and died in Amsterdam in 1670. The illness is attributed to cumulative poisoning by the mercury, arsenic, antimony and lead of his trade [S13]. **[attribution is a modern inference]**
- **Carl Wilhelm Scheele (1742-1786).** He smelled and tasted his new substances. He had kidney disease from autumn 1785, then a skin disease, and died at 43. His doctors called it mercury poisoning. Modern accounts blame cumulative mercury, arsenic, lead and possibly hydrofluoric acid [S14].
- **Frankland's laboratory, St Bartholomew's, 1865.** This case is organic, dimethylmercury, not bench quicksilver. It is included for the timeline. Carl Ulrich inhaled the vapour after a spill. He developed sore gums, numb hands, deafness and failing sight, grew restless and noisy, and died about a fortnight after symptoms were first noticed. A young technician who helped clean up declined over months and died a year later [S30, S15].

**Checks in WOTR.**
- *Crucible palsy* (Alchemetrica l. 246; Codex I l. 145, II l. 175): the sequence tracks real mercurialism well. The tremor starts in the hand, climbs to the head and jaw, and comes with irritability and then shyness and a dislike of being looked at. That the family sees it first is plausible.
- Where WOTR departs from the real disease, and says so in its own voice:
  - "Onset at roughly nine years" is WOTR's own figure. Real onset follows air concentration: weeks at saturation, months to years below 0.1 mg/m³.
  - "Permanent, progressive" is harsher than the real disease, which partly fades once exposure stops but leaves lasting deficits (§1.4).
  - Real tremor spreads through the eyelids, lips and tongue. WOTR names "head and jaw", and Ramazzini's "palsy of the neck" supports the head.
- Codex I l. 145 ("the man who measures takes his own share every day he measures"): the real mechanism is vapour at the bench. The dose given to a patient is not what poisons the physician. The mercury he heats is.
- Codex I l. 289 (quicksilver, calomel and white arsenic each heal at a measure and poison past it): matches §1.5 and §2.4. There were real therapeutic ranges, and they were narrow.

## 2. Arsenic

### 2.1 Acute
- **Symptoms.** Within 1 to 2 hours: burning mouth and throat, drooling, trouble swallowing, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea [S31]. Garlic breath is listed among the early signs [S32].
- **Lethal dose. [range differs by source]**
  - ATSDR: 70 to 180 mg of arsenic trioxide, or 1 to 3 mg/kg [S32].
  - Wikipedia: 70 to 200 mg [S33].
  - LiverTox: 2 to 20 mg/kg, which is about 145 mg to 1.45 g for a 75 kg adult [S34].
  - A death after 2 g is recorded [S31].
- **Period benchmark.** In the 1858 Bradford humbugs, an analyst found about 9 grains (about 580 mg) of arsenic per sweet, later 11 to 16 grains. He said 4.5 grains (about 290 mg, conv.) would kill a man. About 20 died and more than 200 were seriously ill [S35].

### 2.2 Delayed signs after one big dose
- **Neuropathy.** Symmetrical numbness and burning in a glove-and-stocking pattern. It begins 2 to 4 weeks after the first signs of poisoning ease (ATSDR). Another ATSDR page puts onset at 7 to 14 days [S32, S36]. **[figures differ within one agency]**
- **Mees' lines (Aldrich-Mees lines).** Pale transverse bands across the nails. They show "several weeks to months" after poisoning [S32] and mark a pause in nail growth [S37].
  - First descriptions: Reynolds 1901 (arsenic in beer), Aldrich 1904, Mees 1919 [S37].
  - They are not unique to arsenic [S37].
  - Dating: fingernails grow about 3.47 mm a month (about 0.1 mm a day) and replace fully in 5 to 6 months (Yaemsiri et al. 2010) [S38]. The band's distance from the cuticle therefore dates the dose [S32, S39].

### 2.3 Chronic
- **Skin.** Hyperpigmentation with scattered pale spots, "raindrops on a dusty road", and hard nodules on palms and soles (hyperkeratosis) [S36, S31]. WHO: these appear after about five years of exposure at the least [S40]. In water studies, pigmentation is linked to 400 ppb and above. Chronic intake of 0.03 to 0.1 mg/kg/day produces neurological and skin effects [S36].
- **Cancer.** Skin cancer follows after 3 to 4 decades [S36]. Hutchinson (1888) linked medicinal arsenic to skin cancer [S41].
- **Inhaled at work.** Workers breathing arsenic trioxide had ulcers of the nasal lining and perforated septa, sometimes within a week or two of heavy exposure. Contact dermatitis was also recorded [S42]. **[the one-to-two-week figure is single]**
- **Speed.** A modern case on 210 mg a day of an arsenical folk remedy had Mees' lines and hyperkeratosis at six weeks [S31]. **[single]**

### 2.4 Arsenic as medicine and habit
- **Fowler's solution.** Thomas Fowler of Stafford, 1786. It was 1% potassium arsenite (arsenic trioxide about 10 mg/ml), used for ague, skin disease, asthma and, from about 1865, leukaemia. It was given until the skin changes appeared [S43, S34, S41].
- **Styrian arsenic eaters.** Reported by von Tschudi in 1851. They started on a piece about the size of a lentil, under half a grain, several times a week. One man was said to have worked up to 4 grains. Stopping brought withdrawal. Alfred Swain Taylor called the reports exaggerated, and Roscoe backed them [S44]. **[contested in its own century]**

### 2.5 Named cases
- **Adolf Ferdinand Gehlen (1775-1815).** He breathed arsine (arsenic hydride) while working on arsenic compounds and died on 16 July 1815, about nine days later, of haemolysis. This is the first recorded arsine death [S45, S15].
- **Robert Bunsen (1811-1899).** In 1834, with Berthold, he found hydrated ferric oxide as an arsenic antidote (DSB). In 1843 a cacodyl cyanide explosion cost him his right eye, and he was badly poisoned by arsenic [S46, S47, S48]. **[details differ: the Science History Institute says cacodyl chloride; Babcock (1882) gives 1841 and cyanide of cacodyl; the DSB says 1843 and cacodyl cyanide, which is followed here]**
- **Johann Gottlob Lehmann (d. 1767).** An 1882 list has him killed by arsenical fume from a crucible [S15]. Later biography says he died of a bilious fever and that the arsenic story was rumour [S49]. This is kept as an example of how the trade's death-lore grows.

**Checks in WOTR.**
- *Draft-mark* (Alchemetrica l. 247; Codex I l. 45): the "pale bands across the nails that no washing lifts" are real Mees' lines. They are consistent with Furveus giving white arsenic in small measure (Codex I l. 145). A real Mees' line grows out in 5 to 6 months, so a band that "no washing lifts" is right, and a band that never grows out would mean repeated dosing.
- The grey nail-beds and scleral colour have no clean real counterpart for mercury or arsenic. The nearest are silver (argyria: slate-grey skin, nails and conjunctiva, described from the 1840s [S50]) and lead's blue gum line (§3). Draft-mark is WOTR's own sign.
- The idea that a renderer can read a career off the hands has a real ground. Nitric-acid yellowing of skin and nails (the xanthoproteic reaction, noted by Glauber in 1648-58 [S51]), Mees' lines, Burton's line and argyria are each specific to one substance.

## 3. Lead at the bench
- **Where it comes from.** Cupellation and litharge, lead-chamber vitriol, lead vessels. In 1656 Samuel Stockhausen of Goslar blamed the smelters' "Hüttenkatze" on litharge fume, and he is sometimes called the first occupational physician [S52]. In 1767 Sir George Baker traced the Devonshire colic to lead in cider presses [S53].
- **Signs.**
  - Colic with obstinate constipation (painter's colic).
  - Wrist-drop: a motor neuropathy that weakens the forearm extensors. It is a late sign of chronic heavy exposure.
  - Anaemia.
  - Burton's line: a narrow blue-black line of lead sulphide along the gum margin. Henry Burton described it in 1840 in *Medico-Chirurgical Transactions* [S53, S54, S55].
- **Blood-lead figures (modern).** Gastrointestinal colic occurs at 8 to about 100 µg/dL. Neurobehavioural effects in workers appear at 40 to 120 µg/dL. Adult encephalopathy occurs at 100 to 120 µg/dL [S55].

**Checks in WOTR.** No WOTR condition is keyed to lead. If Draft-mark is ever keyed to lead, Burton's gum line is the real sign. Otherwise WOTR has no counterpart.

## 4. Antimony at the bench
- **Tartar emetic (antimony potassium tartrate).** Mynsicht described it in 1631.
  - Emetic dose: about 30 to 65 mg [S56, S57].
  - Fatal dose: 0.2 to 0.5 g, with death usually within 24 hours. One death is recorded at 130 mg, and a child survived 2.3 g [S56, S57].
  - Oliver Goldsmith (d. 1774) died after about 132 to 198 mg of antimony, following 18 hours of vomiting and purging [S57].
- **Occupational signs.** "Antimony spots": itchy papules around the sweat glands, commonest in summer, after months of exposure to dust or fume. Also antimony pneumoconiosis, with fine dotted opacities on X-ray, and chronic cough and bronchitis [S57, S58].
- **The antimony war.** In 1566 the Paris Faculty of Medicine declared antimony a poison. The Parlement of Paris lifted the ban in 1665-66 [S56, S59]. **[the reversal year is given as 1665 or 1666]** Antimonial cups, left overnight full of wine, were used as purges [S59].

**Checks in WOTR.** *Vein cough* (Alchemetrica l. 248: "from calcination fume, particularly sulphide roast", twenty-year onset, chronic) has real counterparts in:
- smelter chronic bronchitis from SO₂ and particulates, a recognised compensable disease. Chronic 0.4 to 2 ppm is associated with faster FEV₁ loss [S60];
- antimony pneumoconiosis [S57];
- Paracelsus's *Von der Bergsucht* (written c. 1533-34, printed 1567), the first monograph on miners' and smelters' lung disease [S61].

The twenty-year onset is WOTR's own figure. The real disease is dose-dependent chronic obstructive disease.

## 5. Fumes and acids

### 5.1 Nitric acid fume (nitrous fume, NO₂): the delayed killer
- **What happens.** A red-brown gas with a sharp smell. Because it dissolves poorly in water, it spares the upper airway and reaches deep. After a symptom-free interval of **3 to 30 hours**, pulmonary oedema follows. Bronchiolitis obliterans can follow weeks later [S62, S63].
- **Thresholds.** NIOSH IDLH is 20 ppm. The OSHA ceiling is 5 ppm [S62].
- **Case.** In 1863 an Edinburgh professor, Stewart, broke a vessel of fuming nitric acid. Breathing became hard within a few hours, and he and his assistant both died [S15]. **[single]**

### 5.2 Oil of vitriol (sulphuric acid)
- **Dilution.** Pouring water onto the concentrated acid boils the water at the interface and throws acid. Mixing 100 ml of each at 19 °C goes past 131 °C in under a minute [S64].

### 5.3 Arsine and other gases from reductions
See Gehlen (§2.5). Arsine comes from zinc and acid acting on arsenical material, the same chemistry as the Marsh test (§7).

**Checks in WOTR.**
- *Strongwater* (Alchemetrica l. 197, 213) is a real match for nitric acid: distilled from saltpetre and vitriol, with red-orange fume in the receiver. The claim that its fume is the commonest killer in the trade is consistent with NO₂.
- The fault. Alchemetrica says a failed lute's fume "kills everybody in it in the order they were standing nearest". The real killer is usually **delayed by hours**. Men walk out of the room and drown in their beds that night (3 to 30 h). Nearest-first holds for how big a dose each man took, not for when he dies. A fire-scene or table ruling should use the delay.
- *Blackstone Oil* (l. 213: meets water violently enough to break the vessel) matches oil of vitriol's heat of dilution, >131 °C in under a minute.
- *Salt-spirit* (l. 214) is hydrochloric acid. Its "least feared, most ruined hands" line has no measured real counterpart. **[no figure found]**

## 7. The tests (inside the 1800-1900 span)
- **Before Marsh.**
  - Scheele (1775) turned arsenic trioxide into garlic-smelling arsine with zinc and acid.
  - Metzger and Rose (to 1806) had reduction and precipitation tests that were not sensitive enough for a court.
  - In the Bodle trial (1832), Marsh's hydrogen-sulphide yellow precipitate had spoiled by the time it reached the jury, and Bodle was acquitted [S74, S75].
- **Marsh test (1836, James Marsh, Royal Arsenal, Woolwich).**
  - Method: digest the sample in acid, add zinc, and lead the arsine through a heated tube or onto cold porcelain. It leaves a silvery-black mirror of arsenic.
  - Sensitivity: about 0.02 mg.
  - Antimony gives a similar mirror. The two are told apart by solubility (hypochlorite dissolves the arsenic mirror).
  - It remained the standard until the 1970s [S74, S75].
  - Orfila used it to convict Marie Lafarge in 1840, the first conviction resting mainly on direct toxicological evidence [S74, S76].
- **Reinsch test (1841, Hugo Reinsch).** Heat bright copper foil in the acidified sample. Arsenic, antimony, bismuth and mercury deposit as a grey-black film, which is then sublimed off to identify it. It is quicker than Marsh and is used as a screen [S77].
- **Antidotes of the period.**
  - Hydrated ferric oxide for arsenic (Bunsen and Berthold 1834) [S46, S47].
  - Egg white for corrosive sublimate (Thénard 1825) [S15]. **[single]**

**Checks in WOTR.** No WOTR canon I read names a test for a poison. The Imperial Age span (R53-01) allows the Marsh (1836) and Reinsch (1841) tests. Any WOTR analogue would be new canon and is not assumed here.

## 8. The whole-career picture
- **Real lifespans.** Scheele died at 43 [S14]. Glauber was disabled at about 56 and dead at 66 [S13]. Mercury miners, gilders and hatters were typically disabled rather than killed.
- **Real reform: ventilation and removal.**
  - In 1818 d'Arcet won the Ravrio prize of 3,000 francs from the Académie with a gilders' forge whose draught carried the fume away (*Mémoire sur l'art de dorer le bronze*) [S78, S79].
  - Electroplating (Elkington, 1840) ended fire-gilding [S19].
  - The St Isaac's Cathedral dome gilding is said to have killed 60 workers [S19, S80]. **[weakly sourced; both trace to unverifiable web articles]**
- **Real prognosis.** Get the man off the bench and into air, and the mouth heals in weeks (Triumph, 16 to 27 days). The tremor may fade over years or leave residual signs (§1.4).

**Checks in WOTR.** Alchemetrica l. 252 has the trade's private estimate of "twenty good years" for a pure alchemist, and says the Archives have not gathered the figures. That is consistent with the real record, which has no career-length statistic for alchemists. Scheele (about 25 working years) and Glauber (about 35 before disability) are the nearest real points. WOTR's "the quiet" (l. 249) and law-fragment burn (l. 250) have **no real counterpart**. The flattened affect of "the quiet" resembles erethism's apathy and depression only loosely [S2]. Keep it WOTR's own.

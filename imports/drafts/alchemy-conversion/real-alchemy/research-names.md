## 1. Decknamen: why the cover-names were deliberate

### 1.1 What the term means and who coined the analysis

- A *Deckname* (plural *Decknamen*) is literally a "cover name": a word that hides the identity of a substance or operation. The Newton Project glossary gives the example "hermaphroditical body" for the martial regulus of antimony [NEWTON-GLOSS]. The German school (Julius Ruska, E. O. von Lippmann) argued in the early twentieth century that alchemy's allusive terms form exactly such a secret vocabulary; Ruska and Wiedemann published "Alchemistische Decknamen" in 1923 [NEWMAN-1996 p.160].
- Newman's 1996 paper is the standard demolition of the rival Jungian reading (that alchemical images were projections of the unconscious rather than chemistry). He decodes Starkey/Philalethes' most obscure allegory using only texts inside the Philalethes corpus and concludes the imagery concealed laboratory practice on purpose [NEWMAN-1996 pp.159-188]. Principe and Newman's joint position is the same: the images are chemistry written in code [PRINCIPE-2013; SMITHSONIAN-2014].

### 1.2 The concealment toolkit (four named devices)

| Device | Real definition | Sources |
|---|---|---|
| Decknamen | Cover-names for substances, operations, apparatus | [NEWMAN-1996; NEWMAN-PUP-2018; NEWTON-GLOSS] |
| *Dispersion de la science* | Break off a process at the crucial point and resume it somewhere else in the text, or in another text; the reader reassembles it. The term is Paul Kraus's (from his 1942-43 study of the Jabirian corpus). | [NEWMAN-1996 p.164] **[single-sourced for the Kraus attribution]**; dispersion as a device also in [NEWMAN-PUP-2018] |
| Syncope | Newman's term: an elliptical description that leaves out a step, substance or piece of apparatus in order to conceal. His example: Philalethes' short recipe for the philosophical mercury that omits both antimony and silver. | [NEWMAN-1996 p.187; NEWMAN-PUP-2018] |
| Parathesis | Newman's term. In 1996: the heaping-up of synonyms for one process, substance or apparatus to bewilder the reader (Philalethes' profusion of names for antimony). In 2018 he glosses it more loosely as adding unnecessary terms and processes. | [NEWMAN-1996 p.187; NEWMAN-PUP-2018] |

- Newman says he took "syncope" and "parathesis" from Greek and changed their usual sense for this purpose [NEWMAN-1996 p.187]. They are a modern historian's labels, not period terms.
- **Why conceal.** Secrets were lucrative and not to be given away lightly [NEWMAN-1996 p.165]. Craft secrecy passed recipes only to those judged worthy [SHI-MEYER-2014]. Gold-making could debase coin: rulers are thought to have used alchemists to debase their own coinage while forbidding others to do it [NOVA-NEWMAN-2005]. The reader who could decode also had to see that the writer was an adept: commentators decoded older authors to claim their authority [NEWMAN-1996 pp.164-165].
- **Starkey's two registers.** Under the pseudonym Eirenaeus Philalethes, Starkey published the process in allegory. In a 1651 letter to Robert Boyle he gave the same process in plain English (the "Key into Antimony"). Newman found and published that letter: Newman, "Newton's Clavis as Starkey's Key", *Isis* 78 (1987) 564-574 [NEWMAN-1987; NOVA-GUIDE; NEWMAN-1996 p.184].
- **Meanings drift between authors.** The same cover-name meant different things to different writers. Newton's *Index chemicus* lists several meanings for "Green Lion", each tied to a named author [NEWMAN-PUP-2018]. Starkey himself let his referents drift: he used Saturn for stibnite, not lead, and it took Newton years to see it [NOVA-GUIDE; NEWTON-GLOSS "Saturn"].

**WOTR check.** Alchemetrica's *Cover-Names* section matches the real practice on four points: the Green Lion standing for vitriol "or for something else entirely depending on which house's book" (real: Newton's Index lists several meanings, and Ripley, Philalethes and others used it differently, see 1.3); "Our Lead" for antimony (real: Philalethan Saturn = stibnite, 1.3); syncope as an omitted step (real, Newman); dispersion across several texts (real, Kraus/Newman). **One divergence:** Alchemetrica defines parathesis as a false step inserted that a knowledgeable reader will recognise as absurd and skip. Newman's parathesis is the piling-up of synonyms (1996), or more loosely the adding of unnecessary terms and processes (2018). Neither definition is quite "a false step the reader spots as absurd". The Grey Bride, the Winter Salt and the Third Cask are WOTR's own names and have no real referent to check. The Master Codex Alchemical Index carries no cover-names; Alchemetrica is the only WOTR canon for them.

### 1.3 Named examples decoded

**The grey wolf (Basil Valentine, First Key).**
- Text: the *Twelve Keys* appeared in German in 1599 (Johann Thölde); the woodcuts first appeared in the 1602 edition; Maier gave a Latin version in *Tripus Aureus*, 1618 [WP-12KEYS]. The First Key tells the reader to take a ravenous grey wolf, feed it the body of the king, then burn the wolf to ashes, and the king comes back [WP-12KEYS; HOPKINSON-2026 reviewing PRINCIPE-2013].
- Decoded: wolf = stibnite (antimony sulfide ore); king = gold. The recipe is the old assayer's way of purifying gold with antimony sulfide [PRINCIPE-2013 via HOPKINSON-2026 and WP-12KEYS, citing pp.144, 152-153]. Newman notes that antimony sulfide "kills" other metals and absorbs them in gold refining, citing Agricola's *De re metallica* (Hoover translation, 1950, pp.237-239, 451-452) [NEWMAN-1996 p.183].
- Real metallurgy: parting by stibnite is first described in the *Probierbüchlein* (assaying booklets, early sixteenth century). The stibnite is stable at higher temperature than plain sulfur. The base metals and silver go into sulfides; the gold is left. The process is quicker than salt cementation and gives purer gold, but it can carry some gold off [CRADDOCK-2000 via WP-PARTING]. That the wolf is then "burned" matches heating the gold-antimony button in air until the antimony fumes off as oxide. **That last step is my reading of standard metallurgy; I did not find a historian saying it in these words.**
- Replication: Principe tested chemical readings of several of the Keys in the laboratory, using the 1602 woodcuts as guides, and argues that the early Keys encode real techniques and results [WP-12KEYS citing PRINCIPE-2013 pp.152-153, 157-158].

**The green lion.**
- Ripley's version (the *Bosom Book*, printed in *Collectanea Chymica*, London: William Cooper, 1683; attributed to Ripley, but Rampling treats the Bosom Book as a sixteenth-century compilation, not Ripley's own [CALCINATIONS; RAMPLING-2014]). Primary figures: 30 lb of "sericon" is dissolved, 1 lb to a gallon of twice-distilled vinegar, to make about 21 lb of gum. The filtered liquor is evaporated in a water bath (*balneum Mariae*) until the sericon sets into a green gum called "our Green Lion". The gum is then distilled from a well-luted retort. First comes a faint water, then a white fume, then a reddish fume, then drops "like blood". A white frost-like sublimate forms in the neck, and a black soot-like residue ("our Dragon") is left. The blood-red liquor is put to putrefy for fourteen days [BOSOM-1683, primary].
- Decoded: sericon was an impure red lead (lead oxide, minium). The green lion is the green gum it gives with vinegar: crude lead acetate ("sugar of lead") [RAMPLING-2014; LARB-2021; CALCINATIONS]. Rampling traces how the identity of sericon was reinterpreted between the fifteenth and seventeenth centuries: later readers took it as antimony, which is why the 1683 print says "sericon, or antimony" [RAMPLING-2014; BOSOM-1683].
- The real chemistry of the distillate: dry distillation of lead acetate gives acetone. Libavius first recorded this in *Alchymia* (1606, p.123) [WP-ACETONE]. The "ardent water" in the Bosom Book's later separation step fits acetone. **The identification of Ripley's "ardent water" as acetone is my inference from the chemistry; I did not find it stated by a historian in what I could read.**
- Other green lions. Ripley's own verse distinguishes a "green lion of fools", common vitriol, from the philosophers' lion [CALCINATIONS] **[single-sourced]**. In Philalethes the green lion is crude antimony, also called the Babylonian Dragon, "killing all by means of his venom" [NEWMAN-1996 pp.181-184]. The popular gloss that the green lion devouring the sun is aqua regia dissolving gold appears on Wikipedia and in blogs without a scholarly source [WP-SUNS; DISTILLATIO-2013]. **[Unconfirmed; treat as a modern gloss.]** Lyndy Abraham's *Dictionary of Alchemical Imagery* (CUP, 1998) ties the green lion to matter that reduces metals toward their first matter [DISTILLATIO-2013 citing ABRAHAM-1998] **[single-sourced]**.

**The red king and the white queen (the wedding).**
- The *Rosarium philosophorum* (Frankfurt, 1550, part II of *De alchimia opuscula*) carries twenty woodcuts with German captions that show the sun-king and moon-queen in conjunction [WP-ROSARIUM]. The standard reading is that king and queen are gold and silver and, at the level of principles, sulfur and mercury [LEARNRELIGIONS; WP-SUNS]. **This general gloss rests on popular sources; the specific, historian-decoded case is Philalethes, below.**
- Decoded in Philalethes (Newman): the King is gold. He weds the Water-bearer's daughter, who is the philosophical mercury. Both "take on the color of silver" as he is dissolved into her; they die, putrefy through livid, black, bluish and yellowish, and lie in a black tomb that promises resurrection if the fire is kept constant [NEWMAN-1996 pp.169-171, 186]. In the *Open Entrance* the same event is written as a regimen: the Regimen of Mercury "despoils the King of his golden garments" [OPEN-ENTRANCE ch.XXV, primary]. The real operation is dissolving gold in the prepared mercury in a sealed vessel and heating it for months (section 3).

**The grey wolf's cousins in the Philalethes corpus (all decoded by Newman).**
- "Saturnia", the daughter of Saturn, is crude antimony. A poem describes the ore as metalline, sable, with silver veins, which Newman calls an unequivocal description of stibnite [NEWMAN-1996 pp.177-178].
- "The most warlike God" who dwells in the house of Aries is Mars, that is iron: the ore is to be reduced with iron [NEWMAN-1996 p.179].
- "Cadmus" and his blood are iron and its sulfurous part. The green lion eating the companions of Cadmus is the reduction of antimony by iron [NEWMAN-1996 p.184]. Newman reads Newton's own notebook decoding of the Cadmus myth the same way: Cadmus is iron, the dragon is stibnite [NEWMAN-PUP-2018].
- "The menstrual blood of the sordid whore" is metallic antimony drawn from its ore [NOVA-NEWMAN-2005]. Chemistry World lists "grey wolf" and "sordid whore" as names for stibnite, and Paracelsus's "White Lead of the Wise Men" for antimony [CW-PAGE].
- "The Net": Vulcan catching Mars and Venus in a bronze net becomes an alloy of copper with the star regulus [NOVA-GUIDE; NOVA-NEWMAN-2005]. Newman made it in a crucible [NOVA-NEWMAN-2005].
- "The cold dragon" that creeps in and out of caves is saltpetre [SMITHSONIAN-2014 reporting Principe] **[single-sourced]**.
- "Stinking spirit": usually ammonium carbonate in Starkey's notebooks [NEWTON-GLOSS]; Chemistry World gives sulfur for the same phrase in another author [CW-PAGE]. A second example of drift.

**The eagle.**
- In the Philalethes treatises an eagle is one cycle of the mercury's preparation. Primary: "every single preparation of the Mercury with its arsenic is one Eagle"; the mercury is purged "from their crow-like blackness" and made to fly the seventh flight, and may be taken to the tenth [PREP-SOPHIC, primary]. The glossary gives "sophic mercury of seven eagles" as mercury distilled seven times [NEWTON-GLOSS]. One eagle took about half a day by Starkey's improved method with vinegar and sea salt [PREP-SOPHIC].
- Wider usage: birds stand for volatile matter rising in the vessel (sublimation, distillation) [ABRAHAM-1998 via LAFONTAINE] **[single-sourced]**. I did not find a scholarly source for the common claim that "the eagle" is sal ammoniac. **[Unconfirmed.]**

**The Philosophers' Mercury (sophic mercury), decoded and replicated.**
- Plain process, from Starkey's 1651 letter and the Philalethes texts. Reduce stibnite with iron to metallic antimony (the "martial regulus"). Cool it slowly under a thick slag and it crystallises with a star on its face (the "star regulus"). Fuse the regulus with silver ("Diana's doves"; later he used copper). Amalgamate that with quicksilver. Purge and distil the amalgam repeatedly, each cycle an "eagle" [NOVA-GUIDE; NEWMAN-1996 pp.181-186; NEWTON-GLOSS "Regulus stellatus"; PREP-SOPHIC]. The mercury would then dissolve gold and make it "vegetate" into tree-like growths [NOVA-GUIDE].
- Why the silver matters: Hertodt von Todtenfeldt (1678) was stuck on Diana's doves until he found in Alexander von Suchten that the star regulus will not amalgamate with mercury without silver. Starkey's letter says the same [NEWMAN-1996 p.184]. This is a clean case of syncope: the published recipes leave the silver out.
- Real figures:
  - Primary [PREP-SOPHIC]: one part "Fiery Dragon" (iron) to two parts "Magnetical Body" (stibnite) gave about 8 oz of "true arsenic" (the regulus) at the first fusion. That regulus was then married to silver, 1 part to 2.
  - *The Marrow of Alchemy*: two parts of "Saturn's Son" to one of Cadmus, purified through four reiterations until the star shows [NEWMAN-1996 p.184 n.82, quoting the Marrow].
  - Carolus de Maets' decipherment (1675-76): 1 oz martial regulus fused with 2 oz cupelled silver, then 3 oz of the purest mercury [NEWMAN-1996 p.186 n.89, from British Library MS Sloane 1238].
- Replication: Principe reconstructed Starkey's recipe. He mixed the prepared mercury with gold to a buttery lump, sealed the flask and buried it in a heated sand bath. By morning the flask held a glittering, fully formed tree [SMITHSONIAN-2014; PRINCIPE-2013]. **[The overnight timing is single-sourced, from the Smithsonian report.]** Principe also made the star regulus by heating stibnite with iron and saltpetre; the crystal star forms as the metal cools under slag [PRINCETON-DPUL].
- The Newton-copied manuscript "Experiments for the Preparation of the Sophick Mercury ... by Luna and the Antimonial-Stellate-Regulus of Mars" (printed London, 1677 under Starkey's name) resurfaced at auction in 2016 [WP-STARKEY; CW-2016; NATGEO-2016].
- Related real phenomenon: Diana's tree (the *arbor Dianae*), a dendritic silver amalgam grown from mercury in silver nitrate. Lemery's recipe (*Cours de chymie*, 1675): 1 oz silver in aqua fortis, diluted with about 20 oz water, plus 2 oz mercury, left for forty days. Homberg's fast method took about a quarter of an hour [WP-DIANA] **[single-sourced; the Lemery and Homberg figures should be checked against the primary texts]**.

**Decoding by replication, and the impurity lesson.** Principe found that a Basil Valentine recipe (glass of antimony, from the *Triumphal Chariot*) failed with pure laboratory reagents. It worked with natural Hungarian stibnite, which carries silica, the ore Valentine had specified. The "error" was a cover for an impurity that mattered [HOPKINSON-2026; PRINCIPE-1987]. A second case: a stirring rod of iron put iron compounds into a product that its maker believed was something else [HOPKINSON-2026] **[single-sourced]**. Rampling likewise ran Ripley's lead process under a fume hood [LARB-2021; RAMPLING-2024].

**WOTR check.** Alchemetrica's "Our Lead" for antimony is historically exact (Philalethan Saturn = stibnite). WOTR canon has no grey wolf, no eagle, no red king or white queen, no star regulus and no philosophers' tree. The Codex's Wedding (Vol I: Furveus pours the Sulphur into the Salt and then the Mercury in one movement; Alchemetrica: stage 6, "fast, decisive", where a Rupture happens) corresponds in name to the real conjunction or chemical wedding. In real practice, though, the wedding was a slow sealed digestion over weeks (section 3), not a quick pour. The quick pour belongs to the spagyric recombination (Newton glossary: *spagyria* is the separation of compound bodies into their constituents and their recombination) [NEWTON-GLOSS]. That is the line the Codex uses, so it is consistent. Checking the pour's speed belongs to the process lane.

## 2. The colours of the Work as real observed changes

### 2.1 The schemes

- **Four colours, Greek origin.** Blackening (*melanosis*, Latin *nigredo*), whitening (*leukosis*, *albedo*), yellowing (*xanthosis*, *citrinitas*), and reddening or purpling (*iosis*, *rubedo*). The scheme is traced to the Greek corpus: Zosimos attributes it to Maria, and it appears in the *Physika kai Mystika* of pseudo-Democritus [WP-OPUS] **[single-sourced for the pseudo-Democritus and Maria attributions]**.
- **Three colours, late medieval and after.** After the fifteenth century many writers folded yellowing into reddening and counted three stages [WP-OPUS]. Rampling notes that yellowing appears often in medieval recipes but interested commentators less than blackening, whitening and reddening [RAMPLING-2024].
- **The peacock's tail** (*cauda pavonis*), a show of many colours, is sometimes added [WP-OPUS]. Its place varies. The *Open Entrance* sums the Work up as black, the peacock's tail, white, orange, red. Its regimens put "all conceivable colours" in the Regimen of Jupiter, and the rainbow and the peacock's tail in the Regimen of Mars [OPEN-ENTRANCE ch.XXIX, primary].
- **Stage lists.** The step lists (seven, twelve or fourteen operations) vary in number, order and names from author to author. Ripley's twelve gates run: calcination, solution, separation, conjunction, putrefaction, congelation, cibation, sublimation, fermentation, exaltation, multiplication, projection [WP-OPUS; NEWMAN-1996 p.166].

### 2.2 Real reactions behind the colours (what historians have identified)

| Colour event | Real chemistry | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Whitening of copper | Mercury and arsenic vapours whiten copper to a silver colour (an arsenical or mercury surface alloy). Ancient craftsmen knew this. | [PRINCIPE-2013 pp.14-17, excerpt in SHI-2013] |
| Yellowing of copper | Vapour from heated cadmia (calamine, a zinc ore) turns copper golden by making brass | [PRINCIPE-2013 pp.14-17] |
| White + white = yellow | Zosimos (c. 300 AD) was puzzled that white sulfur vapour, absorbed by white mercury, gave a yellow product, and that both became fixed (non-volatile). Principe calls this a basic chemical principle: a compound does not average its ingredients' properties. | [PRINCIPE-2013 pp.14-17] |
| Black to red (vermilion) | Mercury and sulfur heated in a sealed long-necked glass first give black mercury sulfide (metacinnabar). On sublimation the fumes run straw, then yellow, then red, and red cinnabar (vermilion) collects. Simonini links this sequence to the alchemical colour stages. | [SIMONINI-2025] **[single-sourced for the recipe colour sequence; the black-to-red HgS change itself is standard chemistry]** |
| Black to citrine (Ripley's lead) | The black, soot-like residue left after distilling lead acetate is spread on a stone and touched with a coal. The fire "glides through" it within half an hour and leaves a citrine calx, "very glorious to behold". Real chemistry: the residue is finely divided lead and carbon, which is pyrophoric (catches fire in air) and burns to yellow lead oxide. Rampling reconstructed this in the laboratory and saw the black turn orange-yellow. | primary [BOSOM-1683]; reconstruction [LARB-2021; RAMPLING-2024]; pyrophoric lead [WP-PYRO] |
| Red "blood" | Reddish fumes and blood-red drops at the end of the lead acetate distillation | primary [BOSOM-1683] |
| Black to white (amalgam) | Starkey purges the regulus-silver-mercury amalgam from "crow-like blackness" to "lunary whiteness". The black is dross (oxides and sulfides) washed out with vinegar and salt. | primary [PREP-SOPHIC]; reading **[mine, from the chemistry]** |
| Trees | The prepared mercury and gold grow a dendritic tree in a sealed, heated flask. The *Open Entrance* puts "the philosophical tree" in the Regimen of Venus and "tiny silver trees, with twigs and leaves" in the Regimen of the Moon. | replication [SMITHSONIAN-2014]; primary [OPEN-ENTRANCE chs.XXVII-XXIX] |
| Peacock's tail | **No historian I could reach identifies the chemistry.** The plausible physics is thin-film interference (iridescent oxide or oily films on a melt or residue), but that is a guess found only in popular sources. | [unconfirmed] |

### 2.3 The regimen as timed in the sealed egg (Philalethes, *Open Entrance*, 1667; English in Waite, *Hermetic Museum*, 1893)

Primary figures, all as Philalethes gives them [OPEN-ENTRANCE]:
- Charge: one part common gold, well purified, to three parts "our Mercury", thoroughly purged.
- **Regimen of Mercury**, about 40-50 days. Colours begin about the 20th day. A "most amiable greenness" appears about the end of the fourth week and lasts about ten days, then the whole goes black as coal.
- **Regimen of Saturn**, black as pitch, "when the Lion dies the Crow is born". Newman gives it forty days [NEWMAN-1996 p.176].
- **Regimen of Jupiter**, three weeks: all colours, ending in a snowy white streaky deposit on the glass.
- **Regimen of the Moon**, three weeks: white. It liquefies and coagulates many times a day and shows fishes' eyes and tiny silver trees.
- **Regimen of Venus**, forty days: green, with the philosophical tree. Immoderate heat will turn the green black.
- **Regimen of Mars**: light yellow or dirty brown, then rainbow and peacock's tail.
- **Regimen of the Sun**: red.

Warnings that recur in the text: keep the heat gentle, do not shake the vessel, do not burn the flowers. Ripley's Bosom Book gives the same warning at the gum stage: "burn not his flowers" [BOSOM-1683].

Scale: these are Starkey's claimed stages and times, not measurements. The only modern replication I found (Principe's tree) shows real dendritic growth, but reports it happening overnight, not over forty days **[single-sourced timing]**.

**WOTR check.** Alchemetrica's *Reading the Colour* uses the four-colour scheme: the Blacking, the Whitening, the Yellowing, the Reddening. That is the Greek four, not the late-Latin three. Real points it gets right:
- blackness as putrefaction, expected and welcome. Philalethes calls the black "an image of eternal death" and yet a sight that gladdens the sage [OPEN-ENTRANCE ch.XXV];
- colours read through glass in a sealed vessel (the real philosophical egg: Alchemetrica's Standing Egg);
- heat mismanagement ruining the sequence. Real texts warn that too much heat turns green to black and "burns the flowers"; Alchemetrica says colours arriving too fast mean abandon.

WOTR has no peacock's tail, no green stage, and no regimen durations tied to colours. The Codex's spagyric Whitening (Vol I: a ferment that went black "as it should" in three turns of warm dung, then most of a month in the Returner, the Whitening read as "pale and pearly") follows the real black-then-white order. The real sources in this lane describe the whitening of metals and amalgams, not of a plant ferment, so the pearly colour itself has no counterpart here. The process lane should check it against spagyric sources.

## 3. The Emerald Tablet: textual history

### 3.1 Arabic versions

- **Oldest: the *Kitāb Sirr al-Khalīqa wa-Ṣanʿat al-Ṭabīʿa*** (*Book of the Secret of Creation and the Art of Nature*, also *Kitāb al-ʿIlal*, *Book of Causes*), attributed to Balīnās (pseudo-Apollonius of Tyana). The Tablet is an appendix to it.
  - Dating: Kraus (1943) puts it c. 813-833; Weisser (1980) c. 750-800; Ruska (1926) earlier, sixth to eighth century [WP-TABLET]. A second summary agrees that it was finished by the caliphate of al-Maʾmūn (813-833) [AWS-TABLET].
  - Ruska found and published this version (1925-1926) [WP-TABLET; AWS-TABLET].
  - It is also the first text to set out the sulfur-mercury theory of metals [WP-TABLET] **[single-sourced]**.
- **The Jabirian version**, in the *Kitāb Usṭuqus al-Uss al-Thānī* (*Second Book of the Element of the Foundation*), attributed to Jābir ibn Ḥayyān. Holmyard found and translated this version in 1923 [WP-TABLET; AWS-TABLET; SEARCH-HOLMYARD].
- A further Arabic version sits in the *Sirr al-Asrār* (*Secret of Secrets*), a tenth-century pseudo-Aristotelian mirror for princes [WP-TABLET; AWS-TABLET].

### 3.2 Latin versions

- **Hugo of Santalla** translated the *Book of the Secret of Creation* as *Liber de secretis naturae*, c. 1145-1151 [WP-TABLET; AWS-TABLET].
- The *Secretum secretorum*: a partial translation by Johannes Hispalensis c. 1140, the full one by Philip of Tripoli in the thirteenth century (c. 1243) [AWS-TABLET; WP-TABLET].
- **The "vulgate"**: the most widely copied version, from a twelfth-century compilation (*Liber Hermetis de alchimia*). Steele and Singer tentatively named Plato of Tivoli as translator; later scholarship calls that conjecture [WP-TABLET citing Mandosio 2004]. Selwood (2023) repeats the Plato of Tivoli attribution and ties it to the *Secretum*; Wikipedia's editors call this erroneous [SELWOOD-2023; WP-TABLET]. **Contested.**
- The translator did not understand the Arabic *ṭilasm* (talisman) and transliterated it as *telesmus*. The resulting word became one of alchemy's most debated terms [WP-TABLET] **[single-sourced]**.
- Hortulanus' fourteenth-century commentary, the most widespread, read the text as a recipe for the stone [WP-TABLET; AWS-TABLET].
- First printing: *De alchemia*, Nuremberg, Johannes Petreius, 1541, edited by "Chrysogonus Polydorus" (probably Andreas Osiander), with Hortulanus' commentary [WP-TABLET; FR-WP-TABLET].
- A French verse version appeared in the fifteenth century, reworked as a sonnet by Clovis Hesteau de Nuysement (see 3.4) [WP-TABLET; FR-WP-TABLET].
- Newton's English translation (King's College Cambridge, Keynes MS 28), about 1680 per Dobbs [AWS-TABLET citing Dobbs 1988; WP-TABLET citing Newton 2010, ed. Newman].

### 3.3 A real textual difference the Codex already carries

The Arabic (Holmyard) says the above is **from** the below and the below **from** the above: derivation. The Latin vulgate (Steele and Singer) says the above is **like** the below: likeness [HOLMYARD-1923 via AWS-TABLET and WP-TABLET; STEELE-SINGER-1928 via WP-TABLET]. **This comparison is my reading of the two texts.** The Jabirian version also has no "three parts of the wisdom" line. It ends with the speaker saying he has explained the whole in two of his books [AWS-TABLET]. The Trismegistus line exists only in the Latin line of descent.

**WOTR check.** Codex Vol I is consistent with this:
- Venur's Arabic-derived recitation is the "from" version with no Trismegistus claim;
- only Mu-jin's recitation, the Heralds' version from the Latin vulgate, carries the line that gives the Monastery its "Thrice-Great" epithet;
- Mu-jin reads the principle as a mirror, which is the Latin "like". The Appendix's "Eastern and Western ... one relation in two grammars" sits squarely on the real Arabic/Latin difference.

### 3.4 The three English renderings in Codex Vol I: translators, dates, rights

Diffed word by word against the sources (script in the scratchpad):

**(1) Venur's recitation, beginning "Truth. Certainty. That in which there is no doubt"**
- Translator: **Eric John Holmyard** (1891-1959), "The Emerald Table", *Nature* 112 (no. 2814), 6 October 1923, pp.525-526, doi:10.1038/112525a0 [CROSSREF; WP-TABLET attributing the English to Holmyard 1923 and the Arabic to Zirnis 1979; AWS-TABLET "Translations from Jabir ibn Hayyan ... [Holmyard 1923]"].
- Source text: the **Jabirian** *Kitāb Usṭuqus al-Uss al-Thānī*, not the *Sirr al-Khalīqa*. The Jabirian text itself opens by saying Balīnās mentions the engraving, so Balīnās is named inside it [AWS-TABLET]. **Correction for the Vol I notes (line 172):** they say the Tablet "as preserved in the Kitāb Sirr al-Khalīqa ... and in the Jabirian corpus". Holmyard's translation is of the Jabirian version only; Ruska found the *Sirr al-Khalīqa* version in 1925-26.
- Codex edits to Holmyard: "Truth! Certainty! ... doubt!" is given with full stops; "one [thing]" loses its brackets (Holmyard as printed on AWS reads "working the miracles of one"); "and the Wind nourished it in her belly" is cut to "and the Wind nourished it". Otherwise the wording is Holmyard's.
- Rights: **public domain in the United States** (published 1923; US works published before 1931 are public domain as of 2026). **Still in copyright in the UK and EU until 31 December 2029** (life + 70; Holmyard died 13 October 1959 [WP-HOLMYARD] **[single-sourced death date]**). A credited short quotation may fall under UK fair dealing for quotation; that is a legal judgement, not made here.

**(2) Penn's recitation, beginning "It is a certain point full of admiration"**
- The source poem: the anonymous French verse Tablet as reworked into a **sonnet by Clovis Hesteau de Nuysement** (c. 1550/60 to c. 1623/24), in *Traittez de l'harmonie, et constitution generalle du vray sel, secret des Philosophes, & de l'esprit universel du monde*. English Wikipedia dates it 1621 and cites a 1639 edition, p.10 [WP-TABLET]. French Wikipedia lists the *Traittez* as Paris, J. Périer and A. Buisard, 1620 [FR-WP-NUYSEMENT]. **Date conflict, 1620 or 1621; unresolved.** The French opens "C'est un point assuré plein d'admiration".
- The English: an unsigned **"literal translation" by English Wikipedia editors**, printed beside the French in the Wikipedia article "Emerald Tablet". It is absent from the Wayback capture of 22 May 2023, present in captures from 20 August 2023 to 25 December 2024, and replaced by 1 June 2025 with a revised rendering beginning "It's a sure point, full of admiration" [WAYBACK-WP 20230522, 20230820, 20240609, 20241225, 20250601; WP-TABLET current]. The Codex text matches the 2023-24 wording almost word for word. It cuts the second quatrain (the parents, Phoebus, Diana, the air and the earth) and runs the lines together as prose.
- Rights: the French (1620/21) is **public domain**. The English is **not public domain**: Wikipedia text is licensed **CC BY-SA 4.0**, which requires attribution (the article and its contributors) and share-alike. Whether a strictly literal translation is original enough to attract copyright is arguable; the safe reading is to treat it as CC BY-SA. A fresh translation from the 1620/21 French would be clear of this. This fills the gap the Vol I notes flag at lines 173, 218 and 301 ("translator not identified").

**(3) Mu-jin's recitation in the king's hall, beginning "True it is, without falsehood, certain and most true"**
- Translators: **Robert Steele (1860-1944) and Dorothea Waley Singer (1882-1964)**, "The Emerald Table", *Proceedings of the Royal Society of Medicine* 21, no. 3 (January 1928), pp.485-501 (Section of the History of Medicine, pp.41-57), doi:10.1177/003591572802100361, PMCID PMC2101974 [CROSSREF; EUROPEPMC; WP-TABLET, which cites it as 1927, pp.42/486 for the translation and 48/492 for the Latin; BRAINSURGEON attributes the same opening to them, 1928]. It translates the Latin vulgate as Steele and Singer edited it.
- Codex text: **verbatim** Steele and Singer, including the closing "I am called Hermes Trismegistus".
- Rights: **public domain in the United States** (published January 1928). **Still in copyright in the UK and EU until 31 December 2034** (joint work, life of the last surviving author + 70; Singer died 1964 [WP-SINGER] **[single-sourced death date]**). The Codex quotes the whole text, about 250 words.
- Date note: Wikipedia gives 1927, probably the session year; the issue is dated January 1928. The Vol I notes' "1928" is correct for the printed issue.

**(4) The Vol II flyleaf line, "That which courseth above is as that which courseth below"**: I found no published translation with this wording. Vol II's notes call it Isaac's source-text wording [vol2 notes l.206]. It reads as an original paraphrase.

## 4. Findings that bear on canon (summary for the page writer)

1. Alchemetrica's cover-name section is historically sound, except its definition of **parathesis**. The real term (Newman's own coinage) means heaping up synonyms, or adding unnecessary terms and processes, not inserting a false step a reader spots as absurd. Record it; do not change it here.
2. Alchemetrica's four colour readings follow the **Greek four-stage scheme**; the late-Latin norm was three. WOTR has **no peacock's tail**, no green stage, and no timed regimens.
3. "Our Lead" for antimony is an exact real parallel (Philalethan Saturn = stibnite).
4. Codex Vol I credits: (a) Holmyard's source is the Jabirian *Kitāb Usṭuqus al-Uss al-Thānī*, not the *Sirr al-Khalīqa*; (b) Penn's rendering is the 2023-24 Wikipedia literal translation of Hesteau de Nuysement's sonnet (1620/21), under CC BY-SA 4.0, not public domain; (c) Steele and Singer's translation is quoted verbatim and is public domain in the US only, until 2035 in the UK.
5. The real textual difference between the Arabic "from" and the Latin "like", and the Trismegistus line appearing only in the Latin line, both match how Vol I distributes the recitations.

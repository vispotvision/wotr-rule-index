# The Real Alchemy: research lane, operations and apparatus

Out-of-world research notes for the Real Alchemy page (R18-5; C-110, R61 answer 104, EC5). Scope: the operations and apparatus as real laboratory procedure, with measured figures where they exist, and the WOTR canon each finding checks. Compiled 2026-09-28.

Conventions:
- Bracketed tags ([S1], [S2]...) point to the source list at the end.
- **(single-sourced)** marks a claim confirmed from one source only.
- Quotations are kept to short phrases; everything else is paraphrase.
- "Checks" lines point only at canon that exists: Alchemetrica (wiki page and live render `/tmp/wotr-drafts/alchemetrica-live.md`, which agree on every line quoted here), the Alchemical Index sheet of the Master Codex, the Alftian Codex vols 1 and 2, and The Farrant Papers. Where WOTR has no counterpart, the line says so. Nothing here rules on canon. Where the real record and canon pull apart, that is logged as a tension for the docket, not corrected.

## 1. The fire: degrees, and how heat was judged

**Degrees of fire counted in coals.** Lemery's *Course of Chymistry* (English 1686) sets four degrees by fuel, not by number [S1]:
- First degree: two or three lit coals, the gentlest heat.
- Second: three or four coals. The vessel is sensibly warm, but a hand can stay on it for a while.
- Third: enough fire to boil a pot holding five or six quarts of water.
- Fourth: coals and wood together, the fiercest heat.

Lemery treats sand, iron filings and ash as graded beds that each have their own degrees. **Ash is the mildest because ash cannot hold much heat.** The reverberatory fire has degrees too and runs hotter than any of them. The "wheel fire" rings a crucible with live coals for fusion [S1].

**Heat judged by the hand.** An anonymous English "Treatise", which Newton copied (Newman 2019, appendix 1), sets a digestion in a balneum Mariae at a heat "which you can hardly bear to touch" [S2]. The trade calibrated by the body. Modern tables that give each degree in °C (body heat, fever heat, 50–70 °C, ash, reverberation) come from present-day esoteric websites, not from period sources, and are left out here.

**Instruments came late, and only at the gentle end.**
- Fahrenheit made the first successful mercury-in-glass thermometers from 1713–14 and published his scale in 1724 [S17]. Mercury boils at 356 °C, which caps the instrument [S15].
- Boerhaave was measuring digestion heats on Fahrenheit's scale by the 1730s. He gives mine heat as seldom above body warmth, a "Majal" (May) heat of 50 °F, and a fire of 180 °F held for months [S11].
- Above red heat there was no standard instrument until Wedgwood's clay-shrinkage pyrometer of 1782. Its scale was later shown to be inaccurate [S15].
- Seger's pyrometric cones followed in 1886, and Le Chatelier's platinum/platinum-rhodium thermocouple in 1886–87 [S16][S13].

**Colour of the glow.** Solids begin to glow dull red at about 450–475 °C in a darkened room [S13]. The published colour-to-temperature tables do not agree with each other:
- Howe's table (1900): dull red 550–625 °C, full cherry 700 °C, light red 850 °C, full yellow 950–1000 °C, white 1150 °C [S13].
- Stirling Consolidated Boiler Co. table (1905): dull red 699 °C, cherry 800–1000 °C, orange 1100–1200 °C, white 1300 °C [S14].
- So the same colour name sits 100–150 °C apart between the two tables.

Darling (1911) says any two observers may differ by about 50 °C below yellow, and a trained quenching hand holds to about 20 °C at his usual heat [S13]. Pouillet's 1836 table had no accurate measurement behind it [S13].
- Fault: the colours read true only in dim light, and the reading belongs to the person, not the scale [S13][S14].

**Checks.**
- *Alchemetrica, Heat:* "There is no instrument. There has never been an instrument", and a master names a temperature off the colour "to within a hundred degrees".
  - The colour claim holds. The real error band was ±20–50 °C for one trained eye and about 100 °C between published tables.
  - "No instrument" matches the period before about 1714. It does not match the Imperial Age ceiling, where everything invented 1800–1900 exists (R53-01). Thermometers were already in use, Wedgwood's pyrometer (1782) predates the span, and Seger cones and the thermocouple fall inside it. **Tension for the docket; not resolved here.**
- *Alchemetrica, Still Bath:* "self-limiting at the boil". This matches the physics and Lemery [S1][S24].
- *Alchemetrica, Ash:* "Slower, gentler". This matches Lemery's order, where ash is milder than sand [S1]. "Holds through a night without tending" has no real figure found.
- *Alchemical Index #49 (Slag-Iron Caustic):* "stops glowing red and starts glowing black". This is the real incandescence threshold at about 450–525 °C [S13][S14].

## 2. Baths and furnaces

**Balneum Mariae (water bath).**
- A vessel stands in water over the fire, so the charge never goes above the boil [S1][S24].
- Zosimos (c. 300 CE) credited the bath to Maria the Jewess, along with the kerotakis (a reflux apparatus) and the tribikos (a three-spouted still). Her own writings survive only as quotations in later authors [S24].
- The name's origin was already argued over in the 17th century. The "Treatise" copied by Newton offers both Miriam, sister of Moses, and *maris*, "because it boyls with Waves like the Sea". It names Glauber's water bath as the best design [S2].
- **Vaporous bath:** the vessel sits in the steam of boiling water, not in the water [S1].

**Sand, iron-filing and ash baths.** The vessel is packed with the bed below and on all sides. In Lemery's furnace this is an iron or earthen pan of sand set on the bars [S1]. Newton ran sublimations at three levels [S2]:
- a candle under a glass plate,
- a sand bath with a retort,
- the naked fire, with the charge on a fire shovel.

**Horse-dung bath (fimus equinus, venter equinus).**
- The flask is buried in fermenting horse dung for a long, low, steady heat [S20][S1].
- Lemery prescribes dung digestions of four days, four or five days, and a fortnight. He names lamp-fire, the sun and dung together as the "equal", near-natural heats used for digesting gold [S1].
- *De natura rerum* (1537, attributed to Paracelsus) puts sperm in a sealed vessel in the venter equinus for forty days, then feeds it for forty weeks [S20].
- Brunschwig's *Liber de arte distillandi* (1500) describes distilling in horse dung, with a pelican used for rectification [S28].

**How hot dung actually runs** (modern measurement; no period thermometry of a dung bath was found):
- Hotbeds of manure and straw pass 50 °C within days. A well-built pile holds 61–68 °C for a week or more, and the bed yields useful heat for about two months (single-sourced: gardening and extension literature [S22]).
- Cornell's compost-physics notes: the thermophilic stage runs 40–60 °C and lasts weeks to months depending on mass. Commercial piles reach 60–70 °C in 3–5 days. Heat falls as the stage ends. Turning brings a new peak, until curing sets in and nothing restores it [S21].
- Overheating is real. Above about 70 °C the risk of self-ignition rises, and composting guidance says to break up a pile at 82–93 °C [S23].
- Fault: dung heat is neither constant nor blood-warm. It starts hot, sags over weeks, and has to be turned or renewed.

**Lamp furnace and the sun.**
- Lemery lists lamp-fire beside sun and dung as the steady digesting heats [S1].
- Newton noted a question for a collaborator about "How he contrives his Lamp", which shows the lamp mattered to the digestion [S2].
- No period figures were found for lamp heat (single-sourced mention).

**Boerhaave's little furnace.**
- A small wooden box modelled on the Dutch foot-stove, filled with glowing coal or peat that had stopped smoking.
- Held a constant moderate heat for up to 24 hours, which Boerhaave put at about 85 °F ("summer heat").
- Used for delicate plant distillations [S12].

**The reverberatory (Lemery's standard furnace)** [S1]:
- Fixed, of brick laid in a lute of 1 part potter's earth, 1 part horse dung and 2 parts sand, kneaded in water.
- Walls two bricks thick so the heat is held longer.
- Ash-hole a foot high. Two iron bars about thumb-thick carry the retort. The dome has a stoppered hole and a foot-high chimney that throws flame back onto the vessel.
- About a finger's breadth of space between retort and furnace wall.
- Portable furnaces were made of 3 parts powdered potsherd to 2 parts clay.

**The athanor (piger Henricus, "Slow Henry"; German *Fauler Heinz*).**
- A furnace for uniform, constant digesting heat [S18].
- The 16th-century form has a separate fuel shaft, so charcoal slides down into the fire by itself, like a magazine stove. It could run unattended [S18].
- The name comes from Arabic *al-tannūr*, "oven" [S18].
- Libavius's ideal chemical house (*Commentariorum alchymiae*, 1606) gives the laboratory ten furnaces. It notes that Tycho Brahe's laboratory held sixteen. The "athannor" stands apart in a private sanctuary for incubating the philosophical mercury in an egg-shaped vessel [S19].
- No measured burn time per charge of the tower was found. The endurance figures in §6 are for fires kept going by people.
- Glauber's *Furni novi philosophici* (1646–49) gave the first full, dimensioned furnace designs in print. It sorts furnaces into five kinds [S35].

**Checks.**
- *Alchemetrica, The Long Furnace* ("a fuel tower feeding charcoal down by gravity... weeks or months without a hand on it"). This is the athanor, and the mechanism is real [S18][S19].
  - "Weeks or months without a hand on it" is stronger than the evidence. Newton's six-week runs were watched in shifts night and day [S2]. Boerhaave's months-long digestions sat on tended fires [S11]. Nothing found shows a tower charge lasting weeks with no refuelling. **Tension (mild).**
- *Alchemetrica, The Bed* ("blood-warmth for six to nine weeks without fuel, without tending, and without any possibility of overheating"). The span holds: about 40 days in the sources, and roughly two months of heat measured in hotbeds [S20][S22].
  - Measured dung heat runs well above blood-warmth at first (50–68 °C), then falls. It needs turning or renewing, and a large pile can overheat and even ignite [S21][S22][S23]. **Tension on "blood-warmth" and "no possibility of overheating"; not resolved.**
  - A small bed around one vessel is the most favourable reading.
- *Alftian Codex vol 1* ("The ferment took three turns in warm dung, unopened, going black"). A turn is fourteen days, so three turns is 42 days. That sits on the real forty-day dung digestion [S20] and inside hotbed heat life [S22]. It agrees.
- *Alchemetrica, Still Bath* ("Named for Ithra, credited in three traditions and attested in none"). The real bath's naming is the same kind of problem: a credit through one later writer and competing etymologies [S24][S2]. This is not a data check; the parallel is exact.
- *No WOTR counterpart:* the lamp furnace, the vaporous bath, and the iron-filing bath. Alchemetrica's Heat table has none of them.

## 3. Vessels

**Still (cucurbit and alembic head, with receiver).**
- The cucurbit, or "body", holds the charge. The alembic, or "head", sits on it, catches the vapour and runs the condensate down a beak into the receiver [S5][S2].
- Humphrey Newton lists Newton's apparatus as bodies, receivers, heads and crucibles. He says only the crucibles saw much use [S2].
- Lemery uses a copper body tinned inside, with a tinned copper head and a water-cooled pipe or worm, for plant waters and essences. He uses earthen or glass cucurbits for other work [S1].
- An Oberstockstall glass alembic survives at 41.8 cm tall [S5].

**Retort and receiver.**
- A one-piece belly with a long neck. Vapour condenses in the neck and runs into a receiver [S5].
- Lemery prefers earthen retorts for acid spirits because they stand the utmost fire and "will not melt as glass do". He says good earthenware has pores as close as glass [S1].
- Receivers for acid spirits must be very large so the spirits have room to circulate [S1].
- Retorts are filled to about two-thirds. The aqua fortis charge leaves a third empty [S1].

**Matrass and double vessel.**
- A long-necked flask used for digestions and sublimations [S1].
- The neck of one matrass pushed into the neck of another makes a "double vessel" for circulating spirits, with the joints well luted [S1].
- The "Secret" Newman discusses seals two long-necked matrasses mouth to mouth with lute in a sand bath: 4 oz menstruum to 1 oz ferment [S2].

**Pelican.**
- A circulatory vessel. Vapour rises, condenses in one or two curved arms and runs back into the body, and the charge is never drawn off [S28][S31][S1].
- Illustrated in Brunschwig's *Liber de arte distillandi de compositis* (1512) [S28].
- Related to twin "gemini" flasks described in the pseudo-Lullian corpus, which may never have been built [S31].

**Aludel.**
- A stack of bottomless earthen pots fitted one over another, narrowing upward, over a pot of the charge. The sublimate settles on the walls, with a head at the top [S26][S1].
- The word is from Arabic *al-uthāl*, "the sublimation vessel" [S26].

**Crucibles.**
- Oberstockstall, a 16th-century laboratory in Lower Austria dug in 1980 and 1993–94, yielded a waste dump of 800–1,000 items. About 300 are triangular crucibles, about 40 scorifiers, and bone-ash cupels (counts differ: about 60 [S6], about 100 [S7]). There were also alembics, aludels, cucurbits, receivers and furnace brick [S6][S7].
- All the triangular crucibles are one refractory clay, tempered with sand, grog or graphite. Graphite-tempered ones conduct heat better and resist attack [S6].
- Hessian crucibles, made in Hesse from the late Middle Ages and traded across Europe: kaolinitic clay with little flux and added quartz sand, fired above 1300 °C, which forms mullite. Mullite gives high-temperature strength, creep resistance and thermal-shock resistance. This is the earliest industrial use of mullite in Europe [S8].
- Newton's records show he melted metals in crucibles and noted breakages [S3][S2].

**Cupels.**
- Porous cups of well-washed ashes or calcined bone, used to purify gold and silver [S1].
- In cupellation, lead oxide (litharge) melts at 888 °C and soaks into the bone ash by capillary action, leaving the noble metal on top. The furnace runs at about 960–1000 °C in an oxidising draught [S27].
- Some Oberstockstall cupels are high in bismuth [S6].

**Philosophical egg.**
- An ovoid flask with a long neck, sealed shut for long digestions [S19][S31].
- The "Treatise" Newton copied fills the egg only a quarter full [S2].
- Newton's own notes record breaking his "egg-glass" when volatile vitriolic salts rose during a digestion [S3].
- No period figure for wall thickness was found.

**Glass as a material.**
- Soda-lime glass softens at about 730 °C [S32]. It survives a sudden temperature change of only about 40 °C, against about 170 °C for borosilicate [S32].
- Lemery coats glass retorts in lute because a reverberatory can melt them [S1].
- Glass was preferred wherever the reaction had to be watched [S5].
- Boerhaave's 500 mercury distillations broke retorts along the way [S11].
- Borosilicate glass was developed by Otto Schott at Jena in the late 19th century [S32].

**Joints and stoppers.**
- Before standardisation, joints were made with lute, bladder, cork or rubber bung, or custom-ground glass [S33][S1].
- Newman records a "ground stopper" in Yworth's separatory glass in the 1690s [S2].
- Makers standardised within their own ranges in the 1920s. Interchangeable commercial standards date from about 1930 [S33].

**Checks.**
- *Alchemetrica, The Vessels.*
  - Returner = pelican. The two curved arms and the never-opened circulation are real [S28][S31].
  - Riser = aludel. Stacked earthenware with the sublimate scraped off the walls is real [S26][S1].
  - Standing Egg = philosophical egg. Sealed until finished, with glass failure as the failure mode, matches Newton's broken egg-glass [S3].
  - Belly and Hood = cucurbit and alembic. It agrees, including Lemery's copper body for waters [S1].
  - Retort "No joint to fail". This is half true: the retort-to-receiver joint still has to be luted [S1].
- *Alchemetrica:* glass "cracks under thermal shock, and will not take the top of the range". This agrees with soda/potash glass [S32][S1].
  - Borosilicate (Schott, 1880s–90s) lies inside the 1800–1900 technology ceiling. **Note for the docket.**
- *Alchemetrica:* refractory ceramic is "porous and blind". "Blind" agrees. "Porous" is true of cupels by design [S27]. Lemery [S1] and the Hessian evidence [S8] say good crucibles and stoneware were dense. **Mild tension.**
- *Alchemetrica:* copper "eaten alive by Strongwater". Real practice kept copper stills for waters and essences only [S1]. Corrosion rates belong to the reagent lane.
- *Alchemetrica, The Lute:* "There are no ground joints". This is true of still joints before the 20th century [S33]. Ground stoppers existed from at least the 1690s [S2]. **Note.**
- *No WOTR counterpart:* the cupel and cupellation (no match for "cupel" in Alchemetrica, the Codex or the Papers), the scorifier, the aludel as a named Arabic-derived object (Riser only), the double vessel of two matrasses, and the reverberatory dome.

## 4. Luting and sealing

**What lute was for.**
- Sealing the joints of distillation trains, and coating vessels, especially the bottom and sides of glass, against flame and cracking. It also patched broken vessels [S4][S9][S1].
- Gessner (1583) sorts three lutes: common lute for furnace interiors, lutum sapientiae for the outside of alembics, and a lute for mending broken vessels [S4].

**What an excavated lute actually contains** (Oberstockstall, 16th c.; SEM-EDS and optical microscopy) [S4][S5]:
- A thick clay coat on the outside of a distillation glass.
- Silica, antimony and calcium oxides make up over 80% of it. Antimony is present as powdered stibnite, which appears in no known recipe. There is abundant angular sand, and a calcium-and-sulfur binder that is probably gypsum.
- Elongated voids where hair, cloth or straw burned out, and rounded voids that may be seeds or grain. These organics cut shrinkage and stopped cracks spreading.
- Two long fissures parallel to the glass show the lute was put on in **two coats**.
- Egg white leaves no trace that can be analysed.
- Courts paid for this: the Württemberg laboratory spent 4 gulden 8 kreuzer on hair in 1618–19 [S4].

**How lutes failed.**
- **Hard-setting.** Lemery's vessel lute dries so hard that the joints have to be softened with wet cloths before the retort can be parted from the receiver [S1].
- **Vapour penetration.** In the 1690s William Yworth wrapped his joints in animal bladder under the lute. The fume still came "penetrating the Lute", and on opening the receiver he nearly passed out. He treats this stinking breakthrough, after three to five days of distilling, as the sign to stop [S2].
- **Bladder eaten through** by corrosive spirits, which is why Lemery changes to an egg-white-and-lime paste for acids [S1].
- **Vessel breakage** under luted, repeated high-heat use. A modern reconstructor's luted cucurbit survived three or four high-temperature distillations and broke while solid acid residue was being cleared out (single-sourced, experimental [S9]).
- **Cracking and shrinkage** on drying. This is why dung, hair and cloth were added and why lutes were dried before firing [S4][S10].

**Hermetic seal.**
- Lemery: heat the neck of the glass little by little with burning coals until it is ready to melt, then close it with pincers heated red hot. This is used when the matter must circulate without escaping [S1].
- Sealing was a skilled glass job [S31].

**Other seals.** Boerhaave closed a mercury bottle with a cork dipped in a liquid cement of pitch, rosin, tallow and brimstone, then bound it with linen and packthread [S11].

**Checks.**
- *Alchemetrica, The Lute* ("clay and hair and dung and egg and flour, mixed by recipe, applied wet, and dried before the fire is lit"). Every ingredient is attested, and so is drying before heat [S4][S1][S10].
  - *Joint lute* (egg white, flour, linen strip). This agrees with pseudo-Lull, Rupescissa and Lemery's layers wound with paper [S9][S1].
  - *Fire lute* (clay, sand, chopped fibre; "cracks off in sheets" if fired wet). This agrees with [S10], and the shrinkage behaviour agrees with [S4].
  - *Fat lute* (clay and boiled oil, pliable, for vessels to be reopened). This agrees [S10].
  - "The lute every workshop guards the proportions of." Real recipes were printed and widely circulated in Kunstbüchlein and distilling manuals [S4], but Oberstockstall's stibnite lute departs from all of them [S4].
- *Alchemetrica, Dawi fourth lute kept secret.* WOTR-only. The real lute nearest to it is the unrecorded stibnite lute [S4].
- *Alchemetrica:* "A failed lute vents Strongwater fume into a closed workshop". Yworth's account is the real case [S2]. The toxicology belongs to the poisons lane.
- *Farrant Papers* ("a lute of grey wax over the stopper, cut and pressed back"). Wax and resin cements over stoppers were real, as in Boerhaave's pitch-rosin-tallow-brimstone seal [S11]. It agrees.
- *Alchemetrica, Luteboy (children mixing lute).* No real source was found either way.

## 5. The operations as bench procedure

**Calcination.** Heating a solid in open air or a reverberatory to a calx.
- Lemery calcines tin in an unglazed earthen pan with a great fire for **36 hours**, stirring now and then. In a crucible the same takes **four days**, because the pan spreads the metal out [S1].
- Gold cementation (strata of gold plate and a salt paste): **10–12 hours** of violent heat [S1].
- In a separate procedure, Lemery says that calcining red precipitate harder lightens it and makes it less corrosive: the calx loses weight as the calcination goes on [S1].
- Fixing a red powder on gold leaf in a crucible in a reverberatory: **three days** at high heat (the "Secret" in Newman 2019 [S2]).
- Mercury calcined "per se" to red calx:
  - Lavoisier kept **4 oz** of mercury at nearly its boiling point for **12 days** (report of 1777). Red specks appeared on day two. At the end he had only about **45 grains** of calx, and the enclosed air had lost about a sixth of its volume [S29].
  - Lemery's first-part calcinations of vitriol, lead, copper and common salt run hours to days [S1].
- Fault: yield is small and the rate is slow, so the result depends on how long the fire is kept, not on how fierce it is.

**Solution and dissolution.** A solid is turned liquid by a solvent (menstruum) [S1].
- Newton's standard routine: dissolve in aqua fortis with stibnite and sal ammoniac, and heat until dissolved. A heavy calx usually stays undissolved at the bottom; decant, filter, and boil to dryness [S2].
- Lemery soaks corrosive sublimate in spirit of wine cold for six or seven hours [S1].
- Iron will not dissolve in plain water "though you should boil it a Month". It goes readily once the water is charged with tartar [S1].

**Separation.** Decanting, filtering (Lemery filters through a cone of brown paper), precipitating, washing ("edulcorating") and drying [S1][S2].
- **Lixiviation:** the fixed salt of plants is recovered from the residue left after distillation. The residue is calcined in an open fire until the soot that blackens it has burned off, then the salt is leached out with water [S1].
- **Spagyric separation** (Paracelsian): a plant is parted into Sulphur (oil), Mercury (spirit or volatile part) and Salt (fixed salt from the ash), each is purified, and they are recombined [S34] (single-sourced as an encyclopedia entry; the ash-to-salt step is confirmed by Lemery [S1]).

**Putrefaction and fermentation.** Digestion at a steady low heat in a closed vessel until the matter goes black and "rots" [S1][S2].

| Case | Time | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Homunculus in venter equinus (*De natura rerum*, 1537) | 40 days, then 40 weeks of feeding | [S20] |
| Treatise copied by Newton: mixture in the philosophical egg | putrefies by day 30, then whitens and reddens | [S2] |
| Newton's *Praxis* (planning document) | ferment 40 days; green liquid at 30, black powder at 40 | [S2] |
| Philalethes (in Newman) | the "doves" rise at about the end of three weeks | [S2] |
| Fatio's adept's medicine (1690s) | 7 months through 4 putrefactions | [S2] |
| Lemery, tincture digested in horse dung in a double vessel | a fortnight | [S1] |
| Lemery, silver "tree of Diana", left undisturbed in a cool place | 40 days | [S1] |

- Lemery defines fermentation as a boiling-up raised by spirits forcing their way out, which changes the matter's nature [S1].
- Principe, working from Starkey's notebooks, saw an amalgam in a sealed flask in a sand bath swell like leavened dough. After several days a metallic "Philosophers' Tree" had grown [S30].

**Distillation and cohobation.**
- Aqua fortis (Lemery): saltpetre, white-calcined vitriol and dried clay, 32 oz each, in a luted retort with a third left empty, in a close reverberatory. Warm by degrees. When red clouds pass into the receiver, hold that degree **15–16 hours**, then push to the last extremity until the clouds turn white [S1].
- Oil of vitriol: "eighteen or twenty hours" is not enough. It comes over only **after three days** of continuous distilling. Lemery ran one retort of German vitriol for **three days and three nights**, and mentions five days and nights [S1].
- **Cohobation** is repeated distillation of the same liquor, poured back each time on the residue in the vessel, "to open Bodies, or to Volatilize the Spirits" [S1][S25].
  - It differs from circulation because the liquor is drawn off each time [S25].
  - Modern chemistry finds it gives nothing beyond holding the charge at heat for the same time [S25].
- Newton's recipe: spirit of wine on a sublimed salt, digested 24 hours, then distilled seven times or more [S2].
- Repetition carried to the limit:
  - Boerhaave distilled the same mercury **500 times**, 448 of them in one series. He lost weight to broken retorts and cleaning, and got no transmutation [S11].
  - He weighed mercury distilled 511 times at a specific gravity of 14.11 against about 13.57 for common mercury (his own measurement) [S11].

**Circulation.**
- Heat drives the vapour up and it condenses back down in a closed double vessel or pelican. The aim is to refine the liquor or to open a hard body [S1][S28].
- Rupescissa's quintessence was wine distilled repeatedly, then circulated in a closed glass "for a long period". Period recipes rarely give a time [S9].
- In the adept's menstruum that Fatio described, 5 lb of matter gave 4½ lb in the receiver, which sat several days in a cold place before the vapours settled. It was distilled about ten times [S2].

**Sublimation.**
- A volatile matter is raised to the top of the vessel or the head and collects as a solid [S1].
- Corrosive sublimate (Lemery): red nitrous vapours pour from the mouth of the matrass for **at least three hours**. The residue left at the bottom, the earthy part of the salt and vitriol, weighed 28 oz [S1].
- Newton's "Treatise": sublime on a mild fire for **three days**. The bottom holds a caput mortuum, the middle a cinnabar, the top a white sublimate. The product is mixed and sublimed again two or three more times [S2].
- Newton's own practice was to sublime and resublime, grading the heat from a candle to the naked fire to be sure everything volatile had risen [S2].

**Coagulation, congelation and fixation.**
- Coagulation gives a liquid body by evaporating it or mixing liquors. Congelation is letting a melt set [S1].
- Fixation makes a volatile thing stand the fire:
  - The three-day reverberatory fixation above [S2].
  - Boerhaave's "fixed" mercury powder, held red-hot in a clay-and-sand-coated retort for three to four hours. He took the fire acting through the glass as the fixing cause [S11].

**Conjunction.**
- In practice this was the joining of prepared principles or metals: amalgamating gold with a prepared mercury, or the "Secret's" ferment into its menstruum, then sealing the vessel and digesting [S2][S30].
- The stages that follow were read by colour: black, the iridescent "peacock's tail", green, white, yellow, red [S2].

**Checks.**
- *Alchemetrica, Twelve Turnings and Time.*
  - "A calcination, four hours to three days." Real bench calcinations ran 10–12 hours (cementation), 36 hours (tin in a pan), 3 days (fixation), 4 days (tin in a crucible) and 12 days (mercury per se) [S1][S2][S29]. **The canon upper bound is shorter than some real calcinations. Tension; C-110's "long calcinations" points here.**
  - "Strongwater... roughly a day of graduated heat... red-orange fume... the working's own signal". This matches Lemery's aqua fortis (15–16 hours at the red-cloud degree after warming, then pushed to white clouds) [S1].
  - "Three days of continuous distillation before [Blackstone Oil] comes over". This matches Lemery on oil of vitriol exactly [S1].
  - "The Rot, six to nine weeks in the Bed, unopened... goes black". Real putrefactions: 30–40 days to black. Longer schemes are counted in months [S20][S2][S1]. It agrees.
  - "A circulation in the Returner, weeks or months". Real sources give "a long period", a fortnight, and multi-month schemes [S9][S1][S2]. It agrees in order of magnitude. No exact period figure was found.
  - "The Rising... whatever failed to rise is a loss". Real sublimation left a residue (caput mortuum) and a layered product that was re-sublimed [S1][S2].
    - Alchemetrica's Sublimation row, "There is no partial Sublimation... a fused mass that has to be broken out with a chisel", is a WOTR working rule. The real bench did have partial and repeated sublimations. **Note; the canon row concerns the Wellspring-mirroring working, so it may not conflict.**
  - "Colours arriving out of order mean the sentence is executing wrongly". The real colour sequence was black, peacock's tail, green, white, yellow, red [S2]. Alchemetrica's Blackening, Whitening, Yellowing and Reddening sit on it. The real sequence has green and iridescent stages that the canon table does not name.
- *Alftian Codex vol 1, Furveus's spagyria* (Sulphur the oil from the still, Mercury the spirit from the ferment, Salt the ash burned white and crystallised). This is the real Paracelsian scheme [S34]. The ash-to-white-salt step is Lemery's lixiviation of fixed salt [S1]. It agrees.
- *Alftian Codex vol 1* ("the spirit went into the Returner... for most of a month"; "We read the Whitening by its colour, pale and pearly"). This agrees with real circulation practice and colour reading [S2][S9].
- *Alftian Codex vol 1, the Wedding* (the principles poured together in one movement, then pressure relief at the neck). Real conjunctions were joined in the vessel and then sealed and digested [S2]. The real hazard is pressure in a sealed glass: Newton's broken egg-glass [S3], Principe's swelling amalgam [S30]. It agrees in hazard. The one-pour speed is WOTR's.
- *Alchemical Index #9* (reduce "for 3 hours"), *#42* ("one full night" on a low coal bed), *#12* (cure seven days). These are plausible bench spans. There is nothing real to check beyond that.
- *No WOTR counterpart:* cohobation as a named operation, cementation of gold, lixiviation as a named step, cupellation, and calcination per se of mercury.

## 6. How long the great operations actually ran (real cases)

| Case | Duration | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Lavoisier, mercury calcined near its boiling point | 12 days, continuous | [S29] |
| Newton's laboratory, spring and autumn campaigns (Humphrey Newton, secretary 1685–90) | about 6 weeks each, fire "scarcely going out either Night or Day", the two men sitting up alternate nights | [S2] |
| Newton's chemical records | about 400 experiments, recorded 1678–1695 | [S3] |
| Yworth, a distillation from the egg | 3 to 5 days, stopped when the fume broke through the lute | [S2] |
| Lemery, oil of vitriol | 3 days and 3 nights; up to 5 days and nights | [S1] |
| Boerhaave, mercury shaken in a sealed bottle on a fulling mill | 1 March to 13 November 1732 (about 8½ months) | [S11] |
| Boerhaave, mercury in conical flasks at 180 °F | "several Months" | [S11] |
| Boerhaave, mercury kept on heat | 15 years 6 months on one occasion, over 2 years on another | (single-sourced, secondary summary; not found in his published *Experiments* [S11]) |
| Fatio's adept, medicine | 7 months, 4 putrefactions | [S2] |
| Newton's *Praxis* (a plan, never carried out) | digest 7 months; purge and decoct 5–7 months; digest 10 more months | [S2] |
| *De natura rerum* homunculus (text, not practice) | 40 days + 40 weeks | [S20] |

- Newton was still working at "our Venus" when his dated experiments ended in February 1696. By his own *Praxis* that is at least ten steps short of the stone [S2].
- The long schemes were projected. The long runs actually carried out lasted weeks, or at most months of tended heat.

**Checks.**
- *Alchemetrica:* "A Standing Egg digestion runs months to years", and Eggs in the Archives Eternal have been under heat longer than the Accord has existed. Real multi-year heating is attested only thinly: Boerhaave's 15½ years is single-sourced. Real *planned* regimens run 7–17 months [S2]. The years-to-centuries Eggs are WOTR's own. There is no real counterpart for continuous heat on that scale.
- *Alchemetrica:* "talk at the wrong moment during a Feeding costs a master a month". There is no direct real case. The nearest is Fatio's report that one lost moment "you loose all your operation" [S2].
- *Alchemetrica:* "This is why the great houses are old houses... the ability to wait". The real parallel is patronage. Libavius's ideal house and Oberstockstall under a Fugger patron show the capital and space involved [S19][S4][S6]. This is not a data check.

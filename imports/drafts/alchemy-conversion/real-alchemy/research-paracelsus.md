# The Real Alchemy: research lane "paracelsus"

Paracelsus, spagyria, iatrochemistry, and the doses of the mineral remedies. Out-of-world research notes for the page. Every claim carries its source tags (full list at the foot). **[single]** means I could confirm it from one source only. Quotations are kept to short phrases; the rest is my own summary.

Researched 2026-09-28. The heaviest primary source is the 13th edition of the *Dispensatory of the United States* (Wood and Bache, 1870; [DUS1870]). I read its own text for calomel, corrosive sublimate, metallic mercury, arsenious acid, Fowler's solution and tartar emetic, and for Lemery's *Course of Chymistry* (English 2nd ed., 1686; [LEM1686]). Its date sits at the end of WOTR's Imperial-Age window, which suits the page.

## 1. "The dose makes the poison": what Paracelsus actually wrote

- **Work and date.** The *Third Defence* (*Die dritte Defension wegen des Schreibens der neuen Rezepte*, "concerning the writing of the new prescriptions") of the *Septem Defensiones*. He wrote it in 1538 in Carinthia and it was first printed in 1564, more than twenty years after his death in 1541 [GRAND2016; BOED2023; WIKI-DOSE]. It is a defence written against physicians who accused him of prescribing poisons, meaning his mineral remedies [DEICH1986 by title and scope; GRAND2016].
- **German wording** (the standard modern edition, *Werke* Bd. 2, Darmstadt 1965, p. 510): *Alle Ding sind Gift, und nichts ohn Gift; allein die Dosis macht, dass ein Ding kein Gift ist* [WIKI-DOSE; WIST].
- **Standard English translation** (Deichmann, Henschler, Holmstedt and Keil 1986, repeated by Grandjean 2016). It opens with a question, "What is there that is not poison?", and then says that all things are poison, nothing is without poison, and "solely the dose" decides that a thing is not a poison [DEICH1986; GRAND2016].
- **The Latin slogan *sola dosis facit venenum* is later.** It compresses the sentence and turns it around: Paracelsus said the dose makes a thing *not* a poison. Bödeker and Moebus (2023) show that the maxim has come down in German and Latin versions that contradict each other ("ein Gift" / "kein Gift"). They also argue that modern toxicology no longer holds dose to be the only factor, since age, genetics, timing and context all matter [BOED2023; WIKI-DOSE]. Grandjean (2016) makes the same point for mercury, noting that timing and susceptibility also make the poison [GRAND2016].
- **Fault in the doctrine.** The maxim assumes a threshold: below some dose there is no harm. That holds for acute poisoning but fails for cumulative metals. Mercury and arsenic taken at a "safe" daily dose still build up in the body, and the chronic diseases they cause are what ended the remedies (see §6 to §8) [GRAND2016; NRC1977].
- **Checks in WOTR.** The Codex already states the principle in its own words: "The dose is the point: each heals at a measure and poisons past it, and the man who measures pays in his hands" (*Alftian Codex* Vol I, Iatrochemistry entry; also the Furveus passage). The real history supports this as written. The real addition is that the practitioner pays *cumulatively*, which fits "every day he measures."

## 2. The tria prima as Paracelsus meant it

- **Where and when.** The tria prima (Sulphur, Mercury, Salt) first appears in its full form in the *Opus Paramirum*, c. 1530–31 (printed after his death; the collected edition is Huser, 1589–91) [WIKI-PARA; WIKI-PARACELSIANISM].
- **What the three principles are.** They are not the common substances; they are principles. Sulphur is what makes a body combustible. Mercury is what is fluid and volatile and rises as vapour or smoke. Salt is what stays fixed and incombustible in the fire [WIKI-PARA; NISBET1805 via WIKIQUOTE; NICHOLSON1808 via WIKIQUOTE]. Earlier alchemy had only Sulphur and Mercury (the sulphur–mercury theory of metals). Paracelsus's contribution was the third principle, Salt, standing for fixity and incombustibility [READ1957 via WIKIQUOTE; BROWN1913 via WIKIQUOTE].
- **The burning-wood demonstration.** He illustrated the three with a log burning: the flame is Sulphur's work, the smoke is Mercury's, and the ash that remains is Salt [OXFORD-CABINET, search extract; WIKIQUOTE extracts from Nisbet 1805]. **[single for the attribution of the wood example to Paracelsus himself.]** The later dictionaries confirm the analysis of fire into flame, smoke and ash, but I could not open a primary Paracelsus passage to confirm which text it comes from.
- **Body, soul and spirit.** In one Paracelsian passage Mercury is the spirit, Sulphur the soul and Salt the body [READ1957 via WIKIQUOTE]. Other writers arrange the triad differently: Brown's 1913 table pairs Mercury with soul and Sulphur with spirit [BROWN1913 via WIKIQUOTE]. **The mapping was never fixed.** Boyle's *Sceptical Chymist* (1661) and his 1675 tract complain that chymists used the three words in loose, shifting and almost arbitrary senses, sometimes within one book [BOYLE1675 via WIKIQUOTE].
- **In medicine.** Paracelsians classed symptoms by principle: sulphureous (fevers and inflammations), mercurial (excess moistures) and saline (skin eruptions). Diseases had specific chemical causes seated in particular organs [REM-CHEM].
- **The expansion to five.** By Lemery (1675; English 1686) the chymists counted five principles: three active (Spirit, which is the Mercury; Oil, which is the Sulphur; Salt) and two passive (Phlegm, which is water, and Earth, the *caput mortuum*). Lemery gives the recovery method for each. Spirit rises first in the still. Oil rises after the spirit and either floats on the water (rosemary, lavender) or sinks (box, guaiacum, cloves). Salt stays in the residue and is taken out by pouring water on the earth, filtering and evaporating [LEM1686, pp. 3–4 of the principles chapter; NISBET1805 via WIKIQUOTE].
- **Fault.** Boyle's objection: burning in the fire does not reveal what a body is made of. It makes new compounds. The ash, oil and spirit are products of the fire, not pre-existing parts, and gold cannot be resolved into the three at all [BOYLE1675 via WIKIQUOTE; THORPE1909 via WIKIQUOTE]. Van Helmont objected that the three cannot be drawn from a living body without destroying what made it alive [HEDESAN2016 via WIKIQUOTE].
- **Checks in WOTR.** Furveus reads the three onto a Crystal's layers: Sulphur to the Core, Salt to the Shell, Mercury to the Attraction Layer (*Alftian Codex* Vol I, the Spagyria passage and "The Tria Prima of Furveus"). The real history leaves room for this, because every author mapped the triad onto body, soul and spirit in his own way and nobody agreed. Salt as body and fixity fits the Shell best, and the real doctrine supports that. Sulphur → Core and Mercury → Attraction Layer are WOTR's own choices; the real record neither confirms nor forbids them.

## 3. Spagyria: separating a plant, and putting it back together

- **The word.** It was coined by Paracelsus (it appears in the *Paragranum*, c. 1530) and is traditionally derived from Greek *spao* (to draw out, separate) and *ageiro* (to gather, recombine) [PRINCIPE2013 pp. 128–129 as cited in WIKI-PARACELSIANISM; WIKI-PARA]. In the 16th and 17th centuries "spagyric" was often simply a synonym for chymistry [WIKI-PARACELSIANISM].
- **The theory.** Break a medicine into its three principles, purify each, and recombine them. This keeps the useful part, sheds the harmful part, and can turn some poisons into medicines [PRINCIPE2013 pp. 128–129 via WIKI-PARACELSIANISM]. This is the working link to §1: for a Paracelsian the preparation, as well as the quantity, decides whether the thing is a poison.
- **The operations** were fermentation, distillation, and extraction of salts from plant ash, all borrowed from metal and salt refining [WIKI-PARACELSIANISM; LEM1686].

### The procedure in practice, from a working manual (Lemery, 1686 English edition)

These are real bench instructions from the most widely used 17th-century chymistry course [LEM1686]:

1. **Mercury (the spirit, via fermentation).** Fruit, honey, berries, seeds, leaves and flowers can be fermented like wine. Dry material is beaten and wetted with water, and a little yeast is added to speed the ferment. Distilling the result gives a "burning spirit" as wine does [LEM1686, fermentation chapter]. The spirit rises first in the still [LEM1686, principles].
2. **Sulphur (the oil) and the water, by distillation of an odoriferous herb (balm).** Take fresh balm and beat it in a mortar. Wet it with a strong decoction of more balm and leave it covered **two days in digestion**. Distil in a copper still with a tinned head, the joints **luted with a wet bladder**, at a fire "of the second degree," until about half the added water has come over. Stand the distilled water open in the sun **five or six days** before stopping it. Lemery keeps the fire between the second and third degree: too low and the volatile salt will not rise, too high and the water tastes burnt ("empyreuma"). The drops should come slowly, one after another [LEM1686, ch. on balm].
3. **The extract.** Press the residue through linen, let it settle, filter, and evaporate gently to the thickness of honey [LEM1686].
4. **Salt (the ash).** Dry the pressed residue, burn it (Lemery adds more dried balm), and make a lixivium of the ash to get an alkaline "fixed salt." The general method he gives for a plant residue is to dry it, burn it, and **calcine the ash in a crucible**. Then steep it in **hot water ten or twelve hours**, **boil an hour**, filter the lye, and evaporate to dryness in an earthen or glass pan in sand. For guaiacum he **calcines the ash "some hours" in a potter's furnace**, lixiviates, evaporates, and then **calcines the salt again in a crucible in a strong fire to whiten it**. What is left over, the *caput mortuum*, he calls "good for nothing" [LEM1686, guaiacum and the extract/tincture section].
5. **Digestion** (for tinctures and essences). Seal a double vessel (the neck of one flask inside the neck of another), lute the joint with wet bladder, and set it in **horse-dung** for **four or five days**, or **a fortnight** for a stronger charge [LEM1686]. Lemery's furnace lute is **one part potter's clay, one part horse-dung, two parts sand**, kneaded with water [LEM1686, furnace chapter].
6. **Doses of the products** (balm): the water is given at **two to six ounces**, the extract at **a scruple to a drachm** (1.3–3.9 g), and the fixed salt at **ten grains to a scruple** (0.65–1.3 g) [LEM1686].

- **Horse-dung heat, in real figures.** A well-built horse-manure pile holds about **61–68 °C (141–155 °F) for a week or more** after heating up over about four days. A tamped hotbed gives useful heat for **about a month to two months** [HOTBED sources, gardening; **single** for the exact temperature band]. The one Paracelsian text that gives a length of time is *De natura rerum* (1537, attributed to Paracelsus): a charge **putrefied forty days** in the *venter equinus*, "warm, fermenting horse dung," as Newman glosses it [NEWMAN2005 p. 215 via WIKI-HOMUNCULUS].
- **The pelican.** A circulatory vessel whose two side-arms carry condensate back into the body, so a charge can be refluxed without the vessel ever being opened. It is shown in Brunschwig's distilling books (1500; the *de compositis* edition of 1512) [WELLCOME-PELICAN; BRUNSCHWIG-PUBMED].
- **Fault.** The recombined "spagyric" medicine is chemically ordinary. The fixed salt of almost any burned plant is mostly potassium carbonate (Lemery himself says it is "an alkali, as are all other fixed salts drawn from plants") [LEM1686]. The plant's particular virtue is lost in the fire, and what the salt adds back is alkali. The real chemical risk in the method is not in the plants but in the minerals, where "purifying" often concentrated the poison (§5 to §8).
- **Checks in WOTR.** Alchemetrica's Rot is "six to nine weeks in the Bed, unopened." The Bed "holds blood-warmth for six to nine weeks without fuel." The real hotbed figures (heat for one to two months) support the upper range as long-lived but ordinary. The real core temperature (about 60 °C) is **hotter than blood-warmth**. Alchemetrica's claim that the Bed has no "possibility of overheating" is WOTR canon, and the real record does not back it; the Bed needs to be packed or distanced to hold blood-warmth. The Codex's "three turns in warm dung" and "most of a month" in the Returner sit inside the real ranges (Lemery's two days to a fortnight for digestion; pseudo-Paracelsus's forty days). The Returner is the pelican. Luting with dung and clay is real and exact (Lemery's 1:1:2). The Codex's Salt ("the ash of what is left, burned white and crystallised") matches Lemery's calcine, lixiviate, evaporate and re-calcine to whiten, step for step. WOTR's Whitening as a purity colour sign is a different thing from Lemery's whitened salt. The real record has no counterpart for reading purity by a pearly colour in a spirit.

## 4. The later spagyric and iatrochemical tradition

- **Jean Beguin**, *Tyrocinium Chymicum* (1608 onward, Paris), is often called the first chemistry textbook. It came from public lectures on preparing spagyric medicines, which Turquet de Mayerne helped him obtain permission to give. It already describes calomel: corrosive sublimate ground with as much quicksilver as it will "kill," then sublimed [BEGUIN-WIKI; DAVIDSON-BOYLE-ANNOT].
- **Pharmacopoeia Londinensis** (London, May 1618, revised within the year), the first English official formulary, compiled by the College of Physicians with Mayerne on the committee. It carried two short sections of chemical medicines at the end [RCP2018]. It is said to list **eighty-five chemical preparations**, including mercurials and antimonials, and **mercurius dulcis** (calomel) [search extracts; **single** for the count of 85].
- **Johann Rudolph Glauber** (1604–1670). *Furni novi philosophici* (1646–49) described new furnaces for high and varied heats. *Pharmacopoea spagyrica* (1654–68) gathered his remedies, which leaned on minerals. He made spirit of salt (hydrochloric acid) from common salt and oil of vitriol, and found *sal mirabile* (sodium sulphate, "Glauber's salt") as its by-product, c. 1658. He gave out free medicines daily, including an antimony "panacea" [DSB-GLAUBER].
- **Nicolas Lemery**, *Cours de chymie* (1675; English 1677, 2nd ed. 1686). This is the practical manual used in §3. It takes a mechanical, non-mystical view of the principles but keeps the five-principle analysis [LEM1686].
- **The Paris antimony quarrel.** In 1566 the Paris Faculty of Medicine declared antimony a poison and restricted it. In 1658 at Calais, Louis XIV's physician Vallot treated the king with **emetic wine** (an antimonial), and the Faculty was afterwards obliged to accept it [VALLOT-WIKI; ANTIMONY search extracts]. **[single for the date of formal reinstatement; older histories give 1666, which I could not confirm.]**
- **The end of the tria prima.** Boyle, *The Sceptical Chymist* (1661), and after him Becher's *Physica subterranea* (1669) replaced the three principles [NISBET1805 via WIKIQUOTE; THORPE1909 via WIKIQUOTE]. Paracelsianism declined in the later 17th century, but its mineral remedies stayed in the pharmacopoeias [WIKI-PARACELSIANISM; WEBSTER2013 cited there].
- **Checks in WOTR.** No WOTR canon names a pharmacopoeia or a Glauber figure. The real line runs from a heretic's recipes to a college's official list within eighty years, which suits Alchemetrica's guild and house structure but has no stated counterpart there. Note it as **no WOTR counterpart**.

## 5. Quicksilver and the mercurials

### Paracelsus and mercury
- He sided with **mercury against guaiac wood** in the 1529–30 syphilis quarrel. The Fugger banking house, which imported guaiac, helped get his syphilis work blocked at Nuremberg. His 1530 clinical account of the French disease held that it could be treated by carefully measured mercury [RENMATH2021 blog; SCIENCEDIRECT-PARA2021 abstract via search; **single** for "carefully measured doses taken internally"]. Grandjean says his best-known clinical toxicology was on mercury [GRAND2016].
- **He also attacked the barbers' mercury.** In Grandjean's quotation he complains that some physicians "poison their patients with mercury" [GRAND2016]. Measured versus heroic mercury was his own quarrel.

### How syphilis mercury was given (16th–18th c.)
- Three routes: **inunction** (quicksilver rubbed with hog's lard and turpentine, applied before a fire), **fumigation** (vapour from heated crucibles), and **internal** tablets of red or white precipitate. The course continued until salivation began, commonly **six, eight or twelve days** until the patient "fluxed" copiously. One 18th-century regimen used **five or six ounces** of ointment and red precipitate at around **six to fifteen grains** [EWL-SYPH, quoting an early-modern treatise; **single**].
- **Salivation as the endpoint.** Practitioners took a flow of saliva (sometimes given as about **three pints a day**) as proof the cure was working [SCIFRI; brainvoyage; **single** for the pint figure and its attribution to Paracelsus, which is weak]. The 1870 Dispensatory still gives doses "with a view to salivation" [DUS1870].

### Calomel (mercurous chloride, Hg₂Cl₂; *mercurius dulcis*)
- **Making it.** Quicksilver sulphate is sublimed with common salt and more quicksilver in earthenware or iron. The sublimate is powdered and washed with boiling distilled water until the washings show no corrosive sublimate. The test is ammonia (white precipitate) or ammonium sulphide (black) [DUS1870]. Jewell's method sublimed the vapour into steam in a large receiver, giving an impalpable powder already washed free of corrosive sublimate [DUS1870]. Scheele's precipitation route (1788) later competed with sublimation [WIKI-CALOMEL].
- **The name.** First official in England as *mercurius dulcis* (London 1618). "Calomel" is traditionally from Greek *kalos* + *melas* ("beautiful black"), after the black it gives with alkali [DAVIDSON-BOYLE-ANNOT; search extract; **single**].
- **Doses, 1870** [DUS1870; conversions mine]:

| Purpose | Historical dose | Metric |
|---|---|---|
| Alterative (liver), nightly | ½–1 gr | 32–65 mg |
| Irritable bowel, cholera | ⅛–¼ gr every 1–2 h, to 1–2 gr a day | 8–16 mg per dose |
| To salivate | ½–1 gr three or four times a day, "increased considerably in urgent cases" | 97–259 mg/day |
| Purgative, adult | 5–15 gr "or more" | 0.32–0.97 g |
| Purgative, child of 2–3 years | not less than 3–6 gr | 0.19–0.39 g |
| Yellow fever, tropical dysentery ("heroic") | 20 gr to 1 drachm, repeated at short intervals | 1.3–3.9 g per dose |
| "Common" sedative megadose | 1–2 scruples every half-hour or hour | 1.3–2.6 g per dose |

- **Rush's heroic dose** (Philadelphia, 1793 yellow fever and after): about **20 grains (1.3 g) four times a day**, around 5.2 g/day, together with bloodletting [WIKI-CALOMEL; RISSE1973 by title; search extracts]. **Rush's Bilious Pills** (Lewis and Clark, 1803) held **10 gr calomel + 10–15 gr jalap** each, and the expedition carried **50 dozen** [LEWISCLARK, citing Will 1959].
- **Why megadoses were survivable, and the fault.** Calomel is nearly insoluble, so little is absorbed. The 1870 authors note that its purging does **not rise in proportion to dose** and that "enormous quantities" were given apparently without harm [DUS1870; SCIENCEDIRECT-MERCUROUS extract]. The danger is conversion. The same text warns that calomel turns partly into **corrosive sublimate** in the presence of chlorides (common salt, sal ammoniac), bitter almonds or prussic acid, and nitromuriatic acid, and it records a death from that combination. It also records sea-coast physicians avoiding calomel for sailors on salt meat, and salivation brought on by calomel taken with sour fruit or vinegar [DUS1870]. The failure is in the chemistry, not the dose: the same grains are mild or corrosive depending on what they meet.
- **Lethal figures.** No reliable human lethal dose for calomel exists in my sources. The rat oral LD₅₀ is **210 mg/kg** [WIKI-HG2CL2, citing NIOSH IDLH]. For **corrosive sublimate (HgCl₂)** the human lethal dose is about **1–4 g**; 1 g corrodes the gut, and 2 g raises mortality steeply [PMC10310567 case report; ScienceDirect/Berlin extract]. The 1870 therapeutic dose of corrosive sublimate was **1/12 to ¼ grain (5.4–16 mg)** three or four times a day, for secondary syphilis and skin disease [DUS1870]. Its lethal dose is therefore roughly 60 to 250 times the top single therapeutic dose.
- **Chronic harm.** Salivation proceeds in stages: a coppery taste; sore gums and aching tooth-sockets; swelling; a whitish line along the gum edge; "mercurial fetor" of the breath; then ulceration of mouth and throat, a swollen tongue and face, and loose teeth [DUS1870]. Teething powders with calomel caused **acrodynia ("pink disease")** in infants from the 1920s into the 1950s, when they were withdrawn (Britain, 1954) [ATSDR search extract; WIKI-HG2CL2; DALLY1997 via WIKI-ACRODYNIA]. The US Surgeon General Hammond struck calomel from army supply in 1863 [WIKI-CALOMEL].

### Metallic quicksilver
- It was swallowed **a pound or two** for bowel obstruction, "to act by its weight," a practice the 1870 authors call of doubtful benefit [DUS1870]. Liquid mercury swallowed is barely absorbed (**about 0.04%** of an amalgam dose, per ATSDR), while **inhaled vapour is 69–85% absorbed** [ATSDR tp46 via search extract; LDH factsheet]. The metal in the gut is nearly inert; the metal hot at the bench is the poison.
- **The occupational disease.** Water-gilders, looking-glass silverers and quicksilver miners suffered **"shaking palsy"** with vertigo and brain disorders, and miners were often salivated [DUS1870]. Modern description of **erethism**: irritability, low confidence, **shyness and timidity**, insomnia and memory loss, with a fine tremor that starts in the hands, shows in the **handwriting**, then spreads to the eyelids, lips and tongue ("hatter's shakes") [WIKI-ERETHISM; PubMed 34096847 by title].
- **Checks in WOTR.** Crucible palsy (Alchemetrica) is a tremor in the writing hand spreading to the head and jaw, with a withdrawal of manner: irritability, then shyness, then reluctance to be looked at. It is **a close match to real mercurial erethism with tremor**, and the Codex's Furveus ("Quicksilver does that to the man who measures it") gives the right cause. The real route is **vapour at the heated bench**, not handling; the Codex's "keyed to what he handled most" should be read as working hot. Alchemetrica's **onset at about nine years has no real figure** in my sources. Real onset depends on exposure, from months in hatters' shops to years, so WOTR's number stands as its own. Calomel "for a gut that would not move" is exactly the real purgative use, at 5–15 gr (0.3–1 g) an adult dose.

## 6. White arsenic (arsenious acid, arsenic trioxide, As₂O₃) and Fowler's solution

- **Doses, 1870** [DUS1870]. The average dose was **1/10 grain (6.5 mg) three times a day** in pill, usually with opium; the standard pill mass was 1 gr acid with 10 gr sugar and breadcrumb, divided into ten pills. For intermittent fever (ague) it was **1/20 grain (3.2 mg) three times a day**. A St Lucia physician gave **one-grain doses (65 mg)** in a solution for ague, six to eight doses in all [DUS1870; **single**]. Arsenic was used for cancer (especially of the lip) and for skin disease, chorea, periodic headache and intermittents. It was even smoked in cigarettes for asthma at **¼ grain** [DUS1870].
- **Fowler's solution** (*Liquor potassae arsenitis*; *Liquor arsenicalis* Br.). Thomas Fowler of Stafford published it in 1786 as a substitute for a quack "tasteless ague drop." It entered the London Pharmacopoeia in 1809 [DUS1870; CAMEO/WIKI-KAsO2 search extract]. The US formula is **64 gr arsenious acid + 64 gr potassium bicarbonate** boiled in 12 fl oz water, with ½ fl oz compound spirit of lavender added "to give it taste, and prevent its being mistaken for water," made up to one wine pint. The British formula used 80 gr of each to the imperial pint [DUS1870].
  - **Strength**: **½ grain As₂O₃ per fluidrachm** [DUS1870], which is **about 8.8 mg/ml, 0.88% w/v**. It is commonly rounded to "1%" [CAMEO; WIKI-KAsO2].
  - **Adult dose**: **ten drops two or three times a day** on average [DUS1870]. If a drop is taken as a minim, that is about **5.4 mg As₂O₃ a dose, 11–16 mg a day**; real drops vary, so this is an estimate. Mitchell's "large dose" for intermittents was **15–20 drops three times a day** (about 8–11 mg a dose on the same reckoning). Fuller gave **8–15 minims** for rheumatic gout. Dewees gave a six-week-old infant **six drops of a 1:13 dilution every four hours** [DUS1870].
  - **Uses by the 1880s**: ague and malaria (as a cheaper rival to quinine), sleeping sickness, psoriasis and eczema ("lepra"), chorea, asthma, anaemia, rheumatism, and later leukaemia [DUS1870; CAMEO/WIKI-KAsO2 extract; NRC1977 extract].
  - **Its own decay**: kept in flint glass it slowly decomposes, smells of garlic, and silvers the inside of the bottle with lead reduced from the glass. It also takes up oxygen to arsenate, so it is kept in small bottles filled to the stopper [DUS1870].
- **Lethal dose.** **100–300 mg** of arsenic trioxide can be fatal to an adult (ATSDR medical management, via search extract). IPCS gives **50–300 mg** of inorganic arsenic [IPCS-PIMG042]. Another toxicology source gives **1–2.5 mg/kg** [search extract]. Set against the 1870 doses, that is **about 15 to 45 of the tenth-grain pills**, or **roughly 11–34 ml of Fowler's**, less than an ounce of the bottle. Recovery was recorded from swallowing **at least a drachm (3.9 g)**, with symptoms delayed sixteen hours (Hartshorne, 1855), and from **about two drachms** treated with magnesia, with arsenic still in the urine on the twentieth day (Maclagan) [DUS1870]. These survivals exist because huge doses are often vomited.
- **The acute poisoning picture, 1870.** Christison's three orders: (1) gut inflammation, killing in **one to three days**; (2) collapse with little inflammation, death in **five or six hours**; (3) gut symptoms followed by nervous ones: partial palsy of the limbs, fits, coma. Some cases resemble cholera in collapse [DUS1870].
- **Chronic harm.** Prolonged medicinal use at the usual **0.04–0.09 mg/kg/day** (about 3–6 mg/day for 70 kg) often produced mild poisoning [NRC1977 extract; **single**]. Signs include puffy swelling (*oedema arsenicalis*), salivation, and loss of hair and nails [DUS1870]. **Mees' lines**, white transverse bands across the nails, appear weeks after absorption [IPCS-PIMG042]. Hyperpigmentation, **keratoses of the palms and soles**, and skin cancer follow: Hutchinson (1887/88) tied epitheliomas to long courses of arsenic, five of his six cases being psoriasis patients [NRC1977 extract; PubMed 14802140 by title]. Later came angiosarcoma of the liver after Fowler's [IPCS-PIMG042 references]. The **Styrian arsenic-eaters**, peasants said to take arsenic as a tonic and to suffer if they stopped, were reported by Tschudi. The 1870 authors judged the accounts true in part but "greatly exaggerated" [DUS1870; IPCS-PIMG042 cites Tardieu 1867].
- **Checks in WOTR.** "White arsenic in small measure for a girl with no colour in her": anaemia was a real indication (by the 1880s), and "small measure" is right, since the real dose was a tenth of a grain or ten drops. **Draft-mark** under the nailbeds has a partial real counterpart in arsenic's **Mees' lines** and the skin darkening. The **sclera** part of Draft-mark has **no counterpart** in my sources for these three poisons; it stands as WOTR's own.

## 7. Antimony: tartar emetic (antimony potassium tartrate)

- **Doses, 1870** [DUS1870; conversions mine]:

| Purpose | Historical dose | Metric |
|---|---|---|
| Alterative | 1/32–1/16 gr | 2–4 mg |
| Diaphoretic or expectorant | 1/12–1/6 gr | 5.4–10.8 mg |
| Nauseating sudorific | ¼–½ gr, every 1–4 h | 16–32 mg |
| Emetic, full dose | 2–3 gr | 130–194 mg |
| Emetic, usual way | 1 gr in a tablespoon of water every 10–15 min until the patient vomits | 65 mg per dose |
| Rasori's contrastimulant practice (Milan, from 1800) for pneumonia, pleurisy, rheumatism | 1–2 gr or more every 2 h; in one series raised from **4 gr to 16–20 gr a day** over 8–16 days | 0.26–1.3 g/day |

- **Tolerance.** Rasori held that a patient in high fever could bear doses that would poison a healthy one, and called this **tolerance**. The 1870 authors repeat that such doses "are sometimes borne with safety" in acute inflammation [DUS1870].
- **Lethal figures.** In 1870, **ten grains (648 mg)** was the smallest dose then reported to have killed [DUS1870]. Modern: **0.2 g** reported fatal (Miller 1982) [UKPID]; **130 mg** fatal in a case cited from 1967 [SCIENCEDIRECT-APT extract]. Survivals are enormous: a three-year-old survived **2.3 g** [UKPID], and an adult survived **half an ounce** (about 15 g) treated with green tea and tannin (McCreery, US Navy, 1853) [DUS1870]. Oliver Goldsmith died in 1774 after an estimated **132–198 mg** of antimony, with eighteen hours of vomiting and purging [UKPID].
- **Fault.** The **full emetic dose (130–194 mg) overlaps the lowest modern fatal doses**. What makes tartar emetic survivable is its own action: **the vomiting removes it** before much is absorbed [SCIENCEDIRECT-APT extract; UKPID]. When the vomiting fails, the patient is in danger. The 1870 text says that without vomiting and purging "the other symptoms are aggravated," and warns that it "sometimes acts even in small doses with unexpected violence" [DUS1870]. The antidote was tannin (galls, strong tea, cinchona bark), which forms an insoluble antimony tannate [DUS1870].
- **Other signs.** Applied to the skin (plaster or ointment) as a counter-irritant it raises a painful **pustular eruption**. Taken internally it can do the same [DUS1870; UKPID "antimony spots"]. In the body the metal goes mainly to the liver and is cleared by the kidneys [DUS1870].
- **Checks in WOTR.** Alchemetrica names antimony only as a cover-name example ("Our Lead for the antimony"). The Codex does not dose it. **No WOTR counterpart** to tartar emetic's dose table. If the Bench needs an antimonial, the real rule is that it is safe only because it comes back up.

## Open or weak points for the page writer

- The burning-wood demonstration is attributed to Paracelsus in secondary and tertiary sources only. I did not find the primary passage.
- "Three pints of saliva a day" and its attribution to Paracelsus rest on popular sources only. Omit it or mark it.
- The count of eighty-five chemical preparations in the 1618 London Pharmacopoeia is single-sourced.
- The 1666 Paris reinstatement of antimony is unconfirmed; 1566 (ban) and 1658 (the king's emetic wine) are confirmed.
- Calomel's human lethal dose has no figure in reliable sources; use the corrosive sublimate figure and the conversion warning.
- "A drop" in Fowler's doses is not a fixed volume; the milligram figures above take drop = minim.

# THE ALFTIAN CODEX

## Gameung-nok

### Volume the First, by Kwon Mu-jin

## A Note on the Name

I have called this book Gameung-nok. It is the word my house uses for what the Heralds call correspondence, and I mean by it what they mean: the law above held in the law below, as still water holds the face that bends over it. The archivists will shelve it under a name of their own, and I do not mind. I apologise for explaining a title before anyone has opened the book. I will do it again before the first chapter ends.

> "That which courseth above is as that which courseth below. That which burneth in Tempus burneth also in the Soul Crystal. There is but one law, and it is everywhere."
>
> Kwon Mu-jin, written on the flyleaf of the deposit copy

## Chapter the First · Of Frost and the First Glass

I grew up eleven miles from a mountain, and before I was twelve I had helped carry men down off it. The first thing I learned to read was a hand the cold had taken. It goes white and hard. Then it warms and blisters, and the dark blisters near the knuckles are the ones that matter. Over the next turns the dead part draws a line across itself, black on one side and angry pink on the other, and the line tells the surgeon where to cut. The hand usually belonged to a carter off the Uplands road, asking in two tongues whether he would hold a rope again.

Sum-gol is a rice valley in the south of Ketsuen. My house is Mahuo. I write the name once, so the book is honest about where it came from, and not again. The house had thinned before I was born. What remained was sixty roofs, a school with more benches than pupils, and the grandmothers' sentences about the breath, which goes out of a person into the valley and stays there, spread thin. The school's cedar beams still wept sap in summer, and the old men said it was the wards drinking. The ground pressed at my temples on the first day home from anywhere. After a month it stopped.

At twelve I bought a secondhand glass from a peddler, cracked in its second lens and priced as though I would not notice. I noticed, and paid less. For three years I charted the Weight with it against the fixed field and the Nail at the pole, from the school wall and then from a court windowsill. The old Hermetic books call it Tempus. A man who has seen it come round twice is old. I had watched a tenth of one circuit and thought myself patient, and I had nothing else to do in winter.

The first law I was taught belonged to our art, which is summoning, and the house has taught it for six hundred years. A summoner cannot compel. He is recognised or he is not, and a summoner who claims a standing he lacks gets nothing, and the nothing is the mercy. I was six. I took it for a rule about manners.

When I was twelve the last of the old house went out of the valley, the last grandmother to the ground and the last cousins to the court. Our practice for the dead does not ask a family to keep the body, and we did not keep hers. Ara was nine. She stood in the yard with her hands pulled into her sleeves and said, "Sum-gol went," in the house tongue, as you would say the snow had come. I put my hand on the top of her head.

By then I spent most of the year at the court, at the bench of its Senior Alchemist, Doryun. He taught me Draftcraft and the chains that close a working, in a voice he never raised and never needed to, reasonable and patient and final, so that you agreed with the end of his sentence before he reached it. Until I was fourteen I did what my teachers told me.

At fourteen a Fixatio array came to his bench for a patient, and I read its chain, and the chain was wrong. As drawn it would have killed her. I corrected it and put the correction in front of him, because the chain was wrong and for no other reason.

The court answered with a name, which I will not write. That year a lamp would gutter when I lost my temper, if it stood in the same room. Two did. I had crossed into Glory, where, as the primers put it, the self burns bright and is noticed, and after the second lamp I learned to keep my temper for the lamp's sake first.

Then the title-writ came, naming what I was worth before it named me, and I left the court on the day it was issued. The only thing I took that was mine was a single round lens on a thin chain, which rides my left eye as I write this, nearly five years on.

I went home at fifteen. Two grown men of the house had come back up the valley to settle me, bench and marriage and apology, and that is the house's second custom: it settles a person's life quietly, on that person's behalf, without asking. They settled mine in the lower hall at the tops of their voices, across a table laid for twelve that the house had not filled in my lifetime. I stood by the door and let them.

Ara was twelve, with silver-white hair loose past her shoulders and cut blunt across the brow, warm brown eyes, a small mole beneath the left one, and a school smock outgrown at the wrists. She walked into the middle of the hall and asked the elder what he wanted. Then she asked the younger. When their answers turned into each other's names she asked again, plainly, until the shouting had nowhere left to go. Afterward neither man could say what he had wanted for me that he did not want for himself.

I have never forgotten it. I haven't learned it either.

That autumn I took the road down to Castlefall and the line a day from there to the Genesio waystation, then two days by hired mule up the passes. The line was two years old. Up there the ground pressed harder at the temples than at home. The Archivum stands at the top of the archive-town, squat and weathered, the oldest house the Heralds keep, with open floors that anyone who reads may climb and sealed stacks below an iron gate. I copied out a book there on breaking the geometry of a net: find the shape that holds the knots in place and break it, and the knots come apart by themselves. I was fifteen and very pleased with that sentence.

Malphas sat most mornings on the bench by that iron gate with his forearms on his knees, not large, filling no room. The reading floor was cold enough to show breath. His fingers on the page were pink and easy. His hair was the grey-brown of straw left out through a wet autumn, his face narrow and unhurried, his coat good once and mended at the elbows. He was an alchemist. A school had put him out, and he never said which. He looked at that gate often, and at nothing else in the room for long. Beside him I never once felt the weight of a man above me on the ladder, and I have since learned that this is done on purpose, and that it tires the man who does it.

Furveus's writing hand shook with a fine fast tremor when he turned a page. His nail-beds had gone grey, and pale bands crossed the nails that no washing lifted. I knew both from the court and said nothing about either for more than two years, the last diagnosis I have kept to myself. He was broad in the shoulder and soft at the middle, in a physician's black gone brown at the cuffs, with hair the colour of wet rope and a laugh that arrived a little before the joke did. He had started at a bench at nine, carrying ash.

Neros's left eye turned outward a little, a poor thing for an astronomer, and he had solved it by shutting that eye. I met him over the charts. He was thin and dark, in a coat a size too large. He collected charts as farmers collect debts, and when he found my three years of the Weight he asked twice to copy them, in case I had not heard. I let him.

At the first frost the three of them came to the door of my room over a baker's in the archive-town, with the expression of men who have already decided what you will do. I had a refusal ready. Malphas set a book on the kitchen table and did not say a word.

It was a primer, and it had no number. Every primer I had held at court carried one, entered against my name on a roll. This one's registration leaf had been cut out close to the spine. Its subject was the Great Work, seven stages taught as a discipline of the matter on the bench and of the maker standing over it, and at thirteen I had asked the court for it and been told it was not for my grade.

I read it that night by the baker's lamp. I told myself reading broke no law, that the schedules watch who teaches and who takes pay, and that a boy alone in a kitchen with a book is neither, and I let that stand for innocence. Still, the state had decided I should not have it. By morning I held an opinion I have not put down since, that nobody should need a friend to smuggle such a thing to them.

Malphas never said where he had got it, and I did not ask. In the morning the book lay where he had set it, and the stub of the cut leaf stood up from the spine, a clean edge where a number had been.

## Chapter the Second · The Flat above the Tanner's

Castlefall smells of lime before anything else. The next spring the four of us took rooms there above a tanner's workshop, downstream and downwind as tanners always are, with the lime pits under our floor and the dung-bate warm in its vats beside them. We paid the tanner four silver a month. The station down the valley was three winters old, and its cedar platform still wept sap onto the boots of carters who greeted one another by asking who keeps your posts. We read at Genesio in the open months and wintered in the flat.

Malphas was glad that first winter, to judge by how much he talked, and one night by the stove he told us about his father's flax ponds, where the parish sinks its pulled flax under stones and sods and lets the water rot the gum from the fibre.

"The whole craft is the hour you lift them," he said. "Early, and the fibre holds to the core. Late, and it is tow. Nothing lives in the stream below." One autumn he weighted a bundle into the far corner of the last pond, where his father did not go, and left it past the hour to see what the rot did next. "He found it before the frost. It had gone grey and soft and kept the shape of a bundle, and it came apart when he lifted it." He smiled at the stove. "He burned it on the bank. I have never known a man so angry at flax."

I wrote the story down that night.

The illness came without declaring itself. Furveus coughed first. Malphas went pale, and his hands lost their ease. Neros, who had never taken up much of a room, took up less of one each day. The physicians came three times. Their notes gave it no name. It went on the way a burn goes on when nobody has told it the fire is out.

It never touched me. I have never known why.

On the fourth night I woke to a seal failing in the back room. I have felt seals since before I could read, a Draft's sealing glyphs pressing on me as a held breath presses on a room, and a failing seal I know from streets away. This one was a few paces off, where Malphas slept and kept a stoppered jar of Uplands herbs working on a shelf, and it had been working a long while. I said so through the door. He got up and resealed it without a word, and I went back to sleep.

I began studying medicine in secret the next morning. I told the others nothing, which I called tact. It was pride.

I wrote to Ara at the court wards, giving her three men's symptoms and the address of the flat and nothing else, and I told myself as I sealed it that I had not asked. She came inside the turn, thirteen years old, her hair plaited out of her way.

"That's asking," she said at the foot of the stair. "Why couldn't you write the word?"

"Correct," I said. "Come up. They're in the back room."

She worked the way she argued, fast and plain, and drew on the ground under the flat without noticing it was there. Her Wellspring's law is that a wound closes because the body remembers being whole: new tissue creeps along the scaffold its own torn edges leave, and her working gives it a reason to hurry. It cost her at the Shell. By the third hour her hands were hot to the wrist.

My Drafts did the other half: Coagulatio suspensions I brewed at the tanner's bench to the court's formula, from Uplands stock bought in the archive-town, which held each man's Shell still while his body got on with its business. They cost me nothing from reserve, and nothing I brewed put back a single unit of what the illness took out of them.

On the second night I took the lens off. I apologise for what follows; I know no other way to say it. When I do that the world goes quiet and specific, and that night it gave me the ground first: Castlefall's read Rill, high and steady, the kind that harms nobody, and my own η sat low in its band, near 0.61. Each man lay with one small bright knot in his Shell. It was still trying to finish something, like a chain with no close. The migraine sat behind my left eye until the next noon, with a taste of tin in it.

Ara slept in the chair toward midnight. We did not sleep. We had not eaten since morning. We read the third knot wrong twice, and caught it on the third reading.

I have left that "we" where it fell. It was me.

The tanner's mother was the one we could not save. A growth she had hidden for a year had reached her liver and yellowed the whites of her eyes. Ara looked at her once and pulled up a stool. She sat the whole night with her and told her the truth about her body, evenly, in the order it would happen. She promised her nothing. The old woman held Ara's wrist and slept toward morning, and did not wake, and at first light the tanner went up the street to tell the carver who kept its posts, which is what Castlefall does for its dead before it does anything else.

On the stair afterward Ara said the ending had been a passage, and her work was to make it an easy one. I wrote that down. I did not understand it.

My friends mended as the knots in them ran down, Neros least. At the end of the season I walked Ara to the line and said "Jal gara" at the carriage door, which is what the house says to a younger one going away, with my hand on her head. She let me. Then she told me my collar was on inside out, and it was.

The hour after midnight in Castlefall belongs to cats and to people who cannot sleep. On the footbridge over the tannery leat that spring I met two monks of the Heralds, and one of them named the Weight's place against the Nail through a gap in the cloud before I could. He was a square man of fifty or so with a nose broken once and set crooked, his beard cut short with a knife, his grey robe gone shiny at the knees. A drake's claw was bound with silver wire into the copper of his amulet. I noticed it and did not mention it. They asked why a boy with ink to the second knuckle was out at that hour, and I told them at far greater length than they had asked, and apologised for the length. The astronomer's name was Penn. They offered me a path.

When the passes opened they took the four of us up to Genesio, through a passage closed to ordinary traffic below the archive-town and across a bridged walkway over a river fed by falls. The hall at its end is older than the Heralds, older than Year Zero and the sealing the years are reckoned from, and its stone says so in the width of its courses. The monks called its ground the hollow. Lamps on brick stands lit the corridor, spheres of the Heralds' opalescent work, each with a slow pulse that reached the skin before the eye.

The Mausoleum was poorly kept. Two coffins lay at its far end, their labels crusted with dust and still legible. The first read: Vis, Alchemical Astronomer. The second: Caelus, maker of contraptions of cyclopean design. Beside them stood a cold forge bigger than any in the craft-streets, and there were trees, rooted through cracks in the floor, their crowns pressed flat against a ceiling that had no business holding the Uplands up.

Malphas asked about the trees. The elder monk answered in the order's theology: stone and root were joined to the Essence in all things, which the Heralds call God-Essence and hold to be Maelor's gift, and the hall stood because it breathed with the world above it. "Nothing is lost," he said, "only waiting to be remembered." It is Maelor's edict, cut over the Archivum's door above us, and I had never before heard it said underground.

I stood at the first coffin longer than at the second. I could not have said why. I took the lens off.

Riptide, ground where the Veil has begun to give. I set down the figures because I read them. To keep my own law in that hall took A-grade output, somewhere between 4.6 × 10¹⁰ joules at the floor of the band and 4.184 × 10¹² at its top. My reserve read somewhere past half a million units, high in Glory's band, and my η 0.66. To hold A at the floor I had to put some seventy thousand units into the act, a seventh of what I carried. I stood at the edge of myself. A site of that kind speaks to the Crystal and not to the mind, and gives a man seconds before it has him. Mine told me the ground under that coffin was holding a weight of its own, closed round it like a hand round a stone. A pull answered it at the floor of my Crystal, toward the coffin. I put the lens back on. The migraine found me on the walk back up the passage. I filed the matter with the others I had not examined, and I record that I filed it, which is all I mean to say.

They opened their curriculum to us, the Great Work taught as a discipline of matter and maker before anyone may use it as a method. I reached the ceiling of their elementary course in a quarter-moon. Furveus called that showing off, and he was right.

Penn took me aside before we went down, in the corridor under the brick-stand lamps. He said I carried a Crystal under more pressure than any student he had taught, that I read Aether as though somebody had taught me young, and that I was spending it in a tanner's back room. He asked whether I would take initiation into the order.

I told him I would return.

## Chapter the Third · Definitions

Furveus and I took the line to Urbis before the passes closed that autumn and left Malphas and Neros at Genesio. The carriage smelled of damp wool and warm brass. Nothing burns in an engine on the main; it rides the conduit on a hum you feel in your back teeth. I waved from the window.

At the Heralds' Medical University the supervisor read our papers as though he meant to dispute them before a magistrate, then said, with some strain, "You are accepted." We did well. I record it with the care I give a dose.

In our second season a lecturer set the class a Fixatio seal series to copy and correct, credited in the University's book to the Senior Alchemist Doryun of the court. I had written it at fourteen, at his bench, in the months between the array I corrected and the title-writ. I had known since then whose name it would carry.

I will describe the court's bench once. I worked the series in the evenings and left my pages on the corner of Doryun's table, and in the mornings they had gone into the court's book with his countersign beside mine. Then, one morning, with his name alone. He found me reading the entry.

"It will serve the court better under a senior hand," he said.

His voice did not rise. I said nothing. I was being carried through the halls that season as proof the court was brilliant, and I did not want a second room deciding what I was for. After I left he issued the series as his own.

Somebody had changed one link since it left my hand. In the fourth chain, where I had set [Ur] Balance to hold the gradient still under load, the University's copy had [Flx] Flux, which breaks rigidity and would break this seal the first time real load came onto it. I apologised for correcting the book, and then corrected it, at a length I apologise for now: a seal is a closing, and a closing with Flux in its middle has been told to open at the one moment it must hold. I put Balance back and said nothing about whose hand the rest was in. The lecturer wrote the correction in the margin, under Doryun's name.

On the stair Furveus laughed first. "That was yours," he said. "Well, it's better now, isn't it?" I did not answer. Then he let it go, which is a genial thing to do and a thing I have not managed.

I left before the Licentiate. I told myself I wanted no credential from a house that taught my work under another man's name, that this was a principle, and I did not look at it further. Furveus took his.

We went back to Castlefall, where he practised for fees and I beside him for none, under his licence. Next door a carver's boy worked his mallet on the practice stone from first light, and every third stroke landed late. The farmers came down off the terraces with split hands and lime-burners' coughs.

His method was Spagyria. A spagyrist parts a plant into three principles, purifies each and puts them back: the Sulphur is the oil from the still, the Mercury the spirit from the ferment, the Salt the ash of what is left, burned white and crystallised. Furveus read the three onto a Crystal's layers, Sulphur to the Core, Salt to the Shell, Mercury to the Attraction Layer, so his medicines treat a man one layer at a time. The same mapping makes the putting-back a Wedding, the most dangerous operation in the discipline.

The ferment took three turns in warm dung, unopened, going black as it should. The spirit then went into the Returner, whose two glass arms curve back into its own body, and rose and ran down into the charge for most of a month. We read the Whitening by its colour, pale and pearly when the purifying was done.

I took the lens off for the first Wedding I watched. Before he poured, Furveus laid the mediation glyphs round the vessel in wet ink, [Wvn] Weave at three points to lock a third glyph in place while the two principles meet, and [Ap] Aperture at the neck to let pressure out before it could spill. Then he poured the Sulphur into the Salt and the Mercury after, in one movement. The room's Aether was the steady Rill of the ground under a tannery, sour with lime, and my own η read 0.64; the bench's own stock carried the whole working, with nothing drawn from either of us. The law it ran on belongs to no single current. Two things that pull toward each other close the gap, as two drops on a pane run into one the instant they touch, and the Weave fixed the one place where they could meet. The strain was all in Furveus's body. His neck went red above the collar from holding the pour steady, and the tremor the pour had stilled came back twice as hard when he set the flask down.

The vessel held. Then I read him.

The tremor was what I had known it was since the Archivum: crucible palsy, early. It comes at about nine years of bench service, and he had twelve. The grey was Draft-mark, keyed to what he handled most: quicksilver, calomel for a gut that would not move, white arsenic in small measure for a girl with no colour in her. Both heal at a dose. Past it they poison, and the man who measures takes his own share every day he measures. Under all of it lay a current I had not known was in him. Mortalis. A true harmonisation, steady, the gate in him standing where a gate stands.

I told him what the tremor was, plainly, in the yard, with the flask still warm between us, and I told him how it ends: the head and jaw next, and then a man who does not want to be looked at. He heard all of it. Then he clapped my shoulder, said he knew and that it was a fair price, and asked if I wanted lunch. The migraine lasted until dark.

On an afternoon of wet snow, at the café on the square, two men sat down at Furveus's table as though they had been invited. The first thing I saw of Gisli Draycott was a dead front tooth, grey among the white. He smiled often enough to show it. He was forty or so, lean, fair hair greying at the temples, in good grey wool with a signet worn smooth on his smallest finger, and he told us within a minute that he had been educated in astrological practice. Ivor Strom was twenty years older, old by the Weight's count, white-bearded, with a wine-coloured birthmark over one ear and a stiff knee held straight under the table, in an oilskin too big for him. He turned his cup, and turned it again, and watched the door.

They talked to Furveus about what they called Taboo, with the ease of men who find the forbidden restful: necromantic theory, the Veil, what a dead man leaves in a room. I stayed and finished my bread. When Draycott called Mortalis the river the dead go down, I corrected him.

"I beg your pardon," I said. "It is a gate. Auren's Still Gate. The river is Sylorin's soulstream, a Pantheon image, and it is no current. Mortalis carries nobody anywhere. A current may be borrowed and stepped out of again. A gate is where a body stops holding itself together."

Draycott looked pleased. I have corrected a great many people, and pleasure is the rarest face I have been given for it. He asked me to say it again more slowly, and watched me the whole while, and I have thought since that he was taking down my manner, having the matter already.

Strom said, to nobody, "There are old things in the mountains that want finishing."

When they had gone I told Furveus not to. I said plainly that these men wanted something from him and had not said what, that I thought it was the Mortalis in him, and that he should get up and walk home with me now. He smiled at me as he smiled at farmers who told him they felt fine.

Furveus did not get up.

## Chapter the Fourth · Corpus Hermeticum

Malphas and Neros came down from Genesio at the next thaw. Malphas's left hand was bound in linen from knuckles to wrist, not yet healed, the binding changed by somebody who did not know how. I asked.

"Two visitors," he said. "One you have met. The other goes by a title. They had the sealed stacks, and I had a pen."

"What do they want written?"

"A treatise. They have named it already: the Necrocursica. They wish my name on it." He lifted the bound hand. "They have given me a title as well. Scribe to the Genesio Archivum, by order. It is a very long name for a man who copies."

He would say nothing more. He was lighter that spring, though: he laughed at Neros's charts and slept through the night. I was glad. I write that I was glad because it is true, and because I will want to have written it down.

When the passes opened I went back up with them and pulled the necromantic texts from the open floors to learn what Furveus was being drawn into, since asking him only got me smiled at. In a commentary bound in cracked calf I found the older thing the tradition calls the Aphorism of Mortalis, a doctrine set inside the necromantic writings to limit them. The power to raise the dead defeats itself. To raise the dead requires a diminishment of the living. Whoever does it is a debtor: he borrows the current of Mortalis and cannot step back out of it without a cost to his own Crystal.

Three shelves along, in a Herald hand two centuries old, was the doctrine I already held: what is above answers to what is below as a face to its reflection, and the Heralds call it correspondence and keep it as law. I copied both into one notebook on facing pages and went to find Malphas.

He was on the stair to the sealed stacks, two steps down already, with the iron gate open behind him and a key on a cord round his neck. The Heralds' seal on the gate's lock plate was worn bright where hands had tried it. He climbed back up to talk to me. He had been looking at that stair as long as I had known him, and I did not ask what had opened it.

I asked what I should do about Furveus. He sat on the bench with his forearms on his knees, the good hand over the bound one.

"Leave it," he said. "Let him find his own way through."

I began to answer.

"Leave it, Mu-jin."

He was older than me and wiser, and I listened, which is not a thing I do easily.

Leaving Furveus alone was the only advice I had been given, and I could not keep it sitting in the flat. So I went to take up the Heralds' offer at Urbis. I came down to Castlefall on the line and rode on the same night, since the line runs by day and I did not want to wait for day. A farmer at the valley's mouth let me the use of his horse for three silver marks, twice what it was worth and the last silver I had. He told me not to ride in that weather. I paid him anyway. It snowed. The last ward-post of the valley stood white on its windward face. Beyond it I stopped at the fourth of the old stones along the high road, weathered blank, to rest the horse.

A man came out of the house beside it and asked whether I had eaten. He had a bowl in front of me before I answered. The hand that set it down was burned across the back, the old burn a lens-grinder takes from his own pitch pot. He was of middle years, broad-faced and weathered brown, grey threaded through black hair tied at the nape, in a sheepskin coat gone dark at the seams. His eyes were a green I have since seen only in bottle glass.

"Venur," he said, when I asked. "Witnessed at my mother's door, and again at the border."

He was an astrologer, he said, and had heard worse stories than mine and outlived them. His attic was under the thatch. He brought a brass telescope down from the rafters and found the Weight before I did; two men had done that to me now. He was born on the New World border, where the old ground of Eresse runs into the chartered arc and two orders that agree on nothing share the same wells, and his learning came from both. Above and below, to him, was one law in a star and a stone. Then he spoke in the voice of a man reciting something he has carried a long way:

> "Truth. Certainty. That in which there is no doubt. That which is above is from that which is below, and that which is below is from that which is above, working the miracles of one thing. As all things were from One. Its father is the Sun and its mother the Moon. The Earth carried it in her belly, and the Wind nourished it, as Earth which shall become Fire. Feed the Earth from that which is subtle, with the greatest power. It ascends from the earth to the heaven and becomes ruler over that which is above and that which is below."

When I left he put an amulet of worked copper in my hand, cut on both faces in a Parunic hand. I read it at once. It was Old High Runic, very old, every arc squared off and every terminal blocked, and cut correctly, which almost nobody manages. I could not date it. The chain was [Ma] Recall, [Ir] Continuum, [Lei] Binding; I was taught to call the first of those Memory. What Venur had recited was his own gloss of that working.

It is a Charm. It does one small working and never tires of it. It draws nothing from the air, since whoever cut it paid once from their own reserve, long ago, and I pay nothing to wear it. What it does is press a slow attunement against its wearer's Shell, as a clock on a shared shelf pulls a second clock into its own swing over a night, the two pendulums falling into step through the wood between them. The three glyphs are the shelf. I rode on to Urbis with it cold against my sternum, and I have since called what I felt that morning Wellspring resonance, which is my word and may be nobody else's.

Urbis in the morning smells of wet cement and brass polish, and they were setting tram-rails into the high street as I rode in. The Prior had heard of my pharmacology. He asked what I wished to learn.

"Everything," I said. Then, because he waited, I explained what I meant by everything, and at the end of it I heard myself give him the court's form of address for a senior master, which has no place in a Herald house. I corrected it, and apologised for the form and for the explaining. He looked at me with the face of a teacher who has been handed the whole syllabus back in one breath. I asked to study without taking the order's vow, and he let me.

The Monastery stands on a high platform behind the great coffee-house on the square, reached by the Heralds' bridge. It is an inscribed array that hums, and stepping through its field is stepping into cold water and finding the water warm. The Heralds call what hums in it God-Essence.

At the first class, on an open platform under the night sky, we were taught God-Essence, the Archonic scale and the Wellsprings mirrored small in every Crystal, all of which I had walked alone. I watched the sky instead, and Penn sat down beside me and caught me looking at the claw.

"My father's," he said. "Ralf. He hunted drakes under a guild licence. The hunters say a drake hatched the year the Weight comes back to its station is the worst of its kind. Weight-bred, they call them. He went after one when I was nine. The claw came back."

Penn Ralfsohn, then, with his father's name in his own each time anyone addresses him, in a house whose whole office is remembering. Before Ralf went out he had given his son this:

> "It is a certain point full of admiration, that the high and the low are but one same thing. To make from a single thing enclosed in the whole world marvelous effects through adaptation. From one thing, all things have been made by meditation. If you change it into earth, it retains its full power: separating by great art, but an easy manner, the subtle from the dense, and the earth from the fire. From earth it ascends to heaven, and then back to earth, from heaven it descends, gradually receiving the virtues of both that it encloses in its belly."

I asked how he had endured.

"Through adaptance," he said. The law that carried his father up from earth to heaven carried grief down, and through, and out the other side, if you did not fight the descent. "All paths lead to the Still Gate. Only peace walks through it. My father was not peaceful, so I have had to walk it for him."

"Death is not the opposite of life," I said. Ara had taught me that on a stair in Castlefall, and then I went further than she had, with more confidence. Nothing that has been is erased, I told him. Maelor keeps it. Anamnesis holds the past because the past is never let go, and every Crystal carries every version of itself, and what appears to end is released into the keeping. He heard me out and did not correct me. That night I put his lesson in the archive I keep in my mind, beside the dose tables and the charts of the Weight, and it has been the most load-bearing entry there.

In the dormitory I dreamed of lucid corridors and towers of books in impossible balance, a Herald sphere pulsing slow on every shelf, and at the top of every tower a book I could not read. I have had the dream every night since. The books at the base are always ones I have read, and the ones at the summit change.

Asclepius held her chalk at an angle nobody could copy, because her right wrist had been broken once and set a little crooked. She was the primary teacher of the higher classes, a High Elf, taller than any man in the room, with hair the white-gold of a candle flame seen through horn, cut blunt at the jaw, a long face, and the fine lines at the eyes that elves wear only after a very great while. Her grey robe had been let out at the shoulders by somebody who sewed badly.

About God-Essence she was a complete atheist: Wellspring Essence in a Herald's robe, she called it, in their own lecture hall. I argued the Heralds' side with her across half a winter. She did not convert. She listened, which I valued more.

"You are the first who has explained it without praying at me," she said. "Or flinching when I push. Do it again."

Still much to learn, I wrote in my margin that night.

In the recess I wrote a manuscript on correspondence as the Heralds read it and as I did, the Wellspring's law held in the Crystal's, each the other's mirror. I wrote in it the thing I was surest of: nothing that has been is erased, since Maelor keeps it, and Anamnesis is the current by which the world holds its own past. It went about the Alftian country copied from hand to hand, for nothing, which is what I had meant by it. Instead of coin I had a letter from the Prior, headed in the order's Latin, Ordo Praeconum Maeloris, saying that the higher classes had found my work significant. It mattered more to me than coin would have. I am still examining why.

A Sacred Discourse was convened in the Monastery's circular stadium, the Prior and the senior teachers tier above tier in dark robes against the stone. They raised me to the highest tier of study. Behind me nobody in the ring moved; I would have heard the robes.

The classes began again next morning and ran into one another as dye runs into water.

## Chapter the Fifth · The Perfect Discourse, and the King Who Would Not Listen

There was a king.

He had been elected to govern a city-state near Urbis, one of the small principalities that stand round the Heralds' city as cold men stand round a stove. After his election he stopped being governable. His advisers tried reason and were dismissed, and his court tried petition and was dismissed with them. His wife tried nothing. She stood in the corner of his hall with her hands folded at her waist and her face turned to the wall-hangings, and along her jaw on the left there was fresh bruising, yellow at the edges and plum in the middle, four days old or five. I saw her before I saw him. He was a heavy man, younger than I had expected, in a coat of state buttoned wrong, and his hands never stopped moving on the arms of his chair.

The Monastery sent me. It gave no reason, and I asked for none.

It is a short run out of Urbis. There was the bell of arrival, then the new cement of the lower district, grey-white and smelling of wet lime, then the long walk up to the castle's embrasure. Two monks came with me and removed the courtiers who tried to intervene, by standing in front of them until they went.

The king and I faced one another across his hall. I took the lens off.

His resonance was dry. It had angles where a living one has curves, and when I spoke of his wife it did not move at all. The migraine began at once, a tight band behind my left eye and then a pulse inside the band. I kept the lens in my hand anyway. Whatever Stage he stood at, and I did not read it, I held my own weight down the whole time. It is a tiring skill. I would not win with pressure; the house law says a summoner who claims a standing he lacks gets nothing, and I have come to think the same holds for a man who uses the standing he has to shout.

I spoke to him. I should write that I spoke over him, since he had begun first, and I went on in the voice I have been told is remarkable or insufferable depending on the listener's disposition. I told him that what a ruler does to those beneath him is done also at his own soul's account. The account is kept whether he reads it or not. I told him about the Weight over his own roof, and the drake-hunters who died under it, and the difference between owning a thing and keeping it. He was stubborn. He answered each thing I said with the thing he had said before it, and his hands went on working the arms of the chair.

So I shifted register. I let the words come from where the bench and the sky and the Hermetic writers had been piling up in me since Malphas put a primer on a kitchen table, and I gave him the Tablet whole, in the version the Heralds teach, its last line in the voice of the one who first spoke it:

> "True it is, without falsehood, certain and most true. That which is above is like to that which is below, and that which is below is like to that which is above, to accomplish the miracles of one thing. And as all things were by contemplation of one, so all things arose from this one thing by a single act of adaptation. The father thereof is the Sun, the mother the Moon. The wind carried it in its womb, the earth is the nurse thereof. It is the father of all works of wonder throughout the whole world. The power thereof is perfect. If it be cast on to earth, it will separate the element of earth from that of fire, the subtle from the gross. With great sagacity it doth ascend gently from earth to heaven. Again it doth descend to earth, and uniteth in itself the force from things superior and things inferior. Thus thou wilt possess the glory of the brightness of the whole world, and all obscurity will fly far from thee. This thing is the strong fortitude of all strength, for it overcometh every subtle thing and doth penetrate every solid substance. Thus was this world created. Hence will there be marvellous adaptations achieved, of which the manner is this. For this reason I am called Hermes Trismegistus, because I hold three parts of the wisdom of the whole world. That which I had to say about the operation of Sol is completed."

The words were mine to say. What came with them I did not choose.

Somewhere in the middle of the recitation a field of my own took the room. The hall's air was thick with him, a year of one man's certainty soaked into the Residue of the stones and the district main humming under the flags. Nothing in that room moved that he had not permitted to move. I have watched the same law at the bench a hundred times. A solution held past the point where it should have set stays clear as long as nothing gives it a place to begin. Then one grain goes in. The whole charge sets round it in a breath. Coagulatio is that law, and my voice was the grain. What set in that hall was the king's conduct against his own soul's account, held still together long enough for each to be read by the other. In me the cost came out as heat at the Shell and a thirst that shut my throat. At the edge of seeing stood a blue with broken edges, arc-blue, the colour my Fulguria takes under strain, shards of it standing off my hands, and the fine hair on my forearms rose. For the first time I could watch my own η while it moved, 0.62 and then 0.67 and back, the waste leaving me as heat and my attention able to take a little of it back.

The primers call that the first seed of a Domain.

The king wept.

I do not know whether it was the words, or the field, or a slip in the weight I was holding down, and I have written all three because I cannot choose among them. He wept with his hands open on his knees. He governed gently afterward, at least while I was near enough to hear of it. The monks took his wife out of the hall to somewhere safer. She went without looking back at him, and I have thought about that more than about anything he did.

Outside it was raining. I sat on the wet stone of the step longer than was practical, my hands not still, the migraine working behind my eye. My eyes blurred first, then cleared on two things at once. For the first time in my life I saw myself from outside: a boy on a step in a scholar's coat gone thin at the elbows, a lens in his fist and rain in his white-blond hair, being turned while he sat there into something a room would want. I had crossed into Refraction.

When I came back to the Monastery they had begun to call me the Thrice-Great. It comes from the Tablet's last line, the line I had given in its own speaker's voice, and I know the shape of a name a room gives a person, having been given one before. Penn used it once, kindly, at the refectory door.

"That is not my name," I said.

## Appendix · Notes toward the Continuing Work

Affixed by the hand of Kwon Mu-jin, for the use of whoever continues this work after his passing or departure from the Monastery. Direction only.

**The Emerald Principle.** As above, so below. The Heralds read it as the structural law of correspondence between the Wellspring scale and the Crystal's, and so do I: the one is the other's mirror. Future study must address the full Hermetic grammar.

**The Tria Prima of Furveus.** Sulphur to the Core, Salt to the Shell, Mercury to the Attraction Layer. The separating and the Whitening are safe in a careful house. The Wedding carries the Rupture risk of any Conjunction, and the method requires formal documentation in his hand while his hand still writes.

**The Great Work.** Seven stages, walked once through the first seven Stages of the ladder and again through the seven above them. It describes the Temperance path itself. I stand, I think, at the Coagulation of the first round. I have written "stand" as though a Coagulation were final, and I withdraw the word. I am setting, which is slower.

**Eastern and Western.** Venur's border learning and the Heralds' curriculum describe one relation in two grammars, bridged by correspondence.

**Iatrochemistry.** Quicksilver, calomel, white arsenic, by their names. The dose is the point: each heals at a measure and poisons past it, and the man who measures pays in his hands. Furveus is paying.

**Malphas's treatise, the Necrocursica.** Written under compulsion. I have not read it. I intend to. Mortalis requires understanding, and avoidance teaches nothing. The Aphorism, the debt of a borrowed current, must be examined with the rigour I gave the doses.

**Draycott and Strom.** I have not resolved the question of their purpose. It does not feel resolved from the inside.

**The Seven Cacodaemonic Corruptions.** The seven stages of the Work turned against themselves, each severed from the current it draws on: Calcination from Cinerion, Dissolution from Dissolution, Separation from Judicium, Fermentation from Rebirthine, Distillation from Sublimare, Coagulation from Coagulatio. Conjunction has no current of its own and runs on Attraction, so its inversion injures Attraction itself. They are the next volume's subject. I met the inversion of Calcination once, briefly, in the king's hall, where it wore the shape of certainty that had never once been questioned.

**A coffin at Genesio.** I have not asked.

## To Ara

When I have something worth saying I will say it to you first. I said that to you in a wool store at Rimward, and you took my wrist instead of my word, which was fair, because the sentence I said after it was a lie. This one was not. So the book opens where I promised, on the question, and it opens to you.

I am writing in a room in Urbis with an east window above a square. The trams run under it on the conduit all day. In the small hours the district is let down for its maintenance and the hum stops, and I wake in the quiet, which is how I know the time. I breathe with one side of my chest. I write with the thumb and forefinger of my left hand, and the Silent Archivists will find the hand worse than the first two volumes. The first two apologised for their explaining. This one does not.

The title page carries a Division and a vow. The Archivum's form asks a depositor for his standing, and I have given it as a filing. Above my name I have still put nothing. The Division sealed the first two volumes, which I learned at my own door, and I deposit this one for common use all the same, because the vow is a teaching vow and a sealed book teaches nobody.

The Sovereign died at Kharven on the day of the road, covering the boy. You saw him carried. I was on the road. I know it the way the Division's clerks know it, from a list, and what you saw is yours; a page would only make it smaller. Ara-ya. I put my hand flat on this sheet where your head would be, which a grown man at a desk has no reason to do. I did it anyway.

The second volume told you nothing of a year it should have told you. This one tells it, and what came after: the night at the Carver's Seat and the woman who watched it; the room at Sum-gol and the nine in it, and how eight of them went; the road, the camp, the Accord I drafted and the letter I sent you from it before morning. Then a letter on my own desk, which I did not open for four months, and where it sent me when I did. The rest of the book is for common use, as the first two were. This part is yours.

## Chapter the Thirteenth · Rimward

The wall was two courses of dressed stone and whatever the roof had been carrying, and it came down into the harbour street toward a boy of eleven. You were not in the street. I give it to you as it was, because in the wool store I was sparing.

The fighting had come down to the harbour, and I was in the street because I had no business being anywhere else, which you told me afterward in fewer words. The boy stood under the line of the wall with a basket of salt fish. I had about a second and a half. No Draft I know forms that fast, and I have never met anyone whose does.

Frithia was under the same wall, going for the boy.

I had not known she was in the town. She had come to the Rimward house with the Sovereign's dressers after the Seat fell, and left our son with the schoolmaster, and nobody had told me. I think she arranged that. She was two strides from the boy with her arms already out, and the stone was already leaning over both of them.

So I asked without the Draft. I asked as though I held a standing I do not hold, and I knew what I was doing while I did it. I had time for one thought. It was that I would rather answer for the asking afterward.

Two laws should have met me there. The first is the house law, which you and I had before we had our letters: a summoner cannot compel; he is recognised or he is not; a summoner who claims a standing he lacks gets nothing, and the nothing is the mercy. By that law the wall comes down on both of them while I stand in the street with my mouth open. The second is the warrant law of the Counted Speech. A warrant that overstates the speaker's standing seals anyway, and takes the gap out of the speaker's body. By that law I am emptied by exactly the amount I lied. I spoke no warrant. I counted nothing, I drew no circle, and there was no seal for anything to close on.

What happened fits neither law. Something outside both answered, and the eye was its price.

The wall did not fall on either of them. The right side of the world went.

The boy lived. When I came up out of it nine days later he was down the hall in a bed, with his mother asleep on the floor beside it.

You remember the rest better than I do. Lanolin under the vinegar. Lye on the boards, and chalk tracked in from a white foreshore, so that every man who walked the hall left a pale print behind him. You had both my hands for four hours when they carried me in, and you know what spent Essence feels like coming back up a patient's arms, and you told me that what came up mine was something else. I told you to say it in Sum-gol, because you argue better in it. You did. I told you I would not discuss what had answered, and why: I had spent nine days trying to give it a shape and it would not take one.

I have spent a season more on it in this room, and it still will not, and I will not lend it one in ink.

You took my pulse in the middle of the argument, which you have always done. Eighty-eight. Then you made me promise nothing would happen to me, and I heard the sentence go out with nothing standing under it, and you called me a liar, and I agreed.

So here is the question, first, as I promised it. At Rimward I asked below, with nothing under my asking, and something answered at once and the whole way. I want to know what answers a man who asks like that, and on what terms, and what it keeps of him afterward.

## Chapter the Fourteenth · The Carver's Seat

It began with page three.

In the spring of the year I was twenty-two, a boy came into the east room behind his cat and told me the third page of the entering grammar was wrong. It was. A copyist had dropped a link from the second chain, and a seal described that way would open under the first real load it met. The entering grammar is a controlled text. The kingdom keeps its standard at Vaeloris, at the Carver's Seat, and a standard with a dropped link in it had gone out to every child in the kingdom entered that year. I carried the correction there myself at the turn of summer into autumn. I could have posted it. I have never once in my life posted a correction I could carry.

The Seat stands in a warm basin over an old vent, built in quarried stone where the rest of the kingdom builds in cedar, and the ground there presses at the temples harder than at home. A carver at the gate asked who kept my posts. I told him the school's carver did, at Sum-gol, and he looked at the lens and let me through. All day the mallets went on the practice stones across the basin. Hundreds of them. The stroke that landed late carried furthest. A technique standardised at the Seat belongs to the kingdom before it belongs to anyone who learned it, and a correction heard there is the kingdom's from the moment it is heard. I had known that when I set out.

They made me wait for a hearing, and I waited in a lodging on the basin's rim.

On the fourth night the Seat's seals went. I have felt a failing seal as a held breath since I was a boy, and this was many breaths from one hall, going one after another. The Seat, I learned afterward, had been testing a Legion-class working for the kingdom's standard, a great many hosts held in one field by one summoner, and the summoner's hold had broken in the test hall. That night I knew only that the seals were going. I went toward them with a piece of chalk and the grammar I had come to correct.

I laid a Draft circle on the test hall's floor in chalk, because chalk over dressed stone is Draft ground if the hand that lays it means it to be. The vent under the basin keeps that ground dense, and a circle on dense ground carries a call further than it would carry at home. Then I called as what I was, a student and a teacher of the house. I asked the hosts for nothing I had no standing to ask. I asked them to stand down. Vocatia works as a struck fork works, which makes another of its own pitch sing across a room and leaves every other fork silent: a call that claims only the standing it has is heard by whatever is tuned to that standing, and by nothing else. They answered one at a time. Each host that answered let go of the field that had held it. The field came apart in the circle as they went out of it, until there was nothing in the hall but the summoner on his knees and a great deal of heat coming off my Shell. The headache after was the calling headache, milder than the migraine, and the chalk had worn down to my fingers.

A woman had watched all of it from the hall door.

The first thing I read of her was a white seam through the meat of her left forearm, a torn wound badly stitched and healed hard. A walrus, she told me later, a long way from any physician. The basin is warm, and her sleeves were shoved up. Then the rest of her. She was taller than I am by a finger's width, long in the bone and angular everywhere. Her brown hair was cut blunt at the jaw and pushed back under a hat of ringed-seal fur that she had made herself and did not take off indoors. A long face, all precise planes. Dark eyes set deep under a straight brow. Three fine lines were tattooed down her chin from lip to jaw, with a fourth beside them; she has told me the three were done when she was fourteen and that she added the fourth herself at twenty-two, and she has never told me what the fourth is for. She wore a sealskin jacket worn pale at the elbows and the cuffs, too warm for the basin. Her hands were split across the knuckles from cold and from work. She was thirty-four. The house noticed the twelve years between us before I did.

She said something to me in her own tongue first, which I do not have. Then she said it in Accord common.

"Show me the page."

I showed her. She looked at it a long time and gave it back.

She told me later that she had come south that year from the ice country to teach the Seat's wardens to fight and to mend in the cold, for pay, and that she thought little of anything a person keeps in a book. I have that from her. It is all I have of why she was in the door.

She came up the valley within the turn. The schoolmaster took her into the house as its nurse, and she taught the older children the knife and the staff in the yard. We had one winter. It was the winter my Stage turned in the schoolroom, and I record that it was the same winter and make nothing of it.

Our son was born at the end of the next summer, and Ara came back off the circuit to deliver him. The labour was long. I noticed first that his hands were too large for him, which I have since been told is not a thing a father says aloud. Then I noticed him. He has her mouth, and he sets it in the same straight line when the world has displeased him. We called him Geturo.

When the child was coming, the house had moved to settle it in the house's way. The schoolmaster wrote to the cousins at court, and their answer came back up the valley settling everything: a marriage, a bench for her in the house, and an apology to the cousins for the order in which things had happened. Nobody had asked her. Frithia heard the letter read out at the kitchen table, with the cousins' seal broken beside the bread, and said one sentence.

"I did not come up this valley to be settled."

Later, to me alone, she said the other thing. "And do not write me down."

I did not. The second volume has no word of her and no word of the child, and that was her asking and my keeping of it. Why she asked, I can only guess. I think her people keep what matters in mouths, and a name on paper is a thing others can read without her in the room. The second volume confesses what I kept, and I stand by the list. Her absence from it is hers.

I had written her into these pages in Urbis before I asked whether I might. I asked when I came home. Her answer is at the end of this book.

Ara, the first condition in the Accord I drafted, that your consent may not be presumed, implied or extracted through family obligation, came out of that winter. So did the letter I wrote you before morning.

## Chapter the Fifteenth · The East Room, the Year After

The second volume went down to Urbis in the spring and into the Archivum for common use. I came back up the valley from the station and taught.

The east room kept its nine names that year. I entered nobody new in it. The school took the new ones instead, from the valley and past it, once the room's name had travelled: families from the lower reach and the potters' settlement, a carver's household from two stations down the warded track, a widow off the Uplands road with three children and a cart. By the spring after there were forty on the terraces in their ranks.

We taught under the valley's standing, on the record, without fee. The school fed me. It fed my son and his mother too. The cedar beams wept sap through the summer, and the old men said the wards were drinking. Every morning the carver's boy by the yard wall went at the practice stone with his mallet, and the stroke that landed late was always his.

Doyun was fourteen and then fifteen. He did everything fast, including grow, and he let his smock down twice that year and still showed his wrists. He had the best hand in the room and the worst patience. He could pour a suspension now without looking at his hands. He asked questions the grammar could not hold, and when it could not hold them he argued with the grammar. The second Nabi slept on my feet through every lesson.

The schoolmaster ruled her lines with the straight edge her fingers could barely close on. Frithia drilled the older children in the yard with the knife and the staff to a count, and they learned to move as a line, to pass what they were handed, and to stop when she stopped counting. I learned not to stand in the yard while she was doing it. Geturo learned to sit up on the boards of the east room, under the bench nearest the door, where the first Nabi died, and the second slept against his back.

The morning I remember best came late in the thaw. Doyun had the board. He was drawing out a Fixatio seal chain for the younger ones, fast and with a great deal of arm, and I read the chain before I looked at the boy, as I always do. His third link stood before the second. A seal drawn that way closes clean and opens under the first real load. The Tak girl, one of the carter's twins, put her hand up from the second bench and said so to the whole room, exactly and correctly and loud. Doyun's ears went red to the tips.

I have been that girl. At fourteen I put a correction in front of a man at a court bench because the chain was wrong and for no other reason, and it cost me the court. The chain was still wrong.

"Correct," I said to her. "Now go up and show him where you would put it back. Say it to the slate."

She went up. She said it to the slate. Doyun moved the link himself, and the room copied the chain down right.

The other lesson I keep was on the terrace in the summer, with the younger ranks. The first grammar is said before it is drawn. The children stood in their ranks and said its links to the long count, one link to a count, while I walked the rows behind them and listened for the link that came out wrong. The Hwang girl, the smith's youngest, had the front of the first rank. She wanted the examination the older ones talked of, and every morning for a turn she asked me whether it was real, and every morning I told her it was, and that the grammar came first, and she said her next link louder. In the third rank one boy was always half a beat behind the rest. I stood at his back for a whole count and heard nothing wrong in any link he said, only in when he said it. I left him behind. The mallet at the yard wall had taught me that much.

Ara walked the circuit with me that year, when she was not in the lower reach with a fever or a birth. A bench and a slate in each settlement; she treated while I taught. In the potters' settlement they began to keep a second bench by the kiln for her, and she began to use it.

## Chapter the Sixteenth · Wherever I Was Sent

The Carver's Seat reported the Legion night to the Guild Accord, as the Seat reports whatever happens inside its walls, and the Division of Alchemetrica entered me on its books as a Registered Natural. I held no academy credential. I left the court at fourteen and read at the Monastery without its vow. A Registered Natural submits to monitoring in place of a record: periodic reassessment, demonstration, disclosure of every new harmonisation.

Four reassessors came up the valley in turn. Each brought a chain or an instrument to test me against. Each time the chain or the instrument was wrong, and I said so, to his face, because it was wrong. Each wrote on my file that I was temperamentally unsuited to field deployment. They were right about me. The chain was still wrong, each time.

The fourth gave me a rank: Archmagus of the Division of Alchemetrica. The writ named my grade before it named me anything else, as a writ had done to me once before, at fourteen, when I walked out of the room. This time I read to the end.

A rank is a licence. An Archmagus may certify teachers and enter pupils for the kingdom's examination in the Draft trade, and no child of our valley outside a blood or a hall had ever been entered for it. I signed for them. I knew what else I was signing, and I signed. They gave me a silver token for my collar, and I wore it and did not use it. The Division that ranked me is one Division, and the one that had already sealed my books is another. I learned the second at my own door.

Then the Division posted me, and it posted me to teach. I went wherever I was sent. The schoolmaster and Doyun kept the east room behind me.

The first posting was a draw-town on a main, two days down the line. The Guild gave me the back room of the meter house, where the hum came up through the floor, and a slate laid flat on a bench buzzed against the wood until a child put a hand on it. Eleven clerks' children came to me after their own lessons. They had been told that a Draft is a small working a clever child can buy the chain for, and the meter-reader's girl had bought one. She was twelve. She wanted a licence to draw, which in a draw-town is the whole of a future: a licence is a seal and a wage, and a child without one reads the meters for the man who has it. She brought me a warming chain copied out of her father's almanac, folded small, the way children bring you a thing they have been told is forbidden.

The chain was wrong in its fourth link. I told her so before I told her it was good, which it also was. Then I took the lens off, because she had done something with it. She had tried the Draft on her own hand in the privy with nobody standing by, and the fourth link had drawn its heat out of the skin under the ink. The blister under the fold of her thumb was the size of a copper.

"Correct the chain first," I said. "Then the hand."

I taught the room from her blister. A Draft takes its heat from somewhere. If the chain does not say where, it takes it from the nearest thing that will give, and the nearest thing is always the one who drew it. They copied that down. The lens-off read cost me the migraine behind the left eye into the next afternoon, and I taught the next day's lesson with the shutters closed and a wet cloth at the back of my neck while the meter-house clerk complained of the lamps. I could not give her a licence. I gave her a chain that would not burn her, and a letter to the Guild house saying that she drew a truer chain at twelve than the almanac she had copied it from. It was true. I doubt it was enough.

The second was a settlement past the last warded station, whose ground had put something out of a Well that spring. The carvers there had driven it off into the hills at a cost, and they wanted their children able to bind a wound before the wounded could be carried in. I taught the tourniquet first, since a cord and a stick cost nothing, and after it the Draft suspension that holds the edges of a wound still while they close. Ara learned the first from me when she was their size, and has taught it better since. A carver's boy of nine wanted the other thing, the working that kills what comes out of a Well. I told him I had none to teach. He did not believe me, which was fair, since I had come up from the station on the Division's mule with a silver token at my collar. He learned the tourniquet faster than any of them, on his sister's thigh, and he counted under his breath while he turned the stick, as I had told them all to, so that whoever came after would know how long it had been on.

I had not slept a whole night in six years, and I did not begin in that settlement. I lay awake every night of the posting with my ear on the ground. In the third turn the post came up from the station with a letter in Doyun's good hand to say that Geturo had crawled the length of the east room to the bench by the door. I read it twice. I had not seen him do it, and I had his first year in pieces after that, the way a man reads a book with every third leaf cut out.

The third was an infirmary in a river town that had heard what Doyun's suspensions do and wanted the method taught. I taught it to six nurses in a ward with the beds pushed back, pouring and pouring again until the suspension held clear in their hands and not only in mine. The eldest of them had a brother on the second bed. He had worked a pump-house on the main for twenty years and run dry at it, and he lay grey and quiet with his hands folded on the blanket, and she had heard that a Draft from the Division could put back what the pump-house took. I told her what I tell every family in every posting: no Draft refills anyone. A suspension steadies a Shell while it mends, and a Shell with nothing left to mend around only steadies. She heard me out. Then she asked me to pour one for him anyway. I did, and it held him quiet through the night and did nothing else. In the morning she was at the bench before I was, pouring for the woman in the fourth bed, and she did not look up when I came in.

That was the plain coin, and I paid it in every posting: the migraine after every read with the lens off, the nights, a son learning the east room without me. And there were the eight, whom I will come to.

In the summer the Division sent me east of Vaeloris, two ridges over, to the Zettari camp. The Sovereign, Dawn Guard, was holding an inquiry into Legion Summoning and the crimes of my kinsman. The Division sent him the one practitioner on its books who had been answered by a Legion-class working, and I took the room with me.

The first man I met at the camp gate took my hand palm up, turned it over, and read the ink in its creases before he looked at my face. "Who has your fire?" he asked. I told him the school at Sum-gol. He let go of the hand and let me in. I met the Sovereign hours before the inquiry sat, and I taught his camp that morning, because teaching is the thing I know how to do in a new place. To soldiers I taught the house law without the house art: the difference between a spirit that chose to serve and one that was never asked.

I sat the inquiry in the afternoon and answered what I was asked. That night the Sovereign asked me to name someone of my house worth trusting, and to draft the terms of an alliance under his seal. I drafted them by lamplight, in a camp still cold from the day's teaching. I give their substance. They condemn my kinsman without qualification and without appeal to inherited grief as excuse. They propose my sister's marriage to the Sovereign and make her consent their first condition, given with no brother in the room. They make me the line's representative until her standing is settled, and they bind me to name the Zettari's failures in whatever room the Sovereign stands in.

Before morning I wrote to my sister. I would not settle her life in the house's way, and I told her so, and I wrote: So I am asking you instead.

At first light I swore myself to the Dawn Guard's teaching vow at the quench, before the camp's Stone Witness, over a blade the smith had quenched at dawn in a barrel already half full of other men's oaths. A Zettari vow wants something older than the man who swears it to hear it, and the stone was older than any of us. They kept the water. The barrel stands stacked and dated in their lines now with my vow in it. Its terms, in my words: I teach any soldier of the camp who asks what I know of lawful practice, without fee. A vow is a restriction the Core keeps as a law. It costs once, when it is sworn, and then it costs the rest of a life.

"Jiwe limesikia," the smith said, and set the blade down.

## Chapter the Seventeenth · Eight Names on the Roll

With the rank I could enter pupils for the kingdom's examination in the Draft trade, which the Carver's Seat sets and marks for the whole of Ketsuen, and I entered eight of mine. They were the first children of the valley outside a blood or a hall ever entered for it. I left them in the candidates' hall at the Seat in the wardens' keeping, as every candidate is left, and took the ninth east with me because he wrote the best hand and I needed lists.

The examination sat on the third morning after the night I drafted the terms. I was teaching in the camp.

That morning the sense that tells me when a seal is failing told me every seal in the Seat at once, two ridges west, and they went as a room goes when every held breath in it is let out together.

I went in with the Sovereign's people. I will not describe what we met there. It is the refusal I made at Rimward, for the reason I made it there: I cannot give it a shape I trust, and a shape I do not trust would be copied. I did not reach the Seat. I was carried out hurt.

The next morning, before they took me down to the Sovereign's ship, I had them stand me on my feet on the basin's rim, where I had lodged two summers before, and I took the lens off.

The ground gave me nothing at all.

I have read ground under a tannery, under a king's hall, under a coffin, and every one of them gave me its tone, if not always its rung. This gave me neither. At nineteen I wrote that nothing that has been is erased, and I record here the first of the corrections this book owes the first: a place can be erased entire.

On the ship Doyun poured me a Draft suspension that held my Shell still while it mended. I taught him how in a room at Sum-gol when he was fourteen. He poured it for me at fifteen on a rolling deck, with his hands steady and his ears red.

None of the eight came back. The Division entered the candidates dead with the Seat's dead, and I entered them so on the room's roll in the school's hand, on the record. When I could walk I went to each door in the valley, with the ground pressing at my temples from the first bend of the river as it always has. Our practice for the dead does not ask a family to keep the body. These families were not of the house. They had nothing to keep.

I enter them here as they stand on the roll: the Hong boy and the Baek boy, from the paddies; the Tak twins, the carter's; the potter Ahn's two girls; the Yang boy, the fisher's, from the lower reach; the Hwang girl, the smith's youngest.

Jal gara.

Within the turn, in the wool store at Rimward, I sent Doyun to the Zettari captain to offer his hand for her list, because a list with the names spelled properly is worth more than a list. I sent a boy to spell strangers' names within a turn of losing eight of his own. He ran at it. He has not wept for them where I could see; he wept at a bench once, for a cat.

## Chapter the Eighteenth · The Orchard, and Four Days

I came home from Rimward with one eye and a binding at the waist, and I had not been home long when the sky over Sum-gol cracked.

I will write what I did and what I read. A man came out of the air above the valley. The pines on the eastern slope came up out of the ground with their root-balls and went into the sky in a ring, and the ring became a spiral, and the stream came off the eastern shoulder and went up in a curtain of beads. Forty children stood on the terraced yard at the high end of the school in their ranks, being taught the long count.

There was a Valorin woman in the valley that season, of the Seat's house, who had come up after the Seat fell. We had never worked beside each other. We raised the barrier together in the time it takes to say so, her half and mine. Hers was her house's old grammar of concealment, which makes a thing expensive to find, turned to make the valley expensive to reach. Mine was Coagulatio. The valley's own density fed the set, the ground that presses at every returning temple, drawn up through the wards the school's carver keeps. A solution seeded past a critical size stops dissolving what it makes and grows, because growing costs it less, and I seeded the set past that size along the whole curve at once and let the law carry it. Heat came off my Shell into the air in shards of arc-blue, and my η held. That is as much of myself as I will put in this book.

The two halves did not fit. I felt the seams where they had been forced to meet, three of them along the curve, and neither of us had time to mend them. It held on strength alone.

The pines came down outside the curve, on the eastern slope and the upper road and the terrace's outer wall, and there were children under the wall. Frithia had drilled the older ones all that year to a count. She had a line passing stone hand to hand before the dust was down, and they dug out every child alive.

The village's dead lay at the low end, where two houses had come apart with people in them who ran indoors because indoors is where you go. We counted them that night: five. I entered them on the record the next morning.

Then the man came down into the orchard, and his name, which I had from him, is Kaalabad. He put a spike through the eye of Raga, my bear, who is mine by compact, and took my right arm at the shoulder without touching it. Eleven of the orchard's thirty-one trees came down at the root, and nobody at the school has been willing to be the one to clear them.

The arm carries no wound. The fingers are warm and the nails grow. The school's physician spent four days on it and wrote no finding, in a hand that got smaller toward the end of the report. Before that turn was out I sat in a room with a table and two cups on it, where a man I will not describe told me to put the arm down and count what was left. I put it down.

I was down four days after the orchard, and I woke on the fourth. The shutter was open two fingers. The room smelled of seal oil and boiled willow, and out past the wall forty voices were running the long count on the terrace, badly together, with one a half-beat behind the rest. Frithia was kneeling by the bed with two fingers under my jaw.

"Eighty-eight," she said.

It was the number Ara had said in the wool store.

"Forty," Frithia said. "All of them. Listen."

I listened. The boy was still behind.

## Chapter the Nineteenth · A Letter Square to the Desk

Frithia did not go back to her own room after the orchard. The snow had come to Sum-gol when the ground began carrying a note I did not like, low and steady and wrong, and I walked toward it. I give that road my own acts only. The rest belongs in other men's books.

On a ditch bank I found a stranger dead, a big man with a split shield still strapped to what was left of his arm. His soul was still coming away. It went slowly and in order, going up, with no fear anywhere in it. I got down on one knee in the snow and drew his lids down with two fingers and held them until they stayed. "Rest in peace," I said. I do not know his name. Then the valley shook out of me as far as the Kharven gates, and I closed my left hand and brought it down over about six seconds. The man who had killed him called me Geuk-hon. That is not my name, I told him, and I told him mine. At the end I opened the field I had carried out of the king's hall and seated on the bridge at Urbis. It has a name now. I named it in the narrows: Open Crucible. Other men saw what it did, and I leave it to their accounts.

The cost is mine to write. His polearm went through my chest, so that I breathe with one lung. Three fingers of my left hand froze past saving. The left eye had gone violet between the orchard and the road, and I had shown it to nobody; at the end of the road it went white from lid to lid. It still sees. What it is, I will not guess in ink. I have not taken the lens off to read myself since the road, and I will not until I know what looks out of that eye.

They brought me to Urbis on one lung and put me in the room with the east window. Inside the turn a woman climbed my stair with a letter.

She held it out and said my name. "Kwon Mu-jin."

There was nothing of her in the air to read. On her left wrist she wore a bracelet of brass bands wound with grey wire, and it had gone over toward me hard as the door opened and stayed there. I know that instrument. It pulls toward a man holding his weight down, so I take it she knew I was holding mine down, and I was. I hold it down in every room with anyone in it. It tires me, and I do it anyway.

I let go of the door frame and took the letter between the thumb and forefinger of my left hand, and turned the wax to the window. Grey wax. A ring crossed by three spokes, the lowest longest, and the registry's small letters under it. I held it there a long while.

"Who held it?"

"The Division, under the seal on your volumes."

"I did not know they were sealed."

I set it on the desk beside a closed book, square to the edge. She stood in the doorway a moment, as though deciding whether to say something, and did not. I think she had carried something besides the letter up my stair and chosen not to deliver it, and I respect the choice, and I did not ask her name. Whoever released the letter sent it by hand, and I take that for a courtesy from someone who had read the second volume and knew what I do with letters that come by post.

There is no working in that wax. I would have felt one from the stair. The only seal on the letter for four months was mine.

The winter came in on the square, and the trams went on under it on their hum. The line drew itself across the three fingers in its second turn, black on one side and angry pink on the other, where I have told carters since I was twelve that it would. Furveus crossed the city from the Heralds' Medical University to name it. He cannot hold a knife now. He stood at the table and named where the line was, and a surgeon of the University cut where he named. I write this book with what the line left me.

I read him on sight, as I always have: the nail-beds grey, a pale band across every nail, the nod gone down into his jaw. I said nothing to him about any of it, and I write here that I said nothing. It was a small Separation.

He laughed first. "It won't fill you, Mu-jin. It'll steady the Shell. It'll heal the..." He put the cup in my hand.

Doyun wrote Frithia's letters to me that winter in his good hand, since she keeps nothing on paper. The one before midwinter said a child was coming.

Neros came up the stair in the depth of the winter with his charts under his arm. He sat at the desk with the letter under his elbow and did not look at it. The Weight had moved again, he said, to the same side as before, since the autumn after the second volume, and faster since the thaw. Each step in his column came some turns after a cluster of wakings at Genesio; someone had laid the Division's returns beside his column for him.

"Late," he said. He did not explain it.

Before midwinter, when word had reached Genesio that I was in Urbis, Malphas sent back the fair copy of the second volume I had given him, care of the Archivum, with no letter. It is the only copy of that volume I have held since I deposited it. Beside the page on Separation there is a line in the margin, in a hand I know. I have set it at the front of this book.

The Thread-Reading passed at midwinter in a city that does not keep it. At home the valley settles its quarrels and pays its debts in the last turns before it, because the Crow reads each of us then. I had nobody in Urbis to quarrel with. The letter lay square to the edge of the desk through all of it, and I wrote the six chapters before this one with my back to it.

I had written this far before I opened it.

I opened it at the thaw. It is the second letter Draycott has written me, and I copy it whole, as I copied the first.

## Chapter the Twentieth · The Platform at Castlefall, Second Bell

The line takes a day from Urbis to Castlefall, and I took it on one lung, with the lens on and my weight held down against a carriage of strangers. Nothing burns on the line. It rides the conduit on its hum, and the hum sat in my back teeth the whole day.

Castlefall at the thaw was fog on the rails and fog on the cedar of the platform, which is eleven winters old now and still weeping. The ground pressed at my temples from the carriage step. At the next stall two carters were quarrelling over the drakes hatched since the Weight went out of its station, and whether any of them would breed true. The second bell went.

Draycott was already at the coffee stall with a cup. With the lens on I read him as anyone could have: a lean man of about forty-seven, greyer at the crown than he had been across a café table from me, standing with his weight on the back foot as though the platform might be withdrawn from under him. The signet on his smallest finger had worn smoother since then. A silver token hung at his collar, the disc turned outward. Under all of it was a stillness I took for a man holding his weight down, as I was holding mine.

I paid my copper and took my cup. It is still terrible.

"Kwon." He looked pleased. "It improves if you stop expecting it to be good."

I stopped expecting it the first spring I came through Castlefall. It has not improved.

He laid his token face up on the counter between our cups, as the Concord does when it means to be believed; a token darkens beside a lie. I laid mine beside it. It was the first time I had used it for anything.

He looked at the two of them a moment, and then at me.

"You came."

"You knew I would."

"I thought you would. There's a difference. I've misjudged you before."

"I received it."

"I know."

"We were not ready to answer it."

He turned the signet on his smallest finger and looked down the platform into the fog. Then he told me why I was there. Ivor had been finishing things since the summer after the second volume. Real things: works their makers died meaning to finish, done now and in the world. "That's the difficulty," he said. The Weight had moved again since the summer, he said, and it had not moved back. "Ivor calls it tolerable deviation."

"And the oldest decision."

"Is yours." He picked his token up and put it back on the counter, face up, in the same place. "I'll say the rest when we're standing at it."

He did not say whose it was, and I did not ask him there.

"What do you want?" I said. "For yourself."

He drank his coffee. "What she wanted. For it to go somewhere it could be used."

I did not yet know who she was.

The up train for the waystation came in at the third bell. He finished his cup, took his token off the counter, and waited while I took mine.

## Chapter the Twenty-First · The Hall at Genesio

A day on the line to the Genesio waystation, and then the passes, which had opened a turn before. At the waystation the clerk entered us in his book. "On the record," he said. The ground pressed at my temples from the first switchback. I went up on a hired mule with Draycott ahead of me, and it took us two days and a great many stops. One lung does the work of two badly at that height. I counted my breaths the way I count a pulse, and at each stop I counted them down again, and at each stop Draycott waited without being asked and without looking back.

The Archivum at the top of the archive-town is squat and weathered, the oldest house the Heralds keep, and over its door Maelor's edict is cut into the lintel: Nothing is lost, only waiting to be remembered. I read it going in.

The archivist of the lower stacks met us on the reading floor. Dessa Mael is small and straight-backed and past seventy, her white hair scraped into a knot and pinned with a steel pen, a narrow pale face lined fine round the eyes, the Heralds' grey under a knitted shawl, and ink on both forefingers. She brought tea before anyone had asked a question. The Archivum reads a gauge at the gate below once a turn, she said, and it had climbed through every season she had logged. At the thaw it stood at twenty-six, higher than any figure in her book. The lamps in the corridor beyond had been dark since the midwinter before last.

I asked for the Scribe.

"The Scribe is below the gate," she said, "and does not come up."

I did not go down to him. The gate admits whom it admits.

The passage below the town is closed to ordinary traffic, and we went through it and across the bridged walkway over the falls with the spray freezing on the rail. The lamps on their brick stands were cold glass. The hall at the end is older than the count itself, and its stone says so in the width of its courses. The trees still stand. Their roots go through the cracks of the floor and their crowns are pressed flat against a ceiling that has no business holding the Uplands up. At the far end of the Mausoleum lay the two coffins. Vis, Alchemical Astronomer. Caelus, maker of contraptions of cyclopean design.

Ivor Strom was sitting on the floor inside the hall with his bad knee out straight in front of him and the oilskin bunched round his hips. His white beard had gone yellow at the mouth, and the wine-coloured birthmark over his ear had darkened in the cold. The ink under his nails was the circle's, and it took the light of Draycott's hand-lamp as it had taken Furveus's in the lower room, drinking at the edge of the flame. He did not get up. I had not expected him to.

"Kwon." He tipped his head at the first coffin. "I left that one. Knew it wasn't for me."

I took the lens off.

The hall read Whirlpool: ground where the planes turn together and time sits wrong, where a Spirit-type site gives a Crystal no warning at all. The first time I stood in it, it read Riptide. It was a rung above its own density now, because it had been drawn on until it turned and began to take. To keep one's own law on ground of that rung takes S-grade output or better, 4.184 × 10¹² joules at the floor of the band, and the rung runs a hundred times higher than that at its top. Against the wall lay Strom's stock in its crates. Render stock, Class IV, which means the dead are in it. Across the floor and up the bark of the trees lay the scar, pale patches where the ground held nothing. There Strom had woken Traces coarsely, and a coarse waking had warmed those patches until they gave everything they had stored.

I went to the first coffin and stayed.

The ground was still closed round its weight like a hand round a stone, and the pull at the floor of my Crystal came as it had come the first time. With the lens off I could read what the second volume could only name. It was a Trace, a decision its maker died holding, laid into the ground where he was bound and kept there as Mnemata. It was aimed at my signature exactly.

Its tone I had heard before, across a table with two cups on it, in the turn after the orchard. I read in it an Elfin constitution and an astronomer who worked in correspondence and died two hundred and sixty years ago with a decision in his hand. Kaalabad has a name for whoever that was, and he called me by it on the road. It is not my name.

It completed into me through Anamnesis, and I did nothing to make it. The hall's density, pressed by Strom's draw a rung too high, stood round the coffin and fed the read. Anamnesis does to a record what heat does to a fired shard: whatever the shard has stored since the kiln it gives back once, as light, when it is warmed, and then it is empty until it has stored more. The Trace was a commitment of its maker's Attraction Layer, and when my reading warmed it, it went where it had always been aimed, into mine, and my Shell ran warm, and my η held. The read was the delivery: there was no vessel and no seal.

It was a correction.

Correspondence is a law of response. What is asked below is answered above, late and in another kind, because the answer is given on credit and settled out of sight. The Great Work is learning to ask a question the world is already answering. A maker begins with the particular below, which is his own error, and lets the above complete it: he holds the unfinished thing long enough for the world to finish it through him.

On the flyleaf of the first volume I wrote: that which courseth above is as that which courseth below. In my gloss now the word is answers. And the Aphorism of Mortalis, which I copied at Genesio on the page facing the correspondence doctrine and did not join to it, I join now: the debt it warns of, a current borrowed and stepped out of at a cost, is paid from the same account the answer is drawn on. It is my position. The Division's readers will argue it, and they should.

Ara, I promised you the question first. The Trace gave me the frame for it, and nothing more. At Rimward I asked below, without standing, and something answered at once and took the eye. The correction leaves what answered unnamed. It tells me that answering is what the world does, late or at once, and that it keeps an account.

The eye is the part of the price I can see.

Then it was spent. The pull at the floor of my Crystal went and did not come back. Behind us in the corridor the lamps on their brick stands began to pulse again, slow, a pulse that reached the skin before the eye. The hall read Riptide once more, a rung down, ground where the Veil has begun to give. What was left in the stone was too thin to wake. The sequence cannot go on.

I was sitting on the floor of the hall. I do not remember deciding to. The migraine had come in behind the left eye and would stay two days, and my breath would not go past halfway.

"Well?" Strom said, from where he sat.

"It finished."

"I felt it go from here." He rubbed the knee. "What was it?"

"A correction. To a misunderstanding about correspondence."

"Say it again," Draycott said. "Slowly."

I said it again, slowly.

Strom sat with it a long time. "Knew it was addressed. Sat on it." He looked at me. "Was I right to leave it?"

"Yes."

He nodded, once.

Draycott and Strom were in that hall when I went to the coffin and stayed. I filed it twice, alone; this time two men stood by while I did not file it.

Coming out, I read Maelor's edict again, and I added in my book the half the Heralds leave off: and remembering it spends it. The next morning Dessa Mael read the gate out of her turn, and the gauge had fallen to twenty.

## Chapter the Twenty-Second · What It Cost, and the Four

The evening of the hall, three of us sat at a fire in the archive-town. The fire was too small for the room.

Draycott told me what the letter had left out, and I give it in summary. The nobleman's wife in the eastern lowlands was a scholar herself, Anamnesis-adjacent, and she had found the residue and written down a decision of her own. She died of the lung-sickness two years before the commission. That is well past the nine days, and the commission drew on what stays after them. At her bedside she had asked Draycott to seal the paper and not to read it while she lived, and he did both. She designed her own commission, and she trusted her husband to open the room. The likeness lasted eleven days. On the last day it took her husband to the paper, and when the sentence was finished it ended in one breath, and was spent. The paper held her finding, set down more completely than Ivor's.

So the credit stands in layers, and I state it plainly, once. Madeleine Ault found it and designed her own commission; Ivor Strom found a cruder half of it on his own; Gisli Draycott framed it and built the means; I named it and set its boundary.

"It had you too," I said. "A different corruption from mine, with the same shape."

He looked at the fire so long that Strom got up and fed it.

"Yes," he said. "The same shape."

My gloss of the Aphorism says that whoever draws on Mortalis to rebuild what has come apart summons himself, arranged in their image, and calls it resurrection. The Aphorism itself is older and speaks of a debt; the gloss is mine. I wrote it as a warning about other men. It is true to the letter. The likeness takes its identity from its maker's own Core, and the maker is in every word it says. The Trace is the one thing in it that was hers, and it was spent when it was delivered. That part the gloss did not foresee.

I asked him whether he was one of the two who held Malphas's hand at Genesio. He laid his token on the hearthstone. "Ask Malphas," he said. The token did not darken. I have written to ask.

Strom's completions have stopped, because the ground will not give him more. The Enforcement Division holds a charge of extraction against him that has not been heard. Until it is, Halveth's office, the Research and Archives Division's in Urbis, holds him under review at the pass-house above the waystation, and he helps to cleanse the hall. His finished works are real, and I say so: buildings their makers died meaning to finish, a survey of the Alftian fold carried to its end, a physician's text extended with its author's own figures. People live in the buildings. The damage to the ground is real as well. Draycott holds no appointment and no rank from any of it. He took the up train.

The Division has a framework in hand for this work. Who draws it is not mine to write. Whatever it becomes will bind only inside a signed circle, and the first was signed at Kharven-Seat this spring.

The office has Strom's old paper out of the stacks below the gate: On a Residue of the Dead That Keeps an Intent, which he deposited there without his name some thirteen years ago. I annotated it at the Division's request, and Draycott added a note on its framing. The copy lies under the Division's seal, and nothing in this deposit unseals it.

In the first volume I promised the Seven Cacodaemonic Corruptions to the second, and the second gave three and promised the rest to this one. Here are the four, one clause each. Conjunction Perverted, the Corruption of Instrumental Union: Conjunction has no current of its own and runs on Attraction, so its inversion wounds Attraction itself, and it joins whatever serves without asking whether the two belong together. Fermentation Perverted, the Corruption of Catastrophic Germination, rooted in Rebirthine: the rot valued for its yield. Distillation Perverted, the Corruption of Sanitized Truth: its root is Sublimare, and it hands on the clean page with the cost boiled off. Coagulation Perverted, the Corruption of Tyrannous Finality, rooted in Coagulatio: the interim form sealed and called final. I give no cases. I teach only what I have seen in myself. I have seen these four in other men. Coagulatio is my own current, and that is the one I will watch. Whoever writes them out fully should be someone who found them in his own coat.

Back in Urbis at midsummer Neros climbed my stair again, with the heliometer, his split glass, in its case under his arm. The Weight had turned back, stepping toward its station as it had stepped away. The Measurewrights' instrument chair at Nalūn had checked his readings.

In the second volume I wrote that the commission bent something below and the Weight showed it above, as still water holds a face. That was my first wrong guess: the commission's mark had faded by the summer after the pour, and what moved the Weight again was one ground pressed by many wakings, answered late.

Neros turned his rule end for end on the chart and set it down straight.

## Chapter the Twenty-Third · The East Room Again

I came up the valley at the start of the autumn, at the end of the eleven months I was lodged in Urbis, on the line and then on foot from the last station, which took me most of a day on one lung. A carter coming down asked who kept my posts, and I told him, and he looked at the arm and touched his hat. The ground pressed at my temples from the first bend of the river.

Doyun met me at the station. He is sixteen, taller by a hand than he was, and he still takes every distance at a very fast walk with a great deal of arm. He took my case without asking and carried it on my right, where the arm hangs. He has kept the east room all the months I was away, and he teaches the little ones the first grammar now, under the schoolmaster's eye. It was his hand that told me in the summer that I had a second son.

In the first lesson the second Nabi sat on my feet.

The morning after, I kept a promise the second volume made and could not keep. I put my left palm on the east beam beside Doyun's, where the sap still comes through the old cedar, and answered the question he asked me there two years before: whether what I read of a thing stays in the thing afterward or only in me, and whether reading wears it.

"Both," I said. "What I read of the beam is in me now, and the beam keeps less of it."

"How much less?"

"You know the potters' shards. A shard keeps a store of light from the ground it lies in, from the day it left the kiln. Heat it and it gives the light back, once, and then it keeps nothing until it has gathered more. A careful reading warms it a little and leaves the rest. A coarse one empties it." I took my hand off the cedar. "What is completed is spent. The shard does not give its light twice. What was in the oldest coffin at Genesio is in me, and nowhere else."

His arms came up. "Then every time you have read this beam you have taken some of them off it, the Tak twins and the Hong boy and all of them, and the roll is only their names, which you told us is not the same as a record, so the beam is the only copy and you have been spending it."

"Yes."

He looked at the beam a long while, and his arms came down. "Then do not read it again. Not until there is a second copy."

I have not. That is the second correction this book owes the first: a record is erased by the reader who takes it.

Geturo is two. He has her mouth, and his hands are still too large for him, and he walked across the east room to me on the first morning and stopped a pace short to look at the arm.

Hiromi was born in the summer, while I was in Urbis. His hair grew in white, the colour of forge-ash before it cools, over pale red irises, and nobody in the house can explain it. I cannot either. I have tried. He sleeps with both fists up by his ears, as his brother did.

Frithia asked me on the third evening, in the east room, with the benches still out from the day. She asked in her own tongue first. Then in Accord common, once.

"I am asking."

I answered her in the house tongue, and it took one word.

Before the wedding she had me read her every page of this book that has her in it. She heard all of it without a word. At the end she had me strike two sentences. I began to argue for the second, and she looked at me, and I said, "Now, now," and struck it. I have left the places where they were.

We were married in that room before the turn was out, with the benches pushed to the walls and the schoolmaster's straight edge on the table beside the roll, and the carver's new post at the door still pale where he had cut the ward into it that morning. My wife kept the ringed-seal hat on indoors, even at her own wedding. The second Nabi sat on her boots through all of it.

Separation Perverted, the Corruption of Non-Commitment. I named it in myself in the second volume. Naming was the half I was good at. At Castlefall I said "Do not" to Furveus once, and then I watched, and wrote down what I watched. The other half is an act, and an act wants a threshold a man cannot argue himself past alone in the dark, so I set mine before witnesses.

On the last evening before I came down to Urbis with this book, I swore a vow of my own in the east room, on the record, before witness and vessel. Doyun and Frithia stood witness. I poured the vessel myself, a small suspension under Coagulatio, one-handed, with Doyun holding the flask steady at the neck because I could not. It set clear while I said the words.

"I will not write down what I have decided not to stop."

Kept, a vow is Coherence bought with conduct. Broken, it is a Shear Break, and I know what a Shear Break does to a Crystal, and I set the price anyway.

The schoolmaster entered it on the roll under the eight names. She did not blot it. Geturo reached up over the edge of the table and put his palm flat in the wet ink, and she let him.

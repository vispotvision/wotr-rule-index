# Western combat writing: working craft for the WOTR combat questionnaire

Research note, 2026-10-04, saved at /home/oridon/wotr-rule-index/imports/drafts/combat-questionnaire/work/research-west.md. Scope: the Western traditions of writing violence, read as technique a WOTR fight could take, bend or refuse. Each entry gives (1) what it is, (2) what it does on the page in a fight, (3) one line on the transposition, (4) what it collides with in current law. No source text is reproduced; techniques are described in my own words, and the few quoted phrases are under twelve words and attributed. The style questionnaire's Western note (imports/drafts/style-questionnaire/work/research-west.md) covered several of these authors for narration; this note looks only at the fight.

**Settled; the questionnaire must not re-ask.** CB1 moves named in the POV's own school (R70-100); CB2 full declaration with a price (R70-101); CB3 duel shape by grammar (R70-102); CB4 one full flashback, POV only (R70-103); CB5 rivals who chose to spare (R70-104); CB6 rites and elegiac image by culture (R70-105); CB7 hill and ditch, cut (R70-106); MY1 to MY9 (R70-68 to R70-76); LR8, LR11, LR12, LR23 (R70-85, R70-88, R70-89, R70-91); DD6 the held instant (R70-57); VO2 the marked roll call (R70-11); K3 Western leads combat, injury and dialogue (R70-3). The older floor the entries lean on: every exchange traced (R48-33), the eight-item floor (R13-4-COMBAT_FLOOR), clinical wounds (R48-34), the aftermath beat (R48-38), brutal lethality for the unranked (R60-01), the reserve clock (R60-03), third person only (R70-12), the rhythm law (R48-06, R4-14), the reconstructible fight (R13-8).

**Two framing facts.** K3 gives combat to the Western strand while K4 (R70-4) drops Martin and Abercrombie as named models, so both appear below as technique sources only. And the Combat Craft Guide's §2 box still carries the old ban on explaining why a proofed round beats proofed plate, which R53-05-PROOF_PLATE (live) has superseded; that cross-reference is stale.

## 1. Homer, the Iliad

**What it is.** The Greek war epic of the Trojan siege, composed for oral performance. Its fighting runs in two set forms: the aristeia (a hero's run of glory, one warrior sweeping the field until a god or a wound stops him) and the androktasia (the man-slaying, the formula for a single kill).

**On the page.** Exchanges are one to three moves: a spear cast, a miss, the answering cast, a death. Each minor victim gets a name, a father, a home and often one pathos line (newly married, an only son), then a wound of exact anatomy, entry to exit; a nineteenth-century army surgeon counted close to a hundred and fifty described wounds, most fatal. Death arrives in a fixed phrase. Impact is measured by the extended simile (a long comparison that leaves the battle for a whole scene of peacetime: a lion in a cattle pen, woodcutters, a flood), placed at the moment of greatest violence. Space is tracked by the battle's drift between the wall, the plain and the ships. Kills end in the vaunt (the killer's boast over the body) and the stripping of armour; Hector dies through the one gap in borrowed armour where the neck meets the collarbone. Aftermath is truces to burn the dead, funeral games, and a father ransoming his son's body.

**In WOTR.** A practitioner's aristeia seen from the levy it cuts through, each man it kills given a line of lineage before his wound.

**Collides with.** The pathos obituary is the narrator knowing a stranger's life, which breaks the POV lock (R48-40, R5-C1-INFO_TRACKED_PER_POV) unless it rides R70-11's roll call. The wound catalogue meets the Mass Combat Guide's ban on catalogued wounds and its one full-detail body per scene (§8) and four or five full-layer hits (§3). A hero sweeping the field from inside his own head is the practitioner POV R3-9 closes during an engagement, and a god turning the spear is what R13-8 forbids. Fits: the armour-gap kill is already Wren Greymane's assignment, and the peacetime simile is DT1's culture image bank.

## 2. Malory, Le Morte d'Arthur

**What it is.** Thomas Malory's English prose gathering of the Arthurian romances, printed by Caxton in 1485.

**On the page.** The joust comes first: two spears break, horses go down, then the knights fight on foot for an hour or two "tracing and traversing" (Malory: circling and side-stepping for advantage), hewing at helms until the ground is bloody. Rhythm is parataxis (clauses laid side by side and joined by "and" with little subordination), with almost no adjectives and wounds stated as wide and sore. Power is given by tallies: how many knights one spear unhorsed. The magic rule is plain and fair: Gawain's strength swells toward noon, so Lancelot simply endures until it ebbs, then wins. Fights pause by consent; the knights lean on their swords, ask names, and sometimes learn they are kin (Balin and Balan kill each other unknowing and share one grave). The end is a formula: yield or die. A knight who yields is spared on terms, sent to court to submit himself.

**In WOTR.** A Guild-hall challenge fought to yield, the beaten man bound by the terms to present himself at the victor's house before witnesses.

**Collides with.** Hour-long fights break R60-01 for the unranked and the body's own clock (Combat Craft Guide §7.1, the stamina row); between practitioners only the reserve clock may stretch a fight. The and-chain meets R4-14-CONJUNCTION_AUDIT and the chain ceiling, with R70-19's one cumulative climax per set piece as the only licence. Untraced hewing fails R48-33 and the floor. Fits: the sent-to-court yield is R70-104's paid mercy almost exactly, and Gawain's noon is a hard rule with a findable counter, Isaac's taste.

## 3. The Icelandic sagas

**What it is.** Prose histories of Icelandic families, written in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries about the tenth and eleventh: Njáls saga, Egils saga, Grettis saga and the rest.

**On the page.** Laconic killing: a fight is three to six sentences, told from outside, with no interiority. One exact physical result carries it: Skarphedinn slides across a frozen river and splits Thrain's head so the back teeth fall on the ice. Space is the farm, the door, the river, the ford, given precisely enough to follow. Wit stands in for reaction; a man speared through at his door remarks that broad spearheads seem to be the fashion now, and dies. Fatigue and cost are rarely felt but always counted: Gunnar's bowstring is cut, his wife refuses him two locks of her hair for a new one, and he falls. Opinion goes to "people said". Aftermath is law: the killer must declare the killing publicly and promptly or it counts as murder, a worse crime; then come lawsuits, wergild (the price paid to the dead man's kin) and outlawry, and the feud runs on for generations.

**In WOTR.** A Kharven killing told in five plain sentences, then a whole scene at the Seat settling the blood-debt in salt and labour (on the nameless day there would be none to settle).

**Collides with.** No interiority runs against deep, running interiority (R48-39-INTERIORITY) and the held instant (R70-57); one detail per wound fails R48-34's minute-by-minute clinical floor. The saga restraint the law once kept for elegy (R5-C2-FLAT_DELIVERY) is superseded. Fits: R60-01's seconds-long fight, R70-104's Ledger of debts, the joke that is also a threat (R5-D-HUMOUR_PERMITTED), and Kharven's own blood-debt and Waiting.

## 4. Joe Abercrombie, The First Law and The Heroes

**What it is.** Grimdark fantasy in free indirect style (third-person narration soaked in the viewpoint character's own idiom, swears and opinions). Logen Ninefingers's other self, the Bloody-Nine, is a killing state he does not control.

**On the page.** Exchanges are close, ugly and unheroic: a slip in mud, a shield shoved into a face, a kick, a missed blow that wrenches a shoulder. Rhythm is fragments in panic and short declaratives, with the POV's refrain (a pet saying) recurring under strain. Impact is rendered through the body's indignities: breath, snot, piss, a tooth loose. When the Bloody-Nine takes over, the voice itself changes: colder and delighted, the narration naming the Bloody-Nine rather than Logen as the one who acts; the state does not pick sides, and his own people fear it. Space is local and often lost. Explanations are minimal and come in a mouth. Fights end abruptly and unfairly. Several of Logen's crew are men he once beat in the circle and let live. In The Heroes a battle sequence hands the viewpoint from each man to the man who kills him. Aftermath is bathos (a lofty moment dropped into the low) and looting; the victory changes nothing.

**In WOTR.** A Hallenfeld courier's first fight told in his own swearing idiom, every exchange ugly, with the floor's measure and tempo stated in the words he has.

**Collides with.** R70-4 drops Abercrombie as a named model. The Bloody-Nine is a power state with no ledger, which R12-1-PACK_SEVEN_SURVIVORS and R60-03 refuse until it has a mechanism and a cost; the voice shift tests R41-1's two axes of narration distance. Lost space and unexplained outcomes fail R13-4 and R13-8. The death relay needs a marked break per hand-off (R70-106) or is a roll call that gives each head one line only (R70-11). Glokta's rhetorical question and answer trips R49-41-QUESTIONS. "A grim smile" is now a hard-ban phrase (R70-35). Fits: free indirect is settled (R5-E-FREE_INDIRECT_CARVEOUT), and the spared crew is R70-104.

## 5. George R. R. Martin, A Song of Ice and Fire

**What it is.** Epic fantasy in rotating close-third chapters, one named POV each.

**On the page.** The Tower of Joy is a set fight told as a fever dream: seven against three, legends against numbers, the whole weight in a formal exchange of lines before steel, and the fight itself elided in a phrase; what it cost comes out later through memory and hearsay. The duel through a spectator: Oberyn against Gregor is watched by Tyrion, whose life rides on it, so every exchange lands twice, as motion and as stakes. Oberyn uses measure (fighting distance, here the long spear's reach), the sun at his back, poison and a repeated accusation timed to his thrusts; he wins on every count except the last, closes to hear a confession, and dies in the giant's hands. The battle through one POV: Tyrion's chapters at the Blackwater see only his own sortie; he is maimed and learns who won when he wakes. Rhythm in action is long, comma-joined momentum broken by short sensory blows. Stakes are real; beloved characters die of their own faults.

**In WOTR.** A Guild duel watched from the rail by a Hallenfeld quartermaster with a season's pay on it, the fight landing twice in his body.

**Collides with.** R70-4 drops Martin as a named model. The elided fight fails R48-33 and the floor, and a fight that exists only as a dream inverts R70-103 (one flashback inside a fight, not a fight inside a flashback). The single-POV battle is what R70-106 replaced: a formation battle now cuts to the hill. Fits: the spectator POV is R5-C1-WRITE_FROM_LEAST_KNOWING; Oberyn's overconfidence is a well-reasoned fault (R5-C1-WELL_REASONED_WRONG_CONCLUSION); his death is R48-31's earned stake; the pre-fight exchange is R70-101's declaration.

## 6. David Gemmell, Legend and the Drenai books

**What it is.** Heroic fantasy; Legend (1984) is the last stand of an aged axeman, Druss, at a six-walled fortress against a steppe horde.

**On the page.** The structure is a countdown: wall by wall the defence falls back, so space and time are the same clock and the reader always knows how much is left. Exchanges are quick and decisive, a few lines each, from many heads, including the enemy's khan, who respects the old man. Cost is the hero's own body: a sixty-year-old's heart and joints, pain hidden from the men. Speeches and maxims hold the line. Minor defenders are given whole small arcs, then killed in the fighting. Mystic warriors who chose their death fight beside the soldiers. The end is a death the hero foresaw and walked into; the enemy honours the body.

**In WOTR.** A Kharven wall held for three nights by an old rider whose body the levy watches fail, each wall lost counted in the woodpile's terms.

**Collides with.** The Mass Combat tell bank names "the heroic hold": lines hold by drill, cohesion and the rank behind, never by will (§10). Siege law makes the parley the scene and the storm the failure of the drama (R3-8-SIEGE_PARLEY_IS_SCENE), the reverse of Gemmell. Head-hopping breaks the three-heads rule (R1-3) and the lock. Mystic warriors fighting in view are the practitioner POV R3-9 closes. Speeches are capped at one crafted speech (R48-43). Fits: the old body on the clock (R60-03, the stamina row), the enemy who honours the dead (R70-105's rites), warmth against grimdark (R70-114).

## 7. Bernard Cornwell, Sharpe and the Saxon Stories

**What it is.** Historical fiction: Richard Sharpe, a rifleman risen from the ranks in the Napoleonic wars; Uhtred, a warrior in Alfred's England, narrating in old age.

**On the page.** Shield walls: overlapping shields, the press from behind, the stink, a short blade worked under the rim at groin and ankle, an axe hooking a shield down to open a man, the dread of the wait before the clash. Volley drill: loading by numbered motions (bite the cartridge, prime, ram, present), smoke that blinds after the first volley, platoon fire (volleys by sections in turn) rolling down a line, squares (infantry faced outward in a hollow block) against cavalry, the breach stormed by volunteers; a musket fires about three times a minute in good hands, a rifle slower but true. Tactics are explained in the action, by Sharpe's read or his sergeant, so the reader knows distances in paces and why the line holds. Uhtred foreshadows from age. Aftermath is plunder, surgeons, the women of the baggage. Every novel ends with a Historical Note separating fact from invention.

**In WOTR.** An Accord line volleying enhanced shot at a Moto charge, the loading drill and the smoke traced by a sergeant who knows what proofed plate costs.

**Collides with.** Uhtred's first person breaks R70-12. Cornwell's clear sight of the field is the hill's privilege; in the ditch sight comes last (Mass Combat §1). The Mass guide has naval, siege and cavalry sections but none for a firearm line, so volley fire has no home yet. Fits: the 1900 ceiling and elite enhanced shot (R53-03, R53-04), the twenty-foot threshold (R11-5), tactics planted before the fight (R70-71), and the Historical Note as WOTR's author notes.

## 8. Steven Erikson, the Malazan Book of the Fallen

**What it is.** Ten-volume military fantasy whose mages fight like artillery.

**On the page.** Cadre mages (sorcerers attached to legions) draw on Warrens (realms of power tapped like reservoirs); a battle between them churns ground and burns hundreds, and the soldiers below experience it as weather and terror. Sappers (military diggers and demolition men) carry graded alchemical munitions (crackers, burners, sharpers, cussers), named and handled like craft tools. The siege of Pale buries a company in its own tunnels under the sorcery. The view stays mostly with grunts, whose gallows humour runs through the worst of it. Mechanism is withheld: readers infer rules from consequences and dialogue. Cost is exhaustion and backlash. Long marches carry attrition (the Chain of Dogs). Aftermath is grief at scale and moral argument; the march's commander is crucified in sight of an allied garrison that never sallies.

**In WOTR.** Alchemical munitions issued by grade to Accord sappers, used against a Domain while the levy experiences both as weather.

**Collides with.** Withheld mechanism is Pack Seven, repealed (R12-1-PACK_SEVEN_REPEALED); R70-71 wants rules planted before. Mage POVs in mid-battle meet R3-9. Mass killing by sorcery runs against §5 (a Domain breaks a formation; the killing happens in the rout) and §4 (decide frontage, sustain and re-form first). Erikson's many heads break R1-3. Fits: munitions by grade echo the ammunition tiers (R20C-22); practitioner as weather is §15 exactly; grunts' gallows wit is R5-D.

## 9. Brandon Sanderson, Mistborn and The Stormlight Archive

**What it is.** Hard magic (a system whose rules the reader knows well enough to predict). Allomancy burns swallowed metals for powers; steel pushes on nearby metal, iron pulls.

**On the page.** Rules first: training scenes and an essay-like clarity mean a fight's surprise comes from clever use, never new power. Sanderson's own first law, paraphrased: magic may solve conflict only as far as the reader understands it; his second: limits matter more than powers. Physics is the choreography: a push moves the lighter of two objects, so a fighter anchors a coin to the ground to launch himself, or pushes on the nails in an enemy's boots. Space is three-dimensional and tracked by lines of force the viewpoint sees. Cost runs: vials of metal empty, flaring burns faster, and the body pays later (pewter drag, a collapse once the metal runs out). Counters are findable: wooden weapons, no metal. Prose is plain and quick; climaxes cut rapidly between viewpoints.

**In WOTR.** A Measurewright trains a cadet on what a Physics Domain can and cannot touch, so the duel two chapters later is the reader doing the sum.

**Collides with.** Little; R70-71 is close to his first law. Self-derived techniques must still be read live (R12-3-NAMED_INVENTOR_RULE). Plain window-pane prose runs against lush throughout (DD1). Rapid cutting needs marked breaks. Ability cards may not carry his kind of applications (R47-1-NO_APPLICATIONS); scenes may. Fits: costs, counters and predictable physics are Isaac's fair-ability taste, with the reserve clock (R60-03) as the empty vial.

## 10. Jim Butcher, the Dresden Files

**What it is.** Urban fantasy narrated in first person by a wizard detective.

**On the page.** Spells are called in invented pseudo-Latin, the words a buffer so the caster does not burn his own mind. Cost runs visibly: exhaustion, headache, a spell that fails when he is spent, injuries carried from chapter to chapter and a burned hand across several books. Tools store and spend energy (rings that hold kinetic force, a shield bracelet). The wizard explains how magic works mid-fight in his own voice. Counters are folk rules made hard: a home's threshold weakens anything supernatural that enters uninvited; iron hurts the fae. Wisecracks under fire set up and land as jokes. Chapters end on cliffhangers; set pieces escalate across a book.

**In WOTR.** A Guild duelist's comic second, carded as a comic voice, trading cracks while the reserve visibly drains.

**Collides with.** First person breaks R70-12. Setup-and-punchline in danger fails R5-D-HUMOUR_BARRED and R70-116 (gallows wit only inside danger) except for carded comic voices (R70-113). Pseudo-Latin fails R50-12-COINED_LATIN. Two release-call rows both print live, one capping releases at one a scene (R8-26-ONE_RELEASE_PER_SCENE), one with no limit (R50-09); the docket should say which governs. Fits: cost in the body, real healing time (Combat Craft Guide §7.1), folk rules that work as findable counters (the counters field of R47-1-NO_APPLICATIONS), the mid-crisis chapter hook (R70-17).

## 11. Cormac McCarthy, Blood Meridian

**What it is.** A novel of scalp-hunters on the 1849 border, written in an archaic, biblical register.

**On the page.** Violence as landscape: the narrator grants a massacre and a rock the same flat weight, what the book calls "optical democracy" (McCarthy). The Comanche attack runs as one enormous cumulative sentence cataloguing the raiders' looted costumes before the killing. Polysyndeton (and after and after and) carries motion. There are no quotation marks and almost no interiority; the central character is nearly blank. Deaths are clauses. Space and time are sun, stars and terrain. Explanation belongs to the judge, a philosophising monster who argues war as the ultimate game. Aftermath is bodies as objects in the heat.

**In WOTR.** A Kharven scene's one held instant before the cut, the snow and the stripped dead given the same weight as the man who matters.

**Collides with.** The and-chain is settled under R4-14, with R70-19's one cumulative climax per set piece as its only door. No interiority runs against R48-39-INTERIORITY and the floor's read (R13-4). Missing quotation marks break the house dialogue form. Fits: the held instant (R70-57), one wide view (DS9), the thing noticed that is not the bodies (Mass Combat §8), arguments carried by characters (the Four Theories), and a narrator who never adjudicates (R5-E-NARRATION_NEVER_ADJUDICATES).

## 12. Patrick O'Brian, the Aubrey-Maturin novels

**What it is.** Twenty novels of a Royal Navy captain and his ship's surgeon in the Napoleonic wars.

**On the page.** Hours of chase and manoeuvre, minutes of action. Clearing for action is a ritual: bulkheads down, sand on the decks for footing and blood, the galley fire out. Gunnery terms stand unexplained (no gloss at all), with the landsman surgeon as the one things are sometimes explained to. Position is tactics: the weather gauge (holding the windward side, which gives the choice of when to engage), raking (firing along an enemy's length through bow or stern). The surgeon below in the cockpit (his station under the waterline) hears the battle and works on its product: splinters from shot do more harm than shot. The action is sometimes skipped and picked up after. Aftermath is the butcher's bill (the period's phrase for the casualty list), repairs, prize money, and burial sewn in a hammock with shot at the feet.

**In WOTR.** A river action told from the surgeon's station below a gunboat's deck, the captain's choices arriving as sounds and as the wounded carried down.

**Collides with.** A skipped action would fail R48-33 and the floor in a duel; a mass action has more room, since only four or five hits take the full layers (Mass Combat §3). Fits closely: the naval hierarchy (sight, then motion) (R3-8-NAVAL_SENSORY_HIERARCHY), no rout at sea, fire and water as the deaths, the captain and the surgeon as hill and ditch (R70-106), zero gloss (R4-13-ZERO_BUDGET), comic voices for the puns (R70-113), and the period inside the Imperial Age span (R53-01).

## 13. The Western and the gunfight

**What it is.** The American frontier story in print and film: Owen Wister's The Virginian (1902, the first walkdown), Portis's True Grit, McMurtry's Lonesome Dove, Elmore Leonard, and Leone's cinema.

**On the page.** The walkdown is a declaration: a man is called out, a time is set, the street empties. The standoff is stillness and the read: hands, eyes, a twitch; then one shot, over in a second. Leone's three-way standoff stretches the stillness to minutes, then ends in an instant. History corrects the myth: the 1881 Tombstone fight was about thirty shots in about thirty seconds at close range, and a frontier saying credited to Wyatt Earp holds that "accuracy is everything" (Stuart Lake's biography). Revolvers impose counts: men carried five rounds, hammer down on an empty chamber; a Colt Single Action reloads one round at a time through a loading gate. Powder smoke hides the street. Wounds are slow and ugly in Lonesome Dove: a leg goes bad, a man refuses the second amputation. Aftermath is the undertaker, the inquest, the photograph of the dead.

**In WOTR.** A Hallenfeld walkdown under the Seat's walls, both men proofed, the read decided before a hand moves.

**Collides with.** Counting rounds on the page is a running count; R70-88 bars running figures for stats but does not say whether a physical count of chambers or vials falls under it. Leone's cut between faces is a camera, not a head; under the lock it becomes the POV's read. Fits: the Verdict grammar (still, then one cut), the held instant (R70-57), R70-101's warning that the opponent may shoot during the recital, the twenty-foot threshold (R11-5), R53-05 freeing the cause of penetration, and slow death by wound (§7.1).

## 14. HEMA-literate fiction and fight choreography

**What it is.** HEMA (historical European martial arts) reconstructs fighting from period manuals: Liechtenauer's verse, Fiore dei Liberi's illustrated book (about 1410), Joachim Meyer (1570). Novelists who train in it (Christian Cameron writing as Miles Cameron; Sebastien de Castell, who has worked as a fight choreographer) write armoured fights from the inside. Stage and film choreography supply the working units.

**On the page.** The phrase (fencing's word for one continuous exchange of attack, parry and riposte, ending in a hit or a break in distance) is the paragraph. The beat is one action, counted. The cue or telegraph is the preparation that warns a partner; in fiction it becomes the tell the reader can catch. The knap is the sound a performer makes to sell an impact; the stage principle that the receiver sells the hit means power is shown in what happens to the target. Film keeps screen direction constant (the 180-degree rule) so the eye never loses who stands where. Hong Kong choreography runs rhythmic counts, several fast beats and a pause, so the finisher lands off the rhythm. Cameron's armoured fights track the weight and heat inside steel, aim at joints and the visor slot, and often go to the ground.

**In WOTR.** Wren Greymane's fight written as three phrases, each ending in a break of measure, the third ending in the one unarmoured inch.

**Collides with.** Even beat counts in even sentences trip R4-14-RUN_RULE (three sentences within forty percent of each other's length fail). HEMA words now belong to Western-trained POVs only (R70-100). Fits: the traced exchange and floor (R48-33, R13-4), the tell bank, and at the table the receiver principle: an NPC's blow arrives as a threat and Isaac's PC's reaction stays his (R49-01).

## 15. Military memoir realism

**What it is.** First-hand accounts of combat: Rifleman Harris and Kincaid from the Peninsula, Jünger's Storm of Steel, Sledge's With the Old Breed, Tim O'Brien's The Things They Carried, and John Keegan's history The Face of Battle, the standard account of what battle is like for the men inside it.

**On the page.** Fear physiology: tunnel vision, auditory exclusion (loud sound going dull or vanishing), time stretched or compressed, fine motor control lost, bladder and bowels let go. Noise is constant and informationless. Confusion: nobody knows what is happening past a few yards, and rumour fills the gap. Most of war is cold feet, hunger, lice and waiting; combat is a short interruption. Catalogues of carried things hold character. The tone of shock is flat. One image recurs for life. The mess afterwards is the work: corpses handled, the smell, flies, the burial details. Comedy is absurd and constant. Period correspondents such as William Howard Russell in the Crimea wrote dispatches that made a battle public.

**In WOTR.** A Kharven levyman's first fight written through deafness and a lost grip, then a correspondent's dispatch from Hallenfeld that gets the battle wrong.

**Collides with.** Memoir is first person (R70-12; documents are the exception, so dispatches and diaries fit). Flat shock runs against lush throughout (DD1). Modern trauma words fall under the pop-psych ban (R51-04-LIST_PSYCH); folk words do the work. Fits: the Mass Combat sensory hierarchy (§1), the aftermath sequence (§7), comedy hard against dread (R70-116), and the dispatch as the document voice (R12-4-VOICE_FOUR_DOCUMENT).

## Open seams the West exposes (question seeds)

Not settled by R70 or the guides, and worth a question each:

1. **Mixed-grammar duels.** Whose shape governs when a Verdict fighter meets a Blade fighter (the Combat Craft Guide says R70-102 does not settle it).
2. **How long a practitioner duel runs on the page.** Malory's hours against R60-01's seconds; the reserve clock bounds it, but nothing sets pages or turns.
3. **Altered states in the POV.** Whether a Bloody-Nine kind of state may change the narration's voice, and what mechanism and cost it owes.
4. **The death relay.** Whether a battle may hand the viewpoint from victim to killer through marked breaks, beyond the roll call's one line.
5. **The elided fight.** Whether a fight may ever be told only in memory or aftermath (Tower of Joy, O'Brian).
6. **Physical counts.** Whether chambers, vials, arrows and charges may be counted on the page under R70-88.
7. **The firearm line.** A Mass Combat section for volley, smoke, square and breach.
8. **Practitioners as artillery.** Frontage figures for battlefield workings, and whether any may kill rather than break (§4, §5, R3-9).
9. **The last stand.** Whether it is ever legal against the heroic-hold tell and the siege-parley rule.
10. **The walkdown.** Whether a called-out gunfight counts as R70-101's declaration.
11. **The legal aftermath of a killing.** Declaration, blood-price and outlawry per culture.
12. **Banter in the fight.** How far a carded comic voice may joke inside danger.
13. **Fear physiology for the unranked.** Whether it joins the floor for an untrained POV.
14. **The spectator duel.** A watched fight told through someone with a stake.

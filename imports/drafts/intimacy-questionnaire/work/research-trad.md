# Intimacy questionnaire: erotic and romantic craft across traditions

Research for the intimacy and explicit-scenes questionnaire, 2026-10-04. It covers Western literary erotica and dark romance, Victorian erotica, the Japanese, Chinese and Korean traditions, LitRPG and progression romance, and how published fiction handles dubious consent and non-consent. Each section gives the techniques, how they transpose to WOTR, and where they meet current law. The closing question list follows Isaac's six picks: courtship by culture, desire and refusal lines, dubcon handling, aftermath, how bonds grow, the new style in bed. Items marked *pitch* are proposals for him to rule on, not canon.

## The floor, first

No bestiality, which in WOTR covers beasts, Well-spawn and summoned creatures; beastkin are people, so an adult beastkin lover sits inside the floor, and the questionnaire can confirm that in one tick.

## 1. Western literary erotica: Nin and *Story of O*

**Techniques.** Anaïs Nin wrote her erotica in the 1940s for a private collector at a dollar a page (*Delta of Venus*, 1977; *Little Birds*, 1979). Her preface records his standing order to cut the poetry, and her answer: sex stripped of feeling and image goes flat. Each piece is a portrait of one person's specific hunger, and the act is where the hunger shows its shape. *Story of O* (Pauline Réage, pen name of Anne Desclos, 1954) reaches the same force the other way: extreme acts in a cool, decorous, classical register, without exclamation or moral comment. Its engine is consent as a series of thresholds. O agrees at Roissy's door, then at every escalation, until agreeing is the self she has built. The house has rules, dress and procedure, and the procedure carries both the horror and the heat.

**Into WOTR.** Nin's rule is Table Rule 11's "a want" made carnal, with the held detail where the want concentrates. Réage's decorum suits the Moto and the Accord, cultures that run on procedure; a Guild-licensed house with forms and seals carries her chill. Consent-as-thresholds structures dark scenes: the question comes at each step, and the reader watches where the answer changes.

**Collides with.** Nothing, so long as the decorum lives in syntax and procedure and never in euphemism (R49-51). Her refusal to adjudicate matches the ban on narrator moral verdicts. Open: whether consent is ever negotiated aloud on the page, or shown only in the body.

## 2. Dark romance and romantasy

**Techniques.** Romance promises a central love story and an emotionally satisfying ending: the HEA (happily ever after) or HFN (happy for now). Dark romance keeps the promise and runs captivity, stalking, crime and CNC (consensual non-consent, a fantasy of being taken that both parties chose beforehand) on the road to it. Its love interest is "morally grey" or plainly villainous, and its proof of love is obsession. Published dark romance prints content notes at the front. Romantasy (Sarah J. Maas's *A Court of Thorns and Roses*, Rebecca Yarros's *Fourth Wing*) adds the slow burn (desire delayed across many chapters, each near-miss raising the stakes), forced proximity (shared tent, cell or bond), enemies to lovers, and the fated mate bond, a metaphysical tie that declares the pairing before either party agrees. Readers rate heat on the spice scale, usually zero to five chili peppers. The genre favours alternating first-person POV.

**Into WOTR.** The fated bond already has a body here. Fracture of Worlds Part Fourteen defines Attraction Force as the law of recognition, by which vows bind and relations survive distance and death, and Obsession Force as its inversion: fixation that collapses Breadth, turns Empathy into a one-way channel, and narrows the Crystal around one hunger. The Axis Sub-Stat measures how much Essence flows across bonded relational connections. Dark romance's obsession is therefore, in WOTR, a diagnosable corruption with a cost, and the heat and the rot can share a scene. The slow burn maps onto the Ledger, each near-miss a debt that comes due; forced proximity is a shared billet or a Well delve.

**Collides with.** VO3 bars first person and POV lock bars head-hopping, so alternation survives only by chapter or scene break (R49-27). The guaranteed HEA meets grimdark and R60-04: no ending is promised. The mate bond meets R29-1, which already allows a bond fixed by declaration whatever the anchor wants. Whether obsession reads as romantic, corrupting or both is the central dark-romance question for this table.

## 3. Victorian erotica and the period voice

**Techniques.** The underground canon: *The Pearl* (a monthly, 1879 to 1880), *The Romance of Lust* (1873 to 1876), *My Secret Life* (eleven volumes, about 1888, by the anonymous "Walter"). Steven Marcus, in *The Other Victorians* (1966), named its world "pornotopia," where everyone is willing and nothing costs. Three voice habits matter. The double register: florid politeness ("the dear creature," "the crisis") wrapped round blunt words ("prick," "cunt," "quim," "frig"). The catalogue: Walter numbers and logs his encounters like a naturalist. And "spend" for orgasm, used of both sexes. Behind the books stood real law and medicine: the Contagious Diseases Acts (1864 to 1869, repealed 1886) and their lock hospitals, where women suspected of venereal disease were confined; vulcanised rubber sheaths after 1844; mercury for syphilis.

**Into WOTR.** The Accord and the Victorian-Edwardian city (R59-07) take this voice whole. Walter's catalogue is a document form DT9 already licenses. The Acts become Guild regulation: licensed houses with seals and inspection, which gives the trade proof-marks, paper and a price. Period contraception and disease give aftermath a procedure inside the 1800 to 1900 ceiling: the sheath, the sponge, the pox and its mercury, abortifacients that poison (pennyroyal, savin).

**Collides with.** The double register fits R49-51 only beside the plain words. "Spend" collides with WOTR's cost vocabulary, where Essence and reserve are spent: a pun the Accord would enjoy, or a word for R51-11's collision list. Pornotopia breaks Rule 9's consequence clause and Rule 3's agency; WOTR takes the voice and refuses the utopia. The trade has no rows on the price table.

## 4. Japan: Genji, the pleasure quarter, Tanizaki, shunga

**Genji.** *The Tale of Genji* (Murasaki Shikibu, about 1008) courts by letter. A man glimpses a woman through a gap in a fence or blind (kaimami, "peeping through the hedge"), then sends a waka (a 31-syllable poem) whose paper, scent, brushwork and attached sprig are all read as message. Her reply, her silence, or her lady's reply in her stead is the first move. After a night together he leaves before dawn and sends the kinuginu no fumi, the letter of the parted robes, named for lovers who slept under both robes and took back their own at dawn. Its speed measures regard; a late letter insults. Three consecutive nights made a marriage, marked by rice cakes on the third. The act itself is almost wholly elided: the night is a gap between arrival and morning poem. Scholars, Royall Tyler among them, read several of Genji's conquests as rape dressed in courtliness, with the morning poem as the fiction that smooths it over.

**The pleasure quarter.** Edo's Yoshiwara (licensed 1617) ranked its courtesans, the tayū and later the oiran at the top. As usually told, a top courtesan held a three-visit ladder: at the first meeting (shokai) she might not speak; at the second (ura) she warmed; only a regular (najimi) on the third visit reached her bed, each visit paid in full.

**Tanizaki.** Fetish as a whole world in one body part: the foot in "Fumiko's Feet" (1919), the tattooed back in "The Tattooer" (1910), blinded devotion in *A Portrait of Shunkin* (1933). *The Key* (1956) is two marital diaries, each written to be secretly read by the other spouse: confession as seduction and as attack.

**Shunga.** Edo erotic prints (Utamaro's *Utamakura*, 1788) are frank, exaggerated and often funny; one name for them was warai-e, laughing pictures. Bodies stay half-clothed, the robe framing what is bared. Kakiire, text written into the picture, carries the lovers' talk and sound words in the margins.

**Into WOTR.** A *pitch* for the Moto: Genji's apparatus of letter and verse, the glimpse, the morning letter graded by speed, three nights to marry. The Kharven folk belief sharpens it: never meet a Moto's eye, because the Moto read through the eyes. A Moto lover who holds a gaze has opened the door, the most intimate act in the house and the most dangerous. The three-visit ladder is a ready "how bonds grow" mechanism with procedure and price. Tanizaki's partialism is the held detail grown into a whole psychology; *The Key* is a document form for a marriage at war. Shunga's margin text meets LR4 (clinical readouts, wit in the margins): the note beside the picture is the ancestor of the screen beside the body. Gitaigo (Japanese words for texture and state rather than sound) arrive through R70-133: nuru-nuru for slick, biku-biku for twitching, jin-jin for a tingling ache.

**Collides with.** Genji's elision against Rule 9: is a cut to morning ever legal? Unsettled. The morning poem is settled; whether it may lie about the night, as Genji's do, is a dubcon question. Shunga's laughter meets the setup-deadpan-reaction ban (R49-38) and HU1; sex as comedy is unruled.

## 5. China: *Jin Ping Mei*, *Dream of the Red Chamber*, the courtesan, the bedchamber arts

***Jin Ping Mei*.** *The Plum in the Golden Vase* (printed about 1610; David Tod Roy's full translation) is explicit and domestic. Ximen Qing's household of wives and concubines is a ledger: sex is traded for rank, silver, protection and revenge, inside an economy of bribes, cloth, food and debt. The concubines' rivalry, poison included, drives the plot. Desire is literally priced: Ximen Qing dies of an aphrodisiac overdose given him by Pan Jinlian. Verse interrupts the explicit passages; a karmic frame closes the book.

***Dream of the Red Chamber*.** Cao Xueqin's eighteenth-century novel runs on restraint. Baoyu and Daiyu's love is never consummated and travels through poetry clubs, quarrels, gifts, illness, and Daiyu burying fallen petals. In chapter five the Fairy Disenchantment names Baoyu's nature yiyin, which David Hawkes renders "lust of the mind": desire lived in the imagination. The continuation's veiled-bride substitution makes the arranged marriage a betrayal.

**The courtesan.** The cultivated courtesan was poet, musician and painter: Xue Tao in the Tang, Liu Rushi in the late Ming. Kong Shangren's *The Peach Blossom Fan* (1699) gives the refusal line its most famous object. Li Xiangjun, refusing a forced marriage, strikes her head; her blood spatters the fan her lover gave her; a painter turns the drops into peach blossoms.

**The bedchamber arts.** Fangzhongshu ("the arts of the bedchamber"), from the Mawangdui manuscripts to the *Classic of the Plain Girl*, treats sex as cultivation: conserve jing (vital essence), draw on the partner's, prolong. The classical euphemism "clouds and rain" comes from the goddess of Mount Wu. Modern xianxia (immortal-cultivation fantasy) turns this into shuangxiu, dual cultivation: sex as a power-up.

**Into WOTR.** The household-as-ledger is the engine for any WOTR house with lineage marriages and heirs, and canon agrees: *Value, Coin and Trade* names marriage the standard instrument by which a lineage acquires certified capability. The aphrodisiac is an alchemical Draft whose failure shows at every use (AL7), priced at the bench. The blood-fan is refusal rendered as an object that outlives the night. Yiyin suits the lineage halls and any POV whose desire stays in the head.

**Collides with.** Jing and dual cultivation meet Essence and the Axis Sub-Stat: does intimacy move Essence or strengthen a Spirit Axis? No canon says so, R12-5 forbids a figure until Isaac rules, and a sex power-up must still meet "fair abilities with findable counters." "Clouds and rain" is free in a mouth; in narration it meets R49-51.

## 6. Korea: gisaeng, sijo, Chunhyang, spring pictures

**Techniques.** Joseon's gisaeng were state-registered entertainers of low-born status, trained in music, dance, calligraphy and verse; their art was conversation with men who outranked them and could not command their regard. Hwang Jini, the great gisaeng poet of the sixteenth century, has a sijo (a three-line lyric) proposing to cut a midwinter night in two, fold half away, and unroll it the night her lover returns. *Chunhyangjeon*, the pansori classic (a sung epic for one voice and a drum), turns on a refusal: Chunhyang, a gisaeng's daughter secretly married to a nobleman's son, refuses the new magistrate, is beaten and jailed, and is vindicated when her husband returns as a secret royal inspector. Chunhwa (spring pictures, cousin to Chinese chungong and Japanese shunga) and Shin Yun-bok's paintings of gisaeng and scholars under the moon show flirtation as public theatre. *The Handmaiden* (2016) makes a collector's erotica library, read aloud by a woman to an audience of men, first a coercion and then her means of escape. Two words carry the emotional grammar: jeong, attachment that accrues through shared time and hardship, even toward someone disliked; and han, grief and grievance left unresolved.

**Into WOTR.** The Mahuo stratum already has canon here. In the letter *To Ara Min Mahuo*, Kwon Mu-jin writes to his sister (twenty-one on her card) about a Zettari Sovereign's marriage offer and refuses to decide her future "quietly, on someone else's behalf." The sijo exchange is DL4's verse contest in Korean form. Jeong is the best single model in this report for how bonds grow: slow, accumulative, unchosen, able to bind enemies. Han is the aftermath that never resolves.

**Collides with.** Chunhyang's rescue ending is the HEA in Korean dress; grimdark must decide whether the inspector arrives. Regard that cannot be commanded meets Pressure, since a high-Stage patron can bend the room. Korean mimetic words (dugeun-dugeun, a pounding heart) join R70-133 for a Mahuo POV.

## 7. LitRPG and progression romance

**Techniques.** The genre's relationship systems come from games. Persona's Social Links rank a bond up through time spent, and each rank strengthens the player's powers. Fire Emblem's supports rise from fighting side by side. Harem lit (one protagonist, several devoted partners) is a large web-serial subgenre. Among Isaac's models romance is thin and mostly chaste: Cradle's Lindon and Yerin are a slow burn built on competence, banter and shared danger; Bleach and Naruto run on long unspoken devotion; Jujutsu Kaisen makes love itself a curse. Solo Leveling has the best faculty-romance device in the set: Cha Hae-In smells other hunters' mana as a stench, and Jinwoo's as sweet. Dungeon Crawler Carl plays sex as the leer of the alien audience, and the joke works because the audience is the villain.

**Why it often fails.** The partner becomes a reward meter with no refusal (Rule 3 dies); partners blur into one voice; gift-spam turns affection into a purchase; sex arrives as loot with no consequence; the level gap goes unnoticed; the harem never quarrels, where *Jin Ping Mei* shows a multi-partner house is a war. Worst for this table, a screen that reads feelings kills the read, and the read is the game (Rule 6).

**Into WOTR.** Canon already holds a bond system: Axis, Spirit Axes, Attraction against Obsession, Resonant Pairs. A *pitch*: a bond prints only as Crystal fact, never as feeling; a readout may register that a Spirit Axis exists, and whether she loves him stays for the read. Cha Hae-In's device transposes to scent and the beastkin read (combat FT13). Pressure makes the level gap honest. Under NATALIE.md's ladder, a lover two Stages up brings sweat and nausea unless he suppresses, and suppression costs his body (MC17). The power gap becomes physical, visible and priced.

**Collides with.** LR8's list of moments that earn an in-scene readout (appraisal, a wound, Essence spent) has no bond on it. R12-5 forbids a bond figure until canon holds one. Whether Pressure can counterfeit or amplify desire is open, and it is the WOTR-native dubcon question.

## 8. Dubcon and non-con with craft

**The labels.** Dubcon (dubious consent) and noncon (non-consent) are fandom tagging terms; AO3 (Archive of Our Own) carries a "Rape/Non-Con" warning. Published fiction handles the material in three frames.

**The fantasy frame.** The 1970s bodice-ripper (Kathleen Woodiwiss, *The Flame and the Flower*, 1972) let the hero rape the heroine and then love her; the genre largely abandoned the move. CNC moves the choice before the scene. Jacqueline Carey's *Kushiel's Dart* (2001) builds consent into the world: the Night Court's houses, the signale (a safeword with legal force), the marque (a tattoo earned out by a courtesan's fees). Magic compulsion is a subgenre of its own: Laurell K. Hamilton's ardeur, a hunger that forces its bearer to feed through sex, and the fandom "sex pollen" device, a drug or spell supplying desire neither party chose.

**The procedural frame.** Margaret Atwood's *The Handmaid's Tale* (1985) writes the Ceremony as flat procedure, the horror in the institution's calm.

**The trauma-realist frame.** Tonic immobility (involuntary paralysis under threat): in Möller and colleagues' 2017 Stockholm study of women seen after sexual assault, about seven in ten reported significant immobility, and it predicted later PTSD. Arousal non-concordance (genital response and felt desire running apart, documented in Meredith Chivers' laboratory work and popularised by Emily Nagoski): the body's wetness or erection is not consent, and dark fiction gains force when the character knows it. Judith Herman's *Trauma and Recovery* (1992) gives recovery three stages, safety, remembrance and mourning, reconnection, which map onto the Ledger across sessions. Recurring craft habits in good work: the POV fixes on one irrelevant object during the act (the held detail as dissociation); aftermath arrives in the body first (washing, the hurt, sleep that will not come, the startle); the perpetrator's account is a lie in his own mouth (Rule 8); the bill reaches the world as feud, pregnancy, disease, scandal or blood-debt.

**Into WOTR.** Four native gradients of consent, each with a real cost: rank and debt (patron, lord, labour-debt); intoxicants (the skin, a Draft); metaphysics (Pressure in the room, an Obsession Domain bending every path back to the fixation, the Moto read through the eyes); bargains (passage, protection, a contract clause). Canon sharpens them. The Fusi Vā is gone, so a marriage vow at the Seat has nothing underneath it. Nothing binds on the nameless day. R29-1 already lets a bond be fixed by declaration, consent or no.

**Collides with.** Nothing says how a roleplay turn handles Isaac's PC on either side of a dubcon beat. R49-01 bars writing Sodoku's desire, sensation or consent, and combat K9's "outward cost only" covers fights, not beds. Table Rule 1 (stop at his next decision) against R48-28's long turns means an explicit roleplay turn cannot run long without acting for him. Content notes, a lying morning poem, and whether Pressure can make a body want are all unruled.

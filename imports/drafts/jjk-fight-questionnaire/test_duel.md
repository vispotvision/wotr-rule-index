*The Brass Lantern, Corvane Quarter. Third bell, every lamp lit.*

Ketsu read the man coming across the floor the way he read a ledger, line by line, hunting the entry that did not add up. Weight on the heels. Hands that did not belong to the arms they hung from, big and scarred and loose, a stonemason's hands on a gambler's frame. A coat of plum-coloured wool, far too good for the quarter, with a smell lifting off it that said smoke and not tobacco. Dagan Voss carried no weapon, and a man who walks into the Brass Lantern at third bell with nothing on his belt has either already won the evening or has never once lost one.

Every lamp along the wall leaned toward him. The draught had nothing to do with it; the flames themselves bent, a row of small gold tongues straining after the plum coat as if it owed them money. The dogs under the tables slid out through the door without being called. The cat on the bar poured itself over the edge and was gone. Ketsu felt his breath shorten of its own accord, and behind his ribs a small old animal decided to be very still. *Two Stages above me. Maybe three. And he hasn't even tried yet.*

"Don't mind the lamps!" Dagan said, and grinned, and the grin held too many teeth. "They like me. Everyone in this room has been burning something for an hour, and I'm the only man who ever comes back to say thank you. Sit, sit! I'll buy you a drink before I break you."

"I'm working," Ketsu said.

"So am I! WICK-HAND!"

*FWOOOMPH.* The nearest lamp threw its flame across the room in a bar as thick as a man's thigh, and the table where Ketsu had been sitting went up like a haystack. He was already rolling, shoulder to the boards, a hiss of heat passing over his back, and he came up against the wall with his left sleeve smoking and his heart going like a drum in a cellar.

Dagan did not wait for him to stand. He crossed the floor in four long strides, faster than a man that size had any right to be, and threw a right hook at Ketsu's head from the hip. The back heel ground a quarter-turn into the boards, the hip rolled after it, the shoulder came through behind the hip, and the fist arrived last with a hundred and twenty kilograms stacked behind it like cordwood. Ketsu saw the heel turn and was already dropping. He folded at the knees with his chin buried against his collarbone, and the knuckles went over his head close enough to lift the hair from his scalp. The wind of it cooled the sweat on his neck.

The fist kept going. It met the brick wall behind him and *KRAAAK*, a piece of the wall the size of a loaf blew out in a cloud of plaster and red dust, and when Dagan pulled his hand free the skin over his first two knuckles hung off in a wet flap with the white of the bone shining through. He looked at it with interest. He did not stop smiling.

"Everything I've ever lit in a room stays in the room!" he roared, delighted, as if the punch had been a toast. "That's the secret, friend, that's all of it! It goes up as smoke and the smoke remembers, and when I call, it answers. Every ember I pull back costs me a breath I don't get back, so I'm very, very careful to start with the lamps. This room's been lit all night. I am standing in my armoury!"

Ketsu came up out of the crouch driving through his right leg, the whole line of his body unspooling upward from heel to shoulder, and put his left elbow into the soft gap under Dagan's ribs. The point of the olecranon found the eleventh rib, the floating one, the one no muscle covers, and bent it inward until it broke. *Crack*, small and wet, like a green stick. Dagan's breath left him in a pink spray that speckled Ketsu's shoulder and the side of his neck, warm as spit. The big man did not fold. He grunted, a sound of faint surprise, and Ketsu understood that the rib was broken and that the man had decided it did not matter.

Dagan's knee came up. It was a plain, short, ugly blow, thrown without any art, and it struck the outside of Ketsu's thigh and drove the great muscle into the nerve beneath it. White light went up the leg from the point of impact. Then the leg went dead from hip to ankle, a post of wood nailed to the bottom of his body, and he staggered sideways and caught his weight on the edge of a table, which broke under him with a crack like a pistol shot.

The open palm followed him down. It came round and cracked across his cheek from the jaw to the temple, rocked his head on his neck like a flag in wind, and split his lip against his own teeth. Blood came hot down his chin and a molar came loose and ticked across the boards and rolled under a bench. The room tilted. He lay in the splinters tasting iron, and under the iron the copper of fear, which had a much older flavour.

*Left leg gone. Twenty seconds before the blood comes back to it. Don't plant it. Don't pivot on it.* He watched the floor instead of the hands. The smoke came off the boards in threads and the threads bent toward Dagan like the lamp flames, and where the threads were thickest the fire came, always a hand's breadth above the ground. *The hands are for the crowd. He's pouring it up from the floor.*

There was a crowd, of a kind. Up in the rafters, where she had gone the moment the first lamp turned, Marit hung from a beam with one arm hooked over it and the other pressed to her ribs. "He isn't aiming!" she shouted down, thin and furious. "He's pouring! Watch the floor, not the man!"

"Thank you, I had noticed!" Ketsu snapped, and tested it, because a theory is only a theory until it has cost you something. He hooked a splinter of table-leg with his good foot and flicked it into the smoke. The threads left Dagan's boots for a heartbeat and swarmed the wood, and the leg was ash before it hit the wall. *It takes whatever is nearest, and it takes it greedily.* He filed that and rolled onto his knees, and as he rose he saw the other thing.

Dagan had not once stepped toward the far end of the room. He had walked the long way round the bar. He had put out a boot, begun a stride toward the kitchen door, and changed it into a swagger, quick as a cough. Behind that door, bolted into the wall and fat as a bell, stood the Lantern's cistern: eight hundred litres of district water on a standpipe line, black iron with a welded seam. A man who is afraid of nothing does not walk around a water tank. Ketsu filed that with the rest and let his face stay stupid.

"You're quiet," Dagan said, coming in behind a jab that snapped Ketsu's head back and a second that mashed his lip against the gap where the tooth had been. "I hate it when they're quiet. The loud ones are fun. Go on, shout, say your name, cry for your mother!"

"Ketsu," Ketsu said through the blood. "Of the Ledger Hall. The mother's a bookkeeper. She'd say you owe the Lantern for a table."

He drew on the word *table*. The sabre came out of the scabbard in a rising cut from the right hip, a long diagonal across the body that opened at the knee and climbed to the left shoulder, and Dagan, who had been expecting another fist, leaned back from it a half-second late. The point caught the plum coat at the belt and tore it open to the collar, and went on, and opened the skin across his chest from the belt to the right collarbone in one clean line. *Shhhk.* The cold came off the steel in a breath of mist and the spilled wine on the floor skinned over with frost. For a heartbeat the cut was white and bloodless, the lips of it curled back from the fat and the muscle underneath. Then the blood came all at once in a bright curtain, running down into the ruin of the coat.

"AH!" Dagan shouted, and looked down at himself as at a present. "Oh, that's *good!*"

---

The blade was white.

It was a plain grey Corvane sabre until it cleared the scabbard, and then the frost ran along its length and the steel went the colour of a winter dawn. Ketsu had forged the edge himself, quench after quench in a trough of salted snow, until it pulled heat out of whatever it touched. A cut from Frost-Edge went white first and bled a breath late, and when the blood did come it came out cold.

"It costs me," Ketsu said. He had never said that to an enemy before, but a showman gives you the price of his trick and the fair answer is to give him yours. "Every cut takes warmth from my sword hand. Past ten, I can't feel the grip. So I'll make mine count."

"HA! You're a *fair man!* Then let's see you count!"

Dagan threw the left hook before the laugh had finished. It was a long slow arc of fist and shoulder, and Ketsu's instinct told him to step back onto the bad leg. He put his weight on the right instead and turned the sabre in his hand, and the hook met the flat of the blade with a ringing *TANG*. The cold bit the knuckles. The fist glanced off and kept going into the lamp bracket on the wall, tore the whole fitting out of the brick, and flung it across the room, lamp and bracket and burning oil, where it burst on the floor in a bloom of fire that licked at the legs of the benches.

The swing had pulled Dagan's body round and his left side hung open. Ketsu cut the forearm. The backhand stroke came across at the level of the throat and Dagan threw the arm up to check it, and the edge opened the meat of the forearm from the wrist crease to the elbow, through the long flexor muscles and down to the shining bone, and the bone rang like a tuning fork. The cut went white along its whole length. Dagan roared, surprised and outraged and delighted all at once, and swung his right fist over the top of the sabre.

It landed on the cheekbone like a thrown brick. The orbital rim cracked, a short dry snap Ketsu felt in his teeth, and the world flashed white and then red and then the dim gold of the burning room. His knees bent. He used the bend, dropping under the follow-up and driving his right shoulder into the big man's belly. It was like running into a door. Dagan's hands closed on the back of his coat and lifted, and Ketsu's feet left the floor.

*Not like this.* He reversed the sabre in his fist and hammered the pommel into the hinge of Dagan's jaw. *KRAK.* The head snapped sideways on the thick neck, a spray of spit and blood and a tooth, and the grip opened. Ketsu fell, landed on the good leg, spun, and laid the flat of the blade across the back of Dagan's knee. The cold bit. The leg locked straight for a heartbeat, and Ketsu stepped in and kicked the planted foot out from under him. Dagan went down on one knee with a crash that rattled the glasses on the shelf.

"Not bad!" Dagan said from the floor, picking the tooth out of his palm and looking at it with real interest. "Not bad at all! Now you'll want to see the second thing."

He laid his bleeding hand flat on the boards, and the smoke came up through his fingers.

It wrapped Ketsu's ankles first, grey and warm as a hound, and hauled. The dead leg gave and he went down on his back with the air punched out of him. The frost that Ketsu's own edge had laid across the wine and the boards began to steam. The smoke had gone into it and it drank the cold the way a hot iron drinks a bucket, and the stretch of floor between them boiled up in a white scalding sheet. *KSSSSSHHHH.* Ketsu threw himself backward over a bench, but the steam took the skin off the back of his left hand anyway, a red stripe that blistered before he landed.

"Everything that touches the fire is mine eventually!" Dagan sang, pulling himself up by the bar with the knee already thawing. "A fire is never one thing. It's every cold thing it has ever eaten, and every hot one. You cut me and you hand me the knife!"

"He's turning your counters into fuel!" Marit called from the beam. "Stop laying cold on the floor! It's a gift!"

"I can *see* that!" Ketsu snarled, rolling off the bench with the blister screaming, and realised that for the first time in his life he was fighting someone who made his skill into a larger version of himself. *Then the edge can't touch the floor. The edge has to touch the thread, and the thread is at the root, and the root is the room.* It was a thought, not a plan. A plan would have had somewhere to stand.

Marit gave him the somewhere. She dropped the lamp-chain from her beam, forty links of black iron with a brass hook on the end, and it came down across Dagan's shoulders with a *CLANG* that rang the whole hall, and the big man spun on her with fire already building behind his teeth. In that half-second his smoke forgot Ketsu entirely. Ketsu was across the floor with the sabre low. He stabbed into the thread that ran along the boards, a short cold stab that took four paces of smoke and left them white and dead, and then he drove the pommel into Dagan's right kidney, hard, and followed it with a heel into the back of the same knee that had only just thawed. The leg folded. Dagan sat down on the floor with a grunt like a dropped sack.

He did not stay there. He swung up out of the sitting position with the left arm already coming round, the forearm ruined and streaming, and Ketsu stepped inside it and cut the elbow.

The edge went in on the inside of the joint, between the two long bones, where the tendons lie in a cord. It parted the brachial artery on the way through and found the other side, and the forearm, hand still clenched in its fist, fell away and hit the floor with a heavy wet *thump* and rolled under the bench. For the length of one breath nothing came from the stump but a puff of vapour, because the steel had frozen the cut face white. Then the pressure behind it broke the ice, and the blood leapt out of the arm in three hard arcs, and where the cold air met it the beads froze and hung and rattled down onto the boards like red hail.

Dagan looked at his arm. It lay on the floor with the fingers slowly opening. Ketsu watched the colour leave the big man's face from the forehead down, saw the pupils spread, and thought, with horrible clarity, *he's going to scream.*

He laughed instead. It started low in the chest, shaking the broken rib, and came up through his teeth in a high happy howl, and he slapped the stump against his own thigh, again, and again, to see if it would hurt. "The *edge,*" he said. "Oh, the *edge!* Friend, nobody has ever, in all my life, *hurt me before!*"

His right hand closed on Ketsu's throat.

The fingers met around the windpipe and the thumb dug in under the angle of the jaw, and Ketsu's feet left the floor. The room went dark at the edges, a tunnel closing, and the roaring in his ears was his own blood. He hit the wrist with the pommel, once, twice, a flat dull crunch like a boot through frozen puddle-ice, and felt the small bones give and the grip slacken and tighten again, like a living thing deciding. He put his left thumb into the long wound across Dagan's chest and dug.

Dagan screamed then, a real scream, and the fingers opened.

---

Ketsu hit the floor on his knees. He knelt in the red hail, wheezing, with his throat feeling like it had been shut in a door, and Dagan stood over him swaying, one arm gone at the elbow, the plum coat black with blood, and put the palm of his right hand and the end of his stump flat on the boards.

"That tickles," he said. "Now watch."

Every lamp in the Brass Lantern went out at once. Ketsu had a heartbeat to think *oh, that's where all of it goes,* and the hall breathed in.

All of it came back. Three hours of cooking-fires and lamp-oil and tallow and tobacco, every spark struck under that roof since noon, came up out of the boards as a wall of living smoke and stood behind Dagan as high as the rafters, and then he opened his hand. The light went through the north wall and did not stop. It took the wall, and the street behind it, and the dye-works across the street, and a long clean furrow went out across the quarter toward the river with the edges glowing like a forge-mouth, and the roofs on either side slid slowly into the cut. The sound reached the room a moment later, *GOOOOOOM*, and the floor rang like a struck bell.

Ketsu was not in the furrow. He was on his knees four paces left of it, and his right ear had stopped hearing anything at all.

*Because I paid for it.* He did not remember deciding. He remembered the words, though, said flat and low to the floor, the way you say a thing that must be meant: "My right ear, for as long as the edge stays white." The ear had gone grey to the lobe and silent. The sword in his hand blazed so bright that Marit, up in her beam, threw an arm across her eyes. The cold rolled off the blade in a sheet and the whole left side of the hall was frosted over in the time it took him to breathe.

"There's a vow!" Dagan said, and from the bright joy of his face he could not have been happier. "Oh, that's a good one! I felt it land from here!"

"Shut up," Ketsu said, much too loudly, because he could no longer judge his own voice, "and watch the floor."

*Think.* His aura, which should have been the pale clean blue of a winter morning, had gone thin and bruised at the edges. His forearm shook when he lifted the blade. He counted what was left, and the count was ugly. *Three clean cuts. Four if I'm stupid. The wall of smoke isn't fire yet; it's the thread that feeds it. The thread comes from the room. He pulled every spark out of the walls to build that, and every ember he spent was a breath he couldn't get back.* He glanced at Dagan, and the big man's chest was working like a bellows under the slit wool. There was a grey line around his mouth, and the stump had begun again to drip.

*He's paying too. He's just louder about it.*

"Come on, friend," Dagan said, spreading the one arm. "One more! I can do one more like that! You can't *dodge* them all!"

"I'm not going to dodge," Ketsu said.

The smoke wall came down on him like a collapsing cliff. He stood still. He let the edge of the sabre fall level at the dark thread of smoke, no thicker than a bootlace, that ran along the floor from the wall of flame to the soles of Dagan's boots. Nobody had told him it would work. The cold took heat from whatever it touched, and a thread of old fire was only borrowed heat, and a borrowed thing can be cut off at the root. He swung once, low, and the sword sang.

*TSSSHHHHK.* The thread went white and fell dead. The wall behind it stood for one long, astonished moment, a mountain of flame with nothing under it, and then it lost its legs and came down on the floor in a single huge sigh of light.

"That," Dagan said slowly, "is not in the book." He looked down at his boots. He looked up at Ketsu, and for the first time that evening the smile went out of his face like a lamp turned low.

"Then I'll write it," Ketsu said.

"You know why I don't like the water?" Dagan said. He said it conversationally, as he came across the boards with his fist loose and his breath going like a saw. "Everyone asks. Nobody asks nicely. I was seven. My father kept a chandlery on Tallow Row, forty hundredweight of wax in the cellar, and it went up one night in a gold roar, the loveliest thing I'd ever seen. And the Guild pumps came, and they put it out." His voice did not change at all. "They put it out with my father in it. So I thought, all right. I'll keep it. I'll keep every last piece of it where nobody can wet it down ever again."

"I'm sorry," Ketsu said, and found, to his own surprise, that he meant it.

"Don't be," Dagan said, and hit him.

It was the right move; Ketsu would have done it himself. A man with one hand left has his fist, and Dagan swung like a man who had spent twenty years hitting things and had never once been told no. The right cross took Ketsu on the point of the chin and the floor tilted. The hook that followed landed on the sword hand where it gripped the hilt and crushed two knuckles against the steel, and something in the wrist went with them. The short right to the body found the ribs under Ketsu's left arm, and something there made a noise like a green branch. Ketsu folded around it, and Dagan's knee came up into his face and broke his nose across the bridge with a flat wet crunch, and he went over backward against the bar with blood pouring over his mouth and the whole room ringing.

*Back,* he thought, thick-headed. *Back, back, back. Give ground. Give it a little at a time and make him think he's winning.* He gave it. A stool. A table. The edge of the bar, rattling under his hip. Dagan was breathing in whoops, grey as ash around the lips and still smiling, a man in love with a good fight.

The fire came without his calling it now, small gold tongues lifting off the dead coals at his heels and climbing the one arm like a glove. When he threw the jab the glove came off his hand and went on without him. Ketsu took the first on the flat of the sabre and the frost bit it out of the air. The second came in on his right, the deaf side, and he never heard it. It laid a line of heat across the back of his neck like a drawn wire, and the smell of his own hair burning reached him a half-second before the pain.

"Right side!" Marit shrieked. "He's working your right side! He heard you pay for it!"

"Of course I heard!" Dagan laughed, and swung, and the fire went round the deaf ear in a long curling hook. "A man who's lost an ear walks with his head turned. Poor fellow! Is it a very expensive ear?"

"It's an ear," Ketsu said, and put his back to the bar so that his bad side faced a wall and not a man. *Then he can only come from the left, and the left is where I can hear him.* The fire came from the left. Ketsu was there. The sabre went through the glove of flame and left it hanging in the air with a crust of white on its knuckles, and Dagan stepped back one pace, then another, with the first honest respect anyone had seen on his face all evening. Every pace he gave was a pace nearer the kitchen, and he did not notice, and Ketsu thought only: *keep him talking.*

"Not bad," Dagan panted. "Do you know, I haven't been this happy since the Guild burned my teacher's house? He was a terrible old bore. He'd have loved you."

"Tell me about him," Ketsu said, and stepped to the right, and Dagan stepped with him.

That was when the big man saw the kitchen door at his back and the black iron belly behind it. He stopped. For one half-second his weight went onto his heels and his whole body said *no.*

*There.*

The room went slow. It came on him the way it comes once or twice in a life, all at once and without being asked for: the cooling light on the floor, Marit's voice stretched into a long low note somewhere above him, the line of the sabre running from his shoulder to the weld in the iron, and his own breath, enormous, going out and in. A spark crawled up his broken knuckles. He thought of the quench trough and the snow and his master's hand on the bellows, and the hand that had spent ten years learning to close finally closed.

*KRAKA-DOOM.*

He did not cut Dagan. He cut the seam. The edge struck the weld and a black spark leapt off it and hung in the air like a crack in a window, and every soul in the hall saw it. The iron split like a ripe fruit, and eight hundred litres of cold district water came out in a roar. It hit the floor and rushed across the boards and over the dead smoke and the dying coals, and at the end of the rush it hit Dagan Voss in the chest and carried him backward into the bar.

He lay in the flood and looked at the ceiling. The smoke at his ankles lay flat and grey. It did not answer him. Ketsu limped in, put a boot on his chest, and lowered the point of the sabre to the hollow of his throat, and the big man's eyes came round to him very slowly and did not fight. The water around the stump went pink, then red, then pink again as the flood took it away.

"Old fire's no good wet," Marit said, from the rafters, in a voice gone small. "He knew. That's why he walked the long way."

"I know," Ketsu said, or tried to. His mouth was full of blood. He stood over the big man for a long time before he noticed his left hand would not open. He stood longer before he noticed his right ear was still dead, and the edge of his sword had gone back to plain grey and was shaking, and the roof of the Brass Lantern had a long clean hole in it where the north wall used to be. The blister on the back of his hand had burst. Three streets away, someone was ringing a bell, and he could hear it only on one side.

"Burn it down," Dagan said to the ceiling, very softly. "Somebody. Please."

Ketsu knelt, and with fingers that did not obey him found the lamp on the bar that had never gone out, the little brass one by the till, and set it where Dagan's hand could reach. The big man's fingers closed around it. The flame stood up in the cup, small and gold and shy, and leaned toward him like a dog.

The cat came back and sat on the bar. It looked at the lamp. It had no opinion.

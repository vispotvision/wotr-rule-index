*The Salt Stair, Corvane harbour. Sixth bell, rain lifting.*

The two debt-collectors came down the stair together, and Ketsu knew from the way they came that they had done this before.

Blue clung to his arms and shoulders like breath on cold glass, the pale clean blue of a winter morning, and it brightened at his wrists as he drew. The taller collector, a lean man with a brass-handled cane, set his feet three stairs above and spoke without hurry. "You'll want to know what the cane does, so I'll tell you. It does nothing. It's a cane. My partner does the work."

*A bluff, or the truth told so flatly it sounds like one.* Ketsu read the partner without turning his head: a short wide woman on the landing below, her aura a muddy ochre that lay close to her skin and thickened around her left hand. *Ground-work. Her fist is the technique and the cane is the show.* He filed the lie and the truth together and waited to learn which was which.

The cane came down at his head. It was a plain blow, a downward chop with the whole forearm behind it, and Ketsu stepped inside the arc and let the brass handle strike his shoulder, where the blue thickened into a coat as hard as lacquer. *TONK.* The coat took the blow and rippled once from the point of contact down to the elbow, and went back to a thin sheen. A smaller man would have lost the arm. Ketsu's collarbone ached and nothing more.

His own cut went in under the guard in a rising diagonal, hip to shoulder. The sabre carried the cold from his shoulder through the wrist and out through the edge with no seam anywhere along it, and where the steel opened the lean man's coat the cloth turned white and stiff. The skin beneath went white after it. The cut bled a breath late. When the blood came it came out cold, in a bright stiff curtain, and the lean man looked down at his own chest in a kind of professional distaste.

"Now," he said.

The partner's fist hit the landing. The stone under Ketsu's feet bucked and rolled like the deck of a boat, an ochre wave running up the stair, and the rain-slick steps tilted outward. He had no footing and no time. He pushed the blue into his heels, a burst of pale light that left a long streak of afterimage across the rain where he had stood, and was three stairs up the flight and past the lean man, his back to the wall, breathing through his teeth.

*She moves the ground. He pins me while she does it. They drilled this.* The thought came with a small cold pleasure, the pleasure of seeing a machine whole. *Then the machine has a joint.*

He touched the wall with his off hand and listened. The ochre had a note. It ran through the stone from the landing like a sound through a bell, and he could feel where it thinned: at the stair's turn, where the old mortar had been patched with lime. The Draw flowed there like water finding a crack.

"The edge takes heat," Ketsu said, loudly, to give her something to hear. "That's all it is. Skin, steel, mortar. Whatever it touches."

He cut the patch. The edge went into the lime mortar and the cold followed the note down through the stone to the landing, and the landing went white along its whole length. Frozen mortar loses its grip on the block. The stair shrugged, shuddered, and the whole turn slumped with a long grinding *KRRRRRUMBLE*, dropping the short woman's footing a hand's breadth and her fist a foot short of the lean man's boots. The ochre flashed once and stuttered. She swore in a language of the northern docks.

The lean man looked at the broken stair, then at Ketsu, with something very like respect. He swung the cane again, and Ketsu met it with the flat of the sabre. *TANG.* The brass handle came away in his hand, and the cane, which was a cane, fell on the steps and rolled into the harbour.

"Told you it did nothing," the lean man said, and bled.

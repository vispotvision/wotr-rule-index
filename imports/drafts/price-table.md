# What Things Cost

*Everyday prices and wages in a networked Accord city of the late Imperial Age, at the Accord rate, western standard*

> **One gold mark is twelve silver marks. One silver mark is twenty-four copper. A gold mark is therefore two hundred and eighty-eight copper**, and almost nobody below a shop's counting-room ever handles one.
>
> A Nalūn salt bar passes for one silver mark, more in a hard winter. Imperial bronze goes by weight.

Prices are given as city prices on the main. Off the main, and everywhere north of the arrays, most of this list is paid in kind: grain, salt, cloth, fuel, board, a day's labour owed and settled at year's end. Where coin changes hands out there, it is copper, and in a copper famine it is nothing at all.

---

## Food, Drink and a Bed

| Item | Price | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **A loaf of bread** | 3 copper | The two-pound loaf. Dearer after a bad harvest, and the first price anyone watches |
| **A meal** | 4 to 6 copper at a cookshop; 1 silver at an inn's table | Cookshop fare is broth, bread and whatever was cheap at dawn |
| **A pint of the common drink** | 2 copper | Ale, small beer or a cup of tea at a stall, by quarter and custom |
| **A night's bed** | 3 to 4 copper in a common lodging-house; 1 to 2 silver for an inn room | The lodging-house price buys a bed in a room of twelve, and not the blanket |
| **A room for a month** | 4 to 8 silver | One room in a tenement, unfurnished, draw not included |

## Clothing and Light

| Item | Price | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Boots** | 4 to 6 silver new; 1 to 2 silver worn | Working boots, nailed. A labourer buys one pair in three years |
| **A coat** | 5 to 8 silver for a working jacket; 1 to 2 gold for a greatcoat | The greatcoat outlives its first owner and usually its second |
| **Candles and lamp oil** | Tallow, 1 copper the dozen; lamp oil, 1 copper an evening; a beeswax taper, 1 silver | An Essence lantern is 3 to 8 silver, and the inscription fee on top is the expensive part |

## The Bill

| Supply | Price, monthly | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Sealed domestic, one room** | 3 to 5 silver | Heat, light and a warded doorframe, at a density everyone knows runs below the tariff |
| **Sealed domestic, a household** | 8 to 12 silver | A clerk's house: stove, lamps, the doorframes of every room |
| **Trade supply** | 2 to 5 gold | Any workshop that cuts, pours or renders. Inspected twice a year |

> The bill is the largest line in a poor household after rent, and it comes whether the family has coin that month or not. **The cutter comes when it goes unpaid.**

## Travel and Word

| Item | Price | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **A letter** | 1 copper across a city; 2 to 4 copper across a quarter | Paid by the sender, stamped at the counter |
| **A wire message** | 1 silver for twenty words, and a copper for each word after | Sent by clerk, from the wire office. Addresses count as words |
| **A rail ticket** | 1 copper a mile, third class; 2 to 3 copper a mile, first | Where the line runs. A hundred miles third class is four silver and a long day |
| **A newspaper** | 1 copper | The Guild gazettes run 3 copper and print the rates |

## Beasts and Carriage

| Item | Price | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **A horse** | 15 to 25 gold for a riding hack; 30 to 50 gold for a draught or cavalry horse | Fodder runs a silver a week in town |
| **A mule** | 10 to 20 gold | Dearer than a hack on the pass roads, where nothing else lives long |
| **A cart** | 8 to 12 gold, two-wheeled; a four-wheeled wagon, 20 gold and up | Without the beast |

## Arms and Shot

| Item | Price | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **A sword** | 6 to 10 silver for a trooper's sabre; 4 to 7 gold for an officer's blade | A sword that carries a working is priced on the coil, not here |
| **A musket** | 5 to 10 silver | Old muzzle-loaders, sold off from armouries |
| **A rifle** | 3 to 4 gold, single-shot breechloader; 6 to 10 gold for a repeater, where one can be had at all | Repeaters are mostly state issue and rarely on a counter |
| **A revolver** | 3 to 4 gold | The officer's weapon, and the city's |
| **A box of cartridges** | 1 to 2 silver the fifty | Common shot. Kills men |
| **Proofed shot** | 1 silver the round | Guild-stamped. Beats proofed plate |
| **Enhanced shot** | 2 to 4 silver the round from a hand at Apprentice standing; 1 to 2 gold the round from a Journeyman | Bought by the pouch of twenty, by guard companies and bounty hunters. It goes stale, and a stale pouch is a loss nobody refunds |

> A guard company's full pouch of enhanced shot costs about what a clerk earns in a month. **Ordinary infantry do not carry it.**

## Physicians

| Item | Price | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **A doctor's visit** | 1 to 3 silver; 6 copper at a dispensary | In the north and the poor quarters, the barber, for a few copper and what he knows |
| **A surgeon** | 5 to 40 gold for an operation | Anaesthesia included in a Guild city. The fee is quoted before the knife, and paid before the stitches |

## Wages

| Work | Pay | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **A labourer, by the day** | 12 to 24 copper | Half a silver in a slack season, a silver in a good one. Often paid part in board or kind |
| **A soldier, by the month** | 12 to 15 silver and his keep | Less stoppages for kit, which take a third of it |
| **A clerk, by the month** | 4 to 8 gold | Paid quarterly, in arrears |
| **A Guild inspector, by the month** | 10 to 18 gold | And the gifts nobody records |

### A practitioner's retainer, by Tier of Standing

| Standing | Retainer, monthly | Who pays it |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiate** | 3 to 6 gold | A workshop, a Board, a caravan master |
| **Apprentice** | 6 to 12 gold | A guard company, a mine, a merchant house |
| **Journeyman** | 15 to 30 gold | A great house, a city watch, a shipping line |
| **Adept** | 40 to 80 gold | A crown's household, a charter Board, a bank |
| **Expert** | 100 to 250 gold | Crowns and the largest houses |
| **Master** | 400 gold and up | Crowns, by treaty as much as by purse |
| **Grandmaster and above** | Not quoted | Not retained. Negotiated with |

> A retainer is the smallest part of what a practitioner is paid. Commissions, bonds and fees for attestation run on top, and **a ranked practitioner's term of service is carried in an estate's accounts in the same column as a ship.**

## Notes (not published)

**Status.** Draft for R53-07. Everything above the line is the page; everything below is private.

**The coin anchor.** Canon (wiki, *Value, Coin and Trade*, Published Accord Rates): 1 gold mark = 12 silver marks; 1 silver mark = 24 copper; salt bar = 1 silver mark; bronze by weight. I anchored **1 copper to roughly the British penny (1d)**, so 1 silver mark is about 2 shillings (a florin) and 1 gold mark about 24 shillings, a little over a sovereign. Canon supports the penny scale: the Apparatus page says a ball of shot costs "a fraction of a penny" and prices tallow at "a copper bit for a dozen". Every price above is a real British (sometimes American) 1840 to 1900 price converted at that rate, then bent a step where canon bends it.

**Canon prices quoted as given, not derived.** Tallow candles (1 copper the dozen), lamp oil (1 copper an evening), beeswax taper (1 silver), Essence lantern (3 to 8 silver plus inscription): all from *The Apparatus of the Age*, Light section. Supply grade names (sealed domestic, trade supply) and the cutter: *The Imperial Age Aether Infrastructure*, III and VIII. Proofed shot as a separate ammunition tier: Pack Eleven §3 (R11 three ammunition tiers, ratified R20C-22). Enhanced shot paid per shot, consumed when fired, goes stale with its maker's Duration: *Shot and the Enhanced Weapon*, Part Two. Retainer "same column as a ship": *Value, Coin and Trade*, What Is Actually Valuable. Tiers of Standing names (Initiate to Paragon): the tier-ladders edition, sites ladder, spine Tier column.

**Real-world anchors (approximate, period ranges):**
- Bread: London quartern (4 lb) loaf ran about 6d to 9d mid-century, falling toward 5d by the 1890s; a 2 lb loaf about 3d.
- Meal: cookshop and coffee-house dinners of the 1850s to 1880s, roughly 4d to 8d (Mayhew, *London Labour and the London Poor*, 1851).
- Drink: a pint of beer about 2d through most of the century; a cup of coffee or tea at a street stall 1d.
- Bed: common lodging-house bed about 4d a night (Mayhew; Booth's survey, 1880s to 90s). Inn or hotel room 1s 6d to 3s.
- Room: a single tenement room in London about 2s to 4s a week in the 1880s (Booth), so 8s to 17s a month; I set the month at 4 to 8 silver (8s to 16s).
- Boots and coats: working boots about 8s to 12s; working jacket 10s to 15s; overcoat about £1 to £2.
- Horse: a riding hack about £20 to £30; draught or army remount £40 to £60 in the late century. Mule: roughly $100 to $150 in the 1870s to 1890s American West, about £20 to £30. Cart: a farm cart roughly £10 to £15.
- Sword: trooper's pattern sabre bought by contract for about £1 or less; an officer's sword from a London maker about £5 to £8.
- Muskets: surplus muzzle-loaders sold off for a few shillings to £1 after the breechloader changeover (1860s to 70s).
- Rifle and revolver: a Colt Single Action Army sold for about $17 to $20 in the 1870s; a Webley revolver about £3 to £4; a Winchester repeater roughly $25 to $50 new in the 1870s, far cheaper by the 1890s mail-order catalogues. Converted with $5 = £1.
- Cartridges: pistol and rifle cartridges ran roughly under a dollar to a dollar and a half a box of fifty in the 1880s to 90s (mail-order catalogue range), about 3s to 6s; I set 1 to 2 silver (2s to 4s).
- Doctor: a general practitioner's visit about 2s 6d to 5s; sixpenny doctors and provident dispensaries for the poor; surgeons' fees in guineas, a few guineas to fifty for a major operation.
- Letter: the Uniform Penny Post (1840) put a letter at 1d anywhere in Britain. I widened it to 2 to 4 copper across a quarter, because WOTR's post runs over passes and without one state behind it.
- Wire: after the British telegraphs were nationalised (1870), an inland telegram was 1s for twenty words, addresses counted until 1875 or so; 6d for twelve from 1885.
- Rail: the 1844 Railway Regulation Act required a third-class "parliamentary" fare of 1d a mile; first class ran roughly 2d to 3d a mile.
- Newspaper: after the stamp duty repeal (1855) the penny daily (the *Daily Telegraph*) became standard; *The Times* stayed at 3d, hence the 3-copper gazette.
- The bill: modelled on domestic gas. Working households on slot meters (from the 1890s) spent roughly a shilling or two a week; a middle-class house a pound or so a quarter. I pushed the poor-room bill up to 3 to 5 silver (6s to 10s a month) because canon says draw is "priced at a margin the margin cannot pay" (R20C-18) and "Money before magic" holds (R53-17). That puts it near a quarter of a labourer's month, which is the point.
- Labourer: rural day labourers about 2s a day in the 1870s; urban unskilled 18s to 21s a week in the 1880s. I set 12 to 24 copper (1s to 2s), toward the rural end, because canon says the un-Tempered labourer of the late Imperial Age is worse off in real terms than his ancestor (*Value, Coin and Trade*, Rank as Property).
- Soldier: the British private's shilling a day (to the 1890s), with stoppages for food and kit taking a large share.
- Clerk: roughly £80 to £150 a year for ordinary commercial and railway clerks, so £7 to £12 a month; 4 to 8 gold is £5 to £10.
- Guild inspector: modelled on Victorian factory, mines and excise inspectors, roughly £150 to £300 a year for sub-inspectors and inspectors; 10 to 18 gold is £12 to £22 a month.
- Retainers: no real analogue for a practitioner, so they are pegged to real incomes by social rung. Initiate to a skilled tradesman (35s to 40s a week); Apprentice to a senior clerk or ship's officer; Journeyman to a professional man at £200 to £400 a year; Adept to a successful physician or barrister at £500 to £1,000; Expert to the top of the professions; Master to a landed peer's income. Checked against canon: a Rank V commoner is someone crowns ennoble, and rank is collateral for lending houses.
- Enhanced shot: no real analogue. Priced on the maker's time and the loss when a pouch goes stale, so a pouch of twenty at Apprentice make is roughly a clerk's month (40 to 80 silver). Journeyman make is priced so no company buys it by the pouch.

**Tensions for the call (not resolved, just flagged).**
1. *Value, Coin and Trade* says "A labourer may see one gold piece in a lifetime." At these rates a labourer earns a gold mark's worth in two to four weeks. I read the canon line as about circulation (wages in kind and copper; gold drains into consecration) rather than magnitude, and the page's opening line says so. If the line is meant as magnitude, every price here has to be rebased with copper near a farthing, which breaks the tallow and shot prices canon already gives.
2. The wire: R20C-19 calls it "Guild line, Guild price"; *The Imperial Age Aether Infrastructure* VII says "The Accord does not control it." The page says "the wire office" and names no owner.
3. Rail: R53-01 supersedes the no-rail limit, and the Infrastructure page has rail on ordinary steam. Fares are given "where the line runs" because no page says where it runs.
4. Proofed shot at 1 silver a round is my figure; canon gives the tier, not a price.
5. The Guild inspector: canon mentions Guild inspectors (*The Faiths of the Four Quarters*, the shrine passage) and Board inspectors (Infrastructure III). The row is the Guild's; a Board inspector would sit at about the same pay.

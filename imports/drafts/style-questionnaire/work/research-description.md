# Research: what description can cover, and how (2026-10-03)

For the style questionnaire. Isaac's ask: "descriptions should cover a wide variety of things", in a blend of Western literary fantasy, Eastern traditions (Chinese, Japanese, Korean, cultivation and system web fiction included) and LitRPG (fiction with a game-like system of levels, stats, skills and status screens visible on the page).

**Read for this:** all eleven files in `desktop/inventories/`, the Kharven Inventory inline in `desktop/NATALIE.md`, `imports/drafts/sense-banks.md`, `imports/drafts/inventory-additions.md`, `imports/drafts/price-table.md`, `imports/drafts/well-spawn.md`, `rules/doc-world-texture-law-2026-09-26.yaml` (R53-01 to R53-32), and, for cross-checks, the live R59 and R60 rows, R3-8, R6-5, R6-10, R48-02, R49-22 to R49-29, R51-31, R51-32, and the description census in `work/research-house-prose.md` §2. The sense banks, the Gone, Folk belief and Literacy additions, the price table and the Well-spawn page are all already applied or published (R58-05; `wiki/The Bestiary/Well-spawn.md`).

**The finding in one paragraph.** WOTR's law sets description's *density* hard (R48-02 "Lush throughout"; R53-30 one world-only detail per beat; R51-31 senses drawn from the Inventory first) and says almost nothing about its *range*. Every Standing Inventory carries the same sixteen fields (Food, Greeting, Oaths, Insults, Time, The dead, Objects, Exchange, Proverbs, Body, five Senses, Gone, Folk beliefs, Literacy, Swears, Recurrence; swear tables in seven of the twelve), so a writer drawing from them is steered toward the same subjects in every culture: what people eat, swear, owe, own and smell. Land, sky, plants, animals, architecture as beauty, dress, music, play, children, festivals, courts and travel have no field. The house census agrees: bodies, rooms, documents and wounds run heavy; nature, taste, markets, music, art, children, rites performed in full, wide landscape and the rich run thin or absent. The LitRPG layer has the reverse problem: the data exists in depth (Fracture of Worlds, the tier ladders, Danger Tiers, material classes) and has no on-page form at all.

**Tradition tags used below.** **W** Western (epic, the realist and Victorian novel, modern fantasy). **CN** Chinese (classical poetics and painting, the chapter novel, wuxia, cultivation novels). **JP** Japanese (court and war tales, haiku and haibun, modern literary fiction, manga and light novels). **KR** Korean (pansori, sijo, system and regression web novels). **L** LitRPG, progression fantasy and GameLit (game-literature).

## Part 1: Subjects

A hundred and fifty-one subjects in nineteen groups. Codes (1.1, 1.2) are for the questionnaire to cite. Each group closes with which tradition leans on it hardest, because that is where a blend borrows its eye.

### 1. Land, water and sky (10)
**1.1** Terrain and ground underfoot (rock, soil, slope, permafrost, crater wall, salt floor, black glass). **1.2** Fresh water (river, lake, sump, flood, ice). **1.3** Sea and shore (tide, surf, reef, harbour). **1.4** Weather (wind, rain, snow, fog, thaw, heat). **1.5** Season and its signs (first frost, thaw, harvest; each culture's own named turnings). **1.6** Day sky and natural light (sun, cloud, the colour of the hour, snow-light, dusk). **1.7** Night sky (Shiro the moon, the five wanderers, the fixed field, aurora). **1.8** Darkness and shadow. **1.9** Distance and horizon (mountains in layers, haze, the long view). **1.10** Landmarks and named places, and why they carry the names they do.
*Leaned on by:* CN (landscape painting and poetry make this the highest subject), JP (season as the spine of the year and the poem), W (Tolkien's route and weather, McCarthy's desert). L barely uses it outside dungeon biomes.

### 2. The built world (10)
**2.1** Architecture as style and ornament, not only function and wear. **2.2** Interiors (furniture, layout, hearth, bed). **2.3** Streets, squares, alleys, stairs. **2.4** Districts by class (rich quarter, tenement, outer town). **2.5** Draw infrastructure (standpipe, gauge-housing, meter, main, conduit, the draw-hum and the silence when it stops). **2.6** Works, mills and workshops. **2.7** Bridges, lifts, masts, rail and tram lines as structures. **2.8** Lighting apparatus (Essence lamp, blubber lamp, tallow, salt lamps). **2.9** Water, waste and drainage (privy, standpipe, Nalūn's drain). **2.10** Fortifications, gates, ward-posts.
*Leaned on by:* W (Dickens's city, Peake's castle, Miéville's urban catalogue), JP (Tanizaki on rooms and shadow), CN (palace and garden in the classic novels). L: dungeons and safe zones.

### 3. The living world (10)
**3.1** Trees and forest. **3.2** Fields, crops and harvest. **3.3** Herbs, medicinal and alchemical plants. **3.4** Flowers, blossom and gardens. **3.5** Wild animals. **3.6** Birds. **3.7** Insects, vermin and small life. **3.8** Fish and water life. **3.9** Domestic and working animals (horse, mule, dog, pit pony, the Droval). **3.10** Monsters: Well-spawn, Spirit Beasts, Echo Beasts, Natural Beasts.
*Leaned on by:* JP (insects, birds and blossom as season words), CN (cultivation novels grade spirit herbs and beasts by age and power), L (the monster as stat block and drop table), W (the horse as a character, R3-8-CAVALRY_HORSE_CHARACTER).

### 4. Bodies (11)
**4.1** Face and expression. **4.2** Eyes, bloodline eyes included (Moto ring-lines, the Yukari corona, Zettari silver). **4.3** Hair. **4.4** Hands. **4.5** Build, weight and stance. **4.6** Gait, posture and stillness. **4.7** Voice (pitch, accent, grade, sub-bass). **4.8** Skin and its marks (scars, tattoos, Soul-Runes, Legion Etchings, burn-scatter). **4.9** Age: the child's body and the old body. **4.10** Illness, disability and the body a trade makes (salt-throat, the tender's shake). **4.11** Beauty and desire.
*Leaned on by:* W (the Victorian portrait paragraph, physiognomy, which reads character from the face), CN (the cultivation "appearance paragraph" of beauty and aura), JP (manga's eyes, hair and silhouette as identity). L tracks the body as numbers.

### 5. Dress and adornment (8)
**5.1** Fabric and its feel. **5.2** Cut, fit and silhouette. **5.3** Colour as rank and allegiance. **5.4** Wear, repair, the second-hand. **5.5** Armour and proof-marks. **5.6** Jewellery, beads, cords, rings. **5.7** Hair arrangement, cosmetics and worn scent. **5.8** Insignia (tokens, seals, rank plates, badges).
*Leaned on by:* JP (the war tales catalogue armour lacing and robe colour at every entrance; Genji reads character from layered sleeves), CN (robe colour and ornament as rank), W (Martin's sigils, Mantel's cloth as power). L: gear slots and equipment stats.

### 6. Food and drink (7)
**6.1** Ingredients and their source. **6.2** Cooking as process. **6.3** The meal as rite and the feast as display (who eats first, where one sits, the host's food, the menu as status). **6.4** Hunger and rationing. **6.5** Drink and drunkenness. **6.6** Street food and cookshops. **6.7** Taste itself, in the mouth.
*Leaned on by:* CN (banquet catalogues in the classic novels), W (Martin's feast menus, O'Brian's ship's table), JP (food writing as seasonal pleasure). L: food as buff (a temporary stat boost).

### 7. Craft and labour (7)
**7.1** A process, step by step. **7.2** Tools, workshop and their wear. **7.3** Guilds, marks and quality stamps. **7.4** Apprenticeship and teaching. **7.5** The worker's body made by the work. **7.6** The finished object and its quality (craft beauty). **7.7** Trades dying under the works.
*Leaned on by:* JP (the master craftsman's devotion), W (O'Brian, Sanderson's systems), L (crafting systems with recipes and quality grades).

### 8. Money and trade (7)
**8.1** Coin, bars, notes and scrip: their look and weight. **8.2** Prices called aloud and paid. **8.3** Wages and what they buy. **8.4** Markets, stalls and haggling. **8.5** Shops, arcades, department stores, branded goods and advertising. **8.6** Debt, credit and the ledger. **8.7** The bill and the meter.
*Leaned on by:* W (Dickens, Balzac, Mantel), L (the shop screen and the loot economy), CN (cultivation's spirit-stone economy).

### 9. Power, law and rank (7)
**9.1** Rank, precedence, titles and honorifics in a room. **9.2** Seals, stamps and signatures. **9.3** Documents, permits and forms. **9.4** Courts, hearings and tribunals. **9.5** Punishment and the enforcer's kit (fines, branding, labour-debt, shaming). **9.6** The spectacle of power (processions, entrances, the throne room). **9.7** Succession and inheritance.
*Leaned on by:* KR and JP (speech levels and honorifics make rank audible in every line), CN (court and examination culture), W (Martin's court, Mantel's documents). L: guild rank and ranking boards.

### 10. Ritual, belief and the dead (7)
**10.1** A rite performed in full (bill of goods, order, ways it fails). **10.2** Shrines, temples, altars, household observance. **10.3** Oaths and their apparatus. **10.4** Funerals and mourning. **10.5** Festivals and holy days. **10.6** Folk belief, taboo and omen acted on. **10.7** Prayer and petition.
*Leaned on by:* JP (Noh ghosts, shrine life, ghost tales), CN (records of the strange, ancestral halls), KR (shamanic rite in pansori), W (the gothic, Clarke's faerie).

### 11. Knowledge and record (7)
**11.1** Books, scrolls, archives. **11.2** Scripts, hands and calligraphy. **11.3** Maps, charts and route-books. **11.4** Instruments and readings (gauge, coil, balance, watch). **11.5** Schools, academies, examinations. **11.6** News (wire, broadsheet, rumour). **11.7** Photography and identity plates.
*Leaned on by:* CN (calligraphy as character), W (Wolfe, Clarke, Eco), L (the in-world manual and the skill book).

### 12. Art, play and leisure (8)
**12.1** Music and instruments. **12.2** Song, chant and verse. **12.3** Visual art (painting, carving, ornament, portraits). **12.4** Theatre, music hall, storytellers. **12.5** Games of chance and skill. **12.6** Sport and spectacle (racing, prize-fighting, practitioner exhibitions). **12.7** Cafes, clubs, taverns, reading rooms, pleasure gardens and fairs. **12.8** Children's play and toys.
*Leaned on by:* JP (the arts of the court in Genji; go and shogi as character), KR (pansori itself is performance), CN (the scholar's four arts: zither, go, calligraphy, painting), L (the tournament arc, a staple of cultivation and shōnen fiction).

### 13. War (8)
**13.1** Formations and the shape of a host. **13.2** Arms and firearms: mass, balance, make, shot tier. **13.3** Banners, heraldry, uniforms. **13.4** Logistics (food, fodder, carts, baggage). **13.5** Camp life and the siege's tedium. **13.6** Wounds and field medicine. **13.7** Aftermath (the five stages). **13.8** Cavalry and the horse.
*Leaned on by:* W (Homer, Malory, Abercrombie), CN (Three Kingdoms' generals and stratagems), JP (the war tales; Kingdom's armies), L (party roles and raid composition).

### 14. Magic and the aether (9)
**14.1** The working on the page in its three strata. **14.2** Pressure in the body. **14.3** Residue, leavings, the Veil and lingering Echoes. **14.4** Sites, Wells and Cores. **14.5** Stage display (eyes, aura, projected form, the splash beat). **14.6** Cost in the body (eye bleed, sternum ache, flattening, Starvation). **14.7** Perception modes (the Shingan read, Yukari threads, a carver listening to a wall). **14.8** Essence-bearing materials (Ember Flint, Onceglass, Sacred Gold, Agano Sand). **14.9** Inscriptions, wards and glyphs as visible objects.
*Leaned on by:* CN (qi, meridians and breakthroughs described from inside), JP (technique names and visual signatures), W (Sanderson's rules, Clarke's strangeness), L (the mana bar and cooldown).

### 15. The system made visible (8)
**15.1** The status read (Level, Band, Stage, Grades, Sub-Stats, EU and AU/s, Traits), including the power-gap comparison between two people. **15.2** The breakthrough or level beat. **15.3** Skill and technique cards. **15.4** Item cards (grade, make, properties, price, a flavour line), loot and its division. **15.5** Monster entries (Danger Tier, Sigil, rung, abilities, weaknesses). **15.6** Rankings, registers and boards. **15.7** Treasures and rare materials graded by age and class. **15.8** Sect, academy and guild grounds as a display of power.
*Leaned on by:* L and KR (the status window), CN (realm and treasure grades), JP (light-novel status screens). W literary fantasy almost never.

### 16. Time and history (7)
**16.1** Hours, bells, the Guild hour, the notch. **16.2** Calendars, turnings, the sixty-count, the nameless day, anniversaries. **16.3** The age and the era. **16.4** History in layers (strata, re-used stones, a wall over an older wall). **16.5** Heirlooms and objects with lineage. **16.6** The Gone list: what a culture mourns. **16.7** The speed of change (the traveller who moves through decades, R59-05).
*Leaned on by:* JP (impermanence: the Heike's opening theme, Chōmei's hut), W (Tolkien's elegy, ruins), CN (dynastic cycle, the historian's frame).

### 17. The senses and states as subjects (7)
**17.1** Sound, ambient noise and silence. **17.2** Smell. **17.3** Taste. **17.4** Texture and touch. **17.5** Temperature. **17.6** Motion and balance. **17.7** Pressure and interoception (the body's sense of its own inside: heartbeat, breath, the ground pressing at the temples).
*Leaned on by:* W (modern sensory layering), JP (sound words and the silence between), KR (sound-mimetic words). L: the debuff as sensation.

### 18. Society in motion (7)
**18.1** Crowds and the crowd's reaction. **18.2** Street life and hawkers. **18.3** Households: family, servants, the domestic order. **18.4** Children and the child's world. **18.5** The rich and the poor and how each lives. **18.6** Strangers: how a culture sees an outsider. **18.7** Greetings and etiquette.

### 19. Travel and transport (6)
**19.1** Roads, their surface, and weather on the road. **19.2** Routes and itineraries, distance counted in stations. **19.3** Inns, way-stations, lodging. **19.4** Vehicles (cart, carriage, rail, tram, motorcar, dirigible). **19.5** Ships and boats. **19.6** Borders, tolls and papers.

## Part 2: Techniques

Forty-five ways to deliver a description, in five families. "WOTR now" says whether the house prose uses it today (from `research-house-prose.md` and the live law): **used**, **rare**, or **absent**.

### A. Choosing what to show

| # | Technique | What it does | Tradition | WOTR now |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | **Catalogue** | A deliberate list, long enough that its length is the point | W: Homer's catalogue of ships, Malory, the Victorian inventory. CN: the *fu* (Han-dynasty rhapsody, an exhaustive descriptive prose-poem on a park, palace or capital). KR: pansori's list passages. L: the inventory screen | Used: verbless sense lists at openings; Lambert counting under stress (R52-16) |
| A2 | **The single telling detail** | One specific thing that stands for the whole | W: Chekhov; the "reality effect" (Barthes's name for the apparently useless detail that certifies a world is real). CN: *baimiao* (plain line drawing: a few unshaded strokes, the subject left to stand) | Used: table rule 8's one unexplained thing; R53-30 |
| A3 | **Synecdoche** | The part for the whole: the hand, the boot, the voice through a wall | W | Used heavily (hands) |
| A4 | **Negative space** | Description of what is not there; the silence, the empty place, the gap | JP: *ma* (the meaningful interval) and *yohaku* (the beauty of blank space). CN: *liubai* ("leaving white", the unpainted part of a painting). W: Hemingway's iceberg | Rare: the Gone list (R53-31), the cut district's silence |
| A5 | **The counter-detail** | One flaw set in the beautiful, or beauty found in the worn | JP: *wabi-sabi* (beauty of the worn and impermanent), *kintsugi* (pottery mended with gold). W: the grotesque | Used for wear (R6-5 "the rust described in full"); beauty side absent |
| A6 | **Defamiliarisation** | The familiar described as if seen for the first time, so it looks strange | W: Shklovsky's *ostranenie* (Russian formalist term, "making strange"). JP and KR: the isekai newcomer (a character transported from another world) | Rare |

### B. The vehicle: what carries the description

| # | Technique | What it does | Tradition | WOTR now |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B1 | **Through action** | The room arrives as the body moves through it | W: Hemingway, Abercrombie. CN: wuxia | Used |
| B2 | **Through cost or price** | A thing is described by what it cost and who paid | W: Dickens, Balzac, Mantel. L: the price on the loot | Used (R53-17, the price table) |
| B3 | **Through a craft procedure** | The object is built, step by step, in front of the reader | W: O'Brian's seamanship. JP: the master-craftsman story. L: the crafting recipe | Used for wounds and workings (R12-6, R24-1); thin for crafts |
| B4 | **Through comparison** | Simile, metaphor; the *epic simile* (an extended comparison that becomes a small scene of its own); the *kenning* (Norse compound metaphor, "whale-road" for the sea) | W: Homer, the sagas. CN: *bixing* (comparison and evocation: a natural image set beside a human one, from the Book of Songs) | Used (R49-18; metaphors from the POV's life) |
| B5 | **Through another's eyes** | What a thing looks like depends on who looks | W: Martin's POV discipline. JP: Akutagawa's "In a Grove", the same event told by contradicting witnesses | Used (four voices) |
| B6 | **The explaining onlooker** | Bystanders name and react to what they see, and their reaction measures it | JP: the shōnen spectator who explains the technique. CN: the cultivation crowd that gasps and recognises the move. L: the commentator | Rare; capped by the four-voices rule (two per engagement) |

| B8 | **Through a document** | A record, letter, register entry or report describes it | W: epistolary novels, Clarke's footnotes. CN: *zhiguai* (records of the strange) and the historian's closing comment. L: the status screen, the system message | Used (in-world documents) |
| B9 | **Through disagreement** | Two people argue what a thing is, and the reader sees it between them | W: Mantel, Martin | Used (iceberg rule; the Four Theories argued) |
| B10 | **Through the POV's own body** | What the feet report, what the teeth taste, what the pack weighs | W: Abercrombie | Used (R11-4 descent prose) |
| B11 | **The name-announcement** | A fighter declares name, lineage and deeds before combat, and that is his description | JP: *nanori* (the war-tale warrior's self-announcement). W: Homeric genealogy at a duel | Absent (Dawi grade and Keth-Gorrum compound come close) |
| B12 | **Ekphrasis** | Describing a work of art in words, so the artwork becomes a window on the world | W: the Shield of Achilles. CN: poems inscribed on paintings | Absent |

| # | Technique | What it does | Tradition | WOTR now |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | **The set idiom** | A fixed phrase every reader knows carries a whole picture | CN: *chengyu* (four-character idiom). W: the Homeric epithet (a fixed tag, "rosy-fingered dawn"); the kenning | Used: proverbs in fixed wording (R52-30) |

## Part 3: Controls

### 3.1 Placement

Where a description sits changes what it does.

- **Entrance (arrival).** Today: arrivals open on the senses (R49-25), smell first in practice. Eastern options: far-to-near (D1), the seasonal anchor (C3). LitRPG option: the status read of the place (a Well's density reading at its mouth).
- **Threshold.** The doorway, gate or border where a document or a knowledgeable second can speak (the four voices allow "the document at a scene's threshold"). Options: the poetic insert (C2) here rather than mid-scene; the monster entry (E4) at a Well mouth.
- **Mid-action.** Today: compressed, through the body. Options: the beat between blows (D14); the explaining onlooker (B6); a status flash (E1) at the moment of the read.
- **Lull.** Between exchanges or after the set-piece peak. Options: the pillow shot (D6); the turn in *kishōtenketsu* (a four-part story shape: set-up, development, twist, reconciliation), where the third part can be a descriptive turn to something apparently unrelated.
- **Aftermath.** Today: the five-stage aftermath (R1-3) and the full aftermath beat. LitRPG places its reward here: loot, the level beat, the tally (E2, E3, E6).
- **Exit.** Today: end on an action, a concrete image or a line (R49-26). Options: the pull-back (D3), the noun ending (C7).

### 3.2 Density

- **Per beat:** one world-only detail with sensory layers around it (R53-30). Live, unchecked.
- **Per scene:** lush throughout (R48-02); turns about half texture and talk (R49-29); travel passes in a line when nothing is at stake (R48-29).
- **By scene type** (today's habit, and what each tradition would do differently):

| Scene type | Today | W pull | E pull | L pull |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Duel | Dense, body and mechanism | Choreography and wound | Freeze-frames, named moves, onlookers | Readouts at the read and the finish |
| Mass battle | Line register | Panorama, heraldry | Formation and stratagem | Party roles |
| Council | Faces, hands, rank | Objects as power | Speech level, seating, silence | Ranking of those present |
| Travel | Passes in a line | Route and weather | Travel-piece, season | Distance and danger tier |
| Quiet | Lived-in room, one off-plot sound | Interior, memory | Pillow shot, season, negative space | Downtime crafting, inventory |
| Explicit | Clinical and layered | Body | Sound words | Rarely used |
| System beat | Does not exist | Rare | Breakthrough as spectacle | Status block, notification |

### 3.3 Rotation

The census finds the same three or four groups carrying most scenes (Bodies, Built world interiors, Knowledge and record, Magic, with War when there is a fight). Rotation means a scene, or a run of scenes, is pushed to range. Options, from light to firm:

1. **Lead and shade.** Each scene names one lead group from Part 1 and two shade groups; the lead rotates across a thread.
2. **Spread floor.** Every scene touches at least N groups (five is a natural number for a 3,500-word turn), one of them from a thin list (Part 4).
3. **No triple.** The same lead group may not lead three scenes running in one thread.
4. **Thread census.** Session end lists the groups touched and the ones not touched in K scenes; the next scene brief pulls from the untouched.
5. **Field-driven.** Add Inventory fields for the missing groups (Part 4); the two-item quota (R53-19) then naturally pulls from a wider list.

### 3.4 Culture-keying

Each culture notices different things first, and the blend can key a technique to a culture as well as a subject. What the Inventories already imply:

The Eastern strata with no Inventory cannot be keyed: **Mahuo and Hon-guk** (the Korean register; Kwon Mu-jin's thread), **the Chinese lineage halls**, the **Northern English and Norse chartered families** (Greymane, Stark, Hallenfeld; the Accord sheet covers institutions, not households), **Sancta Lux and the Holy Sea**, and **the far-northern tundra peoples** beyond Kharven. R53-20 says a missing Inventory is drafted before writing; for the blend these are the cultures whose description would carry the Korean and Chinese eye.

### 3.5 Ways to enforce variety

| Mechanism | How it works | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Checklist in the scene brief** | `scene_brief` asks for the lead group, two shades and one thin-list subject before drafting | Cheap, no checker; relies on discipline |
| **Rotation quota** | N groups per scene and one thin-list group, logged at session end | Reliable spread; can read mechanical if N is high |
| **verify_scene census** | A keyword lexicon per group (the house census already prototypes one); WARN when a scene touches fewer than N groups or one group takes more than a set share | Visible and repeatable; keyword counts are rough and false positives will need tuning. Today the checker only counts Kharven's recurrence items (R53-19, R49-54) |
| **Thread census** | `recurrence_report` or `session_end` lists groups untouched for K scenes | Catches drift across a thread without nagging a single scene |
| **Inventory expansion** | New fields so the missing groups have a source | Fixes supply at the root; costs a drafting pass per culture (R53-29: looked up, not invented) |
| **Ear only** | No rule; Natalie ranges by judgement | Zero friction; the census shows it has not happened on its own |

## Questions this research hands the questionnaire

1. **Range rule.** Should description range be law (a lead group per scene, a spread floor, a no-triple rule, a thread census), or stay by ear? Recommendation: lead and shade in the scene brief plus a thread census at session end; a hard per-scene floor reads mechanical.
2. **Inventory template.** Add the template fields above (all, some, or none)? Recommendation: all nine, drafted culture by culture as each next appears in a scene, under R53-20 and R53-29.
3. **Missing cultures.** Draft Mahuo and Hon-guk and the lineage halls first, since the Eastern blend depends on them?
4. **Eastern techniques to adopt by name.** Which of far-to-near (D1), the scroll walk (D2), the pillow shot (D6), jo-ha-kyū (D7), feeling-scene fusion (D13), the seasonal anchor (C3), the poetic insert (C2), sound words beyond explicit scenes (C5), the name-announcement (B11)?
5. **The onlooker.** Does the explaining crowd of cultivation and shōnen fiction (B6) get its own allowance outside the four-voices cap of two per engagement?
6. **The system's page form.** Status block, notification, item card, monster entry, number against number (E1 to E7): which exist on the page, diegetic or not, and at which placement (threshold, mid-action, aftermath)?
7. **Density by mode.** Should density vary by scene type as in 3.2, with a system beat lighter on senses and heavier on readout?
8. **Culture-keyed eye.** Should each Inventory name what its people notice first and what they never notice, so a POV's description is keyed to culture as well as to character?
9. **Senses field.** Add Temperature, Motion and the inner body to the five senses?
10. **Enforcement.** Extend verify_scene from the Kharven-only recurrence count to a subject-group census for every culture, as WARN only?

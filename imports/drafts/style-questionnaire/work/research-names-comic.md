# Comic and punning names: working craft for WOTR

Research note, 2026-10-03, for the NAMES section of the style questionnaire. Isaac's third message asks for names, "a lot of them" funny puns or plain fantasy names, "like Borin and Snorin". This note covers comic naming in the Western and Eastern traditions the new blend draws on (mechanism, how it lands, who carries it, how it fails), then a toolkit of thirteen techniques with invented WOTR examples, and whether the joke lives inside the world or only in the reader's ear. Every invented name is a proposal: under R50-28 it is logged with its scene and becomes canon only if Isaac reuses it. Nothing here touches Sodoku Moto or any character belonging to another creator (Haruki, Dova'Kan, Gorgi, Ma'Kovu, Fushigi, Xhem, Sonzai), and none of their names is a candidate for change.

## Where WOTR stands now

**The cast's dominant sound is high fantasy.** 107 of the 284 Volume I card names carry a V-plus-vowel or "ae" cluster (Vaelith, Vaelthorn, Virellith). A comic or plain name stands out against that wall, which is half its use.

**WOTR already does the comic epithet, quietly.** Gorrum Vaelseth is Toad Guy, Dorrik is the Pot Man, Sadamu is called Futakoto ("Two Words"), and the cards carry a Ribbon-Ox, a Mushroom Fox and a Drunken Oni. What is missing is the comic given name, the rhyming sibling set, and the soldiers' nickname as a habit.

**The Dawi canon already contains Isaac's joke.** The Runic Dawi tongue-page says the language has no voiced stops (b, d, g): Borin is really Porin, Kegshard is Kekshart, and Concord speakers voice the stops without noticing. "Borin", where an English ear can hear "boring", is itself the Concord mishearing. The pun Isaac made sits in canon phonology.

Live rules that bear on this: R50-01 (borrowed names correct, glossed truly), R50-17 and R50-29 (sound-alike names become a beat), R50-27 (a new person is a role until they speak twice), R50-31 (romanised only), R50-33 (bent pronunciation spelled as heard), R49-38 (no setup-deadpan-reaction), R52-14 (Sodoku jokes only by accident).

## Part One: the traditions

### 1. The Dvergatal and the Norse byname

**Mechanism: rhyme, alliteration, and the earned nickname.** The Dvergatal ("tally of dwarfs") is stanzas 10 to 16 of the Völuspá in most editions, a thula (an Old Norse versified list of names, a memory catalogue) that Snorri repeats in Gylfaginning. Tolkien took most of Thorin's company from it: Thorin, Fili, Kili, Bifur, Bofur, Bombur, Dori, Nori, Ori, Gloin, Dwalin, and also Gandalf (Gandálfr, "wand-elf") and Oakenshield (Eikinskjaldi). The list chimes because Norse verse alliterates: Fíli rhymes with Kíli, Bífurr, Báfurr and Bömburr run on B, Nýr stands beside Nýráðr, Vitr beside Litr, and four dwarfs are simply North, South, East and West. Real dynasties did it; in Beowulf, Healfdene's sons are Heorogar, Hrothgar and Halga, all on H, because royal names alliterated by custom. The sagas hand out bynames (viðurnefni, the nickname after a name) that are openly comic and worn by kings: Ketil Flatnose, Onund Treefoot, and Halfdan the Mild and the Stingy-with-Food, generous with gold and mean with dinner.

**How it lands.** The chime reads as family habit, not whimsy: The Hobbit's dwarfs arrive in rhyming pairs, and nobody doubts they are real. **Who carries it:** companies, crews, kin met as a group. **How it fails:** a set longer than the reader's patience, or a chime in a culture that has no reason to rhyme.

### 2. Plautus, Jonson and the Restoration: the name that tells

**Mechanism: meaning.** Plautus's braggart soldier is Pyrgopolynices ("tower-city-conqueror"), and his hanger-on Artotrogus ("bread-nibbler"). Rome's real cognomina (the third name, usually a nickname that hardened into a lineage) are the same joke gone solemn: Cicero is "chickpea", Naso "nose", Strabo "squint", Balbus "stammerer", and Bibulus, Caesar's co-consul, "the drinker". Jonson's Volpone (1606) names its Venetian predators in Italian: Volpone the fox, Mosca the fly, Voltore the vulture, Corvino the crow; The Alchemist (1610) has Subtle, Face and Sir Epicure Mammon. The Restoration kept going: Sir Fopling Flutter, Horner, Pinchwife, Lady Wishfort ("wish for it"), and later Sheridan's Mrs Malaprop.

**How it lands.** Volpone is the model WOTR wants: the joke is in the setting's own tongue, glossed for the audience, and the characters say the names straight. Cicero shows the other half: a joke becomes a lineage, and Rome's gravest orator was Chickpea without anyone laughing. **Who:** villains, fools, frauds; in Rome, everyone. **Fails:** the tag-name announces a whole character on entrance and flattens him to one trait.

### 3. Bunyan and the Puritans: the sentence as a name

**Mechanism: allegorical compound.** The Pilgrim's Progress (1678) runs on it: Mr Worldly Wiseman, Giant Despair, Talkative, Mr By-ends of Fair-speech, Mr Facing-both-ways, and a jury with Mr No-good and Mr Hate-light on it. Bunyan did not invent the habit. Puritan parents gave hortatory names (names that exhort), and Sussex parish registers of the 1580s and 1590s record children baptised Sorry-for-sin and Fly-fornication. Praise-God Barebone sat in the 1653 Parliament that was nicknamed after him.

**How it lands.** As satire through excess, and as history because a sect believed a name was a sermon; a WOTR sect that names children as precepts would be grim, since the Puritans meant it. **Fails:** in narration the allegorical name tells the reader what to think, which is narrator adjudication by other means.

### 4. Dickens and the Victorian comic name

**Mechanism: meaning and sound, built to pass as a surname.** Gradgrind and his schoolmaster M'Choakumchild in Hard Times; Pumblechook, pompous in the very consonants; Uriah Heep; Mr Bumble the beadle; Wackford Squeers, who whacks and squeezes; Sir Leicester Dedlock in a novel about a deadlocked lawsuit. The trick: real English surnames are already this strange. Shakespeare, Wagstaff, Breakspear, Gotobed, Drinkwater, Golightly and Doolittle are all real, a class of phrase surnames (a verb and its object turned into a family name). English also makes pet names by rhyme (hypocoristics, the affectionate short forms): Rob gave Bob, Hob and Dob, Will gave Bill, Meg gave Peg, Molly gave Polly.

**How it lands.** One notch past plausible: the reader suspects a joke and cannot prove it. It is the native register of the Victorian-imperial Draw Age. **Who:** everyone but the hero (Pip and Oliver are plain); villains get the best ones. **Fails:** three notches past (M'Choakumchild is pure satire and cannot stand in a grimdark room).

### 5. Terry Pratchett

**Mechanism: the pun that is also a plausible name.** Nobby Nobbs, Fred Colon, Mustrum Ridcully and Ponder Stibbons could all sit in a parish register. Cohen the Barbarian inverts Conan into an old man with troll-diamond teeth. Cut-Me-Own-Throat Dibbler is a sales pitch that became a name. Ankh-Morpork's map is jokes that work as places: Treacle Mine Road, the Street of Cunning Artificers, the Mended Drum, a Guild of Seamstresses that does not sew. The best device puts the joke in a culture's own system: troll names are minerals (Detritus, Chrysoprase, Mica), and Carrot, a human raised by dwarfs, has the dwarf name Kzad-bhat, glossed as "Head-banger", a compliment in dwarfish and a joke in English.

**How it lands.** The characters take their names seriously; the joke is ours, the dignity is theirs. **Fails:** density. Discworld is comedy first; grimdark cannot spend at that rate.

### 6. Warhammer

**Mechanism: deed-names, sound-comedy, and cultures whose naming is the joke.** The word grimdark comes from Warhammer 40,000's own tagline, and the setting is openly funny. Gotrek Gurnisson's companion Felix Jaeger is Felix ("lucky") Jäger ("hunter"), the lucky hunter who survives everything. Snorri Nosebiter, a Slayer with nails hammered into his skull, sits in the Norse byname line. Ork names are sound: Ghazghkull Mag Uruk Thraka, Mad Dok Grotsnik, Kaptin Badrukk, Wazdakka Gutsmek. Skaven names (Queek Headtaker, Ikit Claw) come with doubled speech ("yes-yes"). Sandy Mitchell's Ciaphas Cain, a coward with a hero's reputation, runs the inversion for a whole series.

**How it lands.** The comic people are lethal, so laughter and fear arrive together. **Who:** orks, skaven, goblins, Slayers, comic leads. **Fails:** a comic species that is only comic loses its teeth.

### 7. Joe Abercrombie

**Mechanism: the soldier's nickname, literal or ironic.** Logen Ninefingers counts his own missing finger. Black Dow, Rudd Threetrees, Harding Grim (who barely talks), Forley the Weakest (who is), the Dogman (who smells trouble), Whirrun called Cracknut. In his North a Named Man is a rank: a name is earned in blood.

**How it lands.** Peers give the names, so the joke belongs to the characters, and soldiers' gallows wit is already grimdark-legal. When Forley the Weakest dies, the name does not make it smaller; it makes it worse. **Who:** soldiers, war-bands, veterans. **Fails:** a nickname cleverer than the soldiers who coined it.

### 8. Monty Python and Douglas Adams

**Mechanism: inversion and sound.** Tim the Enchanter (a mighty sorcerer with a clerk's name), Brave Sir Robin who runs away, Roger the Shrubber; Life of Brian's Romans carry schoolboy Latin jokes. Adams named Ford Prefect after a car, Hotblack Desiato after a London estate agency, and built Slartibartfast by starting from something rude and changing letters until it could be broadcast.

**How it lands.** Through parody: the whole world is a joke. **Fails, for WOTR:** the names point outside the world and break the timeless register. Take the inversion and the sound; leave the reference.

### 9. LitRPG and progression fantasy

**Mechanism: the system as comedian, and the descriptive soldier-name.** Dungeon Crawler Carl (Matt Dinniman) hands the jokes to the game: a snide system AI hands out achievements and titles with open contempt, and a show cat called Princess Donut collects titles like pageant sashes. The Wandering Inn (pirateaba) names its goblin warband the Redfang Five: Headscratcher, Numbtongue, Rabbiteater, Badarrow, Shorthilt. They are funny names, and three of the five die on the page in some of the series' most mourned scenes. Omniscient Reader's Viewpoint (Korean, by Sing Shong) names its hero Kim Dokja, which reads as "reader" and as "only son", and gives its watching gods (the constellations) modifier-titles such as Prisoner of the Golden Headband.

**How it lands.** Titles are an in-world voice, so the system can sneer where narration may not; the Redfang Five prove a comic name survives tragedy once the reader has lived with the person. **Fails:** the system's snark becomes the author's voice.

### 10. Japan

**Mechanism: the pivot, the suffix, the gloss, and the clan that names absurdly.** Kakekotoba (a pivot word: one word read two ways in the same line) is classical: in waka poetry matsu is "pine" and "to wait", nagame is "long rains" and "gazing in reverie". The rakugo story Jugemu (rakugo: the seated comic monologue) has parents give a son every auspicious name a priest suggests, and the joke is the length; some retellings leave the boy down a well while his friends are still reciting it. Suffixes turn a trait into a name: -bō and -suke make kuishinbō (glutton) and nebosuke (sleepyhead) sound like boys. Ateji (characters picked for sound, not meaning) let a name be written as one joke and read as another, and kirakira ("glittering") names show the edge: in 1993 a registry refused a father who tried to name his son Akuma, "devil". Manga mines all of it. Naruto is the fishcake swirl in ramen (naruto-maki); One Piece's Usopp is uso, "a lie" (and Aesop), the Charlotte children are desserts, and the admirals Akainu, Aokiji and Kizaru are the dog, pheasant and monkey of the Momotarō tale. Gintama bends real Shinsengumi names one sound off (Hijikata Toshizō becomes Toshirō) and makes a refrain of a nickname its owner always rejects. Konosuba puts the joke in a culture: the Crimson Demon clan find names like Megumin, Yunyun and Komekko normal and rather cool, and take offence when outsiders laugh.

**How it lands.** The joke is Japanese, glossed for the foreigner; Konosuba's clan is the model of a people who name absurdly and mean it. **Fails:** an English pun on a Japonic name, or a "funny" name that is wrong Japanese (R50-01).

### 11. China

**Mechanism: homophone, the cheap name, the outlaw nickname, the parody.** Xiehouyu (a two-part allegorical saying: a picture, then a punchline the listener completes, often a homophone) is the folk engine: "a nephew carrying a lantern: zhao jiu", lighting his uncle, which sounds like "same as before"; "Confucius moving house: jing shi shu", nothing but books, which sounds like "nothing but losses". Rural cheap names (jianming: a lowly name that fools the spirits who take precious children) like Gousheng, "dog's leftovers", rest on the saying that a cheap name is easy to raise. Water Margin's 108 outlaws carry nicknames, some grand (Timely Rain), some comic (Flea on a Drum). Generation poems (zibei: each generation takes the next character of a family poem) give cousins one shared syllable. The web-novel age added parody: Long Aotian ("dragon proud-sky") is the meme name for the cliché overpowered hero; in Scum Villain's Self-Saving System the hack author's pen name, Airplane Shooting Towards the Sky, hides crude slang only a Chinese reader hears; in Beware of Chicken the transmigrated hero names his rooster and boar Big D and Chunky, which the locals hear as the dignified Bi De and Chun Ke.

**How it lands.** Beware of Chicken is the cleanest two-ear joke in print: dignified inside, silly outside, with one character as the bridge. **Fails:** a pun that needs the written character to land, which WOTR cannot show (R50-31).

### 12. Korea

**Mechanism: the generation syllable, the ugly child-name, the endless name, the pivot.** Dollimja (the generation syllable all cousins of one generation share) produces sound-sets the way zibei does. Child-names (amyeong) like Gaettong-i, "dog dung", kept spirits off a precious child. The Kim Suhanmu routine is Korean comedy's own endless-name gag, a boy's name strung from every long-life word available, in the Jugemu shape. Kim Dokja is the modern pivot.

**How it lands and fails:** as Japan and China. The protective ugly name is the strongest grimdark-and-funny device in this survey: the joke exists because children die.

## Part Two: how comic naming fails in grimdark

**The winking narrator.** The narration points at the pun ("his name, appropriately, was Snorin"). That is the setup-deadpan-reaction beat R49-38 already bans, moved into the name. The fix: let a character laugh, glare, or pointedly not laugh; the narrator uses the name like any other.

**The death-scene test.** A comic name on someone who will die needs a person behind it first. The Redfang Five and Forley pass because the reader knows them; a pun-named walk-on who dies in the same scene turns the death into a gag.

**The English pun in a non-English culture.** A Ketsuen dockhand funny only in English is the author leaning into the frame; under R50-01 a Japonic joke must be a Japonic joke.

**The fake culture.** Tikki Tikki Tembo (1968), an American picture book, dressed the Jugemu joke in an invented Chinese setting with nonsense "Chinese" names; it is the standard example of what not to do.

**Density.** Grimdark spends comic names like salt. A scene where every walk-on is a pun becomes a sketch.

**The outside reference.** A car, a brand, a celebrity, an Earth day or month name (already banned): anything the reader must leave the world to get.

**The wrong target.** Never Isaac's PC or other creators' characters; Sodoku jokes only by accident (R52-14).

## Part Three: the toolkit

Each technique names its source, then gives two invented WOTR examples (proposals only).

**1. The chime.** Siblings or comrades share an element and vary the onset (Dvergatal, Healfdene's sons, zibei, dollimja).
- Dawi: the Kekshart cooper brothers Snorin, Torin and Korin, given names built on one shared second element of the compound inventory. No voiced stops, so they are legal Dawi. When they meet Borin, R50-17 makes it a beat; what Borin makes of it is his card's business.
- Northern English commons: the Garth twins, both christened Robert because neither parent would give up the name, and entered on the muster as Hob and Dob.

**2. The foreign ear.** The pun is made by mishearing (Hobson-Jobson, the Anglo-Indian glossary named for British soldiers' English of a Muharram cry; the trenches calling Ypres "Wipers"; canon's Borin for Porin). R50-33 already spells a bent pronunciation as heard.
- On any Guild form the cooper brothers become Snorin, Dorin and Gorin, because Concord voices the stops. Snorin has no stop to voice and stays himself, which is a joke no Dawi will ever hear.
- Kharven riders call every Accord gate clerk Solly Transit, after the Latin they hear at every gate: "Solutum et transitum" (paid and passed).

**3. The deed-byname.** An earned nickname, literal or ironic (Norse bynames, Abercrombie, Slayers). The Dawi byname slot is already rendered in English (Ironheart), so a comic byname is legal in every culture that has the slot.
- Dawi: Rakko Twelvecasks. Every outsider takes it for a boast; it counts the casks he soured, and the hold enters it with a straight face.
- Kharven: Ulf Two-Bowls, who took a second bowl in the Thin Weeks. The name is the accusation, and the laughter stops when he walks in.

**4. The talking surname.** One notch past plausible, a nature or a trade in the sound (Dickens).
- Draw-district baker: Tobias Shortloaf, of a family fined under the assize so often that the fine became the name.
- Guild gauge-house: Morwen Pinchgauge, an inspector whose readings always come out a hair under the meter.

**5. The phrase name.** A verb and its object (Shakespeare, Gotobed, Bunyan, Dibbler).
- Northern English: the Breakseals, three generations of honest gaugers stopped and searched at every gate for their name.
- Draw-district commons: Kit Burnoil, of a family that has never held a draw licence and wears the fact like a badge.

**6. The register name.** The clerk's guess, frozen (canon's Far-Northern roll-names off the nearest legible thing, Ice and Foss; real surname decrees, like the Habsburg one of 1787, left a body of stories of officials handing out insulting names).
- A sealer the survey entered as Kettle, because the clerk pointed at what he was holding and wrote down the answer. His household has never once used it.
- The Ignotus family of the Keld: the clerk wrote ignotus, "unknown", in the name column, and four generations carry it in the Accord's best Latin.

**7. The cheap name.** Ugliness as protection (Chinese jianming, Korean amyeong, the Ashkenazi custom of renaming a dangerously sick child Alter, "old one", to confuse the Angel of Death).
- Kharven: a sickly girl called Wet Wood through her first winter, on the logic that the cold does not want what will not burn. Her carried name waits until she lives.
- Chinese-stratum hall: an elder whose older sister still calls him Gousheng, "dog's leftovers", at seventy, in front of the whole hall.

**8. The glossed joke.** Funny in the in-world tongue, glossed once in a mouth (Volpone, Cicero, Kzad-bhat).
- Dawi: a shift-hand the hold calls Kurlo, "unbegun", because he never starts anything. Concord clerks file it as his name and think it sounds noble.
- Accord: Magister Aulus Tardipes, "slow-foot" in correct Latin (the poets' epithet for the lame smith-god), inspector of couriers.

**9. The pivot.** One name, two readings (kakekotoba, Kim Dokja).
- Mahuo: the house secretary Seo-gi, whose name means "auspicious foundation" in its characters and is said exactly like the Korean word for secretary. He has held the post forty years.
- Ketsuen: a widow named Matsu, "pine" and "wait". The pivot is not funny, which is the warning: the same tool cuts both ways.

**10. The name-shaped word.** A common word with the shape of a real name, so outsiders write it down (nebosuke, Fantong).
- Ketsuen: a gate sentry everyone calls Nebosuke, "sleepyhead". The -suke makes it sound like an ordinary boy's name, and his real one appears on a single document.
- Chinese-stratum hall: a cook nicknamed Fantong, "rice bucket", the word for a useless eater, which a Concord factor once wrote on a contract as his legal name.

**11. The inversion.** The name says the opposite (Tim the Enchanter, Cohen, Forley the Weakest, Little John).
- Kharven: Kitten, the biggest man on the Seat's wall, named by men who have all since stopped saying it to his face.
- Accord: a pamphlet hero billed as Fortissimus, "the strongest", who faints at blood and appears in every story the Guild prints.

**12. The title stack.** Titles that read heroic and say something else (Dungeon Crawler Carl, the constellations, Jugemu, the Crimson Demon self-introduction).
- A Measurewright's reading that lists a man as Survivor of the Ninth Gauge Breach, Twice Unwounded, First Through the Gate, and does not say which way he went through it.
- Eresse: a minor noble whose full style takes a broth to recite, so the Kharven call him the Broth.

**13. The sound joke.** Plosives, doubled syllables, the comedy-writer's rule of thumb (stated in Neil Simon's The Sunshine Boys) that words with a k sound are funny; reduplication (a word said twice).
- Goblin, Old Vaross: Kadukadu, "hand-hand", a fence whose name is the only honest thing about him (kadu is the canon root for hand).
- Goblin: the sapper twins Gripsnick and Gripsnack, because a goblin speaker adds consonants to a name and nobody adds them the same way twice.

## Part Four: inside the world, or only in the reader's ear

Every comic name has two audiences, the people in the scene and Isaac, which gives four cases.

**Funny to both.** Kzad-bhat, Kurlo, Two-Bowls. The best case: a character can laugh, glare, or refuse to.

**Funny inside, plain to the reader until glossed.** Volpone, Cicero, Fantong. It needs one gloss, delivered by one of the four explaining voices (a Kharven sergeant, a clerk, the opponent), never by narration on its own authority.

**Funny to the reader, invisible inside.** Snorin to a Dawi, Big D to a villager. It is legal only when the world supplies a bridge, and WOTR has three. First, Concord Common is what the page renders as English, so an English pun is a real in-world pun in any Concord mouth. Second, bynames are translated on the page (Ironheart, Ashmantle), so an English comic byname is the honest rendering of a native one, provided the native meaning matches (R50-01). Third, a Concord ear in the room (Borin for Porin) makes the pun inside the story. Without a bridge, it is a wink.

**Funny to neither.** The joke has died, as Cicero's did. This is where a comic name must arrive before it can carry grief: the reader may still smile, but nobody in the room does.

Four working rules follow.

1. **Let the culture do the naming.** A Dawi given name comes from a fixed two-element inventory, so rhyme-sets are a Dawi fact, not whimsy; generation syllables do the same for the Chinese-stratum halls and the Mahuo house; the Northern commons have rhyming pet forms and phrase surnames, the Far North its roll-names, the Accord its body-joke cognomina.
2. **A joke the first time, a person by the third.** R50-27 already makes a new face a role until they speak twice; the soldiers' nickname is the natural bridge from role to name.
3. **Gloss once, in a mouth.** Never in narration, never twice.
4. **Pick the target.** Commons, soldiers, clerks, villains and comic peoples carry the names; Isaac's PC and other creators' characters never do.

## Part Five: candidate questions for the questionnaire

1. **How many?** (a) a seasoning, about one per scene; (b) most soldiers and commons; (c) anyone below the named cast; (d) Pratchett density.
2. **Whose ear?** (a) in-world jokes only; (b) English puns with a bridge; (c) English puns anywhere, Snorin included.
3. **Who may carry them?** (a) walk-ons and soldiers; (b) plus villains; (c) plus major NPCs; (d) anyone except Isaac's PC and other creators' characters.
4. **Body and toilet bynames,** the Norse kind: in, or out?
5. **Rhyme-sets:** a Dawi custom for the Inventory, or open to every culture's own convention?
6. **LitRPG titles:** may the system's titles mock their holders, as Dungeon Crawler Carl's do?
7. **Existing cards:** may NPCs give named cast soldiers' nicknames in play (never a rename)?

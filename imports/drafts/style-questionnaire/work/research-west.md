# The Western side of the blend: working craft for a WOTR style questionnaire

Research note, 2026-10-03. Scope: the Western writing traditions a Western-Eastern-LitRPG blend for WOTR would keep, sharpen or trade away. Each entry gives (1) what it is, (2) the techniques on the page, (3) one line on how it could look in WOTR, (4) what it collides with in grimdark taste or in WOTR's live rules. Rule ids are from `out/rules.live.full.md` as of today. No source text is reproduced; techniques are described in my own words.

A note on where the rules already stand, because several collisions turn on it. R51-16 has already freed stat names, Sub-Stat names and sheet vocabulary in narration. R48-09 lets narration use technical words on its own authority. What still holds: R47-7 (tiers and ladder rungs written by name, never numbered), the canon rule "never invent a number", R49-22 (weather and landscape described, not personified), R48-06 (at most two long sentences in a row; the hit is the shortest sentence), R49-08 (neutral narrator summaries banned), R48-40 (POV lock in written scenes), R48-13 (modern words in speech only), and the AI-tells list in NATALIE.md (no em dashes, no hypophora, no narrator moral adjudication, no setup-deadpan-reaction).

## 1. George R. R. Martin, *A Song of Ice and Fire*

**What it is.** Epic fantasy built from rotating close-third chapters: each chapter belongs to one named viewpoint character and is titled with that name.

**On the page.** Close third person (narration that stays inside one character's perceptions and vocabulary while still saying "he" or "she"), locked for the whole chapter. Each POV has a private lexicon: one sees bloodlines and slights, another sees songs and gowns. Inventories as worldbuilding: feasts listed course by course, tournament fields read as a roll of blazons (formal heraldic descriptions: field colour, charge, tincture, e.g. "a black stag on gold"), house words as compressed ideology. Prologue and epilogue go to a disposable POV who usually dies, so the reader sees the threat the cast cannot. Chapters end on a hook: a reveal, a blow, a line of dialogue that turns the situation, then the book cuts to someone else and makes you wait. Violence is sudden and often happens to the character the reader trusted to survive. Magic is rare, rumoured, mostly offstage until it is not.

**In WOTR.** A Kharven-Seat book in chapters headed LAMBERT, BRIDA, HILD, each closing on the line that tilts the next chapter, with the Seat's feast for a visiting Hallenfeld captain given dish by dish: windmeat, the skin, seal fat, and which bowl went first to whom.

**Collides with.** Little; WOTR's R49-26 endings (action, image or line) and R48-44 POV epithets are already Martin-shaped. The tension is pace: Martin's inventories stall the plot, and the Table Rule "never resolve a multi-decision action in one go" fights his habit of summarising a battle in a paragraph of hearsay.

## 2. Joe Abercrombie, *The First Law* and *The Heroes*

**What it is.** Grimdark's house style: anti-heroic fantasy with the narration soaked in the viewpoint character's own voice.

**On the page.** Free indirect style (third-person narration that borrows the character's own idiom, so his opinions and swears sit in sentences the narrator speaks). Each POV has refrains and pet sayings that recur like a chorus. Profane gallows wit (humour from people whose work is death). Bathos (a lofty build punctured by something low: a speech ruined by a fart, a hero who trips). Action as confused, close and unheroic: breath, mud, a missed swing, then a disgusting wound. In *The Heroes*, a battle chapter passes the viewpoint from one dying soldier to the man who killed him, then to the next, a relay of deaths that tours the field. Interior italics used heavily for the sardonic inner voice. Magic is rare, monstrous and ruinous to the user.

**In WOTR.** A Hallenfeld quartermaster's chapter where the narration counts barrels the way he does and calls the Moto "the eyes" because he will not think their name; the death-relay used across a Kharven wall fight, each man's last thought handing to the next.

**Collides with.** Fits R48-12 (profanity in close POV), R49-05 (italic thought often), R48-11 (humour wherever earned). The collision is NATALIE's ban on hypophora (a question the narrator asks and then answers): Glokta's inner voice is built on rhetorical question and answer. The relay also breaks the written-scene POV lock (R48-40) unless each hand-off is a marked break (R49-27). Grimdark taste itself has tired of the deflating joke; the setup-deadpan-reaction beat is already banned.

## 3. J. R. R. Tolkien, *The Lord of the Rings*

**What it is.** The root of Western epic fantasy: a long walk across a mapped world with deep history behind every name.

**On the page.** Landscape as route: distance, slope, rock type, which way the water runs, what grows where, the walking day measured in miles and meals. Register gradient: hobbits talk like rural England, kings like the King James Bible; the narration rises to archaic high style (inverted word order, "and" leading sentences, old words like "wain" and "mere") at mythic moments. Verse and song inserted whole: walking songs, laments, riddles, histories in ballad form. Names carry invented etymology, so a place has an Elvish name, a Mannish name and a hobbit nickname. Elegy (the mood of mourning something passing): the Elves leaving, the long defeat. Eucatastrophe (Tolkien's coined term: the sudden joyous turn when all seemed lost). Appendices give genealogies, calendars and annals. Magic is mostly understated, felt as atmosphere, not explained.

**In WOTR.** A Kharven ride to the Seat told as route: frozen ruts, the miles a horse makes in snow (R53-08), the old courses at the base of the walls darker than the new stone, and the riders singing the grass-song for the steppe they no longer ride.

**Collides with.** R48-05 and R49-24 already allow high style and full elegy; R50-22 and R50-23 (places carry several names) are Tolkienian. Collision: grimdark taste distrusts eucatastrophe as unearned rescue; WOTR's "victories cost" and "big surprises only if foreshadowed" (R48-30) constrain it. His understated magic runs against Packs Twelve to Fourteen, which put mechanism on the page.

## 4. Steven Erikson, *Malazan Book of the Fallen*

**What it is.** A ten-volume military epic by an archaeologist: vast cast, deep time, gods on the battlefield.

**On the page.** In medias res (opening mid-action) with no orientation; the reader is trusted to catch up. Many POVs per chapter, switched at section breaks. The soldier's eye: sappers, marines and sergeants talk logistics, munitions and grievances; the war is seen from the ditch. Philosophical asides in soldiers' mouths: privates argue history, compassion and futility in long exchanges. Chapter epigraphs from invented in-world books, poems and histories. Convergence: many threads collide at one place for a huge climax. Magic is high-powered and explosive, Warrens (realms of power) named but rarely explained, with costs shown in exhaustion and madness.

**In WOTR.** An Accord siege where the gunners argue Wellspring theory over a cold stove (R48-24 lets characters argue the Four Theories), each chapter headed by two lines from a Guild assay manual or a Kharven grave-song.

**Collides with.** Erikson's undigested jargon and missing explanation fit R51-15 (meaning from context, no gloss) but clash with the Magic Frame's demand that mechanism be on the page. His head-switching inside chapters clashes with the POV lock. His asides can drift into the narrator moralising; in WOTR they must stay in a mouth.

## 5. Gene Wolfe, *The Book of the New Sun*

**What it is.** A far-future story that reads like a medieval memoir, told by Severian, a torturer who claims perfect memory.

**On the page.** Unreliable narrator (a teller whose account the reader cannot fully trust: he omits, misremembers, or lies). Withheld information: major facts are implied, never stated, and reread differently. Real obscure words from the dictionary (Greek, Latin, archaic English) used straight, so the far future reads as ancient. A frame claiming the text is "translated", with an appendix. Nested stories and plays told by characters. Violence and torture described with professional calm. Magic and technology indistinguishable, presented without explanation.

**In WOTR.** Kwon Mu-jin's written volumes as Wolfean memoir: a precise, learned first person who never quite says what he did in Sum-gol, the omission readable in what he dwells on instead.

**Collides with.** First person is outside WOTR's present habit (close third). Deliberate withholding fights the Magic Frame's mechanism-on-the-page and Isaac's "fair and a challenge" (counters must be findable). It fits the WOTR conventions of in-world documents, R48-42 lies and the confident wrong explanation as a voice.

## 6. Mervyn Peake, *Gormenghast*

**What it is.** A baroque fantasy of an enormous decaying castle ruled by ritual nobody remembers the reason for.

**On the page.** Painterly description (Peake was an illustrator): long periodic sentences (sentences that hold the main point until the end) loaded with texture, rot, dust, colour, light. Grotesque caricature: characters defined by one exaggerated physical feature, gait or habit, with Dickensian names (Steerpike, Swelter, Prunesquallor). The building as a character: rooms, roofs and corridors described like a landscape. Ritual as plot: the Earl's day is a list of meaningless ceremonies, and breaking one is an earthquake. Slow, comic, sinister. No magic at all; the strangeness is in the people and the stone.

**In WOTR.** The Moto court at Kharven-Seat as Gormenghast: the oath ceremony still performed with nothing underneath it since the Fusi Vā went, every clansman's eyes on the floor, one courtier who knows the forms are empty and exploits them.

**Collides with.** Peake's long sentences and adjective drifts strain R48-06 (no more than two long sentences in a row) and R49-13 (variance), though R49-16 lets adjectives stack by ear. Caricature fights the R52 voice law's demand for real wants and reasoning. Grimdark taste may read Peake as whimsy.

## 7. Dickens and the Victorian novel

**What it is.** The serial novel of 1830 to 1900 (Dickens, Thackeray, Eliot, Trollope): WOTR's own period of look.

**On the page.** Omniscient irony (a narrator who knows everything and comments wryly, often judging). Caricature by tag: a character fixed by one phrase, tic or object repeated every appearance. Names that describe (Gradgrind, Murdstone). Crowds and the city as organism: fog, mud, traffic and institutions described as one living system, sometimes in verbless sentence fragments piled up. Money everywhere: wills, debts, prices, wages, legacies drive plot. Institutions as monsters: a court case (Chancery in *Bleak House*), a workhouse, a bank. Serial cliffhangers (a monthly part ending on suspense). Thackeray's narrator as puppet-master addressing the reader; Eliot's essayistic asides on human nature.

**In WOTR.** The district main shut off at night and the narration taking the whole quarter at once: the standpipes going dry street by street, the gauge-housings ticking down, the poor edge lighting tallow while the rich side still hums (R53-02), and the price of a lamp-hour (R53-07).

**Collides with.** Strongly with WOTR's POV discipline: omniscience, reader-address and the narrator's moral verdicts break R49-08 and the ban on narrator moral adjudication. Verbless fog fragments are now legal (R49-15 frees concrete image fragments). Sentimentality (the dying child, the reformed miser) is anti-grimdark. Money, institutions and crowds fit R53 world texture exactly.

## 8. Cormac McCarthy, *Blood Meridian*

**What it is.** A Western of scalp hunters on the 1849 border, written in King James cadence.

**On the page.** Polysyndeton (chaining clauses with "and... and... and" instead of commas, giving a biblical, relentless rhythm). No quotation marks, few apostrophes, minimal dialogue tags. Archaic and technical words (saddlery, geology, theology) used without gloss. Landscape vast and indifferent, described with the same weight as men, so a massacre and a rock formation get equal sentences; the idea is often called the landscape's optical democracy (McCarthy's phrase). Violence reported flatly, at distance, in exact physical detail, with no interior reaction. Philosophy given to one monstrous orator, the judge, in long speeches. Interiority almost absent; the kid barely thinks on the page.

**In WOTR.** A Kharven raid told in one long "and" chain: and the horses came over the frozen ruts and the ice rang under them and the first man fell and the snow took his blood and went black.

**Collides with.** R48-06 (two long sentences in a row maximum) caps polysyndeton to occasional use. Absent interiority fights R48-39 (deep running interiority) and R49-03. The indifferent landscape is close to legal under R49-22 if it is described rather than personified. Missing quotation marks would break the checkers.

## 9. Hilary Mantel, *Wolf Hall*

**What it is.** Historical novels of Thomas Cromwell written in present tense, held right against his shoulder.

**On the page.** Present tense close third: the story happens now, with no retrospect. The pronoun "he" almost always means Cromwell, even when it confuses; in the sequel she clarifies with "he, Cromwell". Memory braided into the present without breaks: a smell sends him back to his father's forge mid-paragraph. Politics carried in dialogue: dry, quick, menacing, full of what is not said. Period detail (cloth, food, ledgers, ink) as lived, not displayed. The reader knows the historical ending; tension comes from how.

**In WOTR.** Lambert at a Seat council in present tense: the ledger under his hand, the forearm grips he counts as audits, and a memory of an Accord counting-house surfacing in one clause before the next lie arrives.

**Collides with.** Present tense already governs roleplay turns (R49-02); Mantel is the best Western model of how to do it for long stretches. Books stay past tense by the same rule, so Mantel-style books would need a ruling. Her braided memory fits R49-06 (flashbacks allowed).

## 10. Patrick O'Brian, the Aubrey-Maturin novels

**What it is.** Twenty novels of the Napoleonic navy, written in a pastiche of early-1800s English.

**On the page.** Period technical precision unglossed: sails, rigging, gunnery and navigation named exactly, and the reader swims. A naturalist POV (Maturin, physician and spy) who describes birds, beetles, opium and surgery with a scientist's eye. Dry humour: puns, understatement, social comedy at dinner. Food and music as texture: dishes named, the two friends playing violin and cello. Battles compressed and exact, then a long aftermath of surgery and repair. Big events sometimes happen between chapters and are only reported. Dialogue in period register.

**In WOTR.** Brida's surgery after a wall fight in O'Brian's manner: the third bowl (her shorthand for a Class III haemorrhage) named, every instrument named, and a dry joke about the patient's luck.

**Collides with.** Fits R48-09 (technical words on narration's own authority), R51-17 (bare jargon mid-action) and R48-38 (full aftermath). Offstage battles collide with the set-piece and combat-floor rules. Period-pastiche dialogue runs against the register repeal (R48-13 lets speech be modern), though as a chosen voice it is legal.

## 11. Susanna Clarke, *Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell*

**What it is.** A Regency (1810s England) novel about the return of English magic, written in pastiche of Austen and Dickens, with footnotes.

**On the page.** Well over a hundred footnotes citing invented histories, folklore and scholarly quarrels, some running for pages. Period spelling and diction ("shew", "chuse"). Omniscient ironic narrator with social comedy. Magic as scholarship, bureaucracy and politics (magicians at war over books and the government's use of them). Faerie as amoral and terrifying, its menace delivered politely. Wonders reported in a calm, sceptical voice.

**In WOTR.** A Guild report on a Wellspring site with footnotes from rival Measurewrights disputing the residue reading, one footnote recording a commoner's folk belief left uncorrected.

**Collides with.** Footnotes are a natural form for WOTR's in-world documents and the "document at a scene's threshold" voice; the Necrocursica footnote rulings (R61-53, R69-7) show the habit is already there. In scene prose, footnotes and the omniscient narrator break POV discipline and R49-08. Polite menace fits; the ironic narrator does not.

## 12. China Miéville, *Perdido Street Station* and the Bas-Lag books

**What it is.** The New Weird: urban industrial fantasy in a sprawling, filthy city-state where magic is a trade.

**On the page.** Baroque urban catalogues: streets, markets, sewers, railways, smells, trades, given in long lists of rare words. Thaumaturgy (magic) as engineering, with patents, experiments and industrial accidents. Xenobiology: species described anatomically (an insect-headed artist, bird-men). The Remade: criminals surgically rebuilt with machine or animal parts as punishment. Politics of labour: strikes, police, a corrupt parliament. Horror creatures that feed on dreams. Vocabulary ornate and Latinate.

**In WOTR.** A Draw Age district read as machine: conduit, gauge-housing, brass and slate, a Guild clerk stamping a licence while a Well-spawn (R53-06) is crated two streets over.

**Collides with.** Miéville's lexicon brushes R51-10's hard-banned slop words, and his narrator often lectures. His industrial ugliness is close to the R53-28 look (Victorian imperial) but R53-02 says no factory chimneys. His politics-of-labour lens fits "money before magic".

## 13. Guy Gavriel Kay, *Tigana*, *Under Heaven* and others

**What it is.** Quasi-historical fantasy: a real period (Byzantium, Moorish Spain, Tang China) given what Kay calls "a quarter turn" into invented names and a little magic.

**On the page.** Elegiac omniscience: the narration knows how things will end and says so. Prolepsis (a flash-forward inside a sentence: "years later she would remember this"). Brief excursions into a minor character's head for one scene, often to show the cost of the main plot on someone small. Restraint in magic: one or two numinous intrusions per book. Love, poetry, and court etiquette weighed heavily. *Under Heaven* is a Western writer writing Tang Chinese material, which makes it the nearest Western model and warning for WOTR's blend.

**In WOTR.** The fall of the Banner Houses told with one proleptic sentence: the man who knelt to Muken that winter would sell his banner for salt within the year.

**Collides with.** Prolepsis and omniscience break R49-08 and the POV lock; R49-09 allows only short marked cutaways. His restraint with magic fights mechanism-on-the-page. His method of taking history and bending it once is close to R53-26 (real history taken exactly, then bent one step).

## 14. Robin Hobb, the Farseer books

**What it is.** Fantasy told in first person by FitzChivalry, a royal bastard and assassin, looking back from old age.

**On the page.** Deep first-person interiority: emotional processing, regret, self-justification, slow relationships. Retrospective narrator who foreshadows his own mistakes. Chapter epigraphs from in-world histories and treatises. Magic intimate and felt: one magic is a mind-bond with animals, the other a telepathic hunger that can addict. Slow domestic texture: stables, kitchens, lessons. Violence rare but damaging, and trauma persists for books.

**In WOTR.** A Hild Ice chapter where a Moto reading through the eyes is felt as someone opening a door inside her, and she spends a page of evening on who might have walked through.

**Collides with.** First person is not current WOTR habit. Her slow pace fights R48-29 (skip dull stretches). Her persistent trauma fits the Ledger rule exactly.

## 15. Brandon Sanderson, *Mistborn* and *The Stormlight Archive*

**What it is.** Hard magic: magic with rules stated clearly enough that the reader can predict and solve with it. The Western cousin of Naruto's tactical read.

**On the page.** Sanderson's Laws (his essays, paraphrased): magic can solve a problem only as far as the reader understands it; limits are more interesting than powers; deepen what exists before adding more. Magic tied to physics (pushing on metal moves you if the metal is heavier). Training sequences where the rules are learned on the page. Fights as tactical puzzles readable step by step. The "Sanderson avalanche": many threads converging in a fast final act. Clean, transparent prose meant to vanish (he borrows Orwell's image of prose as a window pane). Clear moral stakes, low profanity.

**In WOTR.** A duel where both fighters' workings were shown earlier with their cost, so when one spends his last reserve on a Pressure surge the reader already knows what it leaves him.

**Collides with.** Fits Packs Twelve to Fourteen and R47 (costs, tells, counters as facts). Window-pane prose collides with R48-01 and R48-02 (lush, beauty from exact detail). Exposition fights the iceberg rule and R47-1 (abilities describe what they are, never how to use them). Clean morality is anti-grimdark.

## 16. Western LitRPG and progression fantasy

**What it is.** LitRPG ("literary role-playing game") is fiction where a game-like system (levels, stats, skills, classes) is visible on the page. The label was coined in 2013 at Russia's EKSMO publishing house; Western web serials made it a genre. Its cousins: GameLit (game-flavoured without hard screens) and progression fantasy (power growth as the spine, often borrowing from Chinese cultivation novels, with or without screens).

**On the page.** Status screens: boxed or bracketed blocks listing name, level, class, stats and skills. System notifications interrupting prose ("Level up!", "Skill gained"). Class names in square brackets inside sentences (*The Wandering Inn*). The System as a character: in *Dungeon Crawler Carl* a sardonic broadcast AI. Stat allocation as a decision scene. Skill descriptions with flavour text. Loot and inventory lists. Numbers as reward rhythm: a fight ends and the gains tick up. *Cradle* (Will Wight) shows Western progression without screens: advancement stages named, power felt and measured by characters.

**In WOTR.** A Measurewright's slate reading after a fight set as a boxed block in the gauge's own print, the numbers pulled from fow_line, read by a POV who knows what each line costs him.

**Collides with.** R47-7 (tiers and ladder rungs never numbered) and "never invent a number": every screen number must trace to the Stat Sheet or a card. NATALIE's Magic Frame says stat names belong "in a mouth, an instrument, a document, or a private count"; a diegetic (in-world) screen such as a gauge or assay card already fits, an author's floating screen does not. R51-16 already frees stat names in narration. Gamer slang breaks R48-13 in narration. Grimdark taste reads number-go-up as cost-free reward; the Ledger must make every gain cost something.

## 17. The Icelandic sagas

**What it is.** Thirteenth-century prose histories of Icelandic families (*Njáls saga*, *Egils saga*, *Grettis saga*).

**On the page.** Laconic understatement (litotes: saying less than you mean, so a mortal wound gets a dry remark; in *Grettis saga* a man speared through comments on the fashion for broad blades). Genealogies open chapters: who a man's father and grandfather were, where they farmed. Deeds, not feelings: emotion shown only in action and terse speech, never named. Feuds told as legal process: lawsuits, compensation, assemblies. Formulaic introductions ("There was a man named..."). Dreams and omens told flatly. Verse inserted as a character's own composed stanza.

**In WOTR.** A Kharven death told as saga: his father kept the third woodpile on the east slope; he said the cold was early this year, and died before the fire was lit.

**Collides with.** Fits "grief goes back to the root" (R52-26) and trained calm (R52-28). The ban on naming feeling fights R49-07 (the POV may name emotion in his own word) and deep interiority. Grimdark taste loves it; the risk is turns too short for R49-30's 3,500 words.

## 18. Homer and Malory

**What it is.** Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey* (oral epic) and Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur* (1470s Arthurian prose).

**On the page.** Epithets (a fixed descriptive tag attached to a name: swift-footed, grey-eyed). Catalogues: ships, heroes, arms listed at length. Extended (Homeric) similes: a long comparison to farm, sea or hunt that opens a window out of the battle. Arming scenes, piece by piece. The small obituary: a minor warrior killed gets a line of father, home and what he will never do. Ekphrasis (a long description of a made object, like a shield showing a whole world). Malory: parataxis (clauses set side by side with "and", without subordination), formula combat, marvels reported flatly, chapter rubrics summarising what follows.

**In WOTR.** Renard Greymane armed piece by piece before the wall, the grey banner described as ekphrasis, and the first man he kills given his father's name and the woodpile he left half-split.

**Collides with.** R49-10 (one epithet per character per scene) allows epithets but not Homer's repetition. Extended similes fit R49-18 and R49-19 if drawn from the POV's life. Catalogues and formula combat fight the combat technical floor and R48-33's traced exchanges.

## 19. The gothic

**What it is.** The tradition from Walpole's *Castle of Otranto* (1764) through Radcliffe, Shelley, Poe, Le Fanu and Stoker: fear, ruins, inheritance, the past returning.

**On the page.** Architecture of dread: vaults, corridors, locked rooms. The sublime (awe mixed with terror at vastness). Radcliffe's distinction, paraphrased: terror (anticipation) enlarges the mind; horror (the thing seen) freezes it. Found documents and epistolary form (the story told in letters, diaries, records: *Dracula* uses phonograph cylinders and typed transcripts). Inheritance and curse. The uncanny (the familiar made strange). The explained supernatural (Radcliffe) versus the real one (Stoker).

**In WOTR.** A Well-spawn hunt told through a Guild clerk's log, a surgeon's case notes and a telegram, the new instruments of the Imperial Age recording something old.

**Collides with.** Dread by withholding fights mechanism-on-the-page; the explained supernatural is close to WOTR's law that everything has a traceable cause. The epistolary form fits documents and R53-01's full Imperial Age technology.

## 20. Western description conventions

**The entrance inventory.** A character's first appearance gets a full physical catalogue (hair, face, body, clothes, wear, marks). Already law: Table Rule 11 and R49-28.

**Pathetic fallacy.** Ruskin's 1856 term for weather and nature mirroring feeling (rain at funerals). Collides with R49-22 (the world described, not personified), though weather chosen to rhyme with mood without being personified is legal; R53-09 requires the actual weather anyway.

**The telling detail.** Chekhov's advice, paraphrased: one exact particular (light on a broken bottle) does more than a paragraph of general description; his "gun" principle says a detail shown must pay off. Fits R48-01.

**The objective correlative.** T. S. Eliot's term: a set of objects or events that evokes an emotion without naming it. Close to "cruelty felt, not explained".

**Sensory layering.** Several senses in one beat, smell often first. Already WOTR law (R48-02, senses banks).

**The set piece.** A long, built scene with its own architecture: approach, escalation, climax, aftermath. WOTR's R48-46 5,000-word floor.

## What this tradition describes that the other side tends not to

The contrast is with the Eastern traditions in general (classical Chinese, Japanese and Korean literature, and the cultivation and system web novel). Tendencies, not absolutes.

**Description SUBJECTS the Western side covers heavily**

- Money in detail: prices, wages, debts, wills, bankruptcy, the household budget (Dickens, Trollope, O'Brian's prize money).
- Institutions as machines: courts, parliaments, guilds, banks, workhouses, the press.
- The city as one organism: crowds, traffic, sewers, fog, whole districts at once (Dickens, Miéville).
- Heraldry and lineage as display: blazons, house words, genealogies (Martin, sagas, Tolkien).
- Feasts by the course, with who sits where.
- Landscape as route and geology: distances, terrain, the day's march (Tolkien, McCarthy).
- Technical apparatus exactly named: rigging, gunnery, surgery, clockwork (O'Brian, Victorian realism).
- Natural history: species, plants, birds through a naturalist's eye (Maturin, Tolkien's herbs).
- Decaying architecture and the haunted house (Peake, the gothic).
- Arms and the arming scene; armour piece by piece (Homer, Malory).
- Law and feud as procedure (sagas, *Bleak House*).
- The guilty, self-justifying mind from inside (Abercrombie, Hobb, Wolfe).
- Religious doubt and argument (Erikson, Victorian novel).
- Documents as objects: letters, diaries, records, footnoted scholarship (Clarke, Stoker).
- Status screens and stat blocks (Western LitRPG, sharing this subject with Korean and Chinese system novels).

**Description TECHNIQUES the Western side owns**

- Free indirect style: narration in the character's idiom.
- Close third locked by chapter, rotating POV chapters.
- The ironic omniscient narrator and the essayistic aside.
- The unreliable narrator and deliberate withholding.
- Prolepsis (flash-forward inside the present).
- The entrance inventory and the caricature tag.
- The telling detail and the objective correlative.
- Pathetic fallacy (restricted in WOTR by R49-22).
- The Homeric extended simile, the epithet, the catalogue, ekphrasis.
- Polysyndeton and parataxis; the periodic sentence.
- Litotes (saga understatement) and bathos (Abercrombie's puncture).
- The chapter-end hook and the serial cliffhanger.
- In medias res openings.
- Footnotes, epigraphs and appendices as a second voice.
- Epistolary and found-document form.
- Verse and song inserted whole.
- The hard-magic rule statement (Sanderson).
- The boxed status screen and system notification (LitRPG).
- The set piece with approach, climax and long aftermath.

## Voice
**Notices first** · The question behind the answer: who asked it, who is paying for the reply, and who is standing next to the speaker when the bottom comes out. He hears how much of a person is actually in what they say before he hears whether it is true.
**Sentence length** · Long. He is a speech-maker: whole paragraphs spoken in one breath, built clause on clause toward a closing line short enough to be carried out of the room, and delivered at the same low volume whether he is addressing a fishwife or the Curia.
**Contractions** · never, in office. The coastal parish speech he was raised in contracts freely, and it comes back only in grief.
**Pet word** · "I would like": the record to show it, her to be specific, you to sit in this seat one day. Under it runs the harbour: the lead line, depth, ballast, what a thing draws.
**Never says** · Anything louder than the room needs, a flattery, or a word he has not paid for. He never tells a man his sentence weighed when it did not, and he gives exact figures only when reading a testimony or the register aloud; of his own life he speaks in round years.
**Stumbles** · Never in public. The speeches are finished before he begins them.
**Gloss rights** · Yes, for the Sounding, the Scale and the doctrine of weight, which he explains at length to anyone who asks and to several who did not.
**Under stress** · The speeches stop. He goes short and declarative and every sentence is an answer, never a question, and everyone who has worked with him a year knows it is the sound of a man who has decided something and is very frightened of it.
**In grief** · The court register drains away and the Coldwater Steps come back: short harbour sentences, a first name, the lead line paid out ("The line ran out, Ilse. All of it."), the Weigher's Boy under forty-odd years of office.
**In joy** · The voice holds its level and its length. The joy is in the hands folding into the wide sleeves, the breath let out through the mouth, and the grey eyes easing where the two lines between the brows never do.
**Sample line** · "Every commentary you will read this year is going to open with astonishment. Astonishment is a way of declining to have expected something. We were told. We were told for four hundred years and we filed it."

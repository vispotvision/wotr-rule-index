## Lore · The Life Behind the Card

*Written 2026-09-24 by the character-lore pass from this card, the fourteen archived scenes it appears in, and the record of Hild's council: the Kōkan Loyalist Coalition. What those sources do not state is new here.*

### Origin

Edward Lambert was born in the Hallenfeld river corridor, the son of a trade administrator who managed the tariff collection on the eastern routes. As a boy he stood at the Hallenfeld junction with his father's tally board and read the carts in against their manifests. When a load and its paper disagreed, his father sent him back out to find the difference. He found it.

Two brothers came after him. Charles took orders and rose to a bishop's bench. Thomas came so late that the family had stopped expecting children.

His father's books paid for his schooling, and the schooling took him into Muken's civil service. The service required a blade. He trained with one every day for forty years.

### The Making

He rose by being correct. At twenty-six he held the far end of a survey line for Muken's wall, on ground so frozen the stakes went in with a maul. Muken stopped at the third stake and called it wrong. Lambert said it was wrong by a foot and a half, because the bedrock shelved there. Muken looked at him a long moment and walked on, and never touched that stake again.

At thirty-one he married Kasumi Kurenai Moto, a quiet practitioner of a Kurenai cadet line, whom he had met across a vegetable stall at a corridor-town market. The courtship was brief. He wrote the quarterly trade reports for the eastern corridor. When he was thirty-nine, Muken saw that their projections had held for eleven consecutive quarters, asked who wrote them, and filed the name. At forty-two he was three weeks from a minor lordship in the Hallenfeld corridor. The grant was signed and waiting in the dispatch queue on the night Muken was assassinated. It never left the desk.

He kept his corridor desk. In the first winter a clerk fed the old crown's ledgers to the office grate to clear the shelves. Lambert reached into the fire and took the tariff book back out, and the scald on the back of his left hand came from that grate. In the same offices he marked a quiet clerk whose figures were always right. The clerk was Osric.

In the second year after the fall the Tenrai consolidated the cadet lines. Kasumi's family had not sworn to the new throne. She told the assessors aloud what their assessment was, where other detainees could hear, and she was killed in a courtyard at Vaeloris.

The report reached him by a Hallenfeld courier who did not know what he carried, among three trade manifests and a tariff update. Lambert read the report and set it on his desk. He read the manifests. He read the tariff update, worked out what it meant for the corridor's grain, wrote his answer, sealed it and put it in the outgoing dispatch. Then he closed his office door and sat in the dark for three days. On the fourth he walked to the Hallenfeld junction and set out for the Ironwood March. A rumour had reached him through the courier network that a Stark woman there had borne a child of Kōkan blood. The child's name was Ice.

### The Cost

He reached the holdfast when Hild was four and announced himself as a man who could count. Fern Stark saw what he was. He swore no oath. He kept the stores through the winter Fern died. He taught the girl the granary, the road, the second sentence and the empty chair. He assessed her as he would a granary and found her the correct investment. From three paces behind and to the right of the broken throne, he held his objections for the debrief and nodded once when she was right.

He would not lie, to himself or to the girl or to the kingdom. Someone had lied to Kasumi. In the eleventh year the people inside the walls ate two thirds of a ration, and he fought Hild in the hall to make her eat a full one. She called him a coward. He entered it in the ledger under acceptable losses. When her father came to the gate, Lambert told him before anyone else could that in eleven years she had asked for him once.

On the morning of the ninth hour he rode out the postern with six riders and no banner, under orders to find Sonzai. At the fourth mile the Stormfold road went under a ridge, and he turned back. At the broken sally gate he fought with forty of the household and about ninety Ashwell tenants, his left hand halfway up his own blade, and took no exchanges. When the line came apart a third time he raised his sword and shouted at them to fight. They went in behind him. At the ninth hour Hild died in the cistern doorway where Osric had put himself.

He read the three thousand and eleven southern dead aloud himself, over six weeks, and delegated no name. He had the three stones cut to one depth of lettering. His brother's stone stood apart. The parish book at Vaeloris no longer held Charles's dates, and six weeks in three archives had given Edward nothing.

On the morning of the first frost Brida came up the path on a stick and stood beside him. "It must be hard to be the oldest," she said. "And be the only one alive." He answered the small stone. "If only I was stronger." Then Robin Ice rode up with word that the Goblin Senate was hanged, and Lambert ordered the four hundred and six hostages killed. Eleven of them were children under twelve, and the truce they stood surety for bore his seal. He said he would still have given the order, and wished it noted that he knew what he had said. He went down to the afternoon council and asked the correct questions about grain.

### Where They Stand

In open council in the seventh month, Tabitha Hallenfeld asked him why he had bought the grain in the first week, before any orc had crossed the wall. He put his hands behind his back until the council moved on. He has never answered her. In the sixth year a press he licensed printed the question. He read the sheet at breakfast, folded it in quarters, and asked for the returns.

Sodoku named the Lamberts the Holy Sea's representatives. Edward retrieved the Alabaster seat and runs its council, and he is teaching Charles's son. At six Dabney named the one chair at the table nobody was sitting in. Edward said the boy was right.

He does not speak of Kasumi. He knows the hour she died, the courtyard, the weather, what she wore, and the names of the man who signed the order and the man who carried it out. People have told him that at least he knows, and he has thanked them. No record says what became of Charles. A dying Tenrai practitioner told Renard Greymane that Lambert had opened the west postern for him, and the claim stands uncorrected. The Inquisition is coming. The rushes in the council chamber still need changing.

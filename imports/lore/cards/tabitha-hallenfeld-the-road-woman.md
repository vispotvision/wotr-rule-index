## Lore · The Life Behind the Card

*Written 2026-09-24 by the character-lore pass from this card, the ten archived scenes it appears in, and the record of Hild's council: the Kōkan Loyalist Coalition. What those sources do not state is new here.*

### Origin

Tabitha Hallenfeld was born into House Hallenfeld of the river corridors. Muken Moto had set the house at the river junction where the kingdom's three principal roads meet: the eastern route toward Ketsuen, the northern toward the Polar Reach, and the western toward Stannvaard's corridor holdings. The house bred quartermasters, road engineers and courier captains. It bred no famous swords.

She was raised to the work at the side of a wagon. She loaded and unloaded, and walked beside the teams in every season. By the time she was grown she could tell a carter where to put a thing in a voice that did not bargain. The road-wind gave her the squint and the crow's feet.

She learned the house's shorthand, the marks a Hallenfeld hand sets against a route to say what state it is in, which nobody outside the house can read. A Hallenfeld map shows routes, junctions, supply caches and way-stations, and the condition of each. It answers what the roads always ask: who is going where, and why the going is happening now.

In Muken's hall her house came last to every assembly. Its lord apologised each time, and Muken let it pass, because a house that came on time and let the roads decay was worth less to him. The roads did not pause for assemblies.

### The Making

When Muken was killed, the house lost the thing its roads had joined together. It kept the roads. Hallenfeld crews graded and drained the polar approach road and kept the river crossings and the signal posts in repair for a crown that no longer existed to pay them.

In the first years after the fall the house's couriers carried the tariff traffic of the eastern corridor, where Edward Lambert kept the accounts and Osric of Hallenfeld wore the plain wool of a minor administrator. One of those couriers set the report of Lambert's wife's death on his desk among three trade manifests and a tariff update. He did not know what he carried. The house learned in those years to carry sealed things it could not read.

Tabitha spent those years on the roads. She spoke for weeks at a time to wagon drivers, customs clerks and frontier informants whose word was worth what was paid for it. When the Dawi closed the Stannvaard corridor, two Hallenfeld freight columns were stopped before they entered it. Their captains were told the closure was absolute. When Wren Greymane went over to the Tenrai, Zaehaerys at Hollenmark sent word to Hallenfeld that every route Wren knew was compromised. Osric made contact with the house. The word he sent back to Kharven-Seat was that Hallenfeld still wished to set its alliance to Hild.

Tabitha walked three days through compromised territory to reach Kharven-Seat and tell Hild that the roads were talking, and that the roads were saying the Tenrai knew where she was.

She came into the throne room beside Osric, late, and made no apology. She looked at the room the way she looked at a road, for what it could carry and how long it could carry it before the surface failed. Hild's mother had read rooms that way. Tabitha looked at the throne and at the feet that did not reach the floor. When Hild asked about the exiles, she unrolled a manifest from the cylinder at her belt and set a finger on the igloo villages at the edge of the polar shelf. "It is true," she said. She gave it in order: a small camp, white hair on the leader, a Dawi aethersmith, a man named Robin Ice. When Hild asked to go south to Bram, Osric read the road as Wren's.

"She is the target," Tabitha said.

The Tenrai did not need to push on the Seat, she told them. They needed the girl to move, and the road was where they would wait. Hild sent Brida south instead.

### The Cost

The walk that carried the warning carried news of its own. The Tenrai knew of her arrival within hours, and Lambert counted it as one of three proofs of a channel inside the Seat's walls. In the Thin Weeks a Tenrai practitioner reached the altar-room door where Hild slept. He wore a Kharven courier's clothes and a courier's satchel, buckled twice.

Hild lodged at the Hallenfeld factor's house on the low street, and the council sat at its long table. There Sodoku Moto said of Stannvaard's council, "If it is an agreement then there is a document and Tabitha can find the document." Hild counted Robin's ride south off Tabitha's own walking: the southern road was out past the fourth ford. Before light one morning Tabitha came in off six days of road, the hem of her riding coat frozen into a plate. She did not bow. She gave the Holy Sea's party as she had counted it, nineteen in grey with a chapter escort in proofed plate. Then she gave the second thing. Their rider had waited in the yard for a written answer and had declined bread three times. Her hand was not quite steady when she reached into her coat for his packet.

On the morning of the ninth hour she stood behind Hild at the foot of the stair while the girl's father kissed her hair and rode out. By the ninth hour Hild was dead in the cistern doorway that Osric had put himself in. A mason cut three stones in one week for Hild Ice, Lorn Stark and Osric of Hallenfeld. The roads were open that winter. The child Tabitha had kept them for lay under the stone that bore her name.

In the seventh month of the year after, she put the roads' own question to the grain. In open council, before the Queen's chair and the whole assembly, she asked Lambert why he had bought the grain in the first week. The advance had come in the fourth. He put his hands behind his back and waited, and the council moved on. She went home, wrote it down and dated it. She asked him twice more in private and was refused twice. In the sixth year the Ashwell Register printed her question under no name, in the words she had used, and she has said she did not give it to them. The editor says so too. He will not prove it, because proving it would mean naming who did.

### Where They Stand

Tabitha Hallenfeld sits the Bench. She keeps the question written and dated, with its two refusals. Sixteen thousand people in the southern reach are alive because the grain was in the right place, and the man who bought it read three thousand and eleven names aloud over six weeks. Lambert has not answered her in council, in private or in print. In the last two years she has begun to think about a third possibility. She has written it down once and burned it.

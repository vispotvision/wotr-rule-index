# THE UNCOUNTED

## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter One: The Map at Halcrest

---

### Before

There was a war fought above the world, and the world paid for it.

The Archons fought it, the fourteen crowned principles, and the Titans fought it, the fourteen laws that bear the weight of things and want nothing. For as long as the war lasted the spiritual world was its field, and the spiritual world did not survive the fighting in the shape it had been given. Whole provinces of it folded like wet paper. Rivers of it ran backward into the places they had come from and did not come out again. When it ended, the scholars called it the Great Spirit War, because scholars must call a thing something, and the people who had lived under it called it nothing at all, because they had buried too many to want a name in the house.

Three houses kept what was left.

The Yukari kept the spiritual world, or what remained of it: a torn country of thresholds and drowned shrines that had to be walked, mended and walked again, stitch by stitch, by people who went in whole and did not always come out so. The Zettari kept the physical world and the mandates of law, the measure and the boundary stone and the sentence handed down, so that whatever the war had done to the world above, the world below would still hold its shape when a man set his foot on it. And the Moto kept fate, which is the oldest of the three charges and the least forgiving, because a house that reads what must happen is always tempted to arrange it.

All three were rebuilding. All three had lost thousands, and some had lost more than others. Among the Moto there had been a line that once led them, older than the others, and in the last years of the war it went into the dust. The Zettari recorded it gone. The Yukari recorded it gone. The Moto lines who had designed its going recorded it gone with the most care of all, in a fine hand, and closed the register on it, and were satisfied.

It was still the age of the Grand World then, before the great split of the continents, when a man could walk from the salt basins of the north to the warm marches of the south without once setting foot in a boat. That is the world this begins in.

Twenty-three years after the war, in the chain-cities of Nalūn, a mercenary of sixteen sat with a map and could not believe it.

---

### I

The room above the salt-tea house at Halcrest was rented by the week and smelled of it. Lamp oil, wet wool, the butter-and-brine fug of the tea boiling below, and under all of it the mineral warmth that came up through the floor from the city cut into the dead sea's bed. The walls were salt, the pale Halcrest kind with its bedding planes showing like the grain of a board, and the lamp on the table was cut from salt too, so its light came through the stone the colour of weak honey. Outside the one small window the upper town lay under its fifth month of snow. Somewhere out past the roofs, the chain at Bridgemarch was taking up slack, and its slow iron clank came through the glass so regularly that nobody in the room heard it any more.

Muken had the map pinned at the corners with whatever was to hand. A whetstone held the north-east. His sister's tin of salve held the south. His own left boot, which he had taken off an hour ago to look at a blister and then forgotten, held the west, and the east was held down by the flat of his scrap-iron sword, a length of black and pitted steel that three different smiths had looked at and declined to put a price on.

He was sixteen, and already built like the work had built him, the size of a grown man and then some: long in the arm, heavy through the shoulder and the back of the neck, with the forearms of someone who had swung a pick before he ever swung a blade. His skin was a deep brown, roughened at the knuckles and cracked white in the creases the way every hand raised in the Cuttings went cracked, as if the salt had got in and stayed. His hair was already going white. It had started at thirteen, at the temples, and by now there was nearly as much white in it as black, and he wore it tied back with a strip of leather because he could not be bothered to cut it and would not pay someone else to. His eyes were slate grey and set deep under a heavy brow. In a certain light, if he was tired or angry, there was a fleck of amber low in the left one, like a spark caught in a fire grate, and he did not know it was there, because he did not own a mirror and would not have looked if he had.

His coat was a dead sergeant's coat, cut down to fit him, and a sergeant's again now, because the Lanternless had made him one at sixteen, the youngest the company had ever had: brown wool gone the colour of tea, the facings picked off and the holes where they had been darned shut by a hand that was not his. A fighting harness sat over it, worn shiny at the right hip where the scabbard rode. There was a pale seam of scar tissue through his left eyebrow and another across the back of his right hand. He had a broken front tooth, the left one, chipped to a corner. He smiled more than people expected him to, and when he did the tooth made him look about twelve, which was nearer the truth than most of his men liked to think about.

He was not smiling now.

"Nah." He pressed one cracked finger to the map, dragged it a long way, and lifted it as if the paper had burned him. "Nah, this can't be right, sis. You're telling me the monsters migrated *that* much?"

Yukazuri, at the washstand with her sleeves pushed up, smiled at her brother in the scrap of mirror nailed above it.

"Yeah. That's what it looked like."

"That's four hundred miles."

"Four hundred and thirty, by the scribe's scale. I measured it twice."

"In two seasons."

"In two seasons," she agreed, and dried her hands.

She was thirteen. She came across the room the way she did everything, without hurry and without one wasted step, a small girl made smaller by her brother's bulk filling half the room, and nobody who had watched her work over the bed of a cart ever called her a child twice. Her hair was silver-white and had been since she was a girl, a white so clean it looked deliberate, and she kept it braided down her back and pinned up off her neck when she was working. Her face was narrow and grave, deep brown like his, with a straight nose and a mouth that rested in a line most people took for disapproval until they saw it move. Her eyes were a deep blue that went grey in poor light, and in the lamplight off the salt they were nearly the colour of his. Along the outside of her left forearm, from the wrist bone almost to the elbow, ran a long scar, flat and very straight and only just gone white, the kind a blade leaves when someone raises an arm to it at the right time and not a moment later. She had been twelve, last winter. She did not talk about it, and she never covered it.

She wore a field coat in undyed grey, cut short for kneeling in, with deep pockets she had sewn herself. One of them clinked with the small brass and glass of her trade: needles, gut, a flask of spirit, the salve tin's twin. A short blade rode at her left hip, its grip wrapped in grey cloth she rewound every morning, and nobody in the company had ever seen her draw it for anything but cutting a bandage.

She bent over the map beside him. When she came close, his breathing slowed. It always did, near her. He had stopped noticing years ago, the way he had stopped hearing the chain.

"Look at the dates first," she said. "Then argue with me."

---

### II

The map was a copy of a copy, and it was beautiful. Someone in a Zettari survey office had drawn the original, Yukazuri said, the kind of thing the Zettari did after the war, measuring the world over again to be sure it was still there. A factor house in Ironlink had bought a copy because factor houses bought everything that might move the price of salt, and a scribe in that factor house had made a second copy on his own time and sold it on the quiet to a small white-haired girl who had sewn his brother's hand back together the winter before.

It showed the whole width of the Grand World's northern quarter in brown ink and red, from the salt basins on the left edge to a blank of boreal forest on the right where the surveyors had given up and written *uncharted, heavy timber*. Over the land, in a finer hand and a paler red, someone had drawn the sightings: little marks like hooked crosses, each with a date beside it in the Zettari reckoning, crowded thick in the south and the middle and thinning to the north-east in a long ragged tongue.

"Right," Muken said. "Dates."

"These, along the Thresh." Her finger touched a dense knot of marks in the south. "Three years ago. The big herds. The stilts, the greywalkers, the things that hunt in the dry beds that nobody's named yet because nobody's come back to name them. That's where everyone was getting killed when we first took a contract."

"I remember. I was there for some of it."

"You were there for most of it. I was there for after." She moved her finger up. "Two years ago, here. The Marrow crossings. Fewer marks. They're bigger, though, look at the notation, the scribe's put a little crown on the ones the surveyors called *great*." Up again. "Last spring. Here." Up again, a long way, out past every road on the map and into the first fringe of the timber. "And this winter. Here."

Muken leaned over the last cluster. They were drawn tight together, almost on top of one another, right at the edge where the brown ink gave out.

"What's out there?"

"Nothing," Yukazuri said. "That's the point. No towns. No convoys. No cuttings. Nothing for them to eat, if they were following food. Nothing to kill, if they were following us."

He was quiet for a moment. Downstairs, someone dropped a pot and swore by their token, and the tea-house keeper swore back.

"So what are they following?"

"I don't know."

"You've got a guess."

"I've got a guess." She straightened, and her hand went to the grey wrap on her blade's grip and rested there. "The surgeon at the Marrow camp told me something last year, when I was boiling his needles for him. He'd been up in the timber with a Yukari walker before the war ended. He said the spirit side of the world isn't torn evenly. Some places it came apart like cloth. Some places it came apart like ice. He said the Yukari go where it's worst first, and the worst is south, where the fighting was heaviest, because that's where people live and that's where it bleeds into them."

"And?"

"And the things on this map aren't animals, Muken. Not really. They're what fell through when the spirit side broke. They came out where it broke worst. And now they're walking somewhere else." She tapped the far edge of the paper, the blank and the timber. "My guess is they're going where it's thinnest. Where it's quiet. Somewhere they can get back."

He looked at her for a long time. She had a way of saying the strangest things in the voice she used for telling a man to hold still while she pulled a splinter of rib out of his lung, and he had learned, slowly, over a lot of bad nights, that this was the voice she used when she was most sure.

"You got all that from boiling needles."

"I got all that from listening while I boiled needles. You should try it."

"I'd burn myself."

"You would," she said. "You'd burn yourself and then tell everyone it was a fight."

He laughed, a short bark, the broken tooth showing, and dragged a hand down his face. His palm rasped over two days of stubble. "Sky above. Four hundred and thirty miles."

"Thirty," she said. "Don't round it. That's how factors cheat you."

### III

They had been raised in the Cuttings, under Halcrest, three galleries down where the warmth was steady and the air tasted of powdered halite and lamp soot. Their mother cut salt for a factor house called Orrel until her lungs went, and then she sorted bars at the stamping floor until her eyes went, and then she did nothing at all for nearly a year in a room the size of a cart, with her children's hands on her and the salt-throat coughing out of her in long dry fits that sounded like someone sawing a board. Her name was Kasane. She had no other name the clerks would take.

That was the whole of it, as far as anyone at Orrel was concerned. A cutting-class woman with no house name, no papers and no token, entered in the drainage book under a number because there was nothing else to enter her under. She had come down into the Cuttings the year the war ended, a young woman, alone, from somewhere she never once named, and the Orrel house had taken her in the way the factor houses took in everything that arrived without a claim on it. Years after, a younger son of the house had taken her in another way, and Muken was what came of it. Three years later there was Yukazuri. Their mother never said whose she was, and Yukazuri, who could read most people the way the scribes read a bill, had never been able to read that out of her.

"Do you ever think about her?" Yukazuri asked. She had sat down on the edge of the cot with the map across her knees, and she was not looking at him. "When you look at maps. All the places she might have come from."

Muken pulled his boot out from under the corner of the map and began, with a great deal of attention, to put it back on.

"No."

"Liar."

He worked his heel down into the boot. The blister bit and he hissed through his teeth. "Sometimes."

"She'd have hated this room."

"She'd have loved this room. It's got a window."

That got a sound out of Yukazuri that was nearly a laugh. She ran her thumb along the map's edge. "She used to say the salt was patient. Do you remember? She'd put her hand on the wall at night and say, *it's patient, it'll keep us*. I thought she meant it'd keep us alive."

"What did she mean?"

"I think she meant it'd keep us. The way it keeps the dead." Yukazuri's voice did not change at all, and that was how he knew it cost her. "She knew she wasn't going to get out. She was talking to the wall about us."

Muken said nothing for a while. He finished lacing the boot and sat with his forearms on his knees and his big cracked hands hanging between them, and he looked at the floor. When he was a boy, the house steward at Orrel had had a habit of asking the cutting children the merchant's question, *What's your oldest?*, as a joke, because the answer was always nothing. A merchant showed his token before his name. A child of the Cuttings had neither, and the steward thought that was very funny, and laughed every time.

Muken had been thirteen the last time the steward asked him. He had answered with his fists. It was the first contract he ever honoured, as he told it later, and the only one he never got paid for.

"I got us out," he said to the floor.

"You got us out," Yukazuri agreed. "I was there."

"Bought your number out of the drainage book with my first season's pay. Every salt bar of it."

"I know. I counted it with you. Twice."

"Don't round it."

"I wouldn't dare."

He looked up then, and she was watching him with that grave face that most people mistook for disapproval, and he saw the corner of her mouth was turned up. He let out a breath. It went long and slow out of him and he felt his shoulders come down from round his ears, and he did not wonder why. He never did.

"Do you remember the mark?" she asked.

He knew which mark. He did not need to ask. "Course I do."

Their mother had drawn it for them once, when Yukazuri was very small and Muken was not much bigger, with the end of a burned stick on the salt of the wall. It was not a letter. It was not any letter in the Concord hand or the scribe's hand or the trader's ciphers or anything else they had ever seen since, and they had seen a great deal of writing by now on a great many contracts. It looked a little like a knot and a little like a closed eye, and a little like neither. Kasane had said, *that's ours. That's our name. Don't show it to the clerks.* And then she had rubbed it out with the heel of her hand and never drawn it again.

Yukazuri had drawn it in secret ever since, on frosted windows and in the dirt at camp and once, when she was eleven, in spilled lamp oil on the stamping floor, where it had burned blue for a moment before the oil went out. Muken had tried, a few times, to draw it himself. His hand would not make it. Every time, the line went somewhere wrong and the shape came apart, and in the end he stopped trying and let his sister keep it for both of them.

"I showed it to a Zettari once," Yukazuri said.

His head came up sharply. "You what?"

"A surveyor. At the Marrow, the same season as the surgeon. I drew it in the frost on his window while he was asleep, and in the morning I asked him what it was."

"Sis."

"He looked at it for a long time," she said, as if he had not spoken. "And then he said it wasn't anything. He said it was a child's scribble. He said it very fast, and then he wiped the window with his sleeve, and he wouldn't look at me after that." She folded the edge of the map down flat under her palm. "His breath went short when he said it. Here." She touched the hollow of her own throat. "Right up high. They do that when they're frightened and they're trying to be polite about it."

The chain at Bridgemarch clanked. The lamp hissed in its salt bowl.

"What's a Zettari frightened of a scribble for," Muken said.

"I don't know." She met his eyes. "I thought you'd want to know he was."

---

### IV

Somebody knocked at the door, two raps and a pause and a third, which was the company knock, and then the door opened before either of them answered it, which was the company manners.

It was not anyone from the company.

The man who stepped in out of the stair had the look of the factor houses all over him. Middle years, middling height, a long grey travelling coat with the snow still on its shoulders, cut close and lined with something dark and soft at the collar. Thin sandy hair combed flat from a high parting. A clean-shaven face that had been handsome once and had settled into the expression of a man who was always, quietly, doing sums. Ink on the second finger of the right hand, the scribe's stain, though he did not have the scribe's stoop. Good boots, oiled, salt rime on the toes. And on his belt, where a fighting man wore a knife, a bronze seal-token the size of a large coin, hanging from a short chain. It hummed. In the small room you could hear it, a faint steady note just under the lamp's hiss, and feel it a little behind the eyes.

"Muken of the Lanternless," the man said, and smiled. "And the Lanternless's surgeon, I take it. Forgive me. Your captain said I'd find you above the tea-house, and the keeper said you'd not mind."

"The keeper's a liar," Muken said pleasantly. He had not stood up. His sword was still lying across the east of the map, and his hand had come to rest on the table about a forearm's length from its grip. "Who are you?"

"Hollis Draycott. I act for the Vessant house." He let a short silence fall where the greeting ought to go, and when neither of them filled it, his eyes went, quickly and quite openly, to Muken's belt, and then to Yukazuri's, and found no token on either, and came back up. It was not rudeness, precisely. It was how a man of the factor houses read a room, the way a sailor looked at the sky. "Ah. Well. I'll keep it short, then."

"Tokenless," Muken said. "You can say it. I've heard it."

"I wasn't going to say anything of the kind."

"No," Yukazuri said. "You weren't." She had not moved from the cot. She was watching his breathing.

Draycott glanced at her, a little longer this time, at the white hair and the grey coat and the long flat scar down her forearm. Then he unbuttoned his coat, took a folded paper out of an inner pocket, and held it up between two fingers without offering it.

"The Vessant house runs a supply contract into the eastern timber," he said. "Iron stock and lamp oil out, furs and resin back. A small post on a river, past the last of the roads. Very profitable. Very old contract, as it happens." He touched the humming token at his belt, briefly, with the inky finger. "This one. Our oldest. It's been sound for thirty-one years and the house would very much like it to stay sound for thirty-one more. But the last two convoys have had trouble on the road."

"Trouble," Muken said.

"Animals."

Muken looked down at the map, at the thick red knot of hooked crosses on the edge of the timber. Then he looked back up at Draycott with the slate-grey eyes and said nothing at all.

Draycott followed his look and saw what was on the table, and for the first time something in his face shifted. It was very small. He had good control. "I see you've done some reading."

"My sister has."

"Then you'll understand why we're paying what we're paying. Escort of a single convoy, eleven wagons, from the Halcrest gate to the river post and back. Twenty fighting men, your captain's choice, plus the surgeon. Forty bars a man, stamped Vessant, full weight. Eighty for you as sergeant. Half at the gate."

It was a great deal of salt. Muken did not let it show on his face, but Yukazuri saw his right thumb move once against the edge of the table, which was where it showed on him.

"What happened to the last escort?" Yukazuri asked.

Draycott turned to her. "Light losses. Three men. It was a rough road."

He touched his token again as he said it. His thumb went to the bronze and ran once around the rim of it, the way another man might touch a lucky coin, and his breath came in high and short, right up under the collarbone, and stopped there a moment before it went out.

Yukazuri looked at her brother.

Muken had seen her look at men like that a thousand times over the bed of a cart and the floor of a tent, when she was deciding whether a man was going to live, and he knew the look the way he knew the chain. He sat back. The chair creaked under him.

"How many came back?" he said.

"I've told you."

"You told her how many died. I'm asking how many came back."

The token hummed. Downstairs, the tea was boiling over, and someone was shouting about it.

"Four," Draycott said.

"Of how many."

"Of twenty-six."

"So that's twenty-two dead."

"Or missing. The road was very confused. One must be accurate." He put the paper on the table, on the corner of the map, beside the whetstone. He did it neatly and without hurry, and he did not look at Yukazuri again. "I'll be frank, since your surgeon seems determined that I should be. The house has had difficulty finding a company. The Grey Lamps turned us down. So did Fennick's. Your captain, to his credit, said he'd hear me out if his sergeant would go, and that his sergeant was the only man in the company who'd go anywhere for money."

"My captain's a liar too," Muken said. "I'll go anywhere for enough money."

"Name it."

Muken looked at the map. He looked at it for a long time, at the long ragged tongue of red marks stretching out of the south, out of everything that had happened in the war and after it, out past the roads and the towns and the cuttings and up into the white space at the edge where the surveyors had given up. He thought about four hundred and thirty miles in two seasons. He thought about things that were not really animals, walking home through the snow.

He thought about a room the size of a cart.

"Double it," he said. "Full at the gate, nothing on return. Half again for every man who doesn't come back, paid to whoever he names. And she doesn't go past the river post."

"She does," said Yukazuri.

"She doesn't."

"She does, or you'll be paying the half-again on yourself." Her voice had not risen. It did not need to. "You can dislike me when we get back."

Muken opened his mouth and shut it again. Draycott looked from one of them to the other with the faint interest of a man watching two other men haggle over a horse he did not intend to buy.

"Double," Draycott said at last, slowly. "Full at the gate. The death-price I can do. The house will want it sealed."

"Seal it, then."

"With what? You've no token for it to bind to." He did not say it unkindly, and it was the truest thing he had said since he walked in. "A Nalūn seal can't take hold of a man who isn't in the book. The Wellspring doesn't see you. You understand that? You could break it and walk away and the salt wouldn't so much as notice."

"I've never broken a contract in my life."

"I believe you," Draycott said, and seemed surprised to find he did. "But the house won't care what I believe."

Muken stood up then. The room got smaller. He was half a head taller than the factor's agent and a great deal broader, and the lamp threw his shadow up the salt wall behind him until it bent over the ceiling. He did not put his hand near the sword. He held out his right hand instead, the one with the scar across the back of it, open and cracked white at every knuckle.

"Then it's on that," he said. "Same as it's always been."

Draycott looked at the hand for a long moment. Then, because he was a man of the factor houses and a factor knew a good price when he was shown one, he took it.

---

### V

When he had gone, his snow melting into dark spots on the floorboards by the door and the hum of his token fading down the stair after him, Muken stood looking at the paper on the table and did not pick it up.

"Twenty-two," he said.

"I know."

"You knew before he said it."

"I knew he was lying. I didn't know by how much." Yukazuri got up off the cot and came and stood beside him, and his breathing slowed, and neither of them remarked on it. "You could still say no. Captain would back you."

"Captain would sell me to Vessant by the pound if they asked nicely."

"Captain would sell you by the ounce," she said. "He'd get more for you that way."

He laughed again, quieter, and put his arm round her shoulders, and she let him, which she did not let anyone else do. They stood like that over the map. The lamp burned down in its salt bowl and the light through the stone went from honey to amber, and outside the window the snow came on thicker over the roofs of Halcrest, and down in the dark the chain at Bridgemarch kept the city hung together, link by link, the way it always had.

"They're going somewhere," Muken said at last. "The things on the map."

"Yes."

"Then that's where the work's going to be. Everyone's going to want an escort out there. Every house with a contract past the roads."

"That's where the dying's going to be," Yukazuri said.

"Same thing, sis. It's always been the same thing."

She did not answer that. She reached past him, across the table, to the small window, and breathed on the cold glass until it fogged white. Then, with the tip of one finger, without looking at her hand, she drew their mother's mark in the mist. The knot that was a little like an eye. The name the clerks were never meant to see.

Muken watched her do it. He had watched her do it a hundred times. This time, for no reason he could have told anyone, a sharp heat started behind his left eye, as if a cinder had blown into it, and for a single breath the mark on the glass did not look like a scribble at all. It looked like a seam. It looked like the place where something had been joined to something else, a long time ago, and was under strain, and he could see exactly where it would give.

He blinked, and it was a scribble on a fogged window again, already beginning to run.

"You all right?" Yukazuri said.

"Fine." He rubbed the eye with the heel of his hand. "Smoke."

"There's no smoke."

"Then it's the salt."

She looked at him the way she looked at men in the backs of carts, for a moment longer than he liked. Then she let it go, and turned back to the window, and watched the mark run down the glass until it was gone.

Far out to the north-east, past the last of the roads, in the white space at the edge of the Zettari map where the surveyors had written *uncharted, heavy timber*, something very large lifted its head out of the snow and turned it, slowly, toward the south.

## Author Notes

**What this is.** An expansion of Isaac's opening for the new lore, kept to every beat he gave: twenty-three years after the Great Spirit War; Titans and Archons; the spiritual world broken and reshaped; the Yukari keeping the spiritual world, the Zettari the physical world and the mandates of law, the Moto fate; a once-great line of the Moto destroyed and believed gone, by the Zettari, the Yukari, and the Moto lines who designed it; the Grand World before the split of the continents; Muken at a map, "Nah this can't be right sis?", Yukazuri's smile and "Yeah that's what it looked like." Past tense, third person (a written opening, R49-02). Mystic register for the frame (law stated, reasons kept, R70-76), close POV on Muken after.

**What canon it stands on.** Muken's card and lore: born in Nalūn, a Kōkan mother taken by a lesser noble house of the city, Yukazuri sharing the mother and none of the blood the Moto would recognise, never acknowledged, fed on what his hands earned, mercenary work taken young, a scrap-iron blade, the Shingan opening "somewhere in that work." The Long Reading §VII–VIII: the Kōkan as the uncounted, their name "written in a mark the instrument could not classify," the factor houses' bonded cutting class, the seal-token as the grammar of debt he learned before he learned his blood. Yukazuri's card: silver-white hair from girlhood, deep blue eyes grey in poor light, deep brown skin, the cut at twelve as her only combat scar, the grey wrap on the grip, the short blade used for nothing but bandages, Still-Water Broadcast (breathing eases near her; never named on the page here, R71-30 held reveal), the voice line "Hold still. You can dislike me when it closes." The Nalūn Standing Inventory supplies Halcrest, Bridgemarch's chain, Ironlink, the Cuttings, salt lamps, salt-throat, salt tea, "What's your oldest?", "tokenless", "on my token", the drainage book, stamped bars, and the Wellspring that will not seal a man it cannot see.

**Originated here (all yours to keep or strike):**
- **Kasane**, their mother. A Kōkan woman who came down into the Cuttings alone the year the war ended and never named where from; cut salt for the **Orrel** factor house, sorted bars, died of salt-throat. The name is Japonic (*kasane*, the layering of things laid one over another), fitting the Moto stratum. She had no name the clerks would take.
- **The mark.** Kasane's "that's ours, that's our name, don't show it to the clerks." This is the Long Reading's unclassifiable glyph, the Kōkan's own name, in a child's keeping. Yukazuri can draw it; Muken's hand cannot. A Zettari surveyor recognised it and lied. Planted signs for a later reveal.
- **Muken's eye.** The amber fleck, and the heat behind the left eye when he sees the mark as a seam under strain: the first stir of the Shingan / Fractura reading (his card's Primary), held unexplained.

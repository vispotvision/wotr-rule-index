Naiser let the silence after the Sovereign's last words run long.

He did it on purpose. The hall was built for it. The stream went on under the boards, steady as breath, and out beyond the open screens a wind moved through the tops of the cedars and set them hissing softly against one another and then let them be. In the clay cups the tea had stopped steaming. He let all of it go on until the room had settled back into its own quiet, the way water settles after a stone goes into it, and only then did he lift his eyes from the table.

He had listened to all of it. To the Sovereign's careful courtesy, and to the line drawn under it, firm and plain as a boundary stone, that Kushara would not hand away its right to defend itself. To the prince's questions, which had come at him like thrown stones, and which were, every one of them, the right questions. To the story of Gimbzo, and the name of the thing that had killed him, and the pleasure in the voice that had told it. He had heard the palm come down on the table. He had felt, through the thin skin of the hall's threads, the heat of the anger that went between the father and the son, and the older, deeper thing beneath it that neither of them would have called by its name in front of strangers.

None of it had surprised him. He had sat through his own father's lectures at the gate. He knew the sound of a father trying to put a whole kingdom's worth of caution into a son in one afternoon.

"Sovereign," he said. "You asked me a fair question, and your son asked it more plainly. I'll answer it plainly, so there's no room left in it for anyone to misunderstand later."

He set both hands flat on the low table, either side of his cup.

"If an army marches on Kushara," he said, "and it marches with spiritual forces at its back that the Yukari have not sanctioned, if anyone, Moto or Seikai remnant or some house we haven't heard of yet, reaches into the spirit side of this world and pulls something out of it to throw at your people," and he paused, and let the pause sit until he was sure that both of them understood exactly how much he meant what came next, "I will not hesitate to annihilate them."

He said it the way he might have said that the stream ran north. There was no heat in it.

"That is what I am offering you," he said. "Defend your borders. Defend them with steel and with your own blood, the way the Zettari always have, with every art your house has made for itself. That strength is yours. It was never ours to give and it is not ours to take away. What I am asking is that you never reach for the other kind. Never take what we hold and turn it into a weapon. And in return, if anyone else ever does it to you, they will not be fighting Kushara. They will be fighting us." He held the Sovereign's eyes. "You don't need to keep a knife from our kitchen under your pillow. We'll stand at your door."

Beside him, Naevra had not moved. But he felt the threads round her draw, very slightly, a little tighter.

"I want to be honest with you about one more thing," Naiser went on, "since honesty seems to be what we're trading in this afternoon. These are not requests. We are no longer asking." He let that land. "We are telling. Our mother has made our position very clear. To go against her word would be to go against the very nature of what we are."

He did not say the name. There was no need. In that hall, on that ground, with the cedars standing round the clearing and the stream running under the floor, there was only one mother the Yukari meant when they said *our mother* in that voice. She had given the world's living things the power to feel. She was the reason there was a Soul Plane to break. She was lying wounded now along a seam that ran through the bottom of everything, and every one of the house's dead was leaking out of her into the courtyards, and the house was all she had left to stand between her and the hands that had done it.

"As for the other half of your son's question," Naiser said, and turned his head a little toward the prince, "what happens when our people aren't there." He almost smiled. "You have no reason to worry about that. My people have tended the spiritual world since it began. Since before there was a Zettari to keep the stones in their places or a Moto to read which way they'd fall. We don't go to it the way a physician goes to a sickbed. It's where we live. It's the thing we do without thinking, the way you breathe." He lifted one hand from the table, palm up, and let it fall. "There is nowhere in the spirit side we are not already."

"That's the trouble with us," Naevra said.

She said it lightly, almost as a joke, and it was the first time she had spoken since the Sovereign struck the table. Both of the Zettari had their eyes on her, he could feel it; he did not look to see. She kept her own eyes on the Sovereign.

"We're always there," she said. "Everywhere you'd rather we weren't, usually. But I understand what's being asked, and it's fair. Kushara can't wait three days for a crow every time something goes wrong in the night." She folded her hands. "So don't. Let us send someone. A reader of the house, with an apprentice or two, to live in Kushara. Not as an ambassador. As a physician. To sit with your healers and your midwives and your stewards and teach them to tell a spirit-side wound from a fever, and a drifting dead from a madness, and a poisoned spring from a bad well. Most of what goes wrong on the spirit side can be held still for a day by someone who knows what they're looking at. A day is time enough for us to come."

"And in time," Naiser said, picking up the thread from her the way they had always picked up each other's threads, "the right teachings will pass between our two houses. Slowly. Properly. Taught, not taken." He looked back at the Sovereign. "That's how the protection of it is meant to work. Between us. I meant what I said before. I would sooner teach the whole world than bury another father."

He let a breath go.

Then he turned and looked, for a long moment, at his sister.

She looked back at him. She did not nod, or frown, or do anything at all that anyone at that table would have been able to read. But he had known her face since before either of them could walk, and he saw it. *Go on. Say it. He needs to hear it.*

He turned his eyes to the prince.

"Yes," he said. "I'm glad he's dead. I said so and I meant it, and I'll say it again in front of anyone. You did what a great many older men couldn't. You struck the root." He paused. "But I don't think you've understood yet what grew out of it."

He kept his voice level. He was careful of that. There was a way of saying these things that would sound like a lord of the house talking down to a boy, and he did not want to say them that way, because he did not think the boy was a fool. He had listened to the way the prince described what he had built in his own body. A fool did not build something like that. A fool did not sit inside his own breath for years, sealing it, until it did what he wanted.

"Let me ask you something," Naiser said. "Not to catch you out. Only because someone ought to ask it, and nobody seems to have. Do you know why the Moto are the way they are?" He watched the prince's face. "Why a house that reads fate would teach itself to arrange it. Why every one of their lines, when it's frightened, reaches for a hand round someone's throat. Or have you been so busy killing them that you never stopped long enough to ask yourself that? It's the first question. It's the plainest one there is."

He let it stand.

"I don't say that to shame you," he said, more quietly. "I say it because kings die, Prince Dougou. Empires die. I have watched two of them die in four years and I'll watch more before I'm done. But the faith of a family that strong, the thing it believes about itself, the morale that holds its lines together when everything around them is falling down, that doesn't die. You can kill every Moto who ever stood in front of you and you will still be fighting what they believe. Gimbzo is dead. What made Gimbzo isn't."

Naevra's fingers moved, very slightly, against the grey silk in her lap.

"I'm afraid," Naiser said, "that his death has brought us nothing. Nothing but more conflict, and more of it coming. You know what he was to the Moto. He was the last fist they had that could still close." He paused. "And he left the house with no Kōkan behind him."

He said the name carefully. It was an old name, older than most of the house's names, and the hall seemed to take it in and hold it for a moment before it let it go.

"The Kōkan were the line that led the Moto, once," he said. "Long before any of us were born. The first of them, the oldest. They're gone now. Every last one, as far as anyone has been able to find, and the house has looked. Some of them, you should know, were killed by Gimbzo himself. With his own hands. For disobeying orders. For refusing to march where he told them to march." He looked at the Sovereign. "Whatever else he was, he hated your house more than anyone alive. I don't think there's been a grudge like his in a thousand years. He wanted us to join him in it. He asked, more than once, in the second year, in the third. He sent riders." Naiser shook his head, slowly. "I hold no such grudge against you. Against anyone. I didn't then and I won't start now. But I want you to understand what he was. He was nothing more than the war itself, wearing a man's face. He was a prime example of what it makes of people. And when you killed him, the war didn't die with him. It only stopped having a name we knew."

The stream ran on under the boards.

He had been thinking, while the prince talked, about the thing the prince had built inside his own body.

*The Iron Bison.* A crude name for a crude thing, crude the way a first sword hammered out of scrap is crude: heavy, ugly, unfinished at the edges, and perfectly capable of killing a man. Seven chambers sealed into one. The breath held in. Every cycle of it packed down a little denser, the body made to carry more and more of its own weight inward until it could not be moved and could not be stopped. He had listened to it described and felt a strange cold prickle at the base of his skull, the way he did when a thread brushed past him that he half recognised. Because it was familiar. Some of the shape of it, under the crudeness, was very familiar indeed. It was like hearing a song your mother sang to you, whistled badly by a stranger in a market three provinces away. The first reinforcement arts, the oldest ones, the ones Ashura had taught the first of the house before there was a house to teach, had begun from the same place. *Keep it in. Let none of it leave. Let the body become the vessel.*

He did not know how a Zettari boy of seventeen had found his way to the edge of that on his own. He did not know whether anyone had shown him. He found that he wanted, very much, to know.

He shook his head, slightly, and put it away for later.

"Sovereign," he said, "you don't need to apologise for him."

He turned back to Shaka-Al, and let his voice soften, a little, at the edges.

"He's young. I was young not long ago. If I were his age and I'd brought down a man like Gimbzo with my own two hands, I'd be telling every hall in the Inner World about it. I'd be telling the horses. It's only natural." He glanced at the prince, and the thing that came to the corner of his mouth was very nearly a smile, and gone again almost before it arrived. "Gimbzo was a monster. He was also, in his way, a truly great man. That's the trouble with the war. It made a great many of those."

Naevra stirred beside him.

"Sealed pressure has to go somewhere eventually," she said.

She said it quietly, to no one in particular, and her eyes had gone very slightly lavender at the rims, and the fine pale threads in the air round her face had lifted and caught the light. She was looking at the prince's hands where they rested on his knees. Then she blinked, and the lavender went out of her eyes, and she looked up, and for a moment her face was perfectly open.

"Forgive me," she said. "It's an old saying in the house. It's what we tell children when they first learn to hold their breath in the working. It came into my head." She inclined her head toward the prince, very slightly. "What you've built is remarkable. I mean that. I've never seen anyone hold that much in." She paused. "I'd only say that when you do want to know more about it, about where it came from and where it goes, there are old books in this valley that might interest you. And there are people here who'd be glad to read them with you."

Naiser did not look at her. He did not need to.

What he felt in her threads ran deeper than anything the others could have read in her face. Under the composure there was something that had very little to do with breath or chambers or the old reinforcement arts. She had been watching the prince since he came through the door. She had watched him look at her, and look away again. She had watched him grin over a dead man. She had found most of it, if Naiser was any judge, distinctly unsavoury, and she had very nearly let it show at the table when the Sovereign's hand came down.

And then, somewhere in the last hour, something in her had turned, quietly, and settled in a different place.

He thought he knew what it was. He thought it was the plainest thing in the world. Seventeen years old, dragged across the Inner World to sit at a stranger's table and be talked over like a horse at a fair. Half of what he had said since he came in, Naiser thought, had been aimed at his own father. Naevra had grown up in a house that had always, in the end, let her choose. Of course she understood. Who in the world would want to be married off to someone they had never met, never chosen, and did not love? Who would sit quietly and smile while it was done to them?

She did not say any of it. She would not. But it was there in the threads, warm and steady and very sad, and Naiser felt it settle beside his own, and was glad of it.

He drew a breath and turned back to the Sovereign.

"So let me be clear about what we're asking for, and what we're not," he said. "We are not asking for authority over the spirit side. Not over yours and not over anyone's. We know that your authority will stand beside ours. It always has, whether either of our houses wanted to admit it." He spread his hands a little on the table. "You keep the physical world. The stone, the law, the boundary, the body. Your house's potential in it has no ceiling that I've ever seen, and I've seen your soldiers fight. All we are saying, all we have ever been saying, is that we hold the same in the spiritual world. The same weight. The same charge. It's not a crown. It's a post. We stand at it because someone has to and because we were made to."

"And what you offered," Naevra said, "consultation, shared instruction, restrictions we set together on anything that could reach the Soul Plane or its Wellsprings." She nodded once. "That's the shape of it. That's what we would have asked for, if my brother had been better at asking." There was a flicker of dry humour in it, there and gone. "We can work out the wording. That's what scribes are for."

Naiser let her have that one.

"As for the Moto," he said.

He was quiet for a moment, looking out through the open screens at the three standing stones in their white gravel.

"I think it's time we stopped fighting," he said. "All of us. I don't think the Moto will start another war with either of our houses. Not for a very long time. I don't think they're capable of it. They're facing a civil war of their own now, the Tenrai drawing in all the other lines under one hand and every one of their own people pushing back against it, and I don't believe they'll come through it whole. Let alone with the strength left to threaten anyone else in the years to come." He shook his head. "They've lost every ally they ever had. Every one. They spent them, one after another, through the war, the way a man spends coins out of a purse he thinks will never empty. The Seikai. The Mahuo. Gimbzo's own lines. They squandered all of it. And they're learning now what everyone who has ever tried it learns in the end." He looked back at the Sovereign. "You can only force something into submission for so long."

"They've begun closing the roads between their own provinces," Naevra said quietly. "That came in two days ago. One of their own grain convoys was stopped at a Tenrai post and turned back, and the escort was from a Moto line. Their own people. Turned back by their own house, at their own border." She looked at her hands. "A house that has started guarding its roads against itself is not looking outward. It's eating inward. To your son's question, Sovereign: no. We don't mean to sit neutral while anyone bleeds. But I don't think the next blood the Moto spill will be ours or yours. I think it will be their own. What we do about that is the conversation I'd like to have next."

Naiser nodded slowly.

Then he was silent for a while, and when he spoke again his voice had gone somewhere else, lower, slower, as if he were listening to something on the far side of the hall that no one else could hear.

"There's one more thing," he said. "I'll tell you, because I'd rather you heard it from me than from a rumour a year from now, with half of it wrong."

His eyes had changed. The violet-black ring round the pupil had brightened, a little, toward silver, and the fine pale thread-marks at his temples had begun to glow faintly under the skin.

"I've seen something," he said. "Coming to the Inner World. Something great." He frowned, very slightly. "I don't know what it is. My vision of it is clouded. I can't see its face. I can't see whose it is or where it stands. When I reach for it, it slides off, the way your eye slides off something you're not meant to look at yet." He paused. "All I can see is that it's coming. That it's been set moving already, somewhere, by someone, and that it can't be stopped now. And that when it comes, it will be large enough that every house in this world will have to decide what it is before it decides what we are."

The silver went out of his eyes. The thread-marks dimmed.

He looked down at his cold tea, and then up again, and he was only Naiser, sitting at a low table in a cedar hall with his hands on his knees.

"I'd rather be standing beside Kushara when it comes," he said, "than across a battlefield from it. That's the whole of my answer, Sovereign. Everything else is wording."

Naevra reached out and refilled his cup from the iron pot, and then the Sovereign's, and then, without any hesitation at all, the prince's. The tea was cold now. She poured it anyway, slowly and carefully, the way the house poured for guests, so that not a drop ran down the side of the clay.

"Since we're telling the truth today," she said, when she had set the pot down, and her eyes rested on the prince for a moment, quite steadily, without any of the guardedness that had been in them when he first came in, "I'll tell you one more. Nobody asked me either." She almost smiled. "We have that much in common, at least. Whatever happens with the rest of it, I thought you ought to know you're not the only one at this table who was told where to sit."

She folded her hands back into her lap and was still.

Naiser sat quietly beside her, with his katana lying sheathed at his right hand and its edge turned toward himself, and waited for the Sovereign of Kushara to answer.

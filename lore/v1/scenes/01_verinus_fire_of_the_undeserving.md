# The Fire of the Undeserving

*Beneath the Grand Church at Altherion. The fourteenth day. Before the Curia sits.*

---

*From the Rubric of Sacrament, the received text, as kept at the Alabaster Court. Marginal glosses in a later hand.*

A Sacrament is fed from the ground it is spoken over. Spoken over thin ground, it takes the celebrant. Spoken over a Well, it takes the Well. Choose the ground before the words, because the words do not wait.

The bill is set once and paid once. What the rite takes, it keeps. There is no office to petition, and the Court has stopped pretending there is.

Consent is required of every soul the seal will cross. The received register holds that consent must be spoken aloud before the third mark is lit. The older register holds that a soul consents when the bill is set in front of it and it does not look away. Both readings are filed. The Court has ruled for neither, and four Palatines have declined to be the one who does.

Do not close the seal with any part of the price unpaid. The record holds five attempts. In each the seal closed on the celebrant, and in each the celebrant is entered in the day-book as still paying.

*Gloss, on the Wellspring of Grace, from the Measurewrights' Mechanism of the Sixty: a rival bond of higher affinity displaces the existing bond, and the displacement is silent, complete, and not felt until the bond is called on.*

*Deepvein station, the Nalūn basin. Extract from the Fixatio watch-log. Same night, fourth hour.* The salt rang. The station floor moved under the bench. Every sealed instrument in the lower vaults took weight at once and the chain-bridges at Ironlink went to their stops and hung there. No drawdown at any basin site. Source not local. The instrument does not take a bearing. Entered, and the watch went back to bed.

---

The hum was the first thing now.

It had been in the flags the whole fourteen days and he had lost it on the second, when it became his blood, and it had come back to him on the twelfth, when Sesk stopped working and nothing was left in the cell loud enough to stand in front of it. Altherion drinking. The Cloister Ward drawing on the Well under the Church through the night, through sealed conduit somewhere behind the wall at his back, a long low note with a grain in it and once in a great while, far overhead, the click of a gauge-housing taking up a notch.

Under the hum, ammonia. Under the ammonia, the sweetness that had come into the wet-iron smell on the twelfth day when the strips of his thighs on the floor stopped being meat and became something else, a smell that belonged to the bindery at Alabaster, hide-glue and old leather, and he had not decided whether that was a mercy. The fascia over the muscles had dried to the texture of vellum. When he moved on the chain it spoke.

The frame at his back had been the third stall on the decani side. He had known that on the first day from the wear on the armrest under his left shoulder, a hollow the depth of a thumb where two hundred years of boys had put their elbows through the long readings, and the polish in the hollow was the only part of the stall that still gave off beeswax. The choir had not sung since the shutdown. Nobody left in the building knew the settings, and the stall that had held the trebles was a frame in a cellar with a man on it, and it would not go back up.

The hum, and the smell, and the shears.

They had not stopped. Fourteen days, indefatigable, on the far side of the membrane where his attention had worn it thin, two blades crossing on green wood and the pause for the hand to move and the blades again, and the small flat bell once in a while telling the pickers to move down a row. He had stopped asking what was being cut. He listened to it for company, the clock in a strange house.

Corwin Sesk had not touched him since the knee.

That had been the eleventh day. On the twelfth Sesk had come down the stair with his apron off and folded over his arm, and had hung it on the nail by the door, and had sat on his crate, and had not put it on since. He fed him. He watered him twice a day from a tin cup with a bent lip, holding it to Aurelian's mouth at the angle that let the water go past the sockets. He took the chain up each morning and let it out each night so the shoulders could be put back. He had made one more entry in the ledger and had not said what it was.

He had said, on the thirteenth day, without being asked, that the arrangement stood.

"Five hundred and ninety-one," Sesk said, to the ledger.

Aurelian's eyes came back into the room.

"Master Sesk."

"Since the eleventh. I stopped writing the others down. I keep this one because it is the only figure in this building that goes up honestly." He turned the stylus once between two fingers. "You said it in your sleep on the twelfth. Twice. I did not count those."

"That was correct of you."

"I have been correct for fourteen days," said Sesk, "and I would give a year's fee to know what it has bought."

The flags under the frame lit.

The lamp on its bracket burned exactly as before, a yellow tongue, unchanged. This came up through the stone. Lines first, thin as a hair laid on a page, running out from under the choir-stall frame in a geometry that had no business under a cellar, curving, meeting, closing on themselves, and the light in them was the colour of the inside of a bell that has just been struck. It ran under the eleven strips on the floor and did not disturb them. It ran under Sesk's crate. It reached the wall behind the lamp and climbed it a hand's width and stopped, and the beeswax in the frame at Aurelian's back began, very faintly, to smell warm.

Under the lines, the hum changed key.

Sesk was on his feet. The ledger had gone off his knee onto the flags and lay open with its one line up and he had not looked at it, which in fourteen days Aurelian had not seen him fail to do.

"Fuck." He backed one pace and his heel found the crate and he did not look down at that either. "What. What the fuck did you do."

Aurelian laughed.

It came up out of the sockets first, wet, and then out of his chest, and it hurt in the shoulder that had been put back twice and in the ribs the chain had been resting his whole weight on since the evening, and it brought blood off his lower lip in a thread that ran and hung and fell into the light on the floor and went black in it. He had no idea why he was laughing. Five hundred and ninety-one, and he had said the line into a cellar for fourteen days the way a man drops a stone down a well to hear whether it has a bottom, and here was the light coming up the wall. The word had done it. He had said it enough and it had come.

"Lux," he said, and had to stop, and get his head up. "Lux. Veniet. Veniet."

Sesk had his back against the wall by the door.

"Omnis dolor abluetur." The lines were brightening. "Omnia patefient. Appendentur."

Out in the corridor, boots.

He knew the boots. Fourteen days of them, the guard's, arriving at the second hour and the sixth and the tenth, four paces short of the door, standing, going. These did not stop at four paces. They came to the door. The bar went up on the far side, badly, a hand in a hurry on the other end of it, and the door came in, and the corridor lamp behind it threw a shape onto the floor of the cell before it threw a man.

Tall once. Bent a little at the top now, the shoulders folded forward over the chest. A staff in the right hand, taller than the shape holding it, its head a thickening the lamp behind could not get through. A cap. The shape stood a moment in the door with the light of the corridor at its back and the light of the floor in front of it, and Aurelian, who had been reading the Plane of Fate for thirty-one years, felt the room's attention go to it before he had decided to give it, and go all at once, the way a congregation stands.

"Iudicabuntur," said Aurelian, because it was the end of the line and he did not stop lines in the middle.

Verinus came in.

He had dressed for the Curia. That was the first thing. The Palatine's gold, the long robe of the Alabaster Court that Aurelian had seen four times in his life and never on this man, who wore a plain coat to his own Curia and let the bench mutter about it, and it was on him now, hem to throat, and it had been put on by somebody else, because the collar sat wrong and Verinus had never in nineteen years let a collar sit wrong. Gold thread going dull at the cuffs where an earlier Palatine's wrists had worn it. The cap on top of it all, the plain grey wool one, which was absurd, and which was the only thing in the door that Aurelian entirely recognised.

Seventy-one. Hair the white of a rope's end that has been in salt water, cropped close under the cap. A long narrow face gone to vertical lines, the nose broken once a long time ago and set true, the mouth a straight line with nothing spare in it. Brown eyes. Big-knuckled hands, the right one round the staff, the fingers of it chalked to the second knuckle because he had been on the slope again, or at the basin, and had come here without washing, and had done that for six years and would do it until he died. The left knee was recalcitrant. He did not let it show, and it showed.

The staff was gold through, the metal itself or a thing that had agreed to be the metal, and it had a head on it that Aurelian could not get his eyes to hold still on, and it did not shine in the lamp. It shone in the floor's light, which went up its length along the lines of the grain, and the lines were moving.

Sesk had come off the wall. He bowed his head and held it two breaths and came up.

"My lord."

"You are the contractor."

"Corwin Sesk, my lord. Under instruction."

"I know whose." Verinus looked at the frame, and at the strips on the floor in the order they had been laid, and at the ledger open on the flags with its one line, and his face did the thing Aurelian had watched it do across a table at Alabaster on the four occasions they had sat at one, which was nothing whatsoever, because a face that softens at a reading is a lie about what the reading cost. "You kept a ledger."

"Six entries, my lord. Half of them supplies."

"Then you are the only man in this building who wrote down what he did. Stand where you are. Do not touch him. Do not touch me. Whatever you were promised for this, it does not cover the next quarter of an hour, and I will not be in a position to make up the difference."

"My lord. There is a man at the head of the stair with a written instruction that nobody comes down it."

"There was," Verinus said.

He did not explain and Sesk did not ask. The boots that had stood four paces short of that door three times a day for fourteen days stayed wherever they had gone, and in the corridor there was only the corridor.

*They will have taken the roll by now. They will have found the seat empty and the staff gone from its stand and the robe gone from the press, and somebody will have said the word abdication in a voice pitched to carry, and the Bishop will have said nothing, which is the single most useful thing he has ever done for me. Let it sit. It has sat without me in every way that mattered for nine years. There is one invoice in my drawer with my name on the bottom of it and it is nineteen years overdue and I am the last signatory still breathing.*

He came to the frame. He did not hurry, and the knee would not have let him. He stopped at the edge of the strips, at the first one, the one from the left hip, and looked down at it, and moved his foot so that the heel of the staff came down on the flag between the first strip and the second, and the light in the floor went to it and up it.

"Aurelian."

"Holiness."

"You were never a man who used that word to me."

"You were never a man who wore the robe." He could taste the sockets. "It is a strange morning."

"It is the fourteenth." Verinus's eyes went over him, hip to knee, finger to finger, the shoulder, the mouth, and Aurelian watched him count and watched him write nothing down. "They took the teeth first."

"Before Master Sesk. He stopped it."

"I have had nothing from you since the shutdown and nothing from the men who hold this Church, and I did not need it, because I have watched this order buy the same thing four times in nineteen years and I know the shape of the receipt. There is an arrangement."

"There is."

"Four people and a room of books."

Aurelian said nothing. Sesk, against the wall, said nothing, and the lines in the floor came up another degree.

"I am not going to ask you what you paid," Verinus said. "I can see what you paid. I am going to tell you what you have been paying with, because you have never let me say it to your face and I have come a long way on this knee to say it. Thirty-one years. You found the interval at twenty-seven and you have been standing in it since, putting everything on the stack and calling the stack devotion, and the Curia wrote it down as devotion, and I signed the minute." His voice did not rise. Nothing in the room rose. "I have watched three men do it before you and they are all in the day-book. A body finds a thing it does not have to pay for and it uses that thing until it stops working, and the thing always stops working, and it stops in front of whoever is standing nearest."

"It is deferred."

The flags went down.

Under the frame, under Sesk's crate, under Verinus's boots, the whole floor of the cell went down the width of a coin and stayed there, and the hum in the stone took the drop and came back a half-tone lower, and somewhere above them, a long way above, a thing that had never in fourteen days made a sound came up audible for the length of a breath and settled.

"That weighed," said Verinus, very quietly. "You believe it. I have never once in forty-five years told a man his sentence weighed when it did not, and it did. It is also the sentence I came here to answer, because deferred means that somebody pays later, and the somebody was never going to be you. It was always going to be whoever stood next to you when the bottom came out." He shifted his grip on the staff. "I have decided that it is going to be me. I have brought the paperwork."

"It is my arithmetic."

"It was. I have audited it. Four people and a room of books is a good number, and it was good on the road and it was good in this cell and I would have taken it myself at fifty-eight. I am seventy-one. I have more to put up than you have and less time to hold it, and a Court that will call whatever I do this morning devotion and put a window in for it inside a generation, which is the one outcome I have spent my life preventing and which I am, this once, going to permit." He looked at the staff. "From this hour the surety is the Palatine's. Your four and your room. This Church will find that I have signed for them in a form it cannot strike from a minute, and I want you to understand that I did it with my eyes open, and that this is worth exactly nothing to you, and that it is the only distinction I have left."

Sesk, against the wall, put his hand flat on the stone behind him.

*He is buying them. He is standing in a cellar in his Curia robe buying four people who went out through the low cloister nine days before I stood in this room and told him they were alive, and I have the roster in my coat. If I open my mouth now, whatever the old man is about to do, he does it for nothing and knows it. If I keep it shut he does it for nothing and does not. Twenty-two years. I have never in my life wanted a written instruction as badly as I want one now.*

"Do you consent," said Verinus.

"No."

"Entered."

And he began.

---

He had read the Plane of Fate for thirty-one years, and he read it now, once, because the old man's strings were doing a thing he had no word for in either tongue.

Verinus's threads were going into the staff and the staff's were coming into him, laid up together, turning, and the turn had a centre, and the centre was black. Present black. The black of a thing that is there, and is becoming more there, and is drawing every string the old man owned down into itself one turn at a time. Aurelian had seen a man's fate cut. He had seen it burned through and he had seen it knotted and he had seen it, once, in a bishop's household, snipped with something that had wanted to be called malice. He had never seen one wound onto a spool. It was a death being written, in the old man's own hand, and there was no other reading of it and he did not look for one.

On the far side, the shears stopped.

Fourteen days. Two blades on green wood and the hand moving and the blades again, and they stopped, mid-stroke, and the whole ambient of the room went still in a way it had not been still in fourteen days, and the bell rang, flat, once, and did not ring again.

He did not know what that was. He would have given the last of himself to know what that was.

"Hoc sit sacramentum."

The lines in the floor answered. Three marks came up in them under the frame, where nothing had been drawn: the devotion mark, the mark for structure, the third one the Rubric called binding ascent and the bindery boys called the ladder, and Aurelian had seen all three in ink a thousand times and never once lit.

"Hic sit ignis sanctus indignorum."

Let this be the sacrament. Let this be the holy fire of the undeserving. Church Latin, the Court's Latin, every ending exact, and the old man saying it in the voice he used to read a lesson, a voice that had not been raised in nineteen years and had not needed to be, and the hum in the stone climbing under it.

"Haec sit mors."

Let this be death.

The hum climbed. The Cloister Ward had been drinking through the night, and now the Church was drinking the ward, and the note that lived in the flags went up through the register, a grain in it, then a grinding, then a pitch, and the pitch was the sound of a thing at the edge of what it was made for, and overhead, a long way up, a gauge-housing somewhere in the Guild-sealed dark cracked, with the sound of a bell rung too hard in a frost.

Then the silence.

Every child in Altherion had heard it described, what happens to the sound of the city when a ward is cut off. The hum went out of the stone the way a heartbeat goes out of a hand. It did not fade. It stopped, and the flags were only flags, cold under the frame, and up every standpipe in the ward the water that had been coming stopped coming, and Aurelian had no way to see that and knew it anyway.

"Nam hic est ritus sacramenti," said Verinus. "Actus veritatis."

For this is the rite of Sacrament. The act of the truth.

And the Well came up.

It was never water, the thing under Altherion. It came up through the flags where the light had been, in a spiral, turning the opposite way from the black on the far Plane, and it was the colour of a thing on the Plane of Fate that Aurelian had been taught at nineteen never to look at directly, and he looked. The Well's own nature, the thing the Guild metered and the ward drank and nobody had ever seen, drawn up out of the ground in a column with a turn in it, and where it reached the old man's gold, it caught.

Fire. Gold fire, standing off the robe, off the staff, off the chalked hand, and it did not burn. It cracked. The air round Verinus went to fracture, a hundred jagged lines opening in it with light in the lines, the pattern of ice going under weight, of a pane struck at the centre, and the fractures were gold, and they were spreading, and the old word for it came up out of Aurelian's thirty-one years of reading before he could stop it. Judger's Flame. The old word, which had named the offering before it named the light. The Court had not lit it in three hundred years because the Court had ruled it too expensive, and here it was on a seventy-one-year-old man with a bad knee in a cellar under Altherion, and it was pulling the Well up through the floor to feed itself, and the Well was coming.

Nineteen years the Alabaster Court had seated him as the weakest Palatine in its rolls. That was the Court's own phrase and Aurelian had heard it at four tables and had not contradicted it, because it was true: a man whose entire craft was that a floor sank when somebody told him a true thing, who had expressed force so seldom that each occasion had a name. The Measurewrights called the axis Ardency and the Court kept a figure for his. The figure was the lowest ever entered against a Palatine's name. The man himself had read it aloud at his own investiture and asked that it be minuted. Nobody had asked what the old man did with rites, because a rite is a thing the world does, and a man only has to know the address.

Verinus rose.

The heel of the staff left the flag. His boots left the flag, a hand's width, then two, the bad knee straightening out under the hem as the weight came off it, and he hung there in the fracture-light with his cap on.

"Solvere," he said. "Solvere."

Be loosed. Be loosed.

"Et reconciliare."

And be reconciled.

"Discedite," Verinus said, and for the first time in nineteen years his voice was not level, "omnes iniquitates tuae."

Depart, all your iniquities.

The wounds went across the room.

The mouth first, because the mouth had been first. Verinus's jaw dropped a fraction and his cheeks hollowed and he made a sound, low, in the chest, a sound Aurelian knew from the inside, and then he leaned his head forward in the air and spat, and blood came out of his mouth in a sheet and four teeth came out with it and rang on the flags, four separate small sounds, out of sequence, none of them keeping time, and Aurelian's tongue went to the back of his own jaw and found gum. Closed over, no teeth in it, gum where the abscess had been, smooth and wrong and whole.

The thighs. The gold of the robe went dark from the hip on the left side in a line, and then a second line beside it, and then the right, six and five, in the order of removal, and the cloth did what cloth does over a wound that has depth. It went in. It sagged into the absence of him. And on the frame the fascia over Aurelian's own legs, that had dried to vellum, was wet, and then was skin. Hairless, pale, too smooth, the wrong skin, laid over muscle that had not been covered in nine days, and the eleven strips of him on the floor lay where Sesk had set them and did not move. The flesh stayed taken. The wound had gone across the room, and somebody else was wearing the taking now.

The fingers. Black came off Verinus's fingertips in ten threads and fell into the light.

The shoulder, a wet pop, the right side, and the old man's arm swung, and he did not lower his head.

The knee. The sound of a fired pot dropped on a flag, and the left leg under the hem went out sideways at the angle legs do not have, and it was his bad knee, the one that had complained on every cold wall in the Alabaster Court for ten years, and he did not go down, because there was nothing under him to go down to.

And then the thing Aurelian had not put on any list, because he did not have a name for it.

The stack moved.

Thirty-one years. Everything from twenty-seven onward, held at the interval, unpaid, and the bottom of it, the thing he had never written down and could no longer remember, that released on a schedule nobody had ever explained to him. It shifted. Something at the bottom of him that had been there since he was twenty-seven years old came off the floor of him and went across the room, and he felt it go, and he could not have said what it was. He would never be able to say what it was now. It had been the thing under everything, and it was under nothing, and he was resting on whatever had been under it.

On the far Plane the old man's strings went into the black at the centre of the spiral, all of them, in one turn.

Verinus's eyes were open.

Brown, and then, from the inner ring outward, gold, a warm dense metallic gold that came up through the brown and held, and behind it a light that belonged to no lamp in that building, and the fracture stood off him in every direction now, and off his shoulders, from the blades of them, in two sheets, high, that caught the Well's turning column and split it and threw it, and every painter who had ever been given a wall in Altherion had put that shape on it, and had called it wings, and had been wrong about what it was and right about the shape.

The gold of the robe was dark to the knee on both sides and shining above it, and the cap was still on.

Aurelian's feet were on the floor.

He had not felt the chain let go. The ring in the ceiling had let it go and the weight had come off his shoulders and gone down through two straight legs into two whole feet, and ten nails, seated in nothing now, came out of ten fingers under their own weight and rang on the stone, out of sequence, none of them keeping time. He put his weight on the flags. All of it, for the first time in fourteen days, and the stone under him took it and went down the width of a coin, and stayed down.

## Notes (author-facing, not canon)

**POV.** Held on Aurelian, as in *The Word Under the Hammer*. Sesk cannot see the Plane, so your string-and-spiral beat needed a Fate-reader's eyes. Verinus's decision about the Curia is carried in his one italic thought and his dialogue, not in narration. Your beats are all present and in your order: the floor lighting, Sesk swearing, Aurelian laughing and finishing the line, the staff winding into his strings and the black spiral, the Latin, the Well torn up and drunk by the Flame, the weakest-Palatine statement, the rite as his hidden craft, the Reconciliation and the wounds crossing, the rise, the robe, the wings.

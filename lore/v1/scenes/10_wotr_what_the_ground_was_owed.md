# What the Ground Was Owed

*Kharven. The year after.*

---

He had lost his daughter and there was no arrangement of the world in which that was survivable, and he survived it, and both of those were true at the same time for eleven months and are true still.

Sodoku Moto did not grieve in any manner the Seat could recognise. There was no shutting of a door and no drinking and no long silence in a cold room, and there was nothing anybody could point at afterward and say that was the week he broke, and the absence of a week like that was what frightened the people around him. He got up. He ate what was put in front of him. He sat the councils and read the returns and asked the correct questions about grain and about the standpipes and about which valleys had lost their ward-keepers, and he did all of it with a competence nobody had seen out of him before the ninth hour, and the competence was the symptom.

What he had instead of grief was a passion, and it had one object, and the object was that the thing which had happened in that cistern doorway would not happen again to anybody's daughter in any valley under the sky, and that this required the four quarters of the north to stop being four quarters.

He was in solace. That is the word Yoko used for it and she used it once and never again. Peace was no part of it and neither was resignation. Solace: the state of a man who has been given the only consolation available to him and has found that it is enough to stand on and not enough to sleep on. He worked eighteen hours and slept five and was courteous to everyone and buried nobody twice, and Emira, who was eighteen and had been watching men lie to themselves since she was four, told Yoko in the third month that she had stopped being able to find the place where her brother ended and the work began, and that she had gone looking for it eleven times and had not found it, and that she was not going to look again because looking was beginning to feel like an accusation.

The south went first because the south was always going to go first.

Edward Lambert did the honours for the dead and there were three thousand and eleven of them in the southern registers alone, and he did every one himself and would not delegate a name, and the reason was the reason it always is with that man, which is that a name delegated is a name approximated and an approximated name is a small lie told about a person who is no longer in a position to correct it. He sat in the Seat's lower hall for six weeks with a clerk on either side and read out the returns from the reach, valley by valley, and had the parish rolls fetched when a return gave a byname instead of an entered one, and where no roll existed he sent riders, and where the riders came back empty he entered the person by what they were called in their own village and marked the entry as unconfirmed and put his initial beside it so the responsibility was legible.

Three thousand and eleven. Fifty-three years old, grey at the temple and nowhere else, collar done up to the throat, gloves on indoors, and a wax tablet he no longer wrote on because there was nothing left worth noting.

He also, in the same six weeks, orchestrated the destruction of the orc host in the southern reach, and did it without ever once being present at an engagement.

That is the thing they will remember about the southern campaign and it is the thing the Bench will spend forty years arguing about, because there is no battle in it with his name on it and there is no order in the file that anyone can point to and call a masterpiece. What there is instead is a supply schedule. Eleven weeks of it, in a dry hand, itemised, and any officer who reads it for the first time comes away thinking he has read an administrative document, and any officer who reads it twice comes away cold.

He starved them out of a country they thought they had taken.

The orc advance out of the Ashenmoor had gone eleven miles past the wall in the first month and had held, and holding is expensive, and Lambert had done nothing about the holding at all. What he did was buy grain. He bought it in Nalūn through four factors who did not know about each other and he bought it at prices that made the Vaultmere houses laugh at him, and he shipped it north, and he did not distribute one bushel of it to any settlement in the southern reach. He let those settlements empty. He let them walk north with what they could carry, on roads he had ordered cleared and picketed six weeks before there was any visible reason to clear them, and he received them at Ashwell and at the Stormfold crossings and fed them out of grain nobody could work out why he had bought.

And he left the orc host in possession of eleven miles of empty country with the winter coming and eleven thousand mouths and no standing crop, and every well salted by the people who had dug them, and every root cache emptied by the families who had cut them, and the whole of it done voluntarily and in advance and without a single order being issued that could be entered as scorched-earth, because Lambert had never issued one. He had simply moved the people and their food out from under an army and left the army standing on ground that had nothing in it.

They ate their draught animals in the second month. They ate the sick in the third and there is a deposition about that which Lambert read once and had entered and never referred to again.

When Bram Greymane came south in the fourth month with eleven hundred men, what he found was not an army. He said so in his report, in four words, and the four words are quoted in every staff college in the Inner World.

*"There was nothing to fight."*

And the thing that will be argued about for forty years is not whether it worked. It is a question Tabitha Hallenfeld asked in open council in the seventh month, in front of the Queen's chair and the whole assembly, and which nobody answered then and nobody has answered since.

*"Why did you buy the grain in the first week?"*

Because the advance came in the fourth. Because on the day Edward Lambert placed his first order with a factor in Vaultmere, no orc had crossed the wall, and no council had been called, and no threat assessment existed in any file in the Seat that would have justified the expenditure, and he spent eleven thousand in Kharven's coin on a contingency that had not yet occurred and told nobody what it was for.

He never answered her. He put his hands behind his back and waited, and the council moved on because councils do, and Tabitha Hallenfeld went home and wrote the question down and dated it, and has asked it twice since in private and been refused twice.

The Great Battle of Stormfold was fought over four days in the fifth month at the crossings above the Stormfold hall, and it is the largest set engagement in the recorded history of the Inner World, and it did not need to happen.

That is the part nobody says out loud in the halls. The host was already broken. The starving was already done. What came over the Stormfold crossings in the fifth month was a thing that had stopped being an army and had become eleven thousand desperate creatures moving north because north was where the food had gone, and Bram Greymane put himself across their line with eleven hundred men and four hundred Ashwell tenants and did not let one of them through.

He held the crossings for four days.

The first day was a fight. The second day was a fight and the fighting was worse because the ground had gone to mud and mud is the great leveller of formations, and on the third day it stopped being a fight in any sense a manual would recognise and became a slaughter conducted uphill in freezing rain by men who had been awake for sixty hours against creatures who had been starving for ninety days, and on the fourth day the Stormfold hall's own bell rang the whole afternoon because the woman ringing it had gone into the kind of state where a body does one thing and cannot be persuaded to stop.

Bram Greymane lost the last three fingers of his left hand on the second day, taken off at the second joint by a falchion he never saw, and he wrapped the hand in a strip of his own surcoat and went on holding a shield with a forearm strap and a stump for the rest of the engagement, and when they finally got the wrapping off in the Stormfold hall on the fifth morning the surgeon said out loud, in front of him, that he had never seen a man do that to himself on purpose.

He has the scars from the second day across the whole left side of his jaw and down the neck. He has a bad hip from the third and walks with the left foot turned out. He has a mark across the back of the skull that his hair no longer covers, and the shield that came off the ridge with him, the same one, the one he threw down once and picked back up, went into the Stormfold hall with a boss cracked through in two directions and has never been rebound and hangs there still.

He lived. That is the whole of what the campaign got him and it took him eleven months to work out whether it was a gain.

Because he learned about his brother in the eighth month.

It came in the ordinary way, which is the cruelty of it. A courier out of the north with a bundle of returns, and among the returns a Reckoner's entry from the Ashgate road with a list of the dead on it, and Wren Greymane's name in the middle of the second column with a note beside it that read *ditch bank, entered by field observation, body not recovered*.

And underneath that, in the same bundle, because Lambert believed that a man learning a thing should learn all of it at once, sixteen depositions from men who had marched sixteen months in a Tenrai column under a Greymane banner.

Bram Greymane read them in a room in the Stormfold hall with the door shut and did not come out for a day and a half.

Sixteen months. Six hundred men chosen for faith and never for skill. Eleven of them with wives who had stopped writing. Cass Holloway burying his mother in the autumn and not being permitted to attend and saying *aye* and going back to the horses. Three killed by their own kin, entered in a book. Two hundred and ten feet at the narrows, counted in summer under a false name with a cart of salt fish, drawn on the back of a psalter.

The smile across two hundred paces of dead men on the ridge.

And nowhere in sixteen depositions, in any hand, from any man, one line explaining why nobody had been told, because none of the sixteen had known either.

He came out of that room on the second morning and gave one order, and the order is entered in the Stormfold book in his own hand with the fingers he had left, and it is four lines long and it is the only thing he ever said about it in public.

*"This campaign is dedicated to Wren Greymane, who was called traitor in every village between here and the Stormfold for sixteen months, by me among others. Any man who says the word in my hearing from this day will answer to me personally. Enter the dedication in the roll. Enter it before my own name."*

Then he went back to the crossings and finished it.

What was left of the Tenrai after Kujo's army came apart was very little and it came north in pieces over the sixth and seventh months, and Kharven took them in as refugees, which is the single most contested decision of the entire post-war and which Sodoku Moto made alone and against the advice of every officer in his service.

They came in wagons and on foot and eleven of them came in a boat that should not have floated. They came having eaten what the orc host had left, which was nothing, and having been the enemy for eleven years, and having in a great many cases personally burned villages that were now expected to feed them. There were four thousand two hundred at the first count and three thousand nine hundred at the second, and the difference is entered as *attrition on the road* and everybody in the Seat knew what the difference actually was and nobody made a record of it.

Lorn Stark would have refused them. Lorn Stark is dead.

Osric would have refused them and would have said so at volume and would have been right on every count he raised. Osric is dead.

Hild would have taken them and would have been called soft for it for the rest of her life and would have carried that, and Hild is dead, and that is the reason her father took them, and he has never once said so.

And Miku Tenrai Moto came in with the eighth column.

They had him in the middle of a wedge of eleven men when the Kharven riders came up on them at the Brine crossing, and the eleven had been with him since the collapse and had walked him four hundred miles and had eaten less than he had, and they closed around him and went to a knee with their weapons out and their faces up, which is the Tenrai posture for a household guard that intends to die where it is standing and wishes the fact entered.

Sodoku Moto rode into the middle of that on Tōga with the frost coming off the animal in a front and eleven hundred men behind him.

He asked once.

He asked in form, in the old register, which is a courtesy the Tenrai had not extended to a Moto in eleven years, and he asked them to stand aside and put the weapons in the mud and be taken in as the others had been taken in, and he gave his word on the boy's life in terms that any Reckoner in four quarters would have entered as binding.

They refused.

They refused because they had been told for eleven years, by men who believed it, that the Kinslayer King had opened his own family's throats in a settlement in the south and that a Moto in his hands is a Moto in a box, and because that was true, and because there was nothing in that man's history that a frightened household guard could weigh against it. Their captain said so out loud. He was perhaps forty and had a face like a gate that had been kicked in and he said, in Common so the boy would understand, that the Tenrai household did not hand its heir to the man who had done the Tenrai settlement, and that they would be obliged if he would get on with it.

Sodoku Moto got down off the Droval.

He killed all eleven and it took him under a minute and he did it with Kurosetsu and he did not use the cold, and Yoko, who was there, entered afterward in her own hand that the refusal to use the cold was the worst thing about it, because the cold would have been faster and the cold was available and a stilling would have put the whole eleven down inside a second and a half without one of them feeling the finish.

He cut them apart in the mud in front of the child instead, one at a time, in the order they came at him, because the boy had been told what he was and there was no version of that afternoon in which the boy did not find out whether it was true, and Sodoku Moto had decided somewhere on the ride down that the finding out would be honest.

The captain went last and went badly. He got a hand on the harness and got the point of a knife into the mail at the hip and it stopped there, and Sodoku broke his arm at the elbow with the flat and then opened him from the collarbone down through the sternum with a cut that came up instead of down, and stood over him for the three or four seconds it took, and did not look away, and did not permit the man the courtesy of being finished quickly, because a household guard who has said what he said in front of his charge has earned the entire truth of the position he took.

Then he cleaned the blade on his own sleeve, which is a thing no king does and which every man in that column saw.

And he turned round and there was a nine-year-old boy standing in eleven men's blood in the mud at the Brine crossing, trembling so hard he could not keep his feet under him, and Sodoku Moto walked over and went down onto one knee in front of him and put his hand on the boy's head.

"You don't have to tremble."

Miku Tenrai Moto could not answer. His whole frame was going and his teeth were going and he had wet himself and had not noticed and would notice later and would remember the noticing longer than he remembered the killing.

"I am not going to hurt you. I promise."

He said it in Common. He said everything in Common that afternoon and he had thought about it on the ride down and had decided that the boy had been given enough things he could not follow.

"Your family has been telling you a lot of things. Corrupting your mind. Making you think that you are destined to continue their hatred." His hand did not move off the boy's head. "But you see it all, don't you."

And Miku closed his eyes and the tears came down his face, and he did not answer because the answer was so far past anything a nine-year-old has words for that the crying was the whole of it.

The Tenrai were gifted with the temporal, which is the thing every hall in the Inner World knows about them and which nobody outside the line has ever properly understood. It is not prophecy and it is not a vision and it does not arrive when it is called. It is the sight of the road forward, given without warning and without mercy, at whatever length the Plane has decided the bearer can carry.

He had watched all of it. Every man who had ever fed him or carried him or wrapped his hands in winter. He had watched them die at the ages he would be when it happened, one at a time, over four years, from the age of five, and he had told an adult about it exactly once and had been beaten for it and had never told anyone again.

He had watched this. The mud, and the eleven, and the captain, and the man on one knee with a hand on his head.

And he had come to the Brine crossing anyway, because there is no version of the temporal that lets a child do anything about it, and because a nine-year-old who knows what is coming still cries when it arrives, and being unable to stop yourself from being vulnerable while watching yourself be vulnerable in advance is a specific torture that the Tenrai have never once written down.

Sodoku moved his hand and put it flat on the boy's back.

"It isn't your fault." He kept it there. "You were destined to carry the hatred of your line. But there is no such thing as lines. We are all one family, Miku. We are all Moto. We are all descendants of Bara. Do you understand?"

The boy did not understand. He was nine.

But he understood the weight. That was the thing about Miku Tenrai Moto and it had been the thing about him since he was five, that he had spent his entire short life watching adults change how they stood when he came into a room, watching them lower their voices and raise their chins and hand him things, and he had worked out very early and entirely alone that the weight was not about him and that it was about something he was carrying, and that everybody could see it except him.

He understood that the man in front of him was asking him to put it down.

Sodoku pulled back and sat on his heels in the mud so their eyes were level.

"We have to do better. We have to be better. For the sake of these lands." He let it sit. "With you. With your support, we can end this endless fight."

And the boy said nothing for a long time, and then said the thing that ended eleven years of war, in a small flat voice, with his hands at his sides.

"I will forfeit. And tell all of my men to pull away. And order whoever is fighting against you to stop."

Sodoku Moto did not thank him.

That is entered in three separate depositions and every one of them notices it. He did not thank the child and he did not smile and he did not tell him he had done well, and Yoko has said since that it was the single most correct thing she has ever watched him do, because a man who thanks a nine-year-old for surrendering an empire has told the nine-year-old that a transaction occurred.

What he said instead was the offer, and the offer was already made before he came down the road, and the boy's answer had changed nothing about it, and that was the point.

"You don't need to worry. Your family will be treated as royalty." He got up out of the mud with the old sound his knees made. "In fact all those, all those of the Moto lines, will take part in becoming the Royal Family of the new empire. The one my father always wanted to build. A union. A body of peace."

Miku Tenrai Moto looked up at him.

And whatever he saw, standing in eleven men's blood at the Brine crossing at nine years old with the temporal running behind his eyes, he has never told anybody, and he is fourteen now and holds a seat at the table and has been asked eleven times, and the answer he gives is always the same and it is always four words.

*"I saw him mean it."*

Seiji Tenrai Moto was taken alive in the ninth month at a hunting lodge four miles inside the Amagiri line by men who had been his own household guard eleven weeks earlier, and there is no deposition anywhere that describes how he was found, because the men who found him were paid and dismissed and none of them has ever been asked.

What is entered is what happened afterward, and it was entered by a Reckoner who was made to stand and watch it and who has since retired and does not speak about it.

He crawled.

That is the first line of the entry and the Reckoner wrote it plainly because it was the plain fact of the matter. Twenty-nine years old, the architect of thirteen years of a design that had cost the north perhaps two hundred thousand lives, and the smile that reached his eyes because he had trained it to, and at the end of it he crawled backward across a lodge floor on his elbows with his heels pushing and his hands up.

"Please spare... me my lord..."

His father's face had been a wall. Say nothing. Be overlooked. Seiji's face had been a door, and the door had worked for thirteen years on everybody it was pointed at, and at the very end he tried it one more time on a man who had spent a lifetime learning what a face is for, and it did not open, and he had nothing behind it.

The chains came up around his legs.

They came out of the floor and out of the air and out of nothing at all, and the Reckoner's entry describes them accurately and at length and the entry is unpleasant to read, because what they did to the man's legs before they reached his waist was structural. The bonds of a body are not metaphor. A body is a set of agreements between parts about staying joined, and Calamity is the argument against every one of those agreements simultaneously, and what it does first is win the small ones.

*"Spare you."*

*"Where was that mercy when you killed my brothers?"*

*"Where was that when we asked you to stop?"*

There is a pause entered between the third and the fourth, and the Reckoner has marked the length of it, and the length is eleven seconds.

*"Seiji. I revoke the name Moto from you."*

*"You're no Moto. You're just a snake dressed up as a Moto. Gifted blood you cannot live up to."*

*"Enjoy this torment."*

And it was slow, and it was meant to be slow, and it took the better part of an hour.

Calamity does not burn and does not cut. What it does is remove the permission by which a structure continues, one structure at a time, from the smallest upward, and a human body has a very great many of those and the small ones are the vessels. Every blood vessel in Seiji Tenrai's body came apart, from the capillary beds outward, over fifty-one minutes, and the man was conscious for forty-eight of them because the brain is well supplied and goes last.

He stopped being able to scream at around the thirtieth minute. He did not stop trying.

There is one further line in the Reckoner's entry and it is the last one and it is the reason the entry is sealed.

*The revocation was performed in form. Full register, correct contours, before a witness. It is valid. The name Moto has been taken off him and cannot be given back by anybody, and he died without one, and I have entered him as elar-ama, contingent, held by nobody, and there is no other form available to me.*

*I have been a Reckoner for twenty-two years and this is the first time I have had to enter a man as having no name while he was still audible in the next room.*

Edward Lambert went to the graves on the morning of the first frost with his hands behind his back and stood in front of three stones in the upper burying ground at Kharven-Seat and did not kneel and did not remove anything.

Hild Ice. Twelve years old. Lorn Stark, who had made an arrangement about that girl twelve years ago and had kept every part of it. Osric of Hallenfeld, who had put himself in a doorway.

They had been cut and set in the same week by the same mason, and the mason had matched the depth of the lettering across all three at Lambert's own instruction, because a difference in depth between stones cut in one week says something about relative importance and Edward Lambert had spent fifty-three years refusing to let a document say anything he had not intended.

Brida came up the path behind him.

She should not have been walking. She had taken it across the abdomen at the sally gate in the ninth hour and had been eleven days in the altar-room with the night-stone ticking as it cooled beside her, and the wound had been what she herself called the third bowl, the one where they stop asking for water because they have forgotten they were thirsty, and she had gone into it and come back out of it, which almost nobody does. She was walking with a stick and would be for a year. Her colour was wrong and would be wrong for longer than that.

She had been on that path forty minutes because it is four hundred yards.

She stood beside him and did not say anything for a while and then said the true thing, which is what she does, which is why the Seat sends her to the ones who will not talk.

"It must be hard to be the oldest," she said. "And be the only one alive."

Edward Lambert did not answer immediately.

Then he looked down at the fourth stone, which was set apart from the other three by the width of a path, and which was smaller, and which had less on it, because there had been less to put on it: a name, and an office, and no dates, because the parish book at Vaeloris that held his brother's dates no longer contained them and nobody in four worlds could say why.

Charles Lambert. Archbishop. Nothing else.

There is no record of what happened to the man. There is a hole where the record should be. The ordination roll has a gap, the vestry minutes for nineteen years are missing a hand that everyone remembers reading, the gate-roll for the morning has no entry, and the Alabaster correspondence file has a nine-year run in a hand nobody can now attribute. Edward had ridden south himself in the second month and had spent six weeks in three archives and had come back with nothing at all, and had never told anybody what he had been doing, and everybody had known.

"If only I was stronger."

He said it to the small stone.

"But only vengeance." His hands stayed behind his back. "Only rage."

Brida put her hand on his arm and left it there and did not tell him it was not his fault, because she had been eleven days in the third bowl and had come out of it with no patience left for the sentences people say at gravesides.

They stood there a while.

And that is where Robin Ice found him, seventeen years old, having ridden through the night as he does, with blood on his left ear that was not his as usual and a courier's coat two sizes too big cinched round twice.

"Edward."

He had stopped saying *my lord* in the fourth month and had never been corrected.

"We have come to report. We hanged all the members of the Goblin Senate." He got his breath. "The Hobgoblins want to speak about peace offerings."

Edward Lambert turned round.

Robin Ice had ridden a hundred and eleven miles in eleven hours and had seen the man's face in every condition it comes in, at the wall and in the hall and on the road, and he said afterward, to a woman in a tavern in the Cooper's quarter who did not believe him, that the face which turned round in the upper burying ground that morning was one he had never seen before and has not seen since, and that there was nothing in it at all.

"Kill all of the hostages."

Robin Ice did not move.

"They are to be examples why they should never raise a hand at the Kingdom of Kharven."

There were four hundred and six hostages. They were the surety for a truce negotiated in the sixth month by Lambert's own office, under his own seal, and eleven of them were children under twelve, and the seal on the instrument was his, and the instrument is in the file with his initial beside the clause on their treatment.

Robin Ice stood on that path for what he later put at four seconds.

Then he said "Aye," and turned his horse, and rode, and did it, and has never once said in any hall that he questioned the order, and has told two people privately that he did not, and both of them believe him, and the reason they believe him is that Robin Ice is a runner and a runner who begins weighing the traffic stops being one.

Brida Ashwell watched the boy go down the path.

"Edward."

"No."

"I have not said anything."

"You have been standing beside me for a quarter of an hour and I have heard all of it."

She let a moment go past.

"Your brother would have."

"My brother," said Edward Lambert, "is a hole in an ordination roll." He was still looking down the path. "And he did whatever it was he did, and nobody knows what it was, and there is no version of the man left that anybody can appeal to. Not by me, not by you, and not by the four hundred and six."

"And if there were."

"Then I would still have given the order," he said, "and I would have had to look at him afterward, and I have been spared that, and I would like it noted that I am aware of what I just said."

He put his gloves on properly and went down to the hall and sat the afternoon council and asked the correct questions about grain.

Bram Greymane stood over the commander of the Third Ashfold in the lower yard of the Stormfold hall on the eleventh day of the tenth month with a cup of something hot in his good hand.

Vresk Dokkan of Kaadre-Six had been Vorruk-Kaan for eleven years. In Old Vaross a name is a service record: given name, patronymic, district, and the rank compound, and only the compound changes in a lifetime, and it changes at every promotion and every demotion, so that any hobgoblin in the Expanse hearing a man's name hears exactly what the institution currently thinks of him. *Vorruk*, ground that tried to move and was stopped. *Kaan*, the hand set to a thing.

The officer whose function is holding ground. Eleven years, and never once given a compound that meant taking any, and he had been sent north to take some, and he had broken forty feet of the Kharven wall in four nights of patient work under a shed roof of his own making, and had then met three hundred men in a gap twenty-two feet wide.

Bram crouched so their faces were level, which took him a while now because of the hip.

"Remember when your men took my fingers."

He held up the left hand. Three stumps at the second joint, healed hard and shiny, the skin over them still bright pink at eleven months.

"You might be asking why I've taken my time to mutilate every bit of you."

He had. He had taken eleven days. It had begun with a fingertip and had gone on from there in a specific order, and the order had a document behind it, and the document was a roll.

"I've taken a fingertip, then all of your toes, for each of my comrades who died during this war."

There were more names on the roll than there were extremities on a hobgoblin, and Bram Greymane had known that on the first morning and had gone ahead anyway, and had moved to other things when he ran out, and had entered each one against a name, and the roll is in the Stormfold book with a mark beside every entry in a hand with three fingers missing from it.

Egil Vald is on it. Cass Holloway is on it, from the depositions, entered by a man who never met him.

Wren Greymane is not on it, and the omission is deliberate, and Bram has never explained it.

"I don't want to kill you."

He said it without heat. That is the thing everyone who was in that yard has said since: there was no heat in any part of it, at any point, on any of the eleven days.

"I am going to return you back to the Hobgoblin Imperials, so they'll see what I've made of their commander."

And that is the cruelty, and it is not the mutilation, and Bram Greymane knew exactly what he was doing because Lambert had briefed him on Old Vaross naming in the fourth month and Bram had listened.

A hobgoblin's name is his record. Vresk Dokkan was going back alive, and the Keth-Gorrum would have to look at what came through the gate, and would have to give him a compound, because a returning officer is renamed and there is no provision anywhere for declining to.

And there is no compound in four hundred years of Old Vaross for what he now was.

It could not be *vorruk*, because he had not held, and it could not be any of the taking compounds, because he had not taken. He was not dead and could not be entered as dead. He was not a captive, because he had been returned. Whatever the Keth-Gorrum wrote beside his name would be a new word, coined for him, and every hobgoblin in the Expanse would hear it and would know it was new, and would know why, for as long as the man lived and afterward.

Bram Greymane had not maimed a commander. He had made a nation invent a word.

"I want you to remember the name of the person who took your virginity."

He put his hand on the man's chin, which had very little left on it, and moved the head so the eyes came round, and smacked it twice, lightly, the way you rouse a drunk.

"Don't die now."

And then he stood up, on the hip, with the cup still in his good hand, and gave the order to load him into the cart, and went inside out of the rain, and drank the rest of it sitting by the fire in the Stormfold hall under a shield with a boss cracked in two directions, and did not say another word for the rest of the evening to anybody.

And in the spring, under a sky that four countries had agreed to describe with the same word, the instrument was drawn.

Kharven, and the Holy Sea of Alabaster, and the Kingdom of Nalūn, and the stretches from the Brine crossing east to the marches of Sum-gol, under one seal and one body of law, with the Moto lines seated as the royal house and a fourteen-year-old boy with the temporal running behind his eyes holding a chair at the table by right of a forfeiture he made at nine years old in eleven men's blood.

The first Accord circle. The thing Muken Moto had wanted and had died four years short of asking for.

They signed it in the lower hall at Kharven-Seat with three thousand and eleven names read out beforehand by a man in gloves who would not delegate one of them, and a woman with a stick standing at the back who should not have been on her feet, and a man with three fingers who had walked in from the Stormfold road and would not sit down, and a king who had lost his daughter and had built a country out of the passion that came after and had still not slept a full night since the ninth hour.

Sodoku Moto put his hand to it last.

And Yoko Mishiro, who had been chained once and had been unchained by that man and had spent nine years finding out what he was, wrote in her own record that night, in the plain form she uses for everything, four lines that the Bench has never been permitted to see.

*He built it for her. Every clause. The refugee clause, the royal house clause, the one about children of the defeated lines. All of it is a twelve-year-old girl in a doorway and none of it says so and none of it ever will.*

*He will not say it because saying it would make her the reason, and he does not want her to be a reason. He wants her to be a person who was killed.*

*I have been beside this man for nine years and this is the first thing he has done that I do not think I could have done.*

*And it is going to hold. That is the part nobody has understood yet. It is going to hold, because a thing built out of grief that refuses to name the grief has no argument in it that anybody can get a grip on, and there is nothing in that instrument for a future council to repeal.*

### The commander, found in the history

**Vresk Dokkan of Kaadre-Six, Vorruk-Kaan of the Third Ashfold.** He is the one who gave the *Grr-Vaa* speech to 2,200 men on the ash-field below Kaadre-Six, and the 2,200 who broke forty feet of Kharven wall in four patient nights and met Bram's three hundred in a twenty-two-foot gap. Same force. It closes.

**And his name is what makes Bram's cruelty land.** Old Vaross naming is a service record: given name, patronymic, district, rank compound, and **only the compound changes in a lifetime**, at every promotion and every demotion. *Vorruk* is ground that tried to move and was stopped; *kaan* is the hand set to a thing. Eleven years as the officer whose function is holding ground, never once given a compound that meant taking any.

So returning him alive is the atrocity. The Keth-Gorrum must give a returning officer a compound, and there is no compound in four hundred years for what he now is — he did not hold, he did not take, he is not dead, he is not captive. Whatever they write beside his name will be **a new word, coined for him**, and every hobgoblin in the Expanse will hear that it is new and know why.

**Bram did not maim a commander. He made a nation invent a word.** And Lambert briefed him on Old Vaross naming in the fourth month, and Bram listened.

**Wren is not on the mutilation roll.** Deliberate. Bram has never explained it.

### What I added, as asked

**Sodoku kills the eleven.** They go to a knee around the boy in the Tenrai household posture and refuse in Common so the child will understand, and their captain says out loud that the Tenrai do not hand their heir to the man who did the settlement — **and he is right, and that is why they die.** Sodoku gets off the Droval and does it with Kurosetsu and **does not use the cold**, and Yoko's entry says the refusal to use the cold is the worst part, because a stilling would have put all eleven down inside a second and a half without one of them feeling the finish. **Miku's temporal is the horror.** He watched every person who ever fed him die at the age he would be when it happened, from five onward, told an adult once and was beaten for it. He watched this. **Sodoku does not thank him**, and three depositions notice it, because thanking a nine-year-old for surrendering an empire tells him a transaction occurred. The offer was already made before he rode down and the boy's answer changed nothing about it, and that is the point.

**Lambert's southern campaign has no battle in it.** He starved them out with a supply schedule: bought grain in Nalūn through four factors who did not know about each other, shipped it north, distributed none of it in the southern reach, and let the settlements empty voluntarily onto roads he had cleared six weeks before there was a visible reason. **No scorched-earth order was ever issued** because he never needed one — he moved the people and their food out from under an army and left the army standing on ground with nothing in it.

**And Tabitha's question is the thing that will be argued about for forty years:** *why did you buy the grain in the first week?* The advance came in the fourth. He never answered. She wrote it down and dated it and has been refused twice since.

**Edward's brother is Charles**, and the graveside works because of the Imperceptible District: the ordination roll has a gap, nineteen years of vestry minutes are missing a hand everyone remembers reading, the gate-roll has no entry. Edward rode south himself, spent six weeks in three archives, came back with nothing, told nobody, and everybody knew. *"My brother is a hole in an ordination roll."*

### On the elves

**Nothing, yet, and that is correct.** The Korvaeth war is the New World, a separate continent, a separate calendar and a fight about names as property. This is the Inner World and a fight about who feeds whom in winter. They do not touch and they should not be forced to.

**But there are three places they could touch, and one of them is already open.**

**The Concord.** Greysalt was a Concord chapter-hold. If the Concord has houses in both worlds it is the only body that has to have a position on both wars, and a body that has to have two positions eventually has two factions.

**The Accord circle itself.** Sodoku has just built the Inner World's first union out of four constituents that had every reason to hate each other. Ilthára Korvaeth is trying to hold nine hundred miles with a contingent name and five houses. One of those two is a working model of the thing the other one needs, and neither knows the other exists.

**And the one already open: Velthaeir keeps the record for four quarters.** They entered the Korvaeth finding. That is where I would join them, and I would do it in about eleven years of story time, and I would do it with a jurist rather than an army.

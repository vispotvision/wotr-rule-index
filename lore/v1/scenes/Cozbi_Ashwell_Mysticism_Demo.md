The village had been called Ashwell before the fires, and the name still fit, because ash was what remained of it and the well at its center was the only thing standing. Renn crouched behind the well's cracked stone lip with his spear held sideways across his knees, breathing through his mouth so the smell wouldn't reach the back of his throat and undo him. It didn't work. Ashwell smelled of a smithy that had forgotten how to stop burning, hot metal and old blood, and beneath both of those something sweeter, something that reminded him of the tallow candles his mother used to melt down for wick-ends, except wrong, except cooked from the inside out. The stone under his palms was still warm from yesterday's fire, gritty with soot that came away black on his skin no matter how many times he wiped it. Somewhere close a support beam ticked as it cooled, an irregular little pulse, a clock that had given up keeping time and settled for just being heard.

He was nineteen and he had joined the Moto line for the stipend and the boots, and neither of those things had prepared him for what was walking through what used to be the market square.

His unit had ridden in at first light expecting a relief posting, a quiet garrison town with a well worth defending and a captain who wrote careful letters home about grain tallies. They'd found the gate standing open on one hinge and no birds anywhere above the rooftops, not one, the whole sky over Ashwell scoured clean of the small ordinary noise a village makes without noticing it makes it. Sergeant Voss had gone in first, the way Voss always went in first, and Renn had watched him cross the threshold and simply stop being visible, no cry, no clash of steel, just a man walking into a doorway and the doorway keeping him. That silence was the worst part of the whole morning until the woman appeared in the square an hour later, because at least the silence had let him imagine something merciful had happened. He didn't have that mercy left in him now. He'd used the last of it up on Voss, and there wasn't more where that had come from.

She wasn't hurrying. That was the first thing Renn's body understood, some old animal part of him that had learned to read predators before it learned to read anything else. Hurrying belonged to things worried about running out of time. Cozbi moved as a debt moves through a ledger, unbothered, collected whenever she got around to it, and the eleven soldiers of Ashwell's garrison scattered across the square hadn't managed to change her mind about the schedule. She stepped over a fallen banner pole without looking down. Her boots left prints in the ash that filled in behind her, the fine gray dust folding itself back into stillness, a tide that preferred not to remember exactly where she'd walked.

The other ten hadn't died clean. Renn had made himself look once, early, before his mind learned to stop him doing that. Corporal Amsel lay face-down near the well's south face with both hands still curled around the haft of a spear that had never left the ground, his back arched up off the stone at an angle a spine shouldn't hold on its own, a scream frozen partway between his throat and the open air. Two others lay close together near the collapsed grain cart, close enough that Renn couldn't say for certain where one man's blood ended and the other's began, both of them missing the particular slackness a body gets once whatever had been driving it stops. They were men caught mid-stride and abandoned there, animated one instant and dropped the next, puppets whose strings had simply been let go rather than cut.

The captain was still alive. Renn could see that much from behind the well, the man propped against the well's opposite face with both hands pressed to a wound in his side that should have killed him an hour ago and somehow hadn't, not yet, not while Cozbi still wanted something from him. His breath came shallow and counted, each inhale smaller than the last, a man rationing air he'd already decided wouldn't last him.

*If she looks this way I am going to die behind a well, an animal caught in its own trap,* Renn thought, and the thought arrived flat and almost calm, stripped of the terror that should have carried it, because there wasn't room left in his chest for terror once it had been this full this long.

Cozbi crouched in front of the captain, unhurried, a woman examining a flower she wasn't certain was worth picking. Up close the wrongness of her wasn't in her face, which stayed gentle, almost kind, the face of someone who had stopped needing her expression to perform anything for anybody. The wrongness lived in the air around her, gone still in a manner air does not achieve on its own. The dust that should have drifted on the afternoon's low wind hung instead, suspended, every fleck of ash a held breath refusing to be let go. The captain's own breath fogged out pale in front of him though the day carried no chill at all, and the fog did not dissipate. It hung there too, a small stubborn cloud the air had declined to take back.

She let the silence sit a while before she spoke, the particular patience of a woman who had an afternoon and nothing else demanding it of her.

"You held them past the point it did any good," Cozbi said. Her voice was low, unhurried, without weight behind it beyond the weight of the words themselves. "Eleven men, for a well and a grain store the Concord wrote off three seasons back. I want you to understand that before we finish this. Nobody is coming. Nobody was ever coming."

The captain's mouth moved, some word or plea Renn couldn't hear, blood at the corner of it gone black rather than red in a way that turned Renn's stomach over on itself.

*He thinks the begging matters,* Cozbi thought, watching the shape his mouth made without troubling to read the words in it. *It never has. I stopped listening for the words years ago. I listen for what happens after them instead, and what happens after is always the same.*

"I know," she said, as though he had asked her something instead of simply bleeding at her feet. "It shouldn't feel this quiet, should it. You'd expect a thing this size to announce itself. Most of them expect that. Most of them are wrong."

She placed two fingers against his sternum, no more force in the gesture than a woman confirming a door was locked, and the captain's spine arched up off the stone behind him with a violence that owed nothing to pain alone, a violence built from something being drawn through a space too narrow to hold it. His mouth opened wide enough that Renn heard the small tearing sound at the corner of his jaw, and no sound came out of it, none at all, though every muscle in the man's throat worked with the full effort of a scream that had nowhere left to travel.

The dust around them began to move. Not blown, not scattered by any wind that touched the rest of the square, but drawn, spiraling inward in slow patient loops toward the space between Cozbi's fingers and the captain's chest, a drain the ground had never admitted to owning until this exact moment. The air took on a taste, copper first and something colder underneath the copper, the specific mineral tang that lives in the mouth after lightning strikes close enough to feel in the teeth, though no cloud had crossed the sky above Ashwell all day and none crossed it now.

The captain's shadow bent wrong against the stone beneath him. It did not track the sun. It leaned instead toward Cozbi, stretching thin and impossibly long across the ash-covered ground, a shape trying to crawl free of the body that cast it and managing only half the distance before it thinned into nothing, unraveling itself from the edges inward.

*This is not the dying the other ten did,* Renn thought, and the thought carried its own teeth, an understanding arriving whole and unwanted, the way a body sometimes finishes a thought the mind hasn't caught up to yet. Something was being taken out of the captain first, and the dying that would follow was only what got left over once she'd finished with the rest of him.

The captain's eyes rolled toward Cozbi with the specific animal panic of a man who has just understood, in the last available second of understanding anything, that death was not, after all, the worst thing on offer this afternoon. His fingers scrabbled at the stone, ten small motions that found no purchase, because there was nothing left in his arms to purchase with. Every strength that should have lived there had already gone somewhere else, drawn toward two fingers resting so lightly against his chest they might have been mistaken, from a distance, for tenderness.

Cozbi's expression did not change. That was the part Renn would carry longest, longer than the sound the captain's throat finally found, one single note that broke somewhere in its middle and never finished. Her face held the same gentleness it had worn since she crouched, the face of a woman tending a bed of something that required patience and nothing more urgent than that.

The dust spiral drew tighter. The suspended fog of the captain's breath, still hanging motionless before his face, folded inward and vanished into the same small point of space as the dust, a tide finding the last inch of a drain it had been circling all along. For half a second the whole square held a silence so complete Renn could hear his own pulse behind his ears, could hear the beam behind him tick once more as it finished cooling, could hear absolutely nothing from the captain at all. No breath. No heartbeat. The particular nothing that exists only where a man used to be loud enough to interrupt it.

Then the captain's body went slack all at once, folding down against the well's stone lip the exact way cloth folds when nothing inside it holds its shape any longer, and the ash that had spiraled inward scattered outward again in a soft gray exhale, settling across the square in a new layer over the old one, already indistinguishable from everything that had fallen there before it.

Cozbi rose without hurry and wiped her fingers once against her thigh, an unremarkable gesture, the sort of thing anyone might do after touching something dusty. She looked toward the well the way a woman finishes a room she already suspects is empty, checking the last corner out of thoroughness rather than hope, not urgency.

Renn's spear was still across his knees. His hands had found it at some point during the last minute without asking his permission, knuckles white around the shaft, though some distant reasoning part of him understood a spear would do exactly nothing against whatever had happened in front of him. His whole body had started shaking somewhere during the silence and hadn't stopped, small and continuous, the particular tremor that comes after a fright has already passed rather than during it, the body settling its account late. His teeth had found the inside of his cheek without him deciding to bite down, and he tasted blood there now, thin and copper, a smaller cousin of the taste that had filled the square a minute ago. He swallowed it rather than spit, because spitting would have made a sound, and sound was the one thing he still had some say over.

His mother's face arrived in his mind uninvited, the particular patient motion of her hands folding bread dough over on itself, over and over, unhurried. He hated that his mind had reached for that memory now. He hated that some part of him had made the comparison before he could stop it, gentle hands and this, before he could put the two further apart than they wanted to sit.

*She isn't even looking for me,* he thought, and the thought should have been a comfort and wasn't. *There is nowhere I could go this afternoon that would matter enough to change her schedule.*

Cozbi's gaze passed over the well and did not stop there. She was already turning toward the granary at the square's far end, toward whatever was left of Ashwell that still owed her something, her patience already spent forward onto the next debt rather than lingering on the one she'd just collected. Her shadow, unlike the captain's, moved exactly as a shadow should, obedient to the low afternoon sun, and there was something in that ordinariness Renn found almost harder to look at than the wrongness that had come before it. A thing that broke every rule while it worked and then simply put the rules back on afterward, wearing them the way anyone would wear a coat, was a thing that meant to keep doing this for a very long time. The dust she had drawn into stillness released all at once behind her, and the wind returned to the square in a low, ordinary gust, some small mercy of ordinary weather finding its way back into a place that had held itself unnaturally still for the length of a man's undoing. It carried the smell of him away from the well and out over the blackened rooftops, thinning as it traveled, until the square smelled only of a burned village again, ordinary in its horror, the kind of horror a body could survive smelling because it had already stopped expecting the day to offer anything gentler.

Renn made himself smaller against the stone. He counted her footsteps without meaning to, the way he'd once counted his own heartbeat as a boy trying to fall asleep during a thunderstorm, needing something ordinary to hold onto while the extraordinary thing finished happening somewhere close by. Nine steps. Ten. Eleven, and he lost the count somewhere after that, his mind refusing to keep tallying the distance between himself and whatever she was still capable of doing. The granary door groaned open somewhere behind him, unseen, and a woman's voice inside it rose once in surprise, thin and startled and entirely ordinary, and then did not rise again, and the silence that followed it had the same complete, borrowed quality the square itself had worn a minute earlier. He set his jaw, tightened both hands on the spear he would not use, and did not move again until the sound of her boots had faded past the granary and out of the square entirely.

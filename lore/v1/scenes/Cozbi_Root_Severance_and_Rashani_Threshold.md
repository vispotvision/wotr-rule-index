Candle wax and old paper filled the reliquary, and underneath both sat something colder that had no business being indoors, a mineral smell out of a cave two hundred feet down where nothing living had breathed in centuries. The flames along the far wall did not gutter. They had not gutted in eleven years. Cozbi had never needed moving air to prove a room was occupied.

His root stood elsewhere, in a courtyard whose stones had forgotten what color they used to be, and it was through the root that he felt the cut arrive.

The aperture behind Mizuyi's threshold had been open a moment ago, feeding him warmth and breath and the small domestic sounds of a woman who did not know she was being watched. Now there was a boundary where no boundary should have existed, a silence shaped exactly like a door closing on a room he had occupied for years without once being asked to leave.

Cozbi's projected mouth curved. Not surprise. He had run out of surprise sometime during the Second Epoch and had never once missed carrying it.

"How impressive." He said it to the empty air above the courtyard, unhurried, patient in the particular register of a teacher whose slowest student has finally, after many wrong attempts, answered correctly. "Ma'Kovu Zettari, tracing the spur home through his own brother's Origin Eye rather than the field it read. I confess I hadn't accounted for someone reading the instrument instead of the sight it produced." He turned his wrist slowly, testing the absence where the aperture used to sit, a tongue finding a missing tooth for the first time. "The Zettari have more to show me than I credited them for. I keep corrections like that. They're rare enough to be worth the filing."

He did not raise his voice. Nothing here required volume of him. The projection carried whatever weight he gave it regardless of decibel, the same way the real Cozbi, three planes distant and seated among candles that never guttered, required no motion at all to remain the most dangerous presence in any room he sat inside.

"You may have broken Gensho's nerve tonight," he continued, addressing the same absent listener, "and Torvane's forty years of hatred for a dead man's shadow along with it. I'll take the wreckage. Grief is excellent raw material once the people carrying it stop insisting it belongs only to them."

The armor waited on the worktable before him, a torso piece with no wearer, black iron veined in copper runework pulsing to a rhythm that matched no human heart. Cozbi's projected thumb traced the ridge of a rivet without hurry, the same unhurried patience that had outlasted three dynasties and would likely outlast the fourth.

"Let's see what's left of you," he told the armor, "now that your little monocle has gone blind."

Something wobbled into the candlelight behind him on legs that did not entirely agree on their own length. Amalgamation designation forty-five had been assembled rather than born, and it showed in every joint, sockets rotated slightly wrong, a servant stitched together from six different kinds of salvage that had never been meant to share a body. It came to a stop at what it understood as a respectful distance and folded itself into an approximation of a bow.

"M-Master. I have g-good news to report." Its voice came out of a throat built for something else entirely, each word arriving a half-beat behind the last, as though the creature had to relocate its own mouth before every syllable could be trusted to leave it.

*It fears me the specific way a made thing fears the hand that made it,* Cozbi thought, unbothered by the observation, filing it beside everything else he had learned to file without ceremony. *Fear is a currency. This one has learned, slowly and at some cost, to spend it correctly.*

"Go on," Cozbi said, inclining his head with the patience of a man who had genuinely never once in eleven years been in a hurry.

"Essence harvesting is nearly forty-five percent," forty-five managed, the numbers coming out steadier than the rest of its sentence, figures apparently the one part of language the creature still trusted itself with completely. "More monsters brought in under the designation Chaos Blade. They're quiet now. All of them."

"Mm. Magnificence, forty-five." Cozbi's grin widened, an expression that had learned to imitate warmth so thoroughly it fooled anyone who had never seen what sat beneath it. He rubbed his chin with two fingers. "I think this particular monocle of games can close a little early tonight." He held his hand out over the armor, palm down, not yet touching it. "The eye that was watching us is gone. No sense performing patience for an audience that no longer exists to appreciate it."

He let two things fall into his open palm, and neither of them was light doing anything. They arrived the way weather arrives, a change in pressure felt first in the ears and the back of the throat, before there was anything at all to see. One was Matter, offered as pure covenant rather than shaped substance, the plain agreement that things remain what they are, poured out until the air around his hand thickened toward solid. The other was its contradiction, poured in equal measure, the specific cold of something that did not deny existing so much as refuse to recognize the terms under which anything else was permitted to.

The two did not blend. They pressed.

The armor absorbed both without complaint. Copper runework flared dull orange for exactly as long as it took the metal to remember it was supposed to resist temperature, and then failed to resist it anyway. A groan came off the black iron, low, almost vocal, the sound a hull makes moments before a keel finally gives.

*Zettari architecture,* Cozbi thought, watching the runes with a clinician's stillness, *built to tell the world hold shape. Let's find out what that costs when the world is no longer asked politely.*

The air around the worktable pressed against the skin now, dense as standing water. Candle flames Cozbi had not personally lit in this location, a courtesy projection so the servant would have something to see by, bent sideways with no wind present to account for it. Above the courtyard's ruined roofline the sky lost its stars in a widening ring, not blotted out but unwritten, the specific nothing left behind on a page a scribe has scraped clean rather than merely blotted.

Forty-five folded lower, arms wrapping around itself, and the stutter dropped entirely from its voice in the one sentence its fear made simple enough to say cleanly. "It's getting cold, Master."

"It's supposed to." Cozbi did not look away from the armor. "Cold is what agreement looks like when two things that shouldn't share a place stop pretending they can."

The rivets went first, popping down the seam one after another, a spine discovering joints it had never asked for, and where each one failed the copper beneath simply ceased, a hole shaped exactly like a rivet with no memory of ever having held one. The stones beneath the worktable's legs began the same slow unwriting, starting at the corners and working inward, an erasure with excellent manners that never once took more than what stood directly beneath the working. Dust that had settled on the table for eleven years lifted, and partway up it simply stopped being dust, a column of absence standing where a column of debris should have remained.

Cozbi's projected body did not brace. Nothing in his carriage acknowledged the castle's foundations groaning three floors below, no more than weather requires a man's permission before it arrives. He watched the armor with mild, sustained interest, the interest of a man watching a kettle he already knows will boil, only curious about the exact moment it finally does.

The arch came last, a seam of pale non-light tearing open above the courtyard where the sky's missing ring had finally widened past patience, Matter and its contradiction agreeing on the single point either of them could agree on: that neither intended to leave room for the other. The pressure broke outward once, a single concussive absence rather than an explosion, air rushing inward to fill a vacuum that had briefly forgotten it owed the world any air at all. Every remaining window in the ruined wing shattered inward. Every candle in the courtyard died at once though none of them had burned low, and none of them relit on their own.

Cozbi lowered his hand.

The armor sat exactly where it had been, whole, black, unmarked by any of it, humming now to a rhythm that finally matched a heartbeat. His own. Borrowed, and returned.

"There we are." He rested two fingers against the breastplate and felt it answer, a good tool settling into a hand it had decided to trust. "You held. Good." He turned his head toward the servant still curled in the wreckage of falling ash and unwritten stone. "Get up, forty-five. The castle will stand a while longer than either of us expected tonight. Everything does, right up until it doesn't."

Forty-five uncurled by degrees, the stutter returning the moment it found enough breath to use one. "Y-yes, Master." It glanced toward the hole where a window used to be, then toward the new absence in the floor where several paving stones no longer bothered to exist, and something in its cobbled-together face attempted an expression its face had not been built with the right muscles to finish. "W-was that s-supposed to happen t-to the f-floor too?"

"The floor volunteered." Cozbi did not look up from the armor. "It's the sort of thing that happens when Matter and its own negation share a room and neither one is willing to be the polite one. Don't take it personally on the floor's behalf."

"I w-wasn't, Master."

"Good. You're learning." He said it the way another man might praise a dog for finally sitting, not unkindly, simply from a great and settled height. "Log the loss. Structural, not essence. Nobody harvests stone."

"Y-yes, Master." Forty-five began, laboriously, to gather what remained of its own dignity along with the ash.

Cozbi turned back toward the dark where the severed aperture used to sit, patient as something that had stopped counting the price of time a very long while ago.

"Fight well, little sovereigns," he said to a listener who could no longer hear a word of it. "I already had what I needed from the watching."

He lifted the armor in both hands, and he did not glance again at the ruined roofline before he carried it deeper into the dark behind him.

Three strides past the threshold his brother had cut for him, Rashani felt the pressure change, a spike registering across the Origin Court's outer field as a beginning with no visible middle and no traceable end.

It did not read as an attack. Attacks had shape, a torque loading somewhere, a foot committing its weight, a spell compressing toward the instant it would stop being potential and start being force. This carried none of that architecture. It arrived already finished, a first cause with the entire chain of consequence missing from between it and its origin, the exact signature his own field notes called access depth exceeded, the honest phrase he used for whatever fell outside what he was permitted to witness rather than merely outside what he could presently do. Somewhere beyond the range his Eye could resolve, something had happened that was allowed to finish happening without ever passing through a beginning built for him to see.

*I don't have the depth to read that.* He did not slow his stride to test the observation further. *Whatever that was, it built itself somewhere past where I'm permitted to look.* The thought sat uneasily even filed away. In eighteen years of chasing beginnings he had learned to distrust exactly one category of event above all others: the kind that arrived with no beginning attached to it at all, because a cause his Eye could not locate was not a cause that had failed to exist. It only meant someone, somewhere, had built it in a room his authority was not invited into.

The corridor his brother had opened smelled of underground stone, cold metal, old paper, and beneath all three the faint living warmth Rashani had spent seven years chasing back to himself. He let the unresolved spike file itself into the part of his Crystal reserved for things too large to solve tonight, the same discipline Judicium had beaten into him at eighteen: rank a hundred small dangers honestly against the one that actually mattered, and move toward the one that mattered. Tonight the one that mattered was already close enough to touch.

Origin Court kept its passive read running behind him without being asked, coordinate lines threading faint gold through the corridor stone, cataloguing every load-bearing groove Azure's decades of paranoia had carved into the passage. None of it registered as worth interrupting his pace for. The single vector that mattered ran forward, not back, an unbroken line connecting his own footfall to a door he had not yet reached.

He crossed the corridor's second turn before the aftershock finished settling into the stone underfoot, a groan the castle's foundation would carry for years without anyone above ground ever learning why. He did not turn to find its source. Cataloguing what he could not yet solve while moving toward what he could was the entire discipline the Mirror Fracture had beaten into him, the lesson that had cost him a hemorrhage and very nearly his life to learn correctly the first time it was offered.

Ma'Kovu's silhouette held two paces ahead in the dark, unhurried, the black folds of his robes swallowing what little light the corridor gave off. Rashani matched his own stride to it without comment, letting the shared resonance of the Immortal Cross carry what neither brother needed to put into words tonight.

The door at the corridor's end had not existed an hour ago. It existed now because his brother had decided the distance between here and Mizuyi no longer deserved to stand, and Origin Court read the threshold ahead as the single cleanest vector in the entire field, brighter than the unresolved spike, brighter than the groaning stone, brighter than seven years spent inside a Transfer that had asked everything of him and told him, at the very end of the asking, that it would give back what it had taken.

He reached the door and set his palm flat against the cold iron of its frame.

He did not knock. He did not call her name through it first to announce himself. Smaller men needed sound to prove they had finally arrived somewhere; he had never been one of them. He pressed his hand to the metal, felt the faint warmth on the other side answer his Eye before it could answer anything else in him, and pushed the door open into the dark.

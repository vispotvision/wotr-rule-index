Stone held the cold of the room the way old stone always holds cold, patient and total, and underneath the copper smell of his own blood Naiser could still make out damp rock, rusted iron, and the faint mineral bitterness of water that had never once seen daylight. A mage's life is measured by what he finished before the dying started, and Naiser Yukari had spent the last hour of his doing the arithmetic honestly, as he had done everything once the pretending stopped being useful.

My son. Not Muken. Not Revari. My son first, because a dying man is allowed one selfish ordering of his grief before the rest of it comes due. I told myself I would build peace. I told myself the boy would grow up in a world that didn't require him to be what I became to survive it. The Elder read the thread over my cradle and told my mother I would be the one who pulled this family out of its own mediocrity, and I believed her long enough to waste the years believing was cheapest.

I failed. Say it plainly, the truth undressed of ornament, because ornament was where cowards hid the shape of what they meant. I failed the family. I failed the prophecy. I am failing my son right now, in this exact hour, in this exact chain, and there is no version of this ending where the failing stops in time to matter.

The chains had opened him in six places, and none of the six were mortal on their own, which was its own particular cruelty, the kind that let a man keep thinking long after thinking had stopped being any use to him. Blood found the floor at a rate slow enough to count. He had counted. Some old habit of a Cymorath trained too young refused to stop measuring even now, even here, logging the interval between drops as it had once logged the interval between breaths and bowstrings, and Naiser let it, because a mind occupied with counting was a mind that had somewhere to stand other than the center of what was happening to it.

Revari had died first, years ago now, in a border skirmish neither side had bothered giving a name. Muken had died more recently, and worse, cut down by a man who wore his own bloodline's grievance like a crown and called the killing justice. The three of them were supposed to be the generation. The Elder had said as much at the Telling, all those decades back, three boys with the mark fresh on their foreheads and the whole family's exhausted hope riding on whichever of them turned out to have the widest shoulders. Revari and Muken had gotten a storybook ending, in the sense that storybooks always end the moment the dying happens and never linger on what came after for the people who loved the ones who died. Naiser understood now that he had gotten no ending at all. Only this room, and this floor, and the specific, unhurried arithmetic of blood finding stone.

His son's face arrived behind his eyes without being summoned, the truest things always arriving once summoning stopped being possible. The boy's care for people who could do nothing for him in return. His refusal to let his power be the first thing anyone saw in him. The fear, carried so quietly Naiser had almost missed it during all those mornings of breath-drills and bowstring lessons, that if the boy ever let anyone see the size of what lived under his quiet, they would stop seeing a son and start seeing a weapon.

Perhaps that fear had been the wrong one to hand down. Perhaps if Naiser had let his own brethren see the whole shape of him, Revari and Muken would still be breathing somewhere, ordinary and irritating and alive. There was no way to test the thought now. There was only the chain, and the floor, and the boy's face refusing to leave.

Naiser's eyes went still before the rest of him did, the specific dimming that a Cymorath-trained mind recognizes in others a hundred times before it ever has to recognize it in the mirror. Water found the corner of one eye and ran without his permission, without any decision behind it that he could locate, an old body doing one last thing that had nothing to do with the careful architecture he had spent his whole life building on top of it. The tears were not for himself. Whatever remained of him in that last half-second understood that much clearly, the last clean thing his mind produced before it produced nothing else at all.

His hand, still closed loosely around the length of chain nearest his chest, opened.

Cozbi's mirrors walked the halls of every house the Moto line still called its own, and the true Cozbi sat on a stone pedestal three planes distant, face tipped toward a sky that held no stars he had any use for tonight.

He remembered the promise easily, a debt he had never once let himself forget paying. His brother's face, decades gone now, had asked him for exactly one thing before the end came for him too. *Make them remember what we were, even if they never learn our names.* Cozbi had given his word completely, because a promise given by half-measures had never once been worth the breath it cost to make it. Vengeance. A war large enough that every venerable bloodline still standing would have to write it into their own histories whether they wanted to or not.

The strong wrote the history books. Cozbi had learned that lesson before he learned to walk without falling, watching his own people, the Mahuo, treated as livestock by armies too busy warring with each other to notice they were grinding a third family into the mud beneath both their boots. The Zettari and the Moto had spent generations trampling towns that weren't theirs to trample, calling it strategy, calling it the cost of a larger war, and no one had ever once stopped to ask the people underneath what the cost had cost them. Cozbi had asked. Cozbi had kept asking, long after asking stopped being useful and started being the only thing left of him that still resembled a person.

He would not live in a world he did not write. He would change the fate of these bloodlines exactly as thoroughly as they had once changed his, and if the changing required him to become a thing history remembered as monstrous, that was a fair exchange for a family that history had not bothered remembering at all.

His justice, he told himself, and meant it in the specific, narrowed way a man means a word he has spent decades sanding down until it fits only the one shape he needs it to fit. Not cruelty for cruelty's sake. Correction. A ledger closing.

The gas moved through the Moto halls at the pace of something that had never once needed to hurry. It found women mending cloth by lamplight, children asleep in rooms their parents had believed were safe, elders too far into their own dying to understand that a second, faster death had just arrived to collect them early. It did not discriminate between the guilty and the merely present, because Cozbi's ledger had stopped making that distinction the day his own people's dead had been sorted the same way, guilty and innocent alike, all of them equally useless to the armies that ground them down.

He felt nothing watching it happen, and the nothing was not numbness. It was verdict. They were useless, all of them, exactly as his own family had once been useless to the Zettari and the Moto both, and a verdict does not require the one delivering it to feel the weight of what it costs the ones receiving it. That was, Cozbi understood with total clarity, the entire privilege of being the one holding the ledger rather than the one entered into it.

The Moto cried out through the halls, the specific sound people make when they have already understood, somewhere past language, that no one is coming. Cozbi listened to the sound the way another man might listen to rain, present, undeniable, and entirely outside his responsibility to stop. By the time the gas finished its slow work through every wing the mirrors had reached, the Moto line that had once filled these halls with generations of noise had been reduced to a handful of names still capable of drawing breath.

The real Cozbi rose from the pedestal on the ridge overlooking the Moto's burning kingdoms, felt nothing at all looking down at the fires he had lit through hands that were never in the room, and turned his back on the view before the last of the screaming finished.

Niran fell from the Veil the way an arrow falls once the bow has already forgotten it was ever holding anything back, a bright, falling weight cutting down through spiritual corruption that froze solid in his wake as though even the corruption understood it had no standing to touch what was passing through it. He struck the earth like a star finally finishing its long argument with the sky, and the ground did not so much break beneath him as agree, briefly, to reconsider its own shape.

He came back into his body naked, and the first thing his new lungs did was reject the water still filling them, a violent, full-bodied cough that emptied him onto the scorched ground in ragged heaves, one hand pressed to his own throat as though he did not yet trust it to keep working without supervision. Everything felt different in a way he did not yet have language for. The world did not feel larger. It felt held, the specific, total sensation of something resting in an open palm rather than existing at arm's length, distant and unclaimed.

He looked up before his body had finished deciding it was ready to be looked through, and the sky answered him with smoke instead of stars. Villages burned along the horizon in a line too long and too even to be accident, whole settlements folding into orange light while thin, distant screaming carried across a distance that should have swallowed it whole and did not. Children were dying somewhere inside that light for the sake of one man's idea of what the world owed him, and Niran's stomach turned over before the rest of him caught up to the fact that it was turning.

*Seven years,* he thought, the thought arriving with the particular clarity his new mind produced for anything that mattered, *and the world still could not wait for me to finish arriving before it needed me.*

The Septarch woke inside that nausea the way a banked fire wakes when someone finally opens the flue. Rage did not arrive as heat. It arrived as clarity, cold and total, a sudden and complete understanding of every distance between where he lay and where the burning was happening, and how quickly that distance could be made to stop existing.

Niran pushed himself up onto his hands and knees, his new body renegotiating its own weight with every motion, muscle and bone still arguing over what shape they were meant to hold now. He felt rejuvenated in the specific, unsettling way a wound feels rejuvenated in the instant before it starts bleeding again, power gathering under his skin faster than his mind had finished cataloguing what it was for.

The pressure came off him in a single spike, a differential that rippled outward across the Inner World fast enough to reach every power still watching the horizon for a sign that something had finally changed. It read, to anyone with the sense to feel it arrive, like a single point of light igniting in the middle of a country that had spent a very long night believing the dark was permanent.

Niran rose the rest of the way to his feet, and did not look away from the fires while he did it.

# Brenna's Table — Aetherion Academy

*Aetherion Academy. The infirmary, south range. Early afternoon.*

In the cold room, at the end, someone had held out a hand to him, and Kwon Mu-jin had reached for it.

He never closed his fingers on it. The Arrears had been holding his body's news back from him all morning, a second and more behind the world, and when the fight went out of the room the dam went with it. Everything arrived at once: the burn, the rot, the gas in his lungs, the fullness in his bones, four hours of migraine. His legs went out from under him like a cut rope. He was on the floor before he knew he was falling, and knew it only when his cheek was already against the salt.

They carried him up out of the vault on a door.

---

Brenna Hask had the infirmary's back theatre cleared before the door came through it.

She was the Academy's chief of medicine. She had been a field surgeon with the Third Accord Rifles for nine years before that, and she ran a theatre the way a sergeant runs a line, by voice and by never once looking surprised. She was a broad woman in her late forties, square through the shoulders, with red hair gone half to grey braided tight and pinned flat against her skull so nothing could fall in a wound. Her face was weathered and freckled, with a long upper lip and pale eyes set deep under heavy brows. Her forearms were bare to the elbow and scrubbed raw pink with carbolic, the skin of her knuckles split from it and healed and split again. She wore an oilcloth apron over a grey dress gone soft with boiling. The last joint of her left little finger was missing. She had lost it to her own scalpel in a tent at Hollin Ford, working in the dark on a boy who had not lived.

"On the table. Face up. Mind the legs, they'll come apart if you look at them wrong."

Torvi Brassbeard and Wat Pellow brought the door in between them. The Dawi orderly carried his end as if it weighed nothing, his rust-red beard swinging in its two lead-weighted plaits. Wat carried his, white to the lips. They slid Mu-jin off the door onto the scrubbed deal table under the big skylight, where the hail had melted on the glass and was dripping, slow and steady, onto an empty tray. Brenna was already reading him.

She did not need a gauge for it. Twenty years of opening men had given her eyes that went in under the skin, a medic's Gnosis worn smooth with use. She had seen Benediction answer her at Invocation, the ninth Stage, in a field hospital with the guns still going. What she read on the table made her mouth go thin.

"Right," she said. "Listen, all of you, because I'll say it once and I'll want it in my hand before I finish the sentence."

Behind her, the theatre door banged open against the wall.

"Mu-jin!"

Frithia came through it like weather. She stood a full hand taller than her husband, long in the bone and angular, her brown hair coming down from its pins and her face gone white around the four blue lines inked on her chin. The ringed-seal hat she had stitched herself was crushed in one fist. Her eyes went past Brenna to the table, to the thing on the table, and her face broke. Tears ran down it, and she did not wipe them, and she did not stop walking.

Torvi stepped into the doorway. He did not touch her. He was a third of her height and twice her width, and he stood there the way a stone stands in a stream.

"Not in here, missus."

"That is my husband."

"Aye. And this is Mistress Hask's floor." He said it gently. "She'll have him off it sooner if she's not minding you."

Frithia looked down at him as though she could step over him. She might have. Then someone caught her arm from behind. Geturo had come up the corridor at a run and stopped dead in the door, broad and settled, his mother's mouth set in his face, his big square hands taking her arm and holding it. He looked once past her at the table. Something went out of his face and he put it away. He did not let go of her.

"Mother. Let her work."

"I'll not leave him."

"You won't. We'll be right here." He drew her back a step into the corridor, and she let him, and her eyes did not leave the table. "Hiromi and Lily are coming. Let her work."

Brenna had not turned round. She knew the sound of a wife at a theatre door. She had heard it a hundred times, in tents and barns and the back rooms of inns, and the only kindness she had ever found to give it was to work fast.

"Wat. The door."

Wat Pellow shut it on Frithia's face, and the boy looked as if it hurt him.

"Now." Brenna rolled her sleeves a turn higher. "Carbolic, the strong bottle, a basin of it at one in twenty and another at one in forty for his skin. Every knife I own, boiled, on the long tray. Lint. Oiled silk. Carron oil, a quart, limewater and linseed shaken equal. The morphia, and the needle. Honey water warmed, and the soft tube. Two buckets of snow off the roof, and blankets in the warmer. And bring the saw."

The round infirmarian in brown froze with the carbolic bottle halfway off the shelf. His spectacles slid down his nose.

"Mistress, the bone saw? Above the knee or below, so I lay out the right blade?"

"Put it on the tray where I can see it," Brenna said. "I want to look at it while I decide I'm not using it."

Then she looked at Mu-jin properly, from the top down, the way she had been taught to look at a man so she did not stop at the first terrible thing and miss the second.

The face first. His lips were blue at the edges and his breath came short and wet, every one with a faint crackle in it like paper being crumpled under water. Blood had dried in his nose and the corners of his mouth and was still oozing from his gums. The small vessels in him had burst everywhere at once, all over his body. His hair was singed at the temples. He smelled of struck matches and sulphur and, under them, of something sweet and rotten that she knew from a dozen field hospitals and had hoped not to smell in this one again.

She bent and put her ear to his chest, and listened. Crackles at both bases. Wet. Not yet drowning.

"What did he breathe?"

"Bad egg gas, Mistress, they're saying." Wat's voice wobbled. "And the brown smoke, when the acid went."

"Both." She straightened, two fingers still on the pulse in his wrist, counting it, fast and thready. Hydrogen sulphide shuts the body's furnaces at the source: it stops the cells using the oxygen the blood brings them, so a man suffocates with his lungs full of air. That part, if he had lived this long, he would probably live through. It was the brown smoke that frightened her. Nitrogen dioxide is a liar. It burns the deepest, finest airways and then lets the patient seem to improve. Then, three hours later or thirty, the lungs fill with water all at once and he drowns in his bed. She had watched it kill two men in a powder magazine after Hollin Ford, both of them sitting up and asking for tea at noon.

"He'll seem better tonight," she said, to nobody. "That's when he'll try to die. Someone sits with him every minute until the day after tomorrow, and the moment his breathing changes, you run for me."

The infirmarian, pouring carbolic, nodded.

"Do we give the nitrite, Mistress? For the gas?" He had read the new papers. Everyone had. Amyl nitrite pearls crushed under the nose, the Guild physicians' newest wonder, good for a failing heart and said by some to be good for a sulphide poisoning.

"No." She did not look up. "Nitrite drops the pressure and spoils the blood's grip on its oxygen. He's bled from a hundred places. Give him nitrite and you'll finish what the gas started. Oxygen and patience."

The thigh. The burn ran from hip to knee down the outer side, a broad patch where the skin had gone leathery, waxy white in the middle and charred black at the edges and split down its length. She pressed a fingertip into the white. He did not flinch.

"Full thickness. The nerves are cooked, so it doesn't hurt him yet, and it'll hurt him more than anything else on him once it starts healing from the edges." She looked at the split, at the clean yellow weep. "That one will keep. It'll need new skin in three weeks, pinch grafts off his other thigh, the new way. Carron oil on it now."

The calves.

She stopped.

Both legs, from the ankle to above the knee, had gone the colour of an old bronze coin, mottled with purple and a dirty green-black. The skin was tight and shining, swollen hard, blistered in places with dark fluid. When she laid her fingers on the right calf and pressed, it crackled under them, a soft dry crunching like stepping on fresh snow. That was gas in the muscle, made there by something living in it that hated air. The sweet rotten smell came up thick when she pressed.

And the punctures were gone.

There should have been holes. The orderlies had said the black hands drove needles in, a dozen to a leg. She could see where they had been, a scatter of faint pale marks like healed pocks, and the skin over them was closed, smooth and whole. Somebody had laid a light over this man down in the vault, and the light had knitted every torn edge it could see.

It had saved his life. She could tell from the blood on the door how much he would have lost through those holes on the stair. But light mends what it can reach, and the rot had not been on the surface. It had gone in through the needles, deep into the meat of the calf, and now it was sealed in, in the dark, with no air, which was exactly where it wanted to be.

"Whoever closed these did him a kindness and stopped him bleeding out," Brenna said quietly. "And the rot's underneath, where the kindness didn't reach." She held out her hand without looking. "Narrow knife."

She worked.

She opened every closed puncture again, a short clean cut along the line of each one, and grey-brown fluid welled out with a stink that made the round infirmarian turn his head away. Bubbles came up in it. She laid the right calf open along its length, through the skin and the thin sheet of tissue over the muscle, and looked in.

Muscle tells you whether it is alive. The old field surgeons had a rhyme for it. Red, firm, twitching, and it bleeds when you cut it, and it lives. Grey, soft, still, and it does not bleed, and it is dead, and you cut it out or it kills you. She went into his calf a strip at a time with the knife and the forceps, and where the muscle was grey and soft and did not bleed she cut it away and dropped it in the basin, and where she nicked it and it flinched and ran red she left it. There was more red than she had feared. Whatever the man had done to himself down there, whatever had burned clean through him, his blood had kept reaching the deep muscle.

"Morphia," she said. "An eighth of a grain, no more, his breathing's bad enough. Now, before he needs it."

"He's not feeling anything, Mistress," the infirmarian said, uncertain.

"He will. Look at his eyes." The pupils were wrong, slow to answer the light, as though every report from his own body was arriving at him late. She had read the notes on the bear and its Arrears; everyone in the faculty had. "His body's running behind him. All of this is on its way to him, and when it arrives it'll arrive at once. I want the morphia waiting for it when it gets there."

The needle went into his arm. The infirmarian pushed the plunger.

Then she laid her hands flat on his opened leg, one above the knee and one at the ankle, and called on what she had.

Anima Spirare came first, the breath-current, which had answered her at the sixth Stage in a fever ward. She drew the air of the theatre in toward her hands, and from it she took the quick part, the part that fire and blood both want, and leaned on that.

The room changed. Everyone in it felt their ears pop, the way they do going down a deep stair in a hurry. The lamp flames shrank and steadied. A pressure built between her palms that she could feel in her own teeth, the weight of three atmospheres pressed down onto a single leg. Under pressure, more of a gas will dissolve into a liquid; a corked bottle of soda holds its fizz for exactly that reason. She pressed oxygen into his blood the way the cork presses fizz into soda, and drove it down out of the blood into the tissue, deep into the dark meat where the rot was living.

The rot could not bear it. It was the oldest kind of life there is, the kind that made its home before the world had air in it, and air poisons it. Under her hands the crackling in the muscle went quiet. The sweet smell thinned. In the open wound the blood that welled from the living muscle changed colour as she watched, from the dark sullen red of a starved limb to a bright clean scarlet, the colour of blood with oxygen in it.

She held it for the count of three hundred. Then she let the air go, slowly, because a body pressed too hard on oxygen will start to fit, and a physiologist in the capital had proved it on sparrows and dogs a dozen years ago. She did the left leg next.

*2,600,000 EU across both legs, a tenth of everything she carried, gone in ten minutes.* She felt it as a heaviness in her arms and a sour copper taste at the back of her tongue, and she did not stop.

Benediction came last. It had answered her at Invocation, in the tent at Hollin Ford, the night she lost her fingertip. It was a cleansing current, a Cross-Family law that took what did not belong in a body and drew it out the way heat draws water from wet cloth. She had felt it before, pulling shrapnel residue and the ink of bad workings out of men. This was different. The rot in his calves was not only rot. There was a working in it, a fine cold grain laid through the dead muscle like a seed through soil, and it did not want to come.

She drew on it. Slowly, as you draw a splinter, never jerking.

It came out of the wound in threads. They were fine and black and cold, a dozen, two dozen, rising from the opened muscle and curling in the air above it like smoke from a snuffed wick. They smelled of a pond bottom. She guided them down into the basin of carbolic at one in twenty, and they hissed when they touched it and went to grey scum on the surface. The last of them came out of the back of his right knee, long and reluctant, and she felt it pull at something deeper, almost at the bone, before it gave.

She stood back from the table, and her hands were shaking, and she let them.

The bones, last.

She laid her palm flat over his right thigh above the burn, over the long bone, and read down into it. Her face did not change, but the infirmarian, who had worked beside her for six years, saw her go still.

The femur was full.

She had no other word for it. A healthy bone holds marrow and blood and the fine Lattice that every practitioner's Resilience is read off. This bone held all of that and something else besides, something packed into the marrow under pressure, humming faintly against her palm. It was not his. It had the cold tick of the bear in it, and a working's sour residue, and the weight of more power than one man should have been able to hold, packed into him like grain into a sack sewn too small. Every bone of any size in him was the same. The arms. The ribs. The vault of the skull.

"Is that... Mistress, what's..." Wat began, and could not find the end of it.

"Somebody else's weather," Brenna said. "In his bones." She took her hand away. "I can't draw that. That's down in the Lattice, deeper than meat, and Benediction won't touch what a man took into himself on purpose. It'll leach out of him on its own, slowly, the way the cold comes out of a stone house in spring. Weeks. It'll ache the whole time. Send to the Measurewrights for someone who understands Domains, and tell them it's urgent, and tell them it's Master Kwon."

She looked at the whole of him one more time, top to bottom.

*His reserve read somewhere past 400,000,000 EU by the depth of the draw she could feel under his breastbone; a man that far above her Stage would not let her read it closer.* A man that deep could feed his own mending while he slept, twelve million an hour at rest by the Division's tables. That would help. It would not be enough by itself, and it would not be fast.

"Snow in the buckets, packed round his ankles, not on the wounds. Carron oil on the burn. Oiled silk over the calves, left open to the air under it. That air is half his chance, so don't you dare bind them shut. Honey water through the tube, a cup every hour; whatever was done to him down there, it ate the sugar out of his blood, and he'll have fits if he runs dry. Fresh lint every four hours and the old lint burned, not washed. Torvi, the jar from the cold store. The larvae."

The Dawi orderly nodded as if he had expected it. The round infirmarian looked faintly sick.

"Mistress, surely the..."

"The surgeons in the siege hospitals at Sennet put blowfly maggots on hospital gangrene and watched them clean a wound overnight. They eat the dead and leave the living. I'll take every bit of help I can get. If the cut edges go grey again by morning, they go on." She pulled the oilcloth apron over her head. "And put the saw back. I won't be needing it."

---

Frithia was standing in the corridor exactly where Geturo had left her. Her hat was still crushed in her fist. Hiromi had come and stood on her other side, white-haired and silent, red-eyed. Lily stood a step apart from them both, her arms folded tight and her chin up, her sleeve scraping the plaster as she shifted to let Brenna by. Geturo had not let go of his mother's arm.

Brenna came out and shut the door behind her.

Frithia did not ask. She looked at her, and Brenna had looked into enough faces in enough corridors to know what the question was.

"He keeps both legs," Brenna said.

Her hand went, without her meaning it to, to the stump of her left little finger, and rubbed it. It was a habit she had never been able to break, from the night at Hollin Ford, and she did it whenever she said a thing she was not yet certain of.

"There was rot in them, deep, the worst kind. Some of the muscle's gone and won't come back, and the rest I've cleaned. The infection's in his blood now and it'll fight him. He'll run a fever tonight that'll frighten you. His lungs took gas, and that gets worse before it gets better. He has a burn on his thigh that'll need new skin in three weeks. And there's something in his bones I can't touch that'll take weeks to leave him on its own."

Frithia's face did not move.

"He'll live?"

"If he's who I think he is." Brenna let her hand drop. "A week before he sits up. Three before he stands, with help. Six before he walks the length of this corridor on his own feet, and he'll want sticks a good while after that. It'll be months before those calves are anything like what they were, and they may never be all the way back."

Frithia breathed out. It shook going out of her. Then she put her hat back on her head, crooked and crushed, and straightened it, and for the first time looked like a woman who had decided something.

"I'm going in."

"Aye," Brenna said, and stood aside. "Sit by his head. Talk to him. Whatever happened to him down there, his body's running late, and the pain is still on its way to him." She looked at the closed door. "Be there when it arrives."

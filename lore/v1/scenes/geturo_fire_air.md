# Geturo: Fire-Air

The wind turned on itself.

Geturo felt it in his teeth first. The inward pull that had been running across the ring for six pulses hitched, caught, and then did two things at once, and the air that had been stacking toward the paper came back out past him in a hard flat gust that flicked grit into his eyes and snapped the tails of his coat. Then another. Then the next pulse pulled it all back in, and the one after spat it out again. The brown wind sloshed across the sand in both directions at once. The mirage over the paper broke into three overlapping rings and none of them was the right one.

His Tide still took. Now, every time it took, it gave the same back. Geturo had built the whole field on the arithmetic only running one way, and somewhere in the last few breaths it had stopped.

At the ring's edge the warden's gauge needle fell back half its climb, and she breathed out through her nose for what sounded like the first time in a minute.

"Evens," said the porter, to nobody. "Evens, now."

Geturo's thumb was still on the wax of the fifth vial. He had not broken it yet.

He tried to backpedal. He knew he was late before his heel left the sand, and his right hand, the one with the vial in it, had already come up off his coat toward his face, which put it nowhere useful.

The kick landed on the outside of his left thigh a hand above the knee.

It made the sound of a mallet on a side of beef. The vastus lateralis had nowhere to go but back against the femur, and the muscle was crushed between the two for the space of a heartbeat and let go. Heat went through the thigh from the outside in. A great hot spreading ache followed it, then numbness under the ache, and the leg below simply stopped reporting. His knee began to fold without asking him.

It felt like being hit with a fence post. He had always known he was the softer body in this class. He had not known it would be this plain.

Then there was a grip on the back of his collar dragging his head down, an arm under his left armpit levering at his shoulder, a skull hard against the hinge of his jaw. Something hooked behind his left ankle. The bad leg. Of course the bad leg.

His left fist was still closed on vial six. He had been holding it since the copying, the grit node, the ball of fixed sand that ticked faintly in his palm, and he did the only thing he had time for. He opened his hand and let it drop behind his own heel.

It struck the sand and the second line broke, and the grit node did not burst. It set.

The shell had been packed from the Tide's own sand, iron-gall sediment and river grit ground together under Fixation, and when the line went it let go of its shape and kept its hold on its grains. The air of the ring was full of Residue: the arrow's leavings, the Tide's leavings, and whatever was now being spat back out every other pulse, hot and dense and thick as soup. Air that full of Aether wants a seed. Dallae had come out of air like this an hour before, feet first, on his father's word. Geturo had no word. He had a ball of sand that was already half stone and a heel that needed something behind it, and the room did the rest. The Residue came down out of the air around the grit and cemented it. In the time it took the hook at his ankle to pull a hand's breadth, a wedge of rough brown sandstone the size of a loaf had grown behind his left boot heel and down into the sand under it.

The pull at his ankle hit rock.

His leg did not go. It could not go; there was stone behind it. The numb thigh shook and the knee wanted badly to buckle, and he leaned his whole weight back onto the chock and into the thick air, and the thick air held him up the way deep water holds a swimmer who has stopped kicking.

Something warm ran out of his right nostril and over his lip. Tin in the back of his mouth. The ache behind his left eye opened into a white line and stayed open.

That was the live cost. He paid it and kept his feet.

"Mine are busy," Geturo said.

His right hand was free. The fifth vial was in it.

He had meant to lose this. He had meant it on the bench and meant it on the steps, and some part of him still meant it now, and his thumb broke the wax anyway.

The fifth vial had been warm since the spring because it had never gone out. At its bottom, in a cradle of wire, sat an ember of alder charcoal no bigger than a pea, and around the ember sat a shell built like the first node's with one difference. Its Fixation was keyed narrower. It did not take air. It took the fire-air, the part of air a candle eats and a mouse in a jar runs out of, and left the rest behind. The shell had been rationing that fire-air to the ember a breath at a time for five months, keeping it barely alive, and drinking fresh fire-air in through the glass to make up what the ember used.

Breaking the first line opened its throat.

It drank the ring. The broken Tide was throwing air past him in great flat gusts every other beat, air that had been crushed toward the paper and was now being paid back out, and the fifth vial pulled the fire-air out of every gust that crossed it and packed it down around the ember. The glass went hot in Geturo's palm. Then hotter. The ember went from dull red to orange to a white so hard it hurt to look at through the wall of the vial, because a coal sitting in pure fire-air under that much pressure does not smoulder. It wants everything at once.

He felt the skin of his palm begin to cook against the glass. He kept hold of it.

This was the part he had never done. Vial four had been open on one face. This had to be open on one face and shaped, and nobody had shaped it at the bench, because at the bench nobody had been standing a hand's width from his chest. Geturo pressed the stopper sideways with his thumb and pushed the Fixation's open face into a cone. A V, the point toward his own wrist, the mouth outward, its walls slanting in toward each other.

His nose was running freely now. The white line behind his eye had become a spike.

He knew the three things happening in his hand. His father had made him say them out loud over every working since he was nine. A charge of compressed fire-air around a white-hot coal burns faster than anything in a hearth, fast enough to throw a pressure front ahead of itself, though not quite fast enough to be the thing miners fear. Every grain of the shaping came out of him live, out of a Welling Crystal that had never been asked to hold a cone against its own hand, and the tug under his sternum became a hook. The work itself was Calcinatio. It was the fire that reduces, and he had never once been allowed to point it at anything that breathed.

A cone does something to a blast that nothing else does. When the burning face runs inward along two slanted walls, the gas coming off each wall slams into the gas coming off the other along the centre line, and none of it can go sideways. It goes forward. All of it. The mining engineers who found that out by accident called it the hollow effect, and the gunsmiths had started calling it something ruder. A V at the mouth, a straight line out of the point of the V. A spear of fire instead of a ball of it.

The iron-gall grit was on everything, on his coat and caked in the creases of his knuckles, and iron dust in fire-air does not burn red. It burns in white stars.

He swung the vial in at Taesyn's centre and broke the second line.

The V opened in white and the colour of a forge's heart. The first stars of burning iron were already streaming out of its point in a straight line, and the air in front of them folded over itself, and Geturo's own eyebrows went in a flat crackle and the smell of scorched hair came up sharp in his nose through the blood.

On the boards, Kwon Mu-jin's right hand was already coming up off his side.

Up in the tunnel mouth the taller Class S boy had come out of the dark to watch. He was all length and very little width, a year or two older than anyone on the sand, black hair oiled flat to his skull and parted with a rule, a long bony face that had been handsome at twelve and was still deciding, and grey eyes set close over a nose that had been broken and set crooked. His Class S grey had been tailored, not issued. It fitted the long back and narrow shoulders without a wrinkle and was pinned at the collar with a silver cord gone dull from wear. A pale scar ran across the knuckles of his right hand, straight as a ruled line.

"There," he said to the girl beside him, quietly. "Write that down. The quiet one isn't soft either."

Her pencil was already moving.

On the sand the white line reached out from the point of the V, and Geturo held the burning glass in his burning hand and did not let go.

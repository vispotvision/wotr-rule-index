# Geturo: Gypsum

The hot coal went out of his chest the way it had come in, along the line, and it took the warmth of his own blood with it.

He felt it leave in strands. The doubled hum that had been going round his Crystal looking for a place to settle found one all at once, and the place was out. It pulled out of him through the host line thread by thread. Where it had been there was a hollowness the exact shape of what he had borrowed, and in the hollowness he was suddenly and completely cold. His hands shook. The dead right arm hung heavier. The line between him and the Constrictor folded over itself for a moment, doubled and twisted, and the fold went through him like a cramp going through a calf.

Then it straightened, and nothing was coming home along it at all.

So that was that. He had eaten someone else's Essence, and it had been taken back, all of it and only it, not a thread of his own touched. He noticed that last part. He noticed it with the part of his mind that noticed good work.

Behind him the rock thumped.

It was his rock. The sandstone chock was still grown into the floor where he had seeded it, the size of a loaf, brown and rough, and something in it was beating. A slow, deep, living beat, coming up through the sand into his boot soles. It had no business being there. Rock does not have a pulse.

The Constrictor's head came round to it.

It did what he had made it to do. It found the nearest heartbeat and went to it. It unspooled from the empty shape it had been holding and poured itself across the sand, the whole slate-blue length of it, and threw three coils round the sandstone and anchored its blunt head under the lowest one and cinched on the next beat.

Stone does not give like flesh.

Flesh gives, and a constrictor takes the give and keeps it. Stone does not give at all until it breaks. The first cinch loaded the chock all the way round its waist and the rock took it. On the next beat the snake took slack that was not there to take, and the load in the stone went up, and up again. Sandstone is strong when it is squeezed straight. It is weak when it is squeezed from every side and has nowhere to go. The grains were only held together by Residue that had come out of the air a few minutes before, and on the fourth beat the cement between them let go. The chock burst all the way round at once, in a flat brown ring of grit and pebble that spattered across the sand. The coils closed on nothing.

The Constrictor lay there, wrapped round a pile of gravel, and waited for the gravel's next heartbeat.

The whole of it had taken two breaths. Geturo had watched it through the grey edges of his sight and had not moved. Then the kick came into the inside of his left thigh, and he moved.

It was the same thigh, the other side, low, into the long muscles that pull the leg in. The dead leg went from ache to fire. It held him up because he told it to and for no other reason.

Then a shoulder was in his chest, an arm was round his waist, and a foot hooked his good leg.

He knew exactly where he was going. The trip drove him out across his own balance, diagonally, over the right side. The right side was the arm. Every instinct he had wanted that arm to come out and catch him. Posting a hand is the oldest thing a falling body does, older than walking, and the right hand was already starting to go out to do it.

He did not let it.

The infirmarian had said *capsule torn*. His father had said nothing, which was worse. A joint that has been out and back in once in a day does not get a second chance on the same day. Put a falling body's weight down through it and it goes out again, and this time the socket's lip goes with it. That is how the world works. It does not care how badly you want to stay standing.

He took the fall instead.

Chin to chest, so the back of his skull would not meet the ground. Left arm out, and the whole of it came down flat on the sand beside him at an angle and slapped, hard, the way the old master at Sum-gol had made him slap the orchard grass a thousand times until his forearm was purple. A breakfall. The slap takes the fall across a whole arm's length of meat instead of one elbow or one wrist. His back hit, then the arm, almost together, and the air went out of him in a bark. The torn shoulder stayed folded against his side and took nothing.

He lay on his back on the warm sand and looked up at the white crack in the dome, and breathed, and did not try to get up.

"You're right," Geturo said. His voice came out of him flat and hoarse, and he had to stop and swallow blood. "Everything I build has rules."

He turned his head a little on the sand.

"That's what it costs to be an alchemist. I don't get to argue with them. I don't get to swap them round until they tell the story I like better. You can argue with a rule." He breathed. "You can't argue with gypsum. You burn it right or you burn it wrong, and it tells you which, and it doesn't care what you wanted."

A little of the colour came back into his voice then, against all sense, lying there with his arm dead and his nose bleeding into his ear.

"That's the whole art. Every other kind of working in the Codex is somebody asking the world to be different. Alchemy's the only one that just listens to it. Closely enough, for long enough, that you can move it without lying to it." He shut his eyes. "It's the most refined thing there is. It's the only magic that doesn't lie."

He had never wanted to change the world. He had said so once, at eleven, to his father, and his father had looked at him for a long time and gone back to his slate. What he wanted was to read it. To read Essence the way his father read an assay, well enough to know what a thing was and what it could be made into and what that would cost, before he touched it. Every arm of the art had turned out to have another arm inside it. Calcination had led him to fire-air, fire-air had led him to the ember, and the ember had led him to eleven months at the bench learning what a Draft could hold. It never stopped going down. That was the part he loved. It was plain. Rudimentary, even. Salt, heat, water, weight, and everything else built on top of them, and none of it pretending.

He opened his eyes.

"Enough."

His father had not raised his voice. He did not need to. He had crossed the rest of the sand while Geturo was lying there talking, and he stood a pace from them both now with his left palm still turned up and trembling very slightly, still holding the seam. His right hand was out, flat, palm down, over the two of them.

"That's the match," Kwon Mu-jin said.

He closed his right hand.

The Constrictor came undone. It came out of its shape the way it had come into it, backwards, the slate going cloudy and then clear and then blue, and the whole long body slumped into a slick of luminous liquor round the pile of burst sandstone and soaked away into the sand. The host line gave one last small warm pulse into Geturo's chest. It was what was left of him, what the snake had not spent. It was not much.

Eleven months at the bench. Maybe two minutes on the sand.

Geturo lay and felt it arrive and did not say anything.

At the service door the infirmarian was already coming, the roll of calico under one arm and the Dawi orderly behind him with the stretcher. He said nothing about permission. He had heard the word.

The old summoning lecturer had not moved from beside the door. She was looking at the wet blue stain in the sand where the Constrictor had been. She put one knotted hand up to the bottle on her chain, and held it there.

"Eleven months," she said, to nobody. "At his age. And he spent it all on one throw." She let go of the bottle. "Good."

The draw-inspector had closed his case on his knees. He did not open it again. He looked at the dome, where the white seam ran across the glass, and at Mu-jin's left hand, and back.

"You can let go of that now, Kwon," he said. "I've seen enough of it to fix it."

Mu-jin did not let go yet.

Tam Rudd rubbed out his whole chalk column with the flat of his hand and wrote one word at the bottom of the rake handle instead. Nobody could read it from the boards.

Up at the tunnel mouth, the silver-corded girl closed her card and put it inside her coat. The tall boy beside her was already walking away.

Geturo lay on his back on the sand. The infirmarian knelt at his right side and began, very gently, to fold his dead arm across his chest.

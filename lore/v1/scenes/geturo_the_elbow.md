# Geturo: The Elbow

The fifth vial came apart in his hand.

It did not burst. It crazed. A web of fine white cracks ran round the glass from the cradle to the mouth in the space of one breath, and the cone he had been holding open with his thumb lost its shape all at once, and what should have gone forward went sideways instead. The heat came back over his own knuckles in a broad flat fan. Sparks of burning iron went past his cheek and into his collar. He opened his hand and let the glass go, and it came away from his palm in three pieces, and one of the pieces took a strip of blistered skin with it.

He looked at the palm for exactly as long as it took to see the blood well up in the crease under his fingers. Then he closed it.

The ember was gone. The shaping was gone. There was nothing in his right hand now but a burn, and a sliver of green glass standing in the heel of it like a thorn.

Fine.

He was prepared for this part too. He had been prepared for it longer than for anything in the coat. His father had put a practice staff in his hands at seven and a pair of padded mitts on him at ten, and he had spent more mornings in the orchard at Sum-gol getting hit than he had spent at any bench, because a combat alchemist who could not survive the three seconds it took to reach into his coat was a combat alchemist who never reached into it twice.

The low kick came for the left thigh again. The bad one. He had known it would; there was no reason on earth to kick anything else.

He did not try to take it. He picked the leg up and stepped it back, a short hard step that cost him the whole weight of the dead thigh coming off the ground, and the kick cut the air where his knee had been a heartbeat before. The wind of it touched his trouser leg. He felt that the way you feel a cart go past in a narrow street.

The step back loaded him.

He was taller than anyone else in Class X by a hand, long in the arm and the shin, built like a young heron, all reach and no weight, and he had spent his whole life being told that length was only useful at length. His father had taught him the other thing. A long arm folded is a long lever. The elbow at the end of it travels further than a short man's elbow can, and speed at the end of a lever is paid for by the whole body turning behind it.

The sandstone chock was still grown into the sand behind where his left heel had been. He put his left foot back on it.

It was the best thing on the floor. It did not shift under him the way sand shifts. It was a post, set in the ground, rough and warm and exactly where he had left it, and a spinning strike wants a post more than it wants anything else.

He stepped across with the right foot, turned his hips through, let his shoulders come round after them, and looked back over his own right shoulder the way the mitts had taught him. It was a feint. He meant it as one. Show the spin at head height, make the other body cover high and step off, buy the half-second it would take to get his left hand into the bandolier. The spin was there to push someone back out of range.

Something came in low under it instead.

The level change arrived at the exact moment his back was turned, and the target he had been showing at head height was suddenly not there, and something else was. The whole of the shot was coming in at the height of his hips. His elbow was already travelling. It was travelling round, at the level of a standing head, into empty air.

He dropped it.

It was not a decision. It was the same thing his body had done with the arrow, arriving before him and waiting for him to catch up. He broke at the waist as the spin came round, and the arc of the right elbow went from flat to falling, and the point of it came down on the top of the skull that was coming in under him.

A spinning elbow on its own is a hard blow. A spinning elbow that meets someone driving forward into it is a harder one. The two speeds add. The collision does not care whose idea it was. What the point of his elbow met came up into it as fast as it came down, and the jolt went up his arm into his shoulder and down his spine into the chock, and the chock did not move.

Then the Essence. He had pushed it without meaning to, the way a man shouts when he stubs his foot, and it went out through the olecranon ahead of the bone. Ardency was the thing his father's slate always had second from the top, in the column of his own marks that he did not show people. It did one plain thing. It made the contact stiffer than bone alone makes it. A blow that stops over a longer time hits softer, and a blow that stops over a shorter time hits harder, even with the same weight behind it; that is why a hammer is steel and not leather. The Essence at the point of his elbow stopped it short. The whole spin arrived at once.

The cost came back up the arm on the same road. The ulnar nerve runs over the point of the elbow a finger's width under the skin, and everything he had just put through the bone went through the nerve too. His right little finger and ring finger went numb from the knuckle out. A hot electric ache sat in the inside of the forearm and would not leave.

He did not let the shot finish. He sprawled.

Both legs went back hard, the good one and the dead one, and his hips came down heavy, and his chest landed across the upper back of the body at his waist. The dead thigh screamed when it straightened. He let it. His weight went where the old wrestling master at Sum-gol had beaten into him it had to go, onto the shoulders, off his own legs, so there were no legs left in front to be taken. He was long enough that the sprawl put his feet a full body's length behind him in the sand. His toes dug in. He held his ground.

"Hands, then," he said, to nobody in particular, with blood in his mouth.

He brought the elbow down again.

It was shorter this time and straight down, a hammer and not a spin, the forearm folded tight and the point of it falling with his whole chest behind it. Essence went through it again. The numbness in his fingers spread to the middle one. He felt the contact in his teeth.

Something shifted in the bandolier under his chest.

He felt it through the canvas: glass, pressed between his own ribs and the back underneath him, rolling a little in its loop. Vial three. The loose one. He had pushed it home on the bench and it had worked loose again somewhere between the steps and now, and it was lying under his whole weight with nothing between it and breaking but the thickness of his coat.

He did not know what it would do if it broke there. He had made it. He still did not know. That was the trouble with vial three.

His right arm came up for the third time.

On the boards, the warden said one word, quite quietly, and the word was his father's family name.

The porter had stopped raking. The chalk on his rake handle had a fresh column started under the old one, and he had not written anything in it yet.

At the service door behind them a man had come in with a leather case under his arm, called by someone when the fire went sideways. He was round and soft all over, short in the leg and thick in the middle, with sparse sandy hair combed across a pink scalp and a small wet mouth in a large, kind, badly shaved face. Heavy spectacles in steel rims had slid halfway down his nose. He wore the Academy infirmarian's brown over a waistcoat that would not button, the sleeves protected by black oilcloth covers to the elbow, and the covers were stiff and shiny with old stains that had never quite come out. There was a burn scar on the back of his left hand shaped like a coin. He looked at the sand, and at the two bodies on it, and put his case down on the boards and began to undo the straps without being asked.

"Burns first," he said to the warden. "Then whatever the elbows have done. Tell me when I'm allowed on."

Up at the tunnel mouth the Class S boy had stopped leaning against the stone.

Kwon Mu-jin's left palm was still turned up at his side, holding the seam of the dome. His right boot came off the boards and onto the sand.

Geturo's elbow was at the top of its arc.

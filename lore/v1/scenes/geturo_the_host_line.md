# Geturo: The Host Line

Something in the shoulder went over an edge and came down on the other side of it with a heavy, wet clunk.

Geturo felt the ball of the joint ride up out of the cup of its socket and sit on the rim, half in and half out, and the pain was a white sheet that came down over everything. His fingers stopped being his. The arm behind his back felt twice its length and full of sand. A word came down to him through the calf across his face, and he did not hear what it was. He did not need to.

Then the Draft came home.

He felt it before he understood it: a thread of warmth under his breastbone that had not been there a breath ago, running the wrong way. Every thread he had ever spent went out of him. This came in. It poured up along something thin and taut that ran from his own Crystal out through the sand to the coils lying beside them, and it arrived in his chest in pulses, and each pulse was a little hotter than the last.

It took him the whole of a heartbeat, lying there with his shoulder hanging off its rim, to understand what he had built.

It had taken a year, and most of it had not been the bench. His father's summonses came up out of the room. You held the pattern on the Attraction Layer, you put the seed where you wanted it, and the Aether in the air did the rest, the way frost does on a window. It was elegant and it was his father's, and Geturo could not do it. He was Welling. He did not have the reach. So he had sat down with his father's method on one side of the slate and the Draftcraft primers on the other, and he had translated. Line by line. Eleven months of nights with his hands flat on the glass and a nail of a headache behind his eyes, turning an art that borrowed its body from the room into one that brought its own, and every drop of that body was him.

And what was made of him stayed his.

A crystal grown out of a solution is the same salt that was in the solution. It only changed its shape. The Constrictor was the blue liquor come out of solution, and the blue liquor was eleven months of Geturo's own Essence, and nothing about the change of shape had cut the line back to where it came from. He had drawn the feeding law to eat what the held limb spent. He had never drawn a law that let it keep what it ate. It did not have anywhere to keep it. It was a pipe with a mouth at one end and his heart at the other. Every scrap it drank ran straight down the host line into the Crystal that had made it, the way the blood in a vein does not stop in the vein.

Somewhere in the last few beats, someone had spent a great deal of live Essence through that leg, and the snake had drunk every thread of it.

Whatever had been done to hold that meal at the boundary, to make it both eaten and kept, had been done between the snake and the leg. Two parties, a loop with two ends, and the snake was not an end. It was a wire running to ground. The loop closed on an empty snake, and the meal went on past it and down the line and into him.

Foreign Essence in his Crystal. Enough of it to feel like swallowing a hot coal.

It was more than enough.

He did not fight the lock. He had nothing left in the arm to fight it with, and a kimura does not care how strong the arm inside it is. It only needs the elbow bent. The whole of it is a lever running from wrist to elbow to the ball of the shoulder, and the lever only works while the arm is folded at the joint in the middle. Straighten the arm and there is no lever. There is only a boy holding a long straight stick by one end.

So he straightened it.

He sent the hot coal down into the arm, all of it, the way he had sent Ardency into the point of his elbow, except this time it went into the long muscle at the back of the upper arm, and the triceps took it like a dry field takes rain. The arm came open against the figure-four. It was not strength. It was more than strength and it was not his, and it drove the elbow out of its bend a finger's width, then a hand's width, and every hair of that width was the joint grinding on the rim of its socket.

He rolled toward the lock at the same time.

The old master at Sum-gol had told him that too: a shoulder lock turns the shoulder one way, and if you cannot stop the turning, you go with it. He tucked his chin, drove off his toes, and rolled forward over the trapped shoulder into the direction it was being wrenched, and the rotation that had been tearing the joint became a somersault instead. His back went over and his legs after it, and the calf slid off his face. The sand hit him between the shoulder blades and then he was on his knees, facing the other way.

The arm came out of the grip with a sound like a wet sleeve pulled off a peg.

Something in the shoulder went back over the edge in the roll, back down into its cup, with a second clunk heavier than the first. The pain afterward was enormous and sickening and oddly clean. He did not know if that was good. He suspected it was not.

He knelt on the sand with his right arm hanging off him and his left hand flat on the ground to keep him up.

The Essence in his chest was still hot. It was not sitting right. His own was a slow, even warmth, the colour of the blue liquor. This had a different grain to it, a fine doubled hum, as if two notes a hair apart were being played on the same string, and it did not settle. It went round his Crystal and round again looking for a place to go and found none. His teeth ached with it. He had no idea how long it would take to leave, or whether it would.

Vial three, crushed against his ribs, had gone so cold it had stopped hurting. The skin over it had gone numb.

"Tell it yourself," Geturo said.

He did not know which way he was facing when he said it. It did not matter.

Beside them the Constrictor still lay on the sand in three perfect coils around the shape of a leg that was no longer inside them. It had not moved. It had nothing to move for. Then the host line ran taut and warm between them, and the blunt eyeless head came up out of the coils, and the row of pits along its upper lip turned, slowly, toward the nearest heartbeat.

On the boards, his father was all the way onto the sand now. Three paces in. His left hand still held the seam of the dome. His right was at his side, closed, and he had stopped walking.

At the service door the infirmarian had seen the shoulder go and come back. He did not look away from it.

"Out and in again," he said to the warden. "That's the capsule torn, most like. He'll not lift that arm above his head for a month." He picked up the two wooden splints and put them down again and took out a long roll of calico instead. "Tell Kwon it's a sling now whatever happens."

The draw-inspector on his stool had not written anything for some time. He was watching the thin blue thread running across the sand between the kneeling boy and the snake, the thread nobody else in the room seemed to be able to see, and his clear blue eyes were very narrow.

"That's a return," he said quietly. "It's feeding him. That child built a summons with a vein in it."

"Is that bad?" said the warden.

"It's new," said the draw-inspector. He began to write again. "Those aren't always different things."

A woman had come in while nobody was looking. She stood just inside the door with her hands folded. She was old and very small and very straight, no taller than the Dawi. Her white hair was pulled back so tight it lifted her eyebrows. Her face was a small brown walnut of wrinkles around a sharp nose and a mouth that had set in a line fifty years ago and never unset. She wore an Academy lecturer's black gown so old it had gone green-brown, and it swallowed her, the hem bunched up in one hand to keep it off the floor. On a chain round her neck hung a small glass bottle of blue liquid, stoppered with grey wax. Her fingernails were cut straight across and her knuckles were swollen and knotted.

She looked at the snake for a long time. Then she looked at Kwon Mu-jin's back.

"Kwon," she said, not loudly. "You told me the eldest had no gift for it."

Mu-jin did not turn round.

"I didn't know," he said.

"No," said the old woman. "You wouldn't have. He kept it in a bottle."

The Dawi orderly had put his hand on the stretcher and left it there.

Up at the tunnel mouth, the Class S boy said nothing at all.

On the sand, Geturo pushed up off his left hand, got one foot under him, then the other, and stood, swaying, with his dead arm hanging and a stranger's Essence humming in his chest. The Constrictor's head swung round and settled on the heartbeat it had found.

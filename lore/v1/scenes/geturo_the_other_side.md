# Geturo: The Other Side

Dallae got Xanelor up the way she had done everything else, slowly and in the order she had told him she would. One hand under his elbow and the other flat between his shoulder blades, not lifting, only there. She walked him across the sand toward the gallery steps at his pace and not hers. The bandaged fingers of his right hand hung against her apron and left small brown prints on it that she did not look at.

Their father got Hiromi up. That took less care, because Hiromi would not allow more than a hand on his forearm and the first half of standing, and then he shook the hand off and walked the rest of the way on his own, gauntlets swinging, grit falling out of his collar at every step. Halfway to the steps he passed Taesyn coming down, and he gave him a stupid little grin on the way past, the one he had worn at six when he had done something at the dinner table he knew he would be sent out for.

Geturo had sat back down. He pinched the bridge of his nose between thumb and forefinger and held it there. The draw-hum was still flat. It had been flat long enough that he had stopped hearing it as wrong and started hearing it as the room, and he did not like that either.

Down at the ring's edge a woman had come out of the service door, already talking.

She was short and very wide, built square through the shoulders and heavier still through the belly and hips, and she walked the way people walk who have spent thirty years on scaffold and don't trust any floor they didn't lay. Her hair was iron grey and cropped close all over, stiff as the bristles of a yard broom. Her face was broad and flat with a heavy jaw and small pale eyes set deep under a brow that had been singed bald on the left side and never grown back. A gauge hung on a strap round her neck over a leather apron that ran to her knees, scorched in overlapping crescents and heavy in the pockets with calipers, a seal-press, chalk, a coil of copper wire. Her sleeves were held up by garters above the elbow. Both forearms were mottled with old conduit burns, glossy and pink, the skin pulled tight across the tendons. The Guild seal on a leather thong at her throat was brass gone brown with handling. Two porters trailed her with a barrow of clean sand and a pair of rakes, and they were already tipping it into the trench at the far end, where the cooled iron crackled and gave off a smell like a blacksmith's quench.

"Kwon." She did not raise her voice; she simply had the kind that went where it was sent. "That's a third of my dome."

"I saw it, Warden."

"You saw it. Good. I felt it, down in the pump room, through my feet." She lifted the gauge off her chest and tapped the glass with a thick fingernail. The needle shivered and settled a hand's breadth left of where it wanted to be. "Seam's weeping. You put another one of those through my field and it won't be a crack, it'll be the ward coming down on your children's heads. I'm closing the floor."

"You're not," Mu-jin said.

She looked at him. He looked back with the one eye.

"There's a tournament in seven days," he said. "I need to see all of them before then."

"Then see them in the yard."

"On gravel, with no ward, where I can't stop anything." He nodded at the dome. "I'll hold the seam."

That stopped her for about a breath. Her small pale eyes went from his face down to his left hand, where the nails were only just pink again, and back up.

"While you're doing what else," she said.

"While I'm doing what else."

The warden sucked her teeth. Behind her one of the porters dropped his rake, and the clatter of it went all the way round the tiers and came back. She did not turn to look at him. Her jaw worked sideways once, the way a jaw does on a word its owner has decided not to say in front of children.

"Nothing through the east side," she said finally. "Nothing faster than a thrown stone at the dome. You feel it go, you call it, I don't care who's winning." She let the gauge drop back against her apron. "And I'm writing this up."

"Write it up," Mu-jin said.

He turned his left palm toward the ceiling. Geturo could not see anything leave it. What he saw was the white line across the dome stop ticking. It had been making a sound he had not noticed until it quit, a thin dry tick every few breaths, and now it was silent, and his father's shoulders went a finger's width lower under the coat and stayed there.

Then his father looked up at the gallery.

"Taesyn. Who are you fighting?"

He did not wait for the answer. His eye had already moved along the bench and found Geturo, and stopped there, and Geturo knew the look. It was the look his father gave a reagent he had already decided to use before he had finished saying so out loud. He had been thinking about it since the arrow. Maybe since before.

"Geturo," Mu-jin said. "Come down. You've got Taesyn."

Geturo took his fingers off his nose.

He did not want to fight Taesyn. He wanted that plainly and without any shame about it, the way he would not have wanted to put his hand into the calcining pan. The boy was a prodigy, the kind that noticed things and did something with them before other people had finished noticing. Geturo had been sitting on the same bench as him for a week. He had not once seen him look surprised. Fighting that meant getting taken apart in front of everyone by someone who would be polite while he did it.

But it was his father on the sand, and his teacher, and both of them had spoken, and there was no version of this where he said no.

Whatever. It was not a big deal. He would go down there and hold back, take a few, go over when it was reasonable to go over, and make the other boy look good doing it. Taesyn would get his data. Everybody would go to supper. He had heard every word his father had just said to Xanelor about holding back, and he had filed it, and he was going to do it anyway.

He stood. The splinter caught his trousers again and he freed it without looking.

On the way down the steps his hands went inside his coat on their own. He wore the vials in a canvas bandolier stitched to the inner lining on the right side, eight loops, eight stoppered tubes of thick green glass each the length of his thumb, and he ran his fingers along them from top to bottom the way some people touch their pockets for their keys. Wax on every stopper. The third one had worked loose in its loop on the bench and he pressed it home. The fifth was warm, which it always was. He shifted the bandolier a little higher on his ribs so it would not swing and buttoned the coat over it to the collar.

The sand was still warm when he stepped onto it. The porters were raking the new fill level over the trench, and it smelled of the river it had been dug out of.

He stopped a few paces from his father. Close enough to talk without the gallery hearing all of it. Not so close it looked like he was asking for something.

"You're sure about this, Teacher?"

Mu-jin looked at him. The left hand was still turned palm up at his side, holding the dome. His face gave nothing, and then, for a moment, it gave a little.

"I'm certain," he said. "Just give it your best."

He stepped back off the edge of the ring, onto the boards, and left his son on the sand.

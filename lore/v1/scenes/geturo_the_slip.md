# Geturo: The Slip

The third elbow came down on a hand.

Geturo felt the difference before he understood it. The first two had met skull, round and hard and unforgiving, and the jolt of them had gone up his arm clean. This one met something flatter that gave. A palm had come up into the path, spread over the crown of the head, and the point of his elbow landed in the middle of it with his chest behind it, and he felt small bones go under the olecranon. Several of them, all at once, a dry crunching like a fist closed on a handful of quills.

A hand over the skull does not soften an elbow. All it does is put the fingers first in line.

And at the same moment the blow came back.

It did not stop. It came back up his own arm as though the ground under the hand had turned into a wall, and it went through the elbow and into the shoulder and on into the joint where the collarbone meets the breastbone, and his right arm hung for an instant between the strike he had thrown and a strike that had been thrown at him. The air round his forearm made a noise like a rope under too much load. Every hair on the arm stood up.

That was a working. He knew that much. He did not know which one or how, and he did not need to. His father's first rule of the floor was older than any of the arts: *when a thing does what things should not do, leave.*

Hands were on his right wrist. Two of them.

He had been taught this at Sum-gol too, in the mud at the bottom of the orchard, by a wrestling master with a ruined ear who smelled of pipe tobacco and liniment and had never once let him win. A grip on a wrist is strong everywhere but one place. The fingers wrap round, the thumb closes the circle, and the circle is weakest where the thumb meets the fingers, because that is the only part of it held by one digit against four. You do not pull straight out of a wrist grip. You turn toward the thumbs and drive the elbow to your own hip, and the wrist rolls out through the gap.

His wrist was the one he had burned.

The skin of the palm had blistered on the fifth vial and torn on the glass, and the blisters had broken when he threw the elbows, and the whole hand was slick to the forearm with blood and the clear thin fluid that comes out of a burn. Burned skin does not hold to the flesh under it. It slides. Geturo felt a strip of it shear off his wrist under the grip, a long wet peel like the skin coming off a boiled plum, and he turned toward the thumbs and ripped the elbow back to his hip at the same time, and there was nothing left under anybody's fingers but blood.

It hurt more than the thigh, more than anything had yet, and he made no sound about it.

He was also longer. His forearm was a long lever against short arms, and a long lever turning against a short grip wins. The wrist came out.

Then the stand-up.

He did it the way the old master had drilled it until he could do it asleep, because there was no time to do it any other way. Left hand down to the sand. Inside foot posted under his hip, the good leg, so the dead thigh would not have to carry the first push. He drove his back up into whatever was behind him, and for a moment it was a loaded yoke. Hips up. Then his post foot took his weight and he back-stepped out. His bad leg swung round behind him and hit the sand and very nearly folded, and did not.

He was standing. He had not needed a single grain of live Essence to do it. The Tide was still running under their feet, thin now and stuttering, and it still taxed every working made inside it. It had nothing to take from a wrist turning through a thumb.

He took one step back, then another, to make space.

Both his hands came up to his head.

He had not decided that either. His elbow had just been thrown back at him by something he could not see, and his body had drawn its own conclusion about where the next strange thing would come from. Forearms up along his temples. Burned hand and numb hand both, palms turned in, elbows tight. His right little finger and ring finger were dead as wood, and now the middle one too, and the arm from the shoulder down felt heavy and wrong, as if someone else had lent it to him.

His hands were guarding his head. That left everything under his elbows with nothing in front of it at all, and he knew it.

Vial three was still in the bandolier. Unbroken. He felt it against his ribs, lying crooked in its loop, and it was very slightly cold, which it had never been before.

"Done," Geturo said.

Blood ran off his wrist and dripped from his elbow onto the sand in a steady line.

At the ring's edge his father had stopped with one boot on the sand and one on the boards. He did not come further. His left palm was still turned up at his side, and his right hand, which had been rising, came back down and closed.

"Go on," he said, to both of them, and to nobody else.

The infirmarian at the service door had his case open and his oilcloth sleeves pushed back to the elbow, and he had laid out on a folded towel, in a row, a jar of lard and lime-water, a roll of lint, two wooden finger splints and a pair of long forceps. He counted them with one finger without looking at them.

"The hand," he said to the warden. "Whoever took that last elbow on the hand, I want to see it. And the wrist on the tall one's come half off."

"When Kwon says," said the warden.

"When Kwon says," the infirmarian agreed, mild as milk, and began threading a needle anyway.

Behind him a Dawi orderly had come in with a folded stretcher over one shoulder. He stood no higher than the infirmarian's chest and was nearly as wide as the door, all shoulder and forearm, his beard the colour of wet rust and braided in two plaits tied off with lead weights that knocked when he moved. His face was broad and flat-nosed and seamed across the forehead in four deep lines, and his eyes were small and black and very steady. He wore a leather jerkin cracked white at the creases over a wool shirt with the sleeves cut off, and his arms were furred to the knuckles and marked with old blue tattooing gone green at the edges. The stretcher poles had been worn pale where his hands went.

He set the stretcher against the wall. He looked at the sand, at the blood line running from the tall boy's elbow, at the sandstone chock still grown into the floor.

"Stone," he said. "Good stone." Then, after a while, to nobody: "Bad place for it."

Tam Rudd had written something at last in his new chalk column. He looked at it, rubbed it out with his thumb, and wrote it again.

Up at the tunnel mouth the Class S girl had turned her card over and started on the back.

Geturo stood with his guard at his temples and his elbows tight and his belly and ribs open to the whole of the ring, and he watched, and waited, and bled.

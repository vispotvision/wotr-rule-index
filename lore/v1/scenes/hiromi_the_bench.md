# Hiromi: The Bench

Hiromi sat down harder than he meant to, and the bench let him know about it through every rib on his left side.

He kept his face still for as long as it took the pain to finish. Then he let his breath out slowly through his teeth, the way you let a door close when someone is asleep in the next room. The gauntlets were off. They sat on the boards between his boots, the knuckle-plates still gritty, and his hands felt strange and light without them, like hands belonging to a smaller boy.

Out on the sand they were getting Geturo onto his feet. Calico round the chest, one arm strapped dead against his ribs, blood on his wrist, and his eyebrows gone. He was refusing the stretcher. Of course he was.

Hiromi watched his brother stand and felt something he did not have a name for yet. He had known Geturo his whole life. He had known about the pencil, and the vials, and the long nights at their father's bench. He had not known about the snake. Nobody had. Eleven months, the old woman at the door had said, and Hiromi had been across the corridor for every one of them and never once heard it.

He would ask. Tonight, probably, with the door shut. Geturo would say something flat and unhelpful and then, much later, tell him everything.

Somebody beside him was speaking to him. He turned his head.

The compliment landed somewhere under the sore ribs, warm, and he did not quite know where to put it.

"Not strong enough," Hiromi said.

He said it lightly, and then heard how true it was, and let it stay true.

"I underestimated him." He turned one bare hand over on his knee and looked at the palm, at the pale half-moon marks the gauntlet straps had pressed into the heel of it. "I had the whole thing in my hands. I had him so heavy he could barely lift his arms, and I stopped watching what the weight was doing." A small, crooked breath of a laugh. "He put it in the bow. Father's right. I built his shot for him and stood in front of it."

He coughed then, not meaning to. It came up out of the bruised side and doubled him over a little, and he pressed the heel of his hand to his ribs until it passed. When he straightened his eyes were wet at the corners from it.

"I've got a lot to learn," he said. And then, because it was also true and he did not see any reason to hide it: "It was fun, though. It was really fun."

He looked at her properly then.

His eyes were red, the red of a coal you blow on, and they had a way of brightening when they settled on someone he liked, which he had never been able to do anything about. It made him look younger than thirteen and older than it, both at once. His white hair had come half out of its tie in the fight and stuck to his forehead in points. There was still a brown crust of dried blood in the groove above his lip.

He smiled at her the way he had smiled at Taesyn on the steps: stupid, and completely unembarrassed about it.

"So." He nudged the gauntlets a little further under the bench with his boot. "Are you going to fight?"

He let that sit a moment, then tipped his head toward the sand, the trench raked over and the new crack in the dome and the wet blue stain where his brother's snake had come apart.

"I'd volunteer," he said. "I'd let you beat me up, even. It'd be good for your ego." His grin went wider, and he winced when it pulled at something in his side, and grinned anyway. "Mine's done for the day. Somebody might as well get some use out of it."

At the end of the row, an old groundskeeper was working his way along the benches with a wooden pail and a long-handled tin dipper. He was bent nearly double, bald and brown as a walnut, a fringe of white hair round his ears, his canvas apron soaked dark down the front. He had one milky eye and one sharp one. He used the sharp one on Hiromi's ribs.

"Water," he said, and held out the dipper. "Not the ribs. Drink it. Don't pour it on anything."

Hiromi took the dipper, drank half, and held the rest out toward her.

# Hiromi: The Picnic

The smell reached him before the paste did. Mint, sharp and green and cold at the back of the nose, and under it something like cut grass after rain. It cut straight through the iron and scorched-hair stink that had been sitting in the arena all afternoon, and for a moment Hiromi forgot to hold his side.

He looked at the little sapling standing in her palm, swaying on nothing, its long leaves bright as new blades. He had watched his father pull a woman out of the air an hour ago. He had watched his brother pour a snake out of a bottle. Somehow this was the thing that made him stare.

"It's fine," he said. "It's nothing. Just bruises."

He put his hand over hers without really deciding to. It was only for a moment, his fingers resting on the back of her hand, over the crushed leaves. Her skin was cool. His knuckles were still grey with arena grit, and a split on the middle one had opened again, and he took his hand back before he could get any of it on her.

"Really," Hiromi said. "I'm fine."

He was not, entirely. The ribs on his left side had settled into a deep, hot ache that sharpened every time he breathed past halfway, and there was a tenderness under his arm he did not like the shape of. But he had watched Geturo refuse a stretcher with his shoulder half torn out, and he was not going to lift his shirt on a bench in front of half of Class X over some bruises.

Her cheeks had gone pink.

He saw it. He did not say anything about it. He looked at the sand for a second instead, where the porters were raking the blue stain under, and felt his own ears go warm and hoped the white hair covered them.

Other girls had tried for his attention since the first bell. He knew how it looked when they did. They laughed a beat too early at things he said. They asked about his father before they asked about him. One of them had asked about his mother's sword before she asked his name. The Paragon of Dominion, Kwon Mu-jin's son, Frithia's son, both of them teaching in these halls. People wanted to stand near that. It was something to be seen with.

She had not asked about any of it. She had told him he did well, and then told him plainly he wasn't perfect, and then offered to fix his ribs with a plant she had grown out of her own hand. None of it sounded like she needed anything back from him. He liked that more than he knew how to say.

So he said something else.

"My mother said I could take you on a picnic."

It came out faster than he had practised it. He had practised it. Twice, in the washroom mirror that morning, before he knew he would spend the afternoon getting his ribs kicked in, and neither time had it come out this quickly.

"If your guards let you," he added. "I know your people are strict. Your parents." He rubbed the back of his neck with the unsplit hand. "She said she'd pack it herself. She makes these rice rolls with pickled radish in the middle. They're better than they sound." A pause. "And she said to ask properly and not just tell you, so. I'm asking."

At the end of the bench the old groundskeeper had stopped with his pail. His sharp eye was on the sapling in her palm, and for once the rest of his face had come along with it.

"That's true mint," he said. "Not the garden stuff. The kind that grows by cold water." He sniffed hard through a nose that had been broken at least twice. "Haven't smelled that in forty years."

Then he went on down the row, the dipper knocking the side of the pail, muttering to himself about river banks.

Hiromi held out his hand, palm up, toward the paste.

"I'll take it, though," he said. "For later. If that's all right."

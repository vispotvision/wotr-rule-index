# Mu-jin: The Card

Mu-jin's hand has gone to the notebook again. He notices it there, flat against his coat over the ribs, and lets it stay, because taking it away now would say more than leaving it.

"I'd tell you what's in it if I could," he says. "I can't. The Academy has put its seal on the work, and there are rules about what leaves the grounds while the seal is on." He turns his beer glass a quarter-turn on the cloth, the way he turns a flask to read the meniscus. "I've given my word on it besides. You know what that's worth to me. You wrote me four pages once, telling me it was the most expensive habit I had."

"Five pages," Malphas says. "Both sides."

"Five." Mu-jin smiles. "Anything else you like. Ask me about anything else at all."

Malphas does not press. He sits back instead, with his long hands folded on the tablecloth and his thumbs laid parallel, and looks at Mu-jin across the ruin of the mutton with an expression Mu-jin remembers from the assay-house: the look of a senior watching an apprentice refuse him and liking the apprentice better for it. *He never gave a word he meant to take back*, the look says, and Mu-jin, who has been read by better men than most, reads it plainly and is warmed by it.

"Then I'll pay for my supper with my own news," Malphas says. "Since you're keeping yours." He turns the brass bill tray a little on the cloth, squaring it to the table's edge. "I'm with a pharmaceutical house now. Private. Manufacturing chemists, tinctures and compounds and the like, for the districts the Guild contracts don't reach. We're finishing up a long piece of research, and I'll not say what it's on, because the men who pay for it would have my hide for a glove." His mouth thins with something that could be humour. "But you know I didn't bring you down to this hotel for business. I came east to finish the work. I stopped here for you. I wanted to see my old friend while we both still had faces."

"For the districts the contracts don't reach," Mu-jin repeats. He knows the phrase. It has been in every letter for three years, in one form or another, like a watermark.

"East of the junction there's a village called Ossery Cut." Malphas says it the way other men say the name of a horse that lost them money. "Two hundred souls and a standpipe that's been dry for thirty years. A core opened under the mill this spring. The Guild quoted them a contract price that was more than the village has earned since it was founded, and while they were writing back to ask for terms, the core took the miller's girl and two of the Aldous boys." He lifts his beer glass, looks at it, and sets it down again without drinking. "My house makes the draughts that keep a body breathing when the Essence pathways fail. That's the whole trade, Mu-jin. We keep them going until somebody comes. Mostly nobody comes."

"Keep them going as what?" Mu-jin asks it gently, and as a real question, the way he would ask it of a student at the board. "I've read the old papers on scaffolding. You keep the lungs going and the heart going, and you run a conduit round the pathway that's failed, and the body stays warm. Then someone has to decide how much of the thing can be carried by the conduit before the thing you saved isn't the thing you saved any more."

Malphas looks at him.

"A child breathing," he says, "and a child not breathing. I've never once found that question harder than that."

"I have." Mu-jin's thumb has found the scars on the inside of his left forearm. He does not look down at them. "When I was fourteen. The Senior Alchemist's array would have killed the patient, so I corrected it, and the man lived. Then the court kept me. They kept the title and the parade and the boy who was right, and they let the rest of me go wherever it liked." A breath. "Keeping the body is the easy part. What's still in it afterwards is the question."

"You were right," Malphas says. "You're always right, Mu-jin. It's the least useful thing about you."

He says it with such warmth that Hiromi laughs, and the laugh breaks whatever has been standing in the room. Through the wall, a commercial traveller in the next private room is telling a long story about a dog, and a table of men are laughing at the end of it, too loudly, the way men laugh who have been on a train all day.

The fire settles in the grate. Out in the cinder yard the eastbound engine lets off a long, slow sigh of steam that crosses the window white and is gone.

"I did have to ask one thing, though." Malphas says it lightly, and he does not lean forward to say it. "Your Academy's been in touch with investigators, hasn't it?"

Mu-jin's glass stops a finger's breadth off the cloth.

He sets it down. He nods, slowly. He had not known it was being said outside the walls. It has been a day. The wardsmiths have not finished their counts.

"Yes," he says. "We have. They're helping us with a matter. I doubt I could give you much of it, even if I wanted to." He studies the man across the table a moment. The dark coat without its seal. The veins at the temples. "You've someone inside the Academy, then. I didn't think you knew a soul there but me."

Malphas chuckles, low in the chest.

"Come now, Mu-jin. I'm a man of a great many correspondents. You're only the one I like best." He opens one hand on the cloth, palm up, the gesture of a man with nothing in it. "That's how a man stays accurate at my age. Twenty letters a week and a good memory for who writes what." His eyes move, easily, to the boy. "Your son, for instance. He's every bit as flippant as the reports say. And entirely reasonable for the Paragon of Dominion, I'd have thought. A boy the world knows by name at thirteen had better be flippant, or it'll crush him flat."

*Reports*, Mu-jin thinks. The word goes into him the way cold goes into a flask that has been set down on stone. It is a small word, and it is plural. He looks at Malphas and sees an old friend smiling at his son over the remains of a mutton supper, and he puts the word away in the part of himself where he keeps glyph chains that do not quite close.

Hiromi does not know what *flippant* means.

He can tell from the old man's voice that it's something, and it came in the same breath as *Paragon* and *Dominion*, which are the two best words anyone has ever said about him, so he decides on the spot that it's a compliment and commits to it with his whole body.

"Hey! *Thanks!*" It fills the room. A glass on the sideboard rings faintly in sympathy. "I'm *very* flippant. Extremely flippant. The most flippant in Class X, probably, and that's with Rikudoku in it." He grins at Malphas and then, when nobody laughs, at his father, and then, because his father's face is doing something complicated with the corner of its mouth, back at Malphas. "But I'm strong too. I'm really strong."

Then the grin eases, because the true things always come out quieter from Hiromi, even when he doesn't mean them to.

"I've got a lot to learn," he says. "I know I do. Xanelor nearly had me today. If Father hadn't thrown the vial..." He stops. His hand has found the fresh cut on the bridge of his nose and is touching it. He takes the hand away. "I just want to get stronger. That's all. Stronger than that."

Malphas chuckles again, but it comes out softer this time, and he looks at the boy for a long moment without saying anything at all.

The door opens.

It is the waiter, a thin grey man in a black waistcoat shiny at the pockets, who has been hovering all evening at the edge of the room with a napkin over his forearm like a flag of truce. Behind him stands the Halt's proprietress, a stout woman of sixty in bombazine gone rusty at the seams, with a ring of keys at her belt the size of a horse's collar and the face of someone who has seen every kind of guest a railway can deliver and been surprised by none of them. She has a folded buff-coloured form in her hand.

"Wire for the gentleman," she says. "Come up the line from the east junction a quarter-hour back. The boy said it was paid for reply." Her eyes go over the table, the ruined mutton, the untouched beer at Malphas's place, the Academy boy with the white hair, and settle on nobody. "I'll not have you say it sat at the desk."

"Thank you, Mrs Dunnock." Velder has stood up from his chair by the fire to take it. He reads it where he stands, turned a little away from the table, his broad back to the lamp. It is short. He folds it once along its crease and hands it to Malphas without a word.

Malphas reads it. His face does not change. He folds it again, into quarters, and puts it inside his coat.

"No reply," he says to the proprietress. "Thank you."

She goes. The waiter goes after her with the bill tray and its one corrected figure.

Hiromi is not looking at the wire. He is looking at Velder's case.

It has stood at the foot of the secretary's chair all evening, a squat black leather case with brass corners and two brass latches, set close enough to the fire that the leather on the near side has gone warm and soft-looking in the light. The latches are wet. Hiromi watches a bead of water gather on the lower edge of the left one, swell, and run down the leather in a thin dark line to the floorboards. There is a small dark patch on the boards already where others have run before it, in a room warm enough that his own collar is sticking to his neck.

He says nothing about it. He puts it away beside the tall Class S boy's back foot and the burn-scars on Velder's hands, and the drawer he is keeping in his head is getting fuller than he likes.

Malphas has taken a card from his waistcoat pocket. It is thick cream stock with a bevelled edge, the kind printed by a good house in the capital, and he lays it on the tablecloth and slides it with one finger across to the boy's place. Not to Mu-jin's. To Hiromi's.

HARROWGATE & VELL, it says in plain engraved capitals. MANUFACTURING CHEMISTS. And under it, in a small neat hand in black ink, a name and a forwarding address.

"Your father won't tell me what he's doing," Malphas says to Hiromi, pleasantly. "Quite right too. So I'll tell you something instead, since you're the one who asked." His dark eyes are kind. They are very steady. "Every strong young man I ever knew who wanted to grow stronger wrote to somebody sooner or later. Most wrote to the wrong people." He takes his finger off the card. "If you ever want to write to the right one, that address will find me. Anywhere on the continent. I answer all my letters. Ask your father."

The card lies on the white cloth between Hiromi's plate and his father's hand.

## Author notes

**Notes**
- **The beat, all kept:** Mu-jin refuses to share the notebook and offers to talk about anything else. Malphas respects that and gives his "pharmaceutical company" story. He didn't come for business, then asks about the investigators. Mu-jin guesses Malphas has an insider, and Malphas answers with "many connections" and calls Hiromi flippant. Hiromi takes "flippant" as a compliment and says he wants to get stronger. The beat said Malphas liked this about "Hiromi"; read as Mu-jin, the man who keeps his vows.
- **Added:** a scholars' argument that echoes Taesyn's question from the same night: how much of a person can be replaced before they aren't the person any more. Mu-jin asks it without knowing he is asking the villain.
- **The card, checked:** Mu-jin's age-14 correction saved the patient.
- **The wire and the ending:** a telegram arrives from the east junction; Malphas reads it and sends no reply. Malphas gives his card to Hiromi, not to Mu-jin. It is lying on the tablecloth, so whether Hiromi keeps it is the player's.
- **Lies on the table:** Malphas's story is that a core took the miller's girl at Ossery Cut. Set against his train scene, the girl more likely went to him. Together with "my secretary" in the last post, that makes two uncorrected lies.
- **What Hiromi notices and nobody explains:** water beading on the latches of Velder's case in a warm room. It could be something cold inside; that stays unsaid.
- **Made up here, not yet ruled:** Harrowgate & Vell, Manufacturing Chemists; Ossery Cut and the Aldous boys; Mrs Dunnock, who runs the Halt; the waiter; the commercial traveller telling his dog story through the wall.

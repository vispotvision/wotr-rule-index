# Mu-jin: The Tenth Bell

Mu-jin laughs.

He does not mean to. It comes out of him short and surprised, the laugh of a man who has been handed back exactly what he gave, and for one full second he looks pleased. Then he remembers the notebook, and the Grand Mage, and the hour, and he arranges his face into something more suitable for a professor.

"Correct," he says. "Witnessing honestly does not require witnessing happily. I'll put it in the margin in your words, and the keepers can argue with you about it." He takes his hand off the back of the bench. "There'll be tea on the stove in the Class X room. It's terrible tea. Complain about that too, if it helps. The hands will be there at ten."

He inclines his head to the boy, the same fraction deeper than a professor owes a student, and goes.

On his way to the door he looks down the long room toward the knot of Class X coats. The bench in the middle of them is empty. There is a plate on the table where Hiromi was sitting, and on the plate is the largest portion of roast in the refectory, and nobody has touched it.

---

Hiromi's room is the smallest on the east gallery, and it is his.

He argued for it on the first day. He wanted his own peace and quiet, he told the housemaster, and the housemaster looked at his file and then at his hair and gave in faster than Hiromi had expected. The room has a bed, a washstand, a window that sticks, and a single draw-lamp on a brass arm over the desk that hums half a note flat because the fitting was never seated right. Under the rug beside the bed, where nobody has thought to look, there is a binding circle chalked on the bare boards. He redraws it every third night. It is the most careful thing in the room.

He has the rug rolled back and the circle freshened and his boots off, sitting cross-legged on the cold floor in his shirtsleeves with a candle on either side of him. On the boards in front of his knees lie the things he has gathered.

A silver disc the size of a thumbnail, bought from the Academy stores with most of a month's allowance, plain on both faces, with a hole punched through for a cord. A scrap of red silk cord. A twist of powdered cinnabar in waxed paper that he took from the cabinet in Geturo's workroom this afternoon, and a pinch of salt from the refectory cruet, and a stub of his father's good chalk.

And a teaching sheet in his father's hand.

It is one of the old ones from the Formula Satchel, soft at the folds, the Fixatio ring drawn out large with every glyph labelled in the same careful script Mu-jin uses on the board: *here the seal closes; here it holds; here, if you have been careless, it lets go*. He took it out of the satchel three nights ago. His father has not missed it. His father does not miss things, which means he has missed it and said nothing, and Hiromi has decided not to think about which.

He smooths it flat on the boards with the side of his hand and reads the ring through twice.

It is for Saeloria. That is the whole point of it. He owes her a gift made by hand, and a thing bought from a shop in the town would not count, and a thing made with Geturo's help would count even less. So it has to be this. A charm to hang at her throat, with one of his own summons folded inside it, small, sleeping, waiting for the day somebody raises a hand to her. Her guards are strict. Her people are strict. He wants to give her one guard that nobody else chose.

He wets the chalk and draws the Fixatio ring on the silver, very small, working with the point of a needle through the chalk-dust, copying from the sheet a glyph at a time. The ring goes round the rim of the disc. Inside it he lays the cinnabar in a thin red line and the salt over it. His tongue is between his teeth. The draw-lamp hums its flat half-note overhead.

Then he calls the warden.

It is his favourite. It has been his favourite since he was eight, broad and faceless and patient, armoured in something pale that the light goes into and does not come back out of, with a shield the height of a man. Today in the Arena a boy with no magic put a palm through that shield and threw the pair of them across the ring together, and when the warden came back into him afterwards he felt it arrive dented. He has not called it since. He calls it now.

It hurts.

His Crystal is scraped to the bottom from the duel, and every draw since has cost what a first call costs, and he knew that before he sat down. The ache comes up from under his sternum and spreads into his jaw. His ears ring. A warm thread runs out of his left nostril and over his lip, and he wipes it with his wrist without looking at it, because Lily would count it if she were here, and she isn't.

The warden rises out of the circle at a quarter of its height, knee-high, dented, its pale shield scored across the face where Xanelor's hand went through. It stands on the chalk and waits for him, the way it always waits.

He begins to fold it down.

This is the part the sheet does not teach, because the sheet was written for reagents, and a summons is not a reagent. A summons has to agree to be small. He has to hold the warden's shape in his head, all of it, and then close it inward a ring at a time, the way his mother rolls a hide for travel, tight and tighter until it will fit through the eye of the Fixatio ring and lie still inside the silver. His hands are shaking. The warden folds once, twice, and on the third fold it catches and will not go.

He feels it resist him. It is too tired to go smaller.

And then something shifts.

It comes from nowhere he can point to. It is a thrum under the floor, under the building, under the ground the building stands on, a pressure he feels in his back teeth the way his father says he feels the conduit mains, and it is not his. It moves through him on its way somewhere else, and in passing it takes the weight of the warden off his hands. The folding stops being a thing he has to force. The warden goes down into the silver as easily as a sword into a good scabbard.

*The world knows what you are before you ask it,* he thinks. That was what the old instructors used to say, when he was six and did not know what they meant. *Dominion.*

He knows the names of the Fourteen from the first week's lesson on the board. He knows which one of them sits over Command. He does not say the name aloud. He is thirteen and alone in a small room at night with blood on his lip, and there are some names a boy does not say aloud in that situation, even if he is going to be the greatest summoner who ever lived.

The candles lean.

Both flames bend inward toward the disc at once, as if something in the middle of the circle had drawn a breath. The salt on the silver darkens. The cinnabar line takes light along its whole length, and the light is red, a deep, clear, steady red that none of Hiromi's workings has ever been, the colour of a coal when you blow on it. It runs once round the Fixatio ring and closes on itself. The ring holds.

It holds exactly where his father's sheet says it will.

He sits back on his heels, breathing through his mouth. The draw-lamp overhead has stopped humming flat. For the first time since he moved into the room it is humming true, and he does not notice.

The disc on the boards is still warm when he picks it up. Inside the silver, if he tilts it to the candle, he can see the flame going round and round the ring, slow as a turning wheel. Folded down small in the middle of it, the warden is asleep.

Somebody knocks on the door.

"Mahuo." It is Odran Sell, the east-gallery prefect, a fifth-year from a chartered family in the Stannvaard marches, with a voice like a man reading from a list he did not write. "I can smell chalk through the door, Mahuo. It's past the ninth. If I can smell chalk, the housemaster can smell chalk."

"Not chalk!" Hiromi says. It comes out at his full volume, and it is the first thing all night that has. "Candle! Reading. I'm reading. Improving myself."

A pause from the corridor.

"Improve yourself in the dark," Odran says, and his steps go off down the gallery.

Hiromi threads the red cord through the hole in the disc and ties it with the knot his mother taught him, and then he holds it up in front of his face and watches the flame go round. He looks at it for a long time. The grin that comes is not the one he uses in corridors. It is smaller, and nobody sees it.

He does not roll the rug back over the circle. He forgets to.

---

The Class X room at night is a different room.

The tiered benches go up into the dark in rings. The slate board is washed clean. One lamp is lit, the one at the front on its brass pillar by the board, and it throws a circle of yellow light on the demonstration table and leaves the rest of the chamber to the moon through the high windows. On the iron stove in the corner the tea is already stewing. Mu-jin has made it too strong on purpose. The boy will have something to complain about.

He has finished tomorrow's notes: tempering, the first three classifications, the exercise with the two flasks that always makes somebody in the back row faint. They are stacked at the corner of the table and squared to its edge. In the middle of the table he has laid out what the copy will need, and only that. Eight sheets of good rag paper ruled at true scale, and the Grand Mage's list of questions pinned flat under a glass weight beside them. Two pens. Fresh ink, a steel rule, a lens. The notebook itself he has not taken out. It stays in his coat until the witness is in the room.

He sets out a second cup. Then he sits down in front of the ruled sheets and does not begin.

He is thinking about the refectory. The plate nobody had touched.

Geturo was never like this. Geturo waited. He waited at doors and in corridors, and he waited eleven months to show anyone the thing he had built, and when things came to him, they came in their own time and found him ready. Lily planned. She knew where the corridor went before she stepped into it. Hiromi went through things. He always had. He went through the binding circle at six and the rules of summoning at seven, and today he went through the far edge of his own Crystal while a boy with a bow stood over him, and he would have kept going through until there was nothing left to go through with.

He is going to grow up hating me, Mu-jin thinks. He thinks it quite calmly, the way he would read a figure off a gauge. It's a result. I can see how the working closes.

The door at the top of the tiers opens.

He knows the step. Nobody else at the Academy comes down stairs like that, heel first and silent, a hunter's step learned on ice that talks back. Frithia comes down the tiers into the lamplight with her sash off and her sleeves pushed to the elbow and the ringed-seal hat in one hand, and she stops at the edge of the demonstration table and looks at the second cup, and then at him.

"Your witness is late," she says.

"My witness is thirteen. He isn't due yet." He does not get up. "I thought you'd be asleep."

"I was." She puts the hat down on the corner of the table, on top of his squared notes, and does not look at what she has put it on. "Then I wasn't. Geturo told me the old man's gone east. He heard from the porter. And Hiromi's gone to his room without supper, and Odran Sell's been down the east gallery twice, sniffing at doors like a dog at a smokehouse." She pulls out the chair across from him and sits in it, and the chair is too low for her, as every chair in the Academy is too low for her, and her knees come up. "So. You'll tell me."

He tells her.

He tells it badly. He tells all of it: the card, and how his hand was on it before he knew, and the cinder yard, and what he said, and what Hiromi said back. He explains why he did it before she asks, and then he explains it again, a different way, in case the first way did not hold. At *smothered* his voice stops. It starts again in a slightly different place.

Frithia listens with her elbows on her knees and her long hands hanging loose between them. When he is finished, she reaches across the table and takes the pen out of his fingers, which he did not know he was holding.

"You shouldn't beat yourself for it, Mu-jin," she says. "He's thirteen and he's tired and a man he liked was taken off the table in front of him. He said the worst thing he had, because that's what boys do with the worst thing they have, the minute somebody gives them a reason. I doubt he meant it."

"He meant it." Mu-jin looks at his empty hand. "He feels I'm holding him back. Every time I stand between him and something, he feels the hand on his collar. He doesn't see that he's going too fast. He won't let anything come to him in its own time. He goes and takes it, and some of what he takes is going to be bigger than he is." His voice has started to stack again, one piece after the next. "The circles before dawn, Frithia. I know about the circles. I've known since the first week. Everybody thinks I don't."

"Everybody knows you do." She turns the pen over in her fingers. It looks very small in them. "Except him."

Somewhere below them, a long way below, something moves.

Mu-jin feels it before he knows he has felt it. It comes up through the floor and the tiers and the soles of his boots and into the place behind his breastbone where he has always felt a seal failing: a held breath, let go, taken again. Very deep. Very brief. He goes still with his hand flat on the table, and he listens with the part of himself that is not his ears.

There is nothing. The stove ticks. The lamp burns. Whatever it was has closed again, so smoothly that if he were asked to swear to it before the Grand Mage, he could not.

"Mu-jin."

"It's nothing." He takes his hand off the table. "The mains. Go on."

Frithia looks at him a moment longer than she needs to, the look she gives a horizon that has changed colour. Then she goes on.

"You can't keep him on a rope," she says. "He isn't Geturo. Geturo you can walk beside. Hiromi was born with more in his hands than you had at his age, more than you have now, and you know it better than any of us, because you were in the hall when he was six. One day he'll be stronger than both of us put together." She sets the pen down on the ruled paper, exactly along the line. "I know you want him safe. So do I. I'd tie him to the bed if I thought the bed would hold. But think what it is from where he stands."

"On sand," Mu-jin says. "With an arrow coming."

"Where he stands," says Frithia, "is in front of his whole class. With his father climbing down into the ring to pick him up." She lets that sit. "Every boy in Class X saw your vial. Every girl. The Zettari boy saw it, and the masked boy saw it from the sand. Tomorrow at breakfast every one of them will know that when Hiromi Mahuo falls, his father catches him. Think what that is to a boy of thirteen who wants to be the greatest summoner who ever lived." Her voice does not rise. It has never needed to. "It shames him, Mu-jin. You save him, and it shames him, and he can't hate the saving, so he hates you."

Mu-jin opens his mouth, and closes it.

He had not thought about it like that. He has thought about it, he realises, in every other way: as a working, as a failure point, as a forty-second window he could see and his son could not. He has not once thought about the benches.

"My father let me go through the ice," Frithia says. "When I was eight. He took me out on the sea-ice in the spring, near the shore, where it was rotten, and he let me walk ahead of him, and he let me go through." She turns her left wrist over on the table, where the old white seam of the walrus scar runs up into her sleeve. That scar came from another spring. She looks at it anyway, the way a person looks at something that is hers. "He was two steps behind me with a line, and he had me out before the cold took my breath. He never told the others. As far as my brothers knew, I pulled myself out of the sea with my own two hands and walked home dripping."

"Did you believe it?"

"For years." She stands up, and her head goes up out of the lamplight into the dark. "I'm alive. I've never trusted ice since. And I never hated him for the line, because I didn't know he was holding it."

She picks up her hat.

"Two steps behind," she says. "Not one. And let the others think he climbed out by himself." At the foot of the tiers she stops and looks back at him, and at the second cup, and at the eight ruled sheets. "Drink your terrible tea. Bring the boy his supper in the morning. Leave it at the door. Don't knock."

She goes up the tiers, heel first and silent, and the door closes behind her.

Mu-jin sits in the circle of the lamp for a while without moving.

Then he takes the notebook out of his coat and lays it on the table in front of the witness's chair, closed, with the Academy's seal uppermost, where it can be seen. The card comes out with it and falls face down on the wood. He looks at it. He puts it back in his coat. He pours the second cup, and sets the pot back on the stove, and sits down in front of the ruled sheets with his hands open on the table, palm up, chalk in the creases, so that whoever comes down the tiers will see them first.

Far off across the quad, the Academy bell begins to strike ten.

---

The eastbound is two miles down the line by now, its tail lamp a red point going small in the dark over the junction. There is a first-class compartment on it with two cups of cold coffee on the folding table, and a porter who has been told not to linger, and nobody in either seat.

Malphas stands in a culvert under the Academy's south wall with water running over his shoes.

It is a conduit service passage, brick-vaulted, four feet wide, built in the last century for the fitters who keep the Academy's mains, and it runs from the goods sidings beside the station straight under the long quad. The main goes along the right-hand wall at shoulder height. It is a sealed iron pipe as thick as a man's body, humming, with a gauge-housing bolted to it every forty feet, a brass dial behind glass and a Guild seal on each access plate. The air smells of wet brick and machine oil, and of the faint hot-copper smell a main gives off when it is drawing hard.

The man with the lantern ahead of them is called Pell. He is a night fitter on the Academy's conduit gang, forty years old, with a fitter's forearms and a fitter's cough, and a sister in the east whose children Malphas's house has kept breathing for two winters when nobody else came. He has not asked what they want under the arena. He does not look at the case.

"Here," Pell says. He has stopped at an iron door let into the left-hand wall, rust-streaked, with a newer lock on it than the door deserves. "Past this it's the old undercroft, and past that you're under the ring. There's a ward-line across the stair at the bottom. I can't open that. Nobody on the gang can. It's not ours."

"I know," Malphas says. "Open the door."

Pell opens the door.

The stair beyond goes down in a spiral, dressed stone, very old, older than anything above it. Pell does not follow them. Velder goes first with the case. Malphas goes after him with one hand trailing on the cold newel, and the stone is cold enough to hurt, and his fingers stay the colour of a living man's fingers the whole way down.

At the bottom the ward-line crosses the last step like a thread of light laid on the stone. It is Academy work, good work: three glyph-chains braided and fixed, and a bell-glyph worked into the braid that will ring somewhere in a warden's lodge if the line is crossed.

Velder sets the case down on the second-to-last step and opens the two brass latches.

Cold comes out of it. It pours over the lip of the case and down the step like water, white, and the ward-line where the cold touches it goes dim, and then dimmer, and frost grows along the braid in fine feathered lines until the light inside it is only a grey glow under ice. Malphas does not look into the case. He knows what is in it. He steps over the frozen line, and Velder closes the latches and follows.

Somewhere far above them a bell rings once.

It is small and clear and very far away, the sound of a glyph doing exactly what it was made to do. It rings a second time. Then a third.

"Eleven minutes," Velder says. "Pell said eleven. For the lodge to send a man down the south stair."

"Then we'll use nine."

The cell is at the end of a short passage. Its door is iron and has no handle on the outside. A brass plate is riveted to the centre of it at eye height, and Malphas lifts his lantern and reads it.

THREAT GRADE IX
MAHUO, C.
NO SPEECH · NO LIGHT · NO NAME

"No name," Malphas says quietly. "They put his name on the door and then told the door not to say it." He puts his palm flat against the iron under the plate. "That is an institution for you, Velder. Write that down somewhere."

Velder puts his shoulder to the door. It was never locked. There is no need to lock a door when the thing behind it is already fastened to the floor.

The cell is round and bare and very cold, and in the middle of it, on the stone, Cozbi Mahuo kneels.

He is smaller than the stories. That is the first thing. The stories have him three planes wide and a mountain tall, with four arms tearing out of his waist and black scale climbing his limbs. The man on the floor is thin, and his black hair has been cropped close to the skull, and his arms are drawn behind him into two iron sleeves worked all over with glyphs, and the sleeves are chained to a ring-bolt sunk in the floor, short enough that he cannot lift his forehead more than a hand's breadth off the stone. Across his mouth lies a band of something that is neither leather nor metal, stitched into the skin at both corners with glyph-thread. His eyes are open. There is dust on the floor around him, a great deal of it, and none of it is moving.

Malphas crouches down in front of him, just out of reach of the chain.

"Grade Nine," he says. "Cozbi Mahuo." He says the name slowly, as if reading it off a plate that was not allowed to speak it. "You're very well known in my line of work, you know. Better known than you'd think. A man who went past death and came back, and made the spirit world kneel for him while he was there. They teach you in the Accord's schools now as a warning." He tilts the lantern so that the light falls on the kneeling man's face. "You could have freed us all. The whole world, from the whole wheel of it. Birth and the core and the grave. You had your hand on the lock, and you used it to settle a family grudge."

Cozbi's mouth does not move under the band.

The voice comes anyway. It comes out of the cold air of the room, from all sides at once and very close, as though the stone itself had been taught to speak and found it had rather a pleasant voice.

"Who are you, then." Unhurried. Warm, even. "Another one come down the stair to look? They send one every season. A professor, usually. They like to stand where you're crouching and tell me what I am, and then they go back up and write a paper." The dust around the chain lies perfectly still. "You're not a professor. You crouch like a man who's knelt by a lot of beds."

"I have." Malphas smiles. "A great many."

"And tonight you ate mutton. You drank nothing. You've had your hand around a Mahuo child's wrist in the last four hours, and held it longer than a handshake wants." The voice considers him. "I can smell the pulse on your fingers. Summoner's blood, young, and running fast from an empty Crystal. Whose son?"

Velder, by the door, goes very still.

Malphas does not answer that. He sets the lantern down on the stone between them, carefully, the way he would set down a flask he did not want to jar.

"I want you to join me, Cozbi," he says. "My house has the architecture half built, and it's good architecture, better than anything the Accord has on paper. We keep bodies going when the Essence fails. We keep the Shell and the pulse and the breath, and we lose what was in them. Every time. Four in seven survive the process, and none of the four is who they were." He spreads his long hands, palms up, empty. "You know where the rest goes. You've been. I can take those sleeves off you. I can have you out of this room and up that stair and on a train before the lodge finds its boots. All I ask is a promise: that you'll turn everything you know about the spirit world to a single end, and help me take death out of the world."

The room is silent for a long time.

Then the voice laughs. It is a low laugh, with no threat in it, and it goes round the round walls of the cell and comes back doubled.

"Oh, the ego of it." There is real delight in the voice now. "The pride. You come down a fitter's stair with water in your shoes and a box of stolen cold, and you ask the God of Summons to kneel down lower than he's kneeling now and carry your pharmacy for you." The dust at the edge of the chain stirs, once, and lies still again. "I'll grant you the ambition. It's very large, for a mortal. I've always liked large arithmetic."

The voice leans in. It does not move, but it leans in.

"But you've got the sum backwards, doctor. Death is the only honest clerk the world has ever kept. It wrote my people down when no historian would. Every Mahuo the Zettari and the Moto ground into the mud, death took down in its book, name by name, and it never once asked who their fathers were." A pause, very gentle. "You want me to burn the only ledger that ever balanced? No. I refuse."

Malphas stays crouched where he is for a moment.

He does not look disappointed. He looks, if anything, the way he looked across a tablecloth four hours ago at an old friend who would not tell him what was in his notebook: a senior watching an apprentice refuse him and liking him better for it.

"Everybody refuses the first letter," he says.

He takes a card out of his waistcoat pocket. Thick cream stock, bevelled edge, engraved in plain capitals: HARROWGATE & VELL. MANUFACTURING CHEMISTS. He turns it over, and with the stub of a pencil he writes something on the back in his small neat hand, and he lays it on the stone with the writing uppermost, a hand's breadth in front of Cozbi's bowed head, exactly where the chain will let him see it and exactly where it will not let him reach.

"Nine minutes, Lord Malphas," Velder says from the door.

Far above, on the south stair, a door bangs, and boots begin to come down.

The card lies on the stone between Cozbi's knees.

## Author notes

**What this is.** A roleplay turn in present tense (R49-02), with four movements built from Isaac's three beats. First, Mu-jin answers Zaire in the refectory. Zaire's lines are his player's and aren't quoted back or extended, and nothing is written for him after them. The Class X movement stops at the tenth bell with the witness chair empty, so his arrival stays his player's to write. Saeloria appears only as the person Hiromi is making the gift for.

**Isaac's beats, kept.** Hiromi crafts a protection charm with a defensive summon folded into it. His favourite summon comes (the warden, from *Xanelor: Juggernaut's Fist*). A thrum makes the spirits easier to manage, the charm swirls with red flame, and Irath is present as Archon of Dominion. Mu-jin is finishing his notes and preparing the working when Frithia comes to check on him, and Isaac's lines are kept close. "Don't beat yourself up; I doubt he meant it how he said it." "He feels I'm holding him back... going too fast, not letting things come naturally." "You can't keep him on a rope, he isn't Geturo, born with abilities past even yours, one day stronger than both of us." "It must be embarrassing to be saved by your dad all the time." Then "Mujin didn't think about it like that." Malphas, guided in through connections, reaches Cozbi's body under the Academy while alarms go off. He names Cozbi by the Grade 9 Threat, calls him the man who surpassed death and held the spirit world on its knees, and says he could have freed everyone from the cycle of death. Cozbi, mouth sealed, arms bound, tied to the floor, speaks anyway: "another come to gloat," then the offer, then "what ego, what pride, the God of Summons lower himself for a mortal... I refuse."

**One-line pushbacks, written around.** (1) Isaac's beat says "his twins." The cards make Hiromi 13 and Lily 12, the youngest of three, so they aren't twins. The prose has Geturo waiting, Lily planning and Hiromi going through, with no twins, which also covers the "push past their limits" line. (2) In Isaac's text "Hiromi shook his head" is Mu-jin's line to Frithia, and it's given to Mu-jin here. (3) The gift is for Saeloria, per *Hiromi: The Picnic* and *Something Special*. The charm is written as hers.

**Irath.** The Fourteen Archons page has Irath as Flame of Will and Dominion, Fire correspondence, Dominion Magic (*Imperaris*). The red flame is the Fire of Irath's station. Hiromi's card title, Paragon of Dominion, is left to point at Irath without the page confirming it, and Hiromi won't say the name aloud. Nothing in the narration claims Irath is paying attention to him. The thrum passes through him "on its way somewhere else."

**Phenomenon line (the charm).** Real phenomenon: stored strain, a spring held compressed behind a latch. Physics Domain: Fixatio (the seal closes, holds, lets go). Wellspring: Fixatio, from Mu-jin's teaching sheet. Mechanism: the summons is folded ring by ring to fit the eye of the Fixatio ring, and the seal holds it at rest like a latch holding a spring. Category: Vocatia-into-vessel, pending. Fault: the fold has to be *agreed* by the summons, and a tired summons catches at the third fold. The cost is on the page. Hiromi's Crystal is still empty from the duel, so every draw costs a first call. The ache runs from sternum to jaw, his ears ring, and his left nostril bleeds. The warden comes back dented.

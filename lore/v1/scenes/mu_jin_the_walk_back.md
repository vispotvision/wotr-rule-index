# Mu-jin: The Walk Back

Mrs Dunnock brings the pudding herself. It is a treacle sponge in a brown crock, with a jug of custard beside it already skinning over, and she sets both down in the middle of the table and looks once at the card lying on the cloth. She gives it the same look she gave the wire: a thing that has arrived at her hotel and will leave it again, and is no business of the house in between.

"Kitchen shuts at ten," she says. "Fire's another fourpence on the coal if the gentlemen want it kept." She goes.

Mu-jin's hand is on the card.

He did not decide to put it there. He finds it the way he found his hand on the notebook earlier, fingers flat on the cream stock and the bevelled edge under his thumb, and by the time he has noticed, the card is off the cloth and inside his coat, lying against the notebook over his ribs. Hiromi's hand was halfway across the table. It stops above the custard jug and hangs there a moment with nothing to do.

Malphas watches all of it. He does not look offended. He looks pleased, the way he used to look at the assay-house when an apprentice caught the error in a column before he could point it out himself.

"Quite right," he says. "I'd have done the same, at your end of the table." He takes up the serving spoon and cuts the sponge into four with its edge, exact quarters, the treacle running out black into the crock. "The offer stands all the same. Offers are cheap, young man. It's the letters that cost."

Hiromi takes his hand back and puts it in his lap. Then he takes the largest quarter of the sponge with the other one, because he is Hiromi.

They stay the hour Mrs Dunnock allows them, and most of it is good. Malphas tells the story of the winter Mu-jin fell asleep over a fixative bath and woke with his cuff sealed to the bench, and how the whole assay-house came to look, and how the Guild examiner charged him a shilling for the coat he had to be cut out of. Hiromi laughs so hard he has to put his spoon down. Mu-jin laughs too, and means it, and hears his own laugh come back off the panelling younger than he is.

Under the laugh the word stays where he put it.

*Reports.* He turns it over while Malphas talks, the way he turns a flask to read the meniscus. A man of twenty correspondents might hear that the Paragon of Dominion had entered Class X; that much is in the Academy's own lists. He might even hear there was a match in the Arena today. Mu-jin counts the honest roads by which a man in a pharmaceutical house far to the east comes to know the tone of a thirteen-year-old's voice, and he runs out of them at three. The fourth is ugly. He does not set it on the bench. He is not ready to look at it under a good light, not with this man across the table cutting pudding into exact quarters the way he always did.

Velder has not left the fire in half an hour. He relights his cigar and puts it out again. Once, as the coal settles, he leans down and draws the black case back from the grate with the side of his boot. He squares it by one brass corner. Then he holds his fingers out to the fire and works them, the way a man does in a warm room after touching something colder than it ought to be.

Mu-jin sees it and puts it with the word.

At a quarter to eleven the eastbound's bell goes on the platform, two strokes and a pause, and Malphas stands.

They say goodbye under the iron canopy, where the steam off the engine comes across the cinder yard in long slow sheets and beads on everything. It drips from the canopy's cast leaves. It beads on the shoulders of Malphas's good dark coat, which has no seal on it anywhere. Somewhere down the platform a lamp-man is going along the standards with his pole, opening the draw-valves one by one, and each lamp comes up with a small cough and a blue flame before it settles white.

Malphas takes Mu-jin's hand in both of his again.

"The wire," Mu-jin says. "Nothing to keep you, I hope."

"The works manager at Harrowgate." Malphas makes a face. "Fretting over a delivery of carboys. He frets by telegraph. It's the only vice I've ever caught him in." He holds the hand a moment longer and lets it go. "Sixteen years, Mu-jin. Don't make it another sixteen. I haven't got them."

Then he looks down at the boy.

"Eat, Hiromi," he says. "Your father never did either, and look at him." And he smiles, and it is a good smile, warm to the bone, and he turns and walks into the steam with Velder a pace behind him. The secretary carries the case at arm's length from his leg, the way a man carries a bucket that has been filled too full.

The steam closes over them both. The engine lets off, and the whole yard goes white.

---

Hiromi walks three paces ahead of his father across the cinder yard, which is the furthest he has ever walked ahead of him on purpose.

The cinders crunch under his boots. His hands are shoved into his coat pockets, the high collar up and the top buckle still undone, and his white hair has gone flat and dark at the ends with the damp. The standpipe at the yard's edge ticks as it cools, a slow iron sound, and beyond it the Academy's black walls climb out of the fog with the conduit runs lit along their faces in thin lines of Guild-green, humming low enough that Mu-jin feels it in his back teeth before he hears it.

"Hiromi."

Nothing. Three paces.

"I've kept the card," Mu-jin says. "I'll go on keeping it. You're not to write to him."

Hiromi stops so hard that cinders skid out from under his heel. He turns.

"What?" It goes off across the yard like a dropped tray. A porter wheeling an empty luggage barrow back toward the platform looks up, sees the Academy coats and the white hair, and decides with great firmness to look somewhere else. "Why? Why not? What did I do?"

"You did nothing. He did." Mu-jin keeps his voice level. It costs him. "Malphas is a very intelligent man, Hiromi. The most intelligent I've shared a bench with, and I've shared benches with men who had their names put on Wellsprings. For sixteen years I've read what he wanted before I broke the wax. Tonight I sat across a table from him for three hours and I couldn't tell you what he wanted once." He stops. He makes himself stop, and then does not manage it. "And there are investigators inside our walls this week, and a matter I've given my word on, and he knew both before he got off the train. He knew about you. He said *reports*. Reports are something a man is sent. Somebody is sending them."

"That's got nothing to do with me!"

"Everything to do with you. He put the card at your place, Hiromi. Not mine."

"Because he was *talking* to me!" Hiromi throws both hands out of his pockets. "Because he actually asked me things! He asked what I wanted and he listened to the answer, he didn't look at me like I'd spilled something on a circle!" His voice cracks upward on the last of it and he hauls it back down by main force. "He seemed like a good man. He was nice to me. He was *your friend*. You read his letters twice. Geturo said so, in front of everybody. And now he's what, some kind of... what? What is he?"

Mu-jin has no answer that fits in the space the boy has left for it.

He opens his mouth. He closes it. The conduit hums along the wall above them, and in the fog the engine gives two short shrieks and begins to move, the chuff of it going slow and heavy and then quicker, carrying east.

"I don't know what he is," Mu-jin says. "That's the whole trouble."

"So you don't even *know*." Hiromi laughs, and there is nothing in the laugh. "You don't know, and you're taking it anyway. Of course. Of course you are." He jams his hands back into his pockets. "Why do you always have to control every little thing I do? Every one. Where I sit, what I eat, who I fight, what ring I fight in. You moved my duel. You threw a *vial* into my duel."

"Because if I didn't," Mu-jin says, "you'd be dead."

It comes out quiet. Hiromi hears it anyway.

"You'd be dead, Hiromi. Today. On the sand." He takes one step closer, onto the grit between them. "An arrow was coming for you as a line of fire, and you were lying in it with your Crystal scraped to the bottom and your nose running blood, and you told the boy *no*. I heard you tell him. You'd have gone on telling him no until the arrow answered for him." He can hear himself starting to explain, the words stacking up the way they do when something is frightening him, one piece of the mechanism after the next. "You believe you can't be beaten. You believe it the way other boys believe the sun comes up. It's the most dangerous thing in you, and it's the thing everybody around you loves best, because it's loud and it's bright and it makes them feel something is certain. It isn't. You were beaten today by a boy with no Wellspring and a bow he made himself, and you were beaten by a hand's breadth, and the hand was mine."

He breathes out. It stands white in front of his face.

"So I'll keep the card. I'll keep deciding which rings you fight in. If that makes you hate me, hate me. I can carry it. I've carried heavier."

For a moment Hiromi says nothing at all.

The volume goes out of him. Mu-jin sees it go, the way you see a gauge-needle drop when a district main is cut, and what is left standing on the cinders in the fog is a boy of thirteen with a cut across the bridge of his nose and grey under his eyes that was not there this morning.

"I don't want your protection," Hiromi says. Quietly. For him, it is almost a whisper. "You've smothered me with it my whole life."

"Hiromi..."

"You told Madam Ha you'd learned your lesson." He is not looking at his father now. He is looking at the standpipe, at the drip gathering on its spout. "About Geturo. Deciding which rooms he walks into. You said it in front of me, this afternoon. You said you'd learned." His jaw works. "He built a whole thing in secret for eleven months and you *learned your lesson*. And I get a card from a nice old man and you take it off the table before I can even read it properly."

Mu-jin stands very still.

It lands where the boy aimed it, and it lands somewhere else as well, lower down. The house at Sum-gol had two customs. It taught one to every child who came to it: a summoner cannot compel, he is recognised or he is not. The other it never taught, because it never had to. The house settled a person's life for them, quietly, and on their behalf, and never once asked. He left Sum-gol at fourteen to get out from under that hand, and he wrote to his sister by lamplight with a Sovereign's seal still warm on the table: *so I am asking you instead*. He was proud of that letter, and he is proud of it still, standing here on the cinders in the fog.

He has not asked his son anything tonight.

He knows it. He holds it anyway, the way he holds a correction he knows will cost him the room, because the thing in his coat is still wrong in a way he cannot yet name, and a wrong chain does not become right because the student at the board is angry.

"Now, now," he says, and it is the most ground he has. "When the investigators are gone. When I know what Malphas wants, and who's writing to him, and why he looked at your wrist when he shook your hand." Hiromi's eyes flick to him at that, fast, and away. "Then you and I sit down, and I put the card on the table between us, and we talk about it. Properly. You'll have your say, and I'll listen to it, all of it, however long it takes you. I give you my word."

"You'll have decided by then." Hiromi turns away. "You always have."

He walks on toward the gate. Four paces, now. Five.

Halfway there, he stops, and half turns back, and Mu-jin watches his son's mouth open on something. Whatever it is, it stays behind his teeth. Hiromi looks at his father for the length of one breath with his face doing something Mu-jin cannot read at all, which has not happened to him with any of his children since they were born. Then the boy shuts his mouth, and turns, and goes.

---

The night porter at the lodge is an Accord man called Hobb Farrow, near seventy, with a brazier going in an iron bucket at his feet and a tin kettle on it that has boiled dry at least once from the look of the bottom. His boots are up on the brass rail of the lodge window. He has the *Junction Courier* folded to the racing, and he does not take the boots down when the professor comes through the gate. Thirty-one years he has kept that gate, and he does not take his boots down for the Grand Mage either.

"Evening, Professor." He jerks his chin toward the long quad. "Lad went through here like the Guild was after him for back dues. Refectory, by the sound. They're still serving till the half."

"Thank you, Hobb."

"Mine were the same at that age." Hobb licks a thumb and turns a page. "Either starving or furious. Never worked out which one I was feeding. Fed 'em both and hoped."

"Did it work?"

"Two of 'em write to me." Hobb considers the racing. "Third one's in the Stannvaard mines. Don't write at all." He looks up over the top of the *Courier*, a long, faded, unhurried look. "Tea, sir? Kettle's nearly honest."

"Another night."

"Always is," says Hobb, and goes back to the horses.

---

The refectory is loud the way it always is at this hour, and the noise meets Mu-jin in the doorway like heat off an oven. Plates on scrubbed deal. Benches scraping. Somebody near the kitchen hatch has dropped a tray and is being applauded for it by a table of Class S boys. The long room smells of roast meat and rice steam and wet wool drying on two hundred backs, and above all of it the draw-lamps along the beams hum on their brass brackets with the sound a full district main makes when nothing in the world is wrong.

Behind the hatch, the cook is a Dawi woman built like a hearthstone, forearms bare to the elbow and a ladle the length of a short sword in her fist, and she is telling a first-year in blunt Dawi-accented common that if he wants the crust he can come back when he has earned the crust. The first-year goes.

Hiromi's voice comes from the far end.

Mu-jin does not need to look to find him. He looks anyway. The boy has dropped onto a bench among a knot of Class X coats. He is already telling a story, both hands going. It is loud enough to turn the nearest table round, and the grin is back on his face as if it had never left it. Only somebody who had been standing on the cinders ten minutes ago would see that it has come back on crooked, and set a little too high.

*Performs loudly so the serious work stays invisible.* Mu-jin wrote that sentence himself, once, in a report on his own son for a board that asked. He did not know then how much it would sound like a description of his own face.

He does not go down the room. Going down the room would be one more thing decided for the boy.

He looks the other way instead, toward the tall windows, and finds what he came into the refectory to find.

The Zettari boy is sitting alone at the last table under the glass, in his loose fighting robes, with a bowl of rice and roasted meat in front of him and the black grounds beyond the window at his shoulder. Mu-jin looks at him for a moment from the doorway. Thirteen. The same age as the one at the far end of the room, telling his story too loud. Handed a House's weight on a gallery this afternoon by a man with a cigarette, and asked, by Mu-jin himself, to carry a little more of it.

Asked, Mu-jin thinks. At least that one I asked.

He crosses to the window table. He does not sit. He stands at the end of the bench with his hand resting on the back of it, the notebook flat and heavy against his ribs beside the card, the chalk from the day still in the creases of his knuckles.

"Zaire." He keeps it low, under the noise of the room, pitched for one table and no further. "I start the copy at the tenth bell. The Class X room, the lamp by the board. Eight stubs at their true size and the Grand Mage's questions in the order they were put, and it'll take me until two, I think, if my hand behaves itself." A small breath. "You're not obliged. I want to say that plainly, since nobody else will have tonight. I told you I'd show you where the line falls, and I can't show you from the far side of the Academy. If you do come, sit where you can see my hand and not my face. Faces are easier to lie with."

He glances once down the length of the refectory, toward the knot of Class X coats and the white head in the middle of them, and back.

"And finish that first," he says. "Nobody ever witnessed anything well on an empty stomach. I've tried."

## Author notes

**What this is.** Isaac's Mu-jin beat (the rest of the evening at the Halt, the card, the argument with Hiromi), rebuilt and extended as a roleplay turn in present tense (R49-02), then carried into the Aetherion refectory as a reply to Zaire's cafeteria post. Nothing is written for Zaire: the turn stops on Mu-jin's line to him, and his answer, whether he comes at the tenth bell, and what he thinks of it all stay his. Taesyn and Verona are not placed on the page; Hiromi sits among unnamed Class X coats so nobody else's character is moved.

**Isaac's dialogue, kept and sharpened.** Every line of his is still in it in some form: "You can't contact him" became "You're not to write to him"; the trust line became Mu-jin's over-explanation (card voice: over-explains under stress); "That isn't fair... he seemed like a good guy" and "Why do you always have to control every little thing I do" are nearly verbatim; "because if I didn't you'd get yourself killed" and "you need to get out of the notion that you're unbeatable" became the Arena speech, grounded in the Xanelor duel; "if you want to hate me that's perfectly fine" became "hate me, I can carry it"; "I don't want your protection, you've smothered me my whole life" is kept almost exactly, with the volume dropped (Hiromi's card: under stress the volume goes).

**Added.** Mu-jin pocketing the card before Hiromi can (Isaac's "he had taken the thing"); the "reports" count; Velder's cold latch (Mu-jin's unexplained notice this turn); Malphas's parting lie about the wire (the session's uncorrected lie: the works manager and the carboys); Hiromi's Madam Ha callback, which turns Mu-jin's own line from *The Old Colleague* against him; the Sum-gol second custom and the letter to Ara, so the argument costs Mu-jin in his own history; Hiromi's unsaid thing at the gate (he saw the wet latches last scene and does not tell).

**Originated, pending ruling.** Hobb Farrow, night porter at the Academy lodge, thirty-one years on the gate, three sons (one in the Stannvaard mines). The Dawi cook at the refectory hatch (unnamed). The *Junction Courier*, a local paper. The tenth-bell copy session in the Class X room. Mu-jin once having written a board report on Hiromi.

**Rules that most constrained it.** R49-02 (present tense for a roleplay turn); R48-40 (italic thoughts in roleplay only, one per character); table rule 1 (stop on an NPC line: here the PC's own line to another player's character, leaving Zaire's beat open); table rule 8 (one notice unexplained, one lie uncorrected); R53-30 (one world-only detail per beat: the draw-valve lamps, the Guild-green conduit, the lodge brazier).

**Ledger.** The Harrowgate & Vell card is in Mu-jin's coat beside the notebook. Hiromi has not seen the address long enough to be sure of it, or has; that is Isaac's to decide. Hiromi and Mu-jin are not speaking. Mu-jin has promised Hiromi a sitting with the card "when the investigators are gone." Malphas left on the 10.45 eastbound with Velder and the case.

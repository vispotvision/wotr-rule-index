# The Left of the Door

The passage behind the throne hall smelled of blubber lamp and old stone, the particular cold-stone smell of a building that had never once in its life been warm all the way through, and under that the sweetness of tallow going rancid in the sconce nobody had scraped since the Thin Weeks. Frost-grit on the flagstones. Renard could feel it through the soles of his boots when he shifted his weight, a fine abrasive give, the floor's own report on the hour. Behind the altar-room door the night-stone ticked as it cooled. Brida breathed slow and even on the pallet inside. The girl did not snore. The girl slept the way she did everything, decided.

He stood where he always stood, which was between.

The Greymane hand rested on the hilt. Longsword, ridge-forged, the grip wrapped in cord his grandmother had waxed. He had been on the door since the second broth and his knees had the ache in them that meant the third was close, and he was thinking about nothing, which was the trade. A guard who thinks is a guard who is elsewhere.

The hall beyond the arch was dark except for the one lamp by the broken throne, and the dark had a quality to it he had stopped noticing weeks ago and noticed now: the air in that hall was heavy. Kharven was always cold and this was something else, a pressure in the sinuses, the weight of a sky before snow. It had been like that since the girl claimed her name in front of the court and the temperature of the whole room fell four fingers on Lambert's glass. Something had stayed behind after. Renard did not have the words for what and had never asked for them.

The lamp by the throne went sideways.

Sideways, toward the arch, and there was no draught on that side to lean it. Renard's hand closed on the hilt and his weight came off his heels and he did not draw, because drawing is a sound and the sound would wake her.

Then the pressure came.

It arrived in the sinuses first, where the heavy air already sat, and then it went down through the back of the throat and settled in the gut and turned. He knew what it was. Every man on the ridge learned what it was the first time a Tenrai officer walked a line of prisoners and let the leash off his Stage just to watch them sway. Two above him. He put the count at two because his mouth had gone wet and his stomach had lifted and the sweat had started along his spine in a room where breath hung in the air. One above and you only wanted to look at the man. Two and the body wanted to be sick and would not let you. Three and you were on your knees before you had decided anything. He was not on his knees. Two.

*Wet wood,* he thought, and did not know if he meant the man coming or himself. *One of us is going to waste a lot of heat.*

The man came through the arch walking. No hurry in it. He was not tall. Renard put him at a hand under his own shoulder and slight with it, built along the lines of somebody who had never carried a shield and had not needed to. Hair black and cropped to a finger's length, combed forward, the flat lightless black of lamp-soot. Face narrow, long from brow to chin, cheekbones set high and close under the eyes so the eyes sat in shadow from any light above them; a straight nose, thin lips that did not quite close over the teeth at rest. Skin the pale even brown of a man from somewhere the sun was a rumour. His clothing was the trouble. Undyed wool, Kharven cut, a deel belted at the waist with a plain hide strap. A courier's clothing. A courier's satchel, even, oiled leather, buckled twice. He had walked in through the gate wearing the kingdom.

His hands were bare. That was the other trouble. Nobody in Kharven-Seat went bare-handed after dark in the Thin Weeks unless they had a reason the cold was not going to argue with.

The frost on his lips was the thing Renard saw and filed and read wrong.

There was rime on the man's lower lip and on the fine hair of his knuckles, and the breath coming out of him made no plume at all, and Renard thought: the girl's cold, the thing that stayed in the hall, it has got into him on the walk across. He is half frozen already. Good.

He drew.

The blade came out slow enough not to ring and he stepped left off the door so that the door was behind his right shoulder, not behind his spine, and took Pflug, hilt at the hip, point at the man's throat, because a thrusting guard holds the centreline and the passage was narrow enough that the centreline was most of what there was.

"Greymane," the man said. His Concord was clean and unaccented and dead flat, and the voice was pitched not to carry. "Which one. The cousin."

Renard said nothing. The sword was already speaking.

"Lambert opened the west postern for me," the man said. "In case you were wondering how. I'm telling you because it won't help you."

It went into him past the skin before he felt it, a splinter. He did not believe it. He did not disbelieve it. He put it on the pile with the frost and the bare hands and kept the point where it was.

The man closed by a step and stopped at the edge of wide measure, just outside the reach of Renard's point with a full pass, and stood there with his weight even and his hands open at his sides, and Renard understood then that the man was not going to fence him. There was no guard, no Alber, no knife-fighter's hanging hand. The hands were the weapon and the man wanted them on him and the only question in the passage was whether Renard would let him have the measure or make him take it.

Renard let the point drop a hand's width. Invitation. Come in under it.

The man did not come. He smiled with the thin mouth and the frost on his lip cracked and he began to explain.

"Trial Flame," he said, and gave the words the weight a steward gives a vintage. "Exuroth. You won't know the House. Your girl has left this hall full of her winter, by the way. It sits in the walls. It makes me slow. It does not make me stop. Heat moves from where there is more to where there is less, and it does not ask permission, and every peasant with a hearth knows that much. What I do is refuse it permission. Halfway. I put a wall in the heat." He lifted the right hand and there was nothing to see. "Your blood wants to boil at a certain warmth. I hold it one hair under. It sits there. It cannot rise and it cannot fall. And everything you spend, every breath, every swing, every bit of that little Stage you're carrying, goes into the gap between not-boiling and boiling and disappears into the wall. You'll feel it as tiredness. Then you'll feel it as nothing." The hand went down. "It's a touch. I don't need to fence you. I need to touch you once."

Renard let that go into the pile as well, and this piece he believed, because the man's eyes had gone to Renard's forearm while he said it, the left one, the one nearest, and men look at what they mean to take.

He also filed the thing the man had not said, which was why his own breath made no cloud. A man burning that much heat should be steaming, a horse after a gallop. The man was rimed. The cold in the hall, Renard told himself again, the girl's cold. It was in the walls and it was in the intruder and it was slowing him. That was why the explanation. Men explain when they have time and the man was buying it.

Wrong on the cause. Right on the buying.

The man came in.

No wind-up, no shift of the shoulders, just the front foot landing inside the point and the body following it faster than the foot, and Renard was in Nach, late, answering. He did what Pflug is for. He thrust. The point went where the throat had been and the man's head was not there and the hands were coming up on the inside line and Renard did the second thing Pflug is for, which is Winden: hands up and across, the blade rolling in the air with the short edge leading, so that the man's right hand met the flat of the blade and the flat of the crossguard came round to break the wrist.

It did not break the wrist. The man's hand came off the steel fast, a hand off a stove, and the wrong hand, the left, went past the blade entirely and laid two fingers on the outside of Renard's left forearm through the sleeve of the gambeson.

Two fingers. A touch.

Renard broke measure. He did it the ugly way, the ridge way, a shove with the pommel into the man's sternum and a full pass backward, and the door was at his shoulder again and the man was three feet off and the two of them stood there breathing.

The man's breath made no cloud. Renard's did.

There was no pain in the arm. That was what he noticed first and it was what he noticed with the part of him that had been cut before. There should have been. There was a warmth in the meat of the forearm, the warmth of the first hour of a fever, and the skin under the quilting had begun, very slightly, to itch. He could grip. He tested it. The fingers closed and the grip was there and the strength behind the grip was not quite what it had been, the arm a day older than the rest of him.

The night-stone ticked.

He understood the shape of it then, if nothing of the law. He did not have to know why heat did what heat did. He had to know this man had put a wall in his arm and every swing was going to pour into the wall. The swing had to be the one that mattered and it had to be soon, and it had to land before the arm went to nothing, and none of that was different from any fight on any ridge in the world. You always had less than you needed. The sky does not ask whether you are ready.

The man came again.

This time Renard did not thrust. He let the man come to the edge of the point and then he stepped, not back, off the line, a full pass to his right so that the passage wall was at the man's left, and cut Zwerchhau. Horizontal, short edge, hilt high beside the head, the cut that takes whatever comes from the roof; and the man's hand was coming from the roof, reaching down for the neck above the collar, and the short edge met the back of it.

It bit. He felt it bite, the specific clean give of a blade going through skin and the tendons that lie corded over the back of a hand, and the man made no sound and Renard saw the extensor tendons part and the fingers of the right hand open on their own, all four of them, and stay open. The hand did not close again. Whatever the hand had been for, it was done.

And there was no blood.

The cut was to the bone across the back of the hand and it should have run, hands run, and the edges of the wound were pale, and then they were not pale, and from the seam of the cut came a thin line of steam.

The man looked at his own hand with something Renard would have called, on another face, interest.

"Ah," the man said.

Renard went in on the Ah.

He did not think about it. Indes is not thinking. The bind had happened at the hand and the hand was open and the man was looking at it, and the moment was there, and the body went into it before the mind arrived. He passed forward and took the blade halfway with his left hand, the wounded arm, the grip closing on steel with the strength it had, and drove the point half-sworded up under the man's left arm where the deel opened at the pit and there was only wool and skin.

The point went in at the armpit and up, four inches, five, into the soft crowded meat under the shoulder where the big vessel runs down out of the chest to feed the arm, and he felt the vessel go, a rope parting under a knife, that sudden lack of resistance, and the man's whole left side dropped an inch.

The man's mouth opened. His breath came out of it and made a cloud.

The first cloud. Renard saw it and did not know what it meant and knew that it meant something.

Then the heat came out of the man's hand.

It came out of the hand, the cut one, the one Renard had opened across the back, and none of it came out of the wound in the pit. It came all at once. The sound of fat dropped on a griddle, and the steam that had been a thread became a gout and the skin of the man's right hand blistered from the knuckles to the wrist in a single wave, the blisters rising and going white and splitting as they rose, and the man screamed then, the first sound he had made, a high thin sound that did not belong to the voice that had explained things.

Renard's own forearm went cold. Sharply, suddenly, ordinary cold. The itch stopped. He nearly dropped the sword from the shock of the arm coming back and did not.

He pulled the blade out of the armpit. It came with a wet sucking drag and the blood followed it, dark, a pulse of it and then a steady flood, running down the man's side and inside the deel and out at the hem to the flagstones, where it steamed on the frost-grit and then stopped steaming and lay there. A lot of it. A man has a few jugs in him and the vessel under the arm empties a jug fast. The man sat down against the passage wall, the legs going out from under him neatly, a spot chosen.

"Wren," the man said, looking up at Renard. "Wren, the door."

Renard stood over him with the point at his throat and said nothing.

"The postern," the man said, and then he frowned, a small precise frown, a man trying to remember which room he had left his hat in. His colour was going to the grey-brown of a wet stone. His good hand plucked at the buckle of the courier's satchel and could not work it. "Tell Wren."

Renard knew the look. He had seen it on the ridge on men who had bled that much: not fear, not pain, a fog. They stopped knowing who they were talking to. They repeated themselves. Brida called it the third bowl, the one where they stop asking for water because they have forgotten they were thirsty.

The man's head went back against the stone. The frost on his lip was gone. His breath clouded, and clouded, and the clouds came shorter, and then the last one hung in the passage and did not go anywhere.

The night-stone ticked.

The lamp in the hall stood upright again.

Renard did not move for the length of a long breath, and then he did the things. He wiped the blade on the dead man's deel, both sides, twice. He sheathed it. He went down on one knee, the left arm stiff and stupid and ordinary, and worked the satchel buckle with his right hand and opened it. Oiled cloth. A wax-sealed packet with no mark on the wax. Under it a brass token the size of a thumbnail, stamped on one face with a shape he did not know: three short strokes and a circle, the strokes leaning left. He turned it over. The other face was blank. He held it a moment and then closed his fist on it.

Behind him the altar-room door opened the width of a hand and Brida's face was in the gap, hair down, eyes going from him to the wall to the floor and back.

"She's asleep," Brida said. Very quiet. "Don't."

He nodded. He put the token inside his gambeson against the skin, where he would feel it, and got his good shoulder under the dead man's arm, and began to drag him down the passage away from the door, the heels leaving two lines through the frost-grit that the morning would have to explain.

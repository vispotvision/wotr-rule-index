# Rovhen — The Dispensary

*Aetherion Academy, east range. Evening, the fourth day of term.*

---

The old dispensary still smelled of what it had been. Forty years of tinctures had soaked into the shelving, camphor and clove oil and the bitter green of wormwood, and under all of it, from a desk drawer nobody had thought to empty, rose the sour ghost of a cheese rind gone hard as horn. Rain came down the one tall window in sheets and ticked in the downpipe outside. The hum ran lower here than in the Visitation wing. The east range sat over the deep conduit, and the draw came up through the flags as a slow pressure in the soles of the feet.

He had moved the benches in the first hour. They had stood in rows facing a master's desk on a dais, the way the Accord built every schoolroom it paid for. Now they made a horseshoe around the slate, open end to the door, the dais pushed back against the shelving where it could hold the lamps. A water bucket stood by the door with a ladle chained to its rim. A slate and a nub of chalk sat at every place, and a lamp at every second place, so that neighbours shared the light and had to lean in to do it. The roll was nailed to the doorframe at a child's eye height.

That was camp standard. The muster camps had laid their schoolrooms out that way for as long as there had been wars enough to fill them, and there had always been wars enough. Rovhen had been sorted into one at seven with a tin tag on a string around his neck. His father had gone into the ground in a war that had not troubled to get a name by the time the news reached the camp gate. After that there had been the bell, the bucket, the slate, and the roll called standing, and those four things were the first structure he had ever been handed. He had held on to them the way a drowning boy holds a spar.

Edmund had come to that camp on a case. A matron's ration ledger had gone missing along with forty sacks of meal, and the Board had sent a Lambert to find both. He left eleven days later with the ledger, the name of the quartermaster who had sold the meal, and a boy.

The boy had come from nothing, and Edmund never let him forget it, and never once used it against him.

The old man would have had words about the hour. Edmund had held that a man who did nothing but work made a poor witness to his own life, and once, at midnight, he had marched Rovhen out of a counting-house in Stannvaard to eat hot eels on the fish pier because, he said, the eels would not keep and the counting-house would. Rovhen had not eaten eels since. The stall was gone. Its owner had died the winter the pier flooded, and the new stall sold whelks.

He set the last pamphlet on the last bench. Fourteen of them, one for every name on the roll, each a single folded sheet in his own hand. Edmund's Sort, the five kinds and the three looks, copied out word for word from the brown ink inside the field book's front board.

He had spent the afternoon measuring the building. It was a habit older than his licence and it had outlived it. The dispensary had three ways out. The door opened onto the east corridor. The old dispensing hatch sat beside it, a shuttered counter where a hundred years of students had queued for powders, its bolt rusted but its hinges greased by someone recently. The third lay under the coconut matting at the back, a coal trap into the cellar, the iron ring worn bright on one side and dull on the other.

The people had taken longer than the doors.

Registrar Anselm Pike kept the roll in a room like a strongbox. He was a narrow man with a wide nib. His hair was the thin sandy colour of a dry paintbrush, combed flat across a skull that showed pink through it, and his face ran to a long chin and pale lashes that made his eyes look unfinished. He stooped at the shoulders from forty years of ledgers. His chest was hollow and his belly a small hard pot under a brown waistcoat buttoned wrong by one hole. Black sleeve-guards covered his forearms to the elbow. The second finger of his right hand carried a callus the size of a lentil where the pen sat. Every entry Rovhen had watched him make came out round, broad, and slightly blotted at the start of each stroke, the mark of a nib cut wide for speed.

The five Class S names on the roll had been written with a narrow pen.

Proctor Wenna Crale kept the evening hours. She was a head taller than Rovhen and built long, with a coal-heaver's shoulders and very little bosom under a dark serge jacket cut for a man. Her hair was black going iron at the temples, cropped close enough to show the shape of her skull. Her face was all bone, high cheeks and a jaw like the back of a spade, a mouth that stayed shut between sentences. Below narrow hips her legs ran long in sturdy grey skirts hemmed above the ankle for stairs, and the night keys hung off her belt on a steel ring as broad as a plate. An old white scar ran from her left eye into her hairline, neat as a surgeon's.

"Last bell's the last bell," she had told him in the lodge, without being asked. "After it, nothing opens. Not for faculty. Not for Visitation. Not for the Headmaster if he comes knocking in his nightshirt." She had looked at him the way a woman looks at a lock she has not yet tried. "Evening students keep evening hours for reasons, Master Talvasciel. I don't ask the reasons. You won't either."

A knock came at the open door.

The boy in the doorway was young, too young for the hour. The face took Rovhen by the throat before the rest of him had arrived. The set of the eyes under the brow, the way the mouth held itself closed as if it were keeping a figure in, the stillness of the head: Edmund's face, forty years younger, not yet worn into its creases.

The boy looked around the empty classroom, at the horseshoe of benches, the bucket, the slates.

"Professor Talvas."

Rovhen's hands kept moving. The pamphlet under them went square on the bench.

"My name is Dabney Lambert. I'll be attending your class. I am from Class X. I know I am relatively young, but I'd love to learn all that you can teach."

Lambert.

"If you wish to learn, then I can willingly teach. Hopefully you'll attend my lecture in an hour. However, it is a bit late for you to be out, especially someone so young."

Dabney nodded. "That is true, sir, but I have my reasons. I promised someone I would focus on getting strong, so I can't take shortcuts."

Rovhen listened to him. The rain filled the pause, and the hum under it.

"Hopefully you'll make your day classes, but I'll let you attend my class if it is what you wish. Just know I don't care if you're Class X or S. The level of class doesn't matter to me. I am willing to teach anyone, no matter social ranking. So please keep that in mind."

"Yes, sir. I will."

Then the doorway was empty, and his footsteps went off down the east corridor, quick and light on the flags, and turned at the far end, and were gone.

Rovhen stood a while in the empty room with his hand flat on the pamphlet.

The hour went by in lamp oil. He lit the lamps along the benches one at a time with a spill from the first, and trimmed each wick until the flame stood still and yellow. The rain slackened, came back, slackened again. Somewhere above him in the range a door opened and closed, and a pair of heels crossed a floor and stopped. The needle of the dispensary's own meter, by the door, stood where the Board had last set it and did not move at all.

The lamp nearest the back corner leaned.

It leaned the way a flame leans in a draught, toward the corner, and held there. There was no draught. The window was shut and the door stood open onto a still corridor. The corner itself had been dark since he lit the room, a wedge of shadow where the shelving met the wall and the dais cut off the lamplight, and he had marked it the way he marked every corner.

Then the corner held a girl, seated on the end of the back bench.

He had not heard the door. He had been facing it.

Her hair was white, arranged for evening, most of it gathered toward the back of her head in an intricate knot, with several long strands left loose to fall over her shoulders. Against the black of her dress they looked almost lit from inside. A silver mourning locket hung at her throat and took the lamplight. Her eyes were milky white. Her fingers went to the locket and adjusted it, and then lay still in her lap.

Rain beaded on the black shoulders of her dress, and her hem was dark with wet to the depth of two fingers.

The flags between the door and the back bench were dry. There were no tracks.

The lamp in the corner straightened.

The others came in on the bell. A clerk first, a woman well into her forties with a widow's black band sewn on her sleeve, square and heavy through the hips and chest, her brown hair scraped up under a cheap straw bonnet gone soft with rain. Ink had got into the cracks of her knuckles and would never come out now. She took the bench nearest the bucket and read the pamphlet before she sat, then read it again after, her lips moving over the part about the hand. Her name was on the roll from the first day, in the registrar's round hand: Joan Aldery.

Two dock porters came after her, brothers by the look of them, both of them bullnecked and ginger, their jackets steaming as the room warmed them. They sat together and shared one lamp and did not speak to anyone else.

The tall girl came last. She wore the same scraped-back hair that lifted her eyebrows and the same grey with the stitched S at the collar tab. She was long-boned and flat-chested, all wrists and elbows, and her shoes had been blacked that evening and splashed since. She had her slate. She took the bench on the open end of the horseshoe, opposite the white-haired girl, sat, set the slate on her knee, and began to write on it without looking down, her eyes on the back corner.

Across the quad the clock tower finished counting the hour.

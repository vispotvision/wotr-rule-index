# Rovhen — The Letter

*Aetherion Academy, the old dispensary. The last hour before last bell.*

---

The girl from the back bench had come forward without a sound on the flags, and she stopped a little way off from them, her notebook held against her chest.

Rovhen turned his head to her. Velthrae, the roll said. N. He had written the name down himself an hour ago under a line about a wet hem.

"Listening for the wrong language," he said. "The man who trained me spent thirty years trying to put it that plainly, Miss Velthrae, and never managed." His hand went to the inside of his coat, where the green-sealed envelope sat against the notebook, and rested there without drawing either out. "The north gate is chained because somebody with a seal decided it should be. That much everybody knows. The rest is rumour, and I don't teach rumour. I teach standing there."

Joan Aldery had turned at the girl's voice. She looked at her for a long moment, the way she had read the pamphlet, all the way through and then again.

"Stayed," she said. "Yes." Her thumb pressed the folded square in her hand flat. "The coroner's man looked at that stair for as long as it took him to fill in his form. That was the last time anyone looked at it but me." She turned back to Rovhen. "You said the stair first."

"I did." Rovhen pulled the nearest bench an inch out from its place in the horseshoe with his foot and did not sit on it. "Then tell me about the stair. Only what you saw. Nothing anyone told you."

She breathed in once, deep, the way a clerk does before reading a long column aloud.

"His name was Walter. Walter Aldery. He kept the books at Hobb and Mercer's, the draper's on Tallow Street, eleven years. There's a stair at the back goes down to the stockroom. Stone, fourteen steps, a turn at the ninth. He was found at the bottom on a Sunday morning by the boy who takes down the shutters." Her voice did not change on any of it. "The lamp was on the third step from the bottom. Standing up. The glass was whole. It had burned itself dry."

The rain had eased to a patter in the downpipe.

"The Deputy Coroner's clerk wrote him down as a fall in drink. A Mister Fenwick. He had a cold that morning and did not take his hat off." She paused. "Walter took the pledge at twenty-two. He had not touched a drop in the eleven years I was married to him. I told Mister Fenwick so. He wrote that down as well, under *remarks of the widow*."

"Anything missing?"

"His daybook." It came out quicker than the rest. "He carried a daybook everywhere, a little black one, marbled on the edges. It wasn't on him. It wasn't at his desk. Mister Hobb says he never saw it."

Rovhen let the room hold that for a moment.

A man falls down fourteen stone steps in the dark, and his lamp is found upright three steps from the foot of them with its glass whole. He had seen that once before, in a warehouse on the Stannvaard wharf, and it had not been a fall then either. Edmund had stood looking at that lamp for a full quarter of an hour without saying a word. Then he had said, *somebody set it down so they could use both hands*.

Rovhen did not say it. He crossed to the dais, took a sheet of the Academy's letter paper from the drawer that still smelled of old cheese, and wrote.

He wrote quickly, in the plain upright hand he kept for officials. *To the Clerk of Inquests, Deputy Coroner's office. The bearer, Mrs. J. Aldery, is to be shown the papers of the inquest held on her late husband Walter Aldery, together with the finding and the clerk's notes, and permitted to copy them. I will answer for the request.* He signed it with his full name and, under it, the words the Board had struck through eleven months ago: *inquiry agent, Lambert's.* Clerks in the Deputy Coroner's offices were not quick to read the Board's lists. They had been reading that second line on letters for twenty years.

The glyph went into the tail of the signature.

It was a small thing, [Ir], folded into the long downstroke of the final letter where the pen dragged a little before it lifted, and it took no more ink than a flourish would. What it took was a thread of him. He held the nib still at the end of the stroke and laid a sliver of his own Essence into the wet line at one fixed pitch, the way a tuner strikes a fork and holds it against the soundboard until the string beside it begins to answer. Two strings tuned alike, a room apart, will ring together. Edmund had shown him that on a cracked viol in a pawnshop. The ink would dry and keep the pitch. Whoever carried the paper would lay their own Essence on it every time they handled it, their taste in the ladle, and he would feel that answer across the city, faint and directional, a second string ringing somewhere to the north or the east of him.

It cost him before he had lifted the pen. A second pulse settled in behind his own, half a beat late, very faint, a hum in the roots of his back teeth. It would stay there as long as the paper did. It would thin with distance and thicken when she held it close. He could not set it down to sleep. If she burned the letter he would feel that too, and it would feel like a burn.

He folded the sheet in three, warmed the end of a stick of green wax over the nearest lamp chimney, and pressed the seal with his bare thumb. The Board had kept his seal when it kept his licence.

"Take this to the Deputy Coroner's office on a weekday morning," he said, and held it out to her across the bench. "Ask for the clerk of inquests. Not Fenwick. They keep the papers a year, and they will let you copy them. Bring me the copy. Don't go down the stair again on your own."

Joan took the letter. As her left hand came up to steady it, the cuff turned back a finger's width and the black thread showed, three short tight lines of it.

"Nail," she said, before he had looked at it for longer than a blink. She turned the cuff under again with two fingers. "At the counting-house door. They've been promising to see to it since the spring."

She put the letter into the pocket of her skirt beside the folded pamphlet and laid her flat hand over both.

Rovhen glanced past her, to the girl standing a few feet off with her notebook against her chest. The glyph was a tail on a signature, under wax, inside a fold. Seeing it would take a working agent at full stretch, and she was an evening student, and young. He doubted it very much.

Behind his own heartbeat, faint and half a beat late, the second one had begun, and it lay now in Joan Aldery's pocket, under her hand.

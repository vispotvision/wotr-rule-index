# Rovhen — The Sort

*Aetherion Academy. The fourth day of term. Noon.*

---

*From the field book of Edmund Lambert, inquiry agent. Inside the front board, in ink gone brown.*

> Every room you are paid to stand in holds one of five kinds of work. Sort it before you touch anything. The sort tells you who you are looking for, and whether there is anyone to find.
>
> **Worked.** Somebody chose it and did it. The hand is in the Residue the way a man is in his handwriting. Find the signature, then find who owns it.
>
> **Spilled.** Nobody chose it. A flare off someone frightened, grieving, or young. The signature is there and it is smeared, and the owner will be sorry when you find him. Arrest him last.
>
> **Standing.** The law was in the body before anything happened, so look at the teeth, the bone, the colour of the blood against a white bowl. A body is a Trait record and a corpse is a better one.
>
> **Running.** The world did it and would have done it with nobody watching. A Well, a rite, an oath come due. There is no one to hang. Write it down anyway, and write down who sent you, because they knew.
>
> **Stolen.** Something was taken that should have been asked for. You know it by the scar, which does not close, and by a room that feels owed. Do not stand in it longer than the work needs.
>
> A sixth, which is not a kind. Sometimes a hand lights a thing and the thing goes on without the hand. Find the hand. Leave the fire to the Guild. It answers to nobody.
>
> Three looks, every room, in this order. The room: what is in the air and on the walls, how much, how old. The law: what the physics did that it should not have. The hand, last. Every agent I have buried looked at the hand first.

---

The under-porter met him at the lodge with the keys already in her fist.

She was short and built heavy through the bosom and the waist, the grey Academy livery strained across her back where the seams had been let out, taken in, and let out again. Her hair was the red-brown of a wet fox, pinned in a knot that had given up on two of its pins. Her face was round and freckled to the ears. A gap where one front tooth had been put a whistle into her s's. Her forearms were thick from a lifetime of doors, and her hips rolled when she walked, the skirt swinging wide over a broad backside. The ring at her belt carried forty-odd keys, each tagged with a scrap of coloured wool. She had turned her collar to hide a darn, and she smelled of starch, onions, and the tallow she worked into stiff locks.

"Agnes Tull. Under-porter. You'll be the new inquiry," she said, and then, "Instructor, sorry. They didn't send a title down the wire, only the nine words, and one of them was your name and it was spelt wrong." She held up the slip between two fingers.

TALVASHEL FOURTH DAY NOON VISITATION ROOM NOT FACULTY HALL.

"Not faculty hall," she said. "Underlined, that, in the office copy. Somebody wanted it twice."

The lodge gave onto the long quad. Aetherion's lecture ranges stood on three sides of it in black dressed stone, four storeys each, with sealed conduit running along under the eaves in brass gone green at the joints. Standpipes stood capped at the corners of the grass, iron, waist-high, each with a Board seal hung off its cap on a wire. A bell somewhere counted off the Guild hour. From behind the east range came the slap of feet on a drill floor and a single voice calling time, and from an open window on the second storey a class was reciting something in unison and getting it wrong in the same place every time. Two first-years crossed the grass carrying a practice dummy between them by its arms, straw leaking from a split under its ribs, arguing about whose fault the split was.

Agnes walked fast for her legs. She talked faster than she walked, and she did not wait to be asked.

"You'll have heard about Monday. Whole grounds heard Monday. The masked lad off the islands, the one the intake board wrote down as having no pressure, no pressure at all, put an arrow clean through the sky-glass. And Master Kwon caught it." She made a fist at the air and opened it. "Out of the air. Bare-handed. That's what the kitchens are saying and the kitchens get it from the infirmary, so." She jingled the keys once. "Stannvaard's put the draw up another copper on the thousand, too. So that's the winter."

They passed under an arch where the stone had been worn into a shallow trough by a few centuries of the same shoes. Her voice dropped.

"And her. The Visitation. She's been down in that ring twice since Monday, before light, on her own. Night porter saw her lamp going round and round the floor." Agnes sucked air through the gap in her teeth. "Round and round. Doesn't tell a porter anything, a Visitation. Nobody tells a porter anything. So I find it out myself, mind, which is harder on everyone."

The infirmary sat at the end of the south range behind a low wall and a bed of rosemary gone woody. Three students sat on its steps in a row. All three wore the Academy's grey with a small stitched S on the collar tab. None of them was talking. The middle one, a tall girl with her hair scraped back hard enough to lift her eyebrows, had a slate on her knee and was writing on it without looking down, her eyes on the infirmary door.

"Class S," Agnes said. "Sat there since breakfast, the three of them, visiting, they told Matron." She sniffed. "Nobody in there's theirs."

The path to the Visitation wing ran past the arena's north gate. The gate stood open a hand's width on a chain, and two sweepers in canvas aprons leaned on their brooms beside it, waiting to be told they could start. The younger had a rag bound round his broom hand and kept working his thumb under it. Through the gap lay a strip of the ring floor. A trench ran across the sand from the near side toward the far, straight as a ploughman's first furrow, its walls sheared clean. Beyond it, off to the left, a black mouth opened in the floor the width of a cartwheel hub, its rim crusted with slag gone the colour of burnt sugar. The air through the gap smelled of scorched stone and wet ash where somebody had thrown buckets. Under both of those ran something sharper that he could only call hot brass. It was warmer at the gate than on the quad by the worth of a coat, and the fine hair along his forearms lifted under his sleeves and stayed lifted.

The older sweeper spat to the side, politely, away from them. "Lad's arrow did that, they say." He looked at the trench for a long while, the broom handle propped under his chin. "Didn't know arrows dug."

"Chain's hers," Agnes said, low. "Headmaster wanted it swept Monday night and she put the chain on. They had words in the lodge, the two of them, with the door open." She considered that. "Well. He had words."

At the door of the Visitation wing she stopped short, as if the threshold had a line painted on it that only porters could see, turned a brass key in a lock that did not need one, and pushed the door open with two fingers.

"I'm not to go in," she said. "Second on the left. Knock anyway."

---

The Visitation Room smelled of beeswax and cold tea. Someone had gone over the wainscot that morning with a rag still carrying linseed, and under the wax lay the older smell of the building, lamp soot worked into plaster across a century of winters. The chair they had given Rovhen was leather gone to cracks, each with a hard lip, and he could feel three of them through his trousers.

Under everything ran the hum. Aetherion sat on its own main, and the draw came up through the floorboards at a pitch just below hearing, a pressure in the back teeth. Children raised on a main slept badly off it. He had read that in a Board pamphlet at nineteen and found it true of himself at twenty-six, the first winter he worked a cut-off district, awake in a rented room above a cooper's yard, listening to nothing at all.

The meter hung by the door where the Board hung all meters, at a clerk's eye height: brass case, slate face, the Board's seal in green wax across the housing screw, the needle a hair left of the last scored mark.

While he watched, it crept another hair to the left.

Across the desk the Examiner finished her line, sanded the page, and tipped the sand back into its box without losing a grain. Examiner Harrowgate of the Grand Mage's Visitation had given her name at the door, title first, and had not offered her hand.

Her hair was the grey of a pewter plate left out in weather, dull and even, cut level with the hinge of her jaw so that it swung when she turned her head and hung dead still when she did not. Her face was long. The upper lip ran longer than it needed to, and the nose had been broken once and set by somebody careful, a white ridge across the bridge where the bone had knit. She was broad through the shoulders and broader through the hips, a body built by thirty years of chairs, the belly soft against a waistband some second tailor had let out. A buttoned waistcoat of Accord green, gone near black along the seams, held her chest flat and square. The frock coat over it had been good cloth once. Its cuffs carried the shine of desk edges now, and the right one was stiff with old ink. The rank token at her lapel was silver. On the back of her left hand, just below the knuckles, sat a small puckered burn in the exact oval of a seal die.

A cup of grounding tea stood at her elbow, three parts drunk. The brown rings inside the porcelain marked where the level had stood at intervals since breakfast.

She looked up. Her eyes were brown and clean, the whites clear.

"We are glad to have you here, Rovhen. We've heard of your work, and we are glad to have you teach and foster the next generation of investigators."

No title. She had none to give him. He had handed his licence back across a counter in Stannvaard eleven months ago, and the clerk had struck it through in front of him with one ruled line.

Rovhen's face did not move.

It had moved once. Edmund used to complain about it, standing in some widow's parlour with his hat on his knee: a boy who showed the witness the answer before the witness gave it. So Rovhen had trained it still, through the Lambert years, behind his master in cellars and counting-houses and one flooded sluice-keeper's cottage with his hands folded where the family could see them. After the funeral it stayed still without being asked. It stayed still in taverns. It stayed still on the river stair at Holme when a drowned girl's mother held his wrist in both of hers and thanked him for finding her.

He had stopped taking commissions from people who could pay. He had started taking them from people who could not, and the ones who could not kept coming, and somewhere in that drift the work became the only hour of the day that did not feel like standing in a room Edmund had just left.

The report on Edmund Lambert's death carried a name in the column headed *agent in attendance*. The name was his, entered in ink.

Edmund's lantern went into the ground with him. Brass, one pane starred from a fall down a cellar stair in Stannvaard, the shutter hinge wired shut with a hairpin because he would not pay a tinsmith for a thing he could mend himself. Nobody makes that pattern now; the Guild lamps replaced it.

"The Grand Mage's office asked me to sit in on your appointment." Harrowgate squared the page against the desk edge. "Asked. The Visitation has no say in whom the Academy hires, before witnesses, I want that plain. We keep an eye. It's all we're for now, keeping an eye." She slid a folded sheet across the leather. "Your roll."

The head of the sheet carried the course as the register had entered it, Latin above and plain below: *Inquisitio Phaenomenorum Operatorum. The Inquiry into Worked Phenomena.* Three sittings a turn. The old dispensary, east range. Scholars of any class.

"Twelve lamps and the dispensary," Harrowgate said. "The Academy pays on the Guild hour and bills you for your own draw. Everyone thinks that's a joke the first month."

Fourteen names. Nine sat in the registrar's round hand, dated the first day of term. Five sat below them in fresh black ink from a narrower pen, dated the fourth day, each with a small S in the class column.

Word went round. It always did. A Lambert-trained agent on a teaching board would draw the ambitious ones the way a lit window draws the cold.

Through the window behind her the arena dome sat on its drum of grey stone, and a third of it was wrong. A seam ran across the ward-glass from the lip almost to the crown, and along the seam the glass had gone the flat colour of a fish's eye. Scaffold climbed the stone below it. On the top board a small man knelt with a hammer the length of a spoon.

Tap. A pause. Tap.

He was bald as a hen's egg except for a fringe at the back the colour of unwashed fleece, and his face was all creases, folded in on itself around a mouth with most of its teeth. His ears stood out. His shoulders were narrow and roped, his forearms scorched hairless and shiny to the elbow, and his belly made a small tight drum above a tool belt heavy enough to pull his trousers low on the hip. Both knees were patched. The apron was scorched in a fan along its hem, and the top joint of his left little finger was gone, the stump polished smooth by years of work.

He struck a ward plate at the seam's edge and the note came back short and dead.

"Another one cased, Examiner," he called through the open casement. His voice was all gravel. "Six on this row, sound as a bell to look at. Ring like a wet boot."

"Thank you, Master Sallow."

"Rings when struck, a solid one. That's all I'm saying."

"Thank you, Master Sallow." She reached back without looking and drew the casement to. The latch caught. His tapping went on, fainter, under the hum.

"The ward was proofed at the quarterly," she said. "I have the inspector's book." She laid her palm on a ledger at her left hand and did not open it.

Then she drew a second sheet from the tray. Printed, not written: the Accord's form, the heading in both Latin and plain, **Finding of Cause**. Every field had been completed in her hand except one. Beside *Party answerable* the line was bare.

"Any damage to a chartered house above a certain sum, the Visitation files a finding. That." She tipped her head a quarter inch toward the window. "Is above it. Two Class X students fought in that ring on Monday and every soul on these grounds heard the end of it." Her thumb smoothed the form flat, though it was already flat. "A finding drawn by the Academy's own inquiry instructor. As his first demonstration, say, with his class there to watch how it's done. It would settle it. It would settle it in a morning, and the children see a proper hand at work, and so does the charter." She uncapped her pen. "What has been said stands said, Rovhen. So it matters a great deal who gets to say it first, and in whose hand."

She laid the pen across the empty line, nib toward him.

"Master Sallow has the scaffold down by the second bell. After that the ring is swept."

She turned the form around on the leather so that the blank faced him, and folded her hands on the desk beside it, the burned one uppermost.

The pen lay where she had put it. At the split of the nib the ink gathered into a bead, black and domed, and hung there.

Outside, the tapping stopped.

The hum filled the space it left. Somewhere below them a door banged, and then boots came up the stair, uneven, a man favouring one knee on the way up who would favour the other on the way down. Harrowgate heard it too. Her hands stayed folded on the leather, but the burned one closed over the other by the width of a knuckle.

The knock came and the door opened on the back of it.

Sallow filled less of the doorway than he had filled the window. Close to, he smelled of hot iron and lamp oil and the sour wool of a shirt sweated through and let dry on him twice. Grit sat in every crease of his face. He had something under his arm wrapped in sacking, and he carried it the way a man carries a thing he means to be paid for.

"Beg pardon, Examiner." He was not sorry. He crossed to the desk and set the bundle down on the corner of it, squarely on top of the Finding, and let the sacking fall open.

It was a ward plate. A curved tile of worked glass a hand and a half across, seated in a frame of brass, its face gone to the same flat fish-eye cloud as the seam on the dome. He tipped it over with two fingers so that the brass back lay uppermost. Struck into the brass sat the proof-mark, PROB EST, and beside it a quarter punch, two small marks, the second struck twice and a hair off true.

"Number six," Sallow said. "Rest of the row's the same, give or take a plate. Thought you'd want it for your book." He wiped his palms down his apron one at a time. "And I'll want paying for the row. Not the crack. The row's the job. The crack's only where it showed."

"The Academy's bursar pays wardwork, Master Sallow."

"Bursar says the Visitation's got the ring, so the Visitation's got the bill." He sniffed. "Can't have the chain and not the bill. That's all I'm saying."

"You say that a great deal."

"Say it till it's paid."

He turned his head then, and looked at Rovhen properly for the first time, a long look from under a brow with no hair left on it. His mouth worked over the gaps in his teeth.

"You're Lambert's lad." It was not a question. "Stannvaard. The cellar on Cooper's Row, with the drowned vats. You held the lamp." He nodded at nothing, at the memory. "I did his lantern once. Hinge went. Wouldn't pay for a new one, the old skinflint, so I showed him the trick with the hairpin, and he stood there in my shop and did it himself so he'd never have to pay me for it again." Something shifted in the creases around his eyes. "Heard he went down. Sorry for it."

The hum ran on under the floor.

"He paid, Lambert," Sallow said, and he said it to Harrowgate. "Always paid. Every job."

"Thank you, Master Sallow. That will be all."

"Second bell." He was already at the door. "Scaffold comes down, then they sweep, and then there's nothing left to look at in that ring but my bill." His boots went down the stair, the other knee now, and a door banged below.

Harrowgate did not look at the plate. She took the Finding by one corner and drew it out from under the brass, slowly, and the frame's edge scraped a pale line across the leather as the paper came free. She set the form square in front of Rovhen again. The pen rolled a quarter turn and stopped against the plate.

"Master Sallow has opinions about everything he's paid for," she said, "and most of what he isn't." She reached for the grounding tea, found the cup nearly empty, and drank what there was anyway. The porcelain clicked on the saucer. "He was on the circuit list at Stannvaard, did you know. Twenty years. He was struck off it for talking."

Across the quad, faint through the closed casement, the first bell began to count.

The bead of ink let go of the nib and dropped onto the blank line beside *Party answerable*, a black dot no bigger than a lentil, and spread.

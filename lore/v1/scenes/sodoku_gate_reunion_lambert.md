# The Gate Opens

*Kharven-Seat, the north gate. Night, the eleventh year.*

---

Wet ash came first, and none of it belonged to any fire currently burning. Eleven winters of thaw had worked the burn down through the mortar joints until the wall itself breathed it out whenever the wind leaned against the stone, and the wind was leaning now, steady off the plateau, carrying that old scorched-mineral smell mixed with pine resin and horse and the sour tallow reek of the gate lamps. Under his boots the gravel was the wrong gravel. His father's engineers had laid crushed granite here because granite drains and does not hold ice, and somebody in the intervening decade had patched the approach with river stone, and river stone rounds under weight and rolls. Sodoku felt it shift beneath his heel and catalogued it before he had decided to, the same way he catalogued the two soldiers still standing too straight at the postern and the fresh mortar in the third course of the wall and the fact that the gate lamps were burning tallow where they had once burned oil.

Tallow burned dirty, stank, and cost a tenth what lamp oil cost. He read the treasury off it and did not enjoy the figure.

Behind him the column had gone quiet in the specific manner of men who have stopped being an expedition and started being an arrival. Chain settled. A horse blew out. Somewhere back near the sledges, Dunstan was conducting a loud negotiation with nobody about whether the cast iron ladle counted as personal property or crown property, crown ground being what they were currently standing on, and Nella was telling him it counted as an assault charge, and Soren, who had spent nine days on a mast-pole and had opinions about ground, said that the ground here smelled like a hearth somebody had died in front of. Nobody argued with him. The wind kept working.

Emira's grip had not loosened.

Iron, that grip. Nine years had done what nine years do to a body that trains, and the hand closed on his wrist above the gauntlet cuff was not a child's hand anymore, and the pulse of vitality coming off her palm was thick enough that the cold in his own arm gave ground half an inch before it remembered itself.

"Of course you can come," Sodoku said.

He let the words sit where they landed. He had a habit of leaving room after a sentence, and men who did not know him filled the room with their own noise and told him things he had not asked for, and men who did know him waited. He put his other hand over hers, gauntlet across knuckles, and the plate was colder than the night and she did not flinch from it.

"You were coming. I saw the pack you built in Stormfold. Nobody packs bone-setting splints for a march they are not taking."

The wind pushed a fistful of dry snow across the gravel and it hissed against the stone.

"And do not offer it to me as payment," he said. "I did not keep a ledger on you. If I had kept one you would be reading it now, and you would find your side of it empty, and mine full, and I do not intend to explain the arithmetic to you standing in a wind like this." He released her hand. "When I break something, you will set it. That is not a debt. That is a physician standing where the physician stands."

Whatever went across her face after that, he could not read it. He had never been able to read it. He had given up some years ago on pretending it was a skill available to him.

Yoko's ears came around before the sound reached him.

She was three paces off his right shoulder, hood down despite the cold, and her nose lifted a fraction and held. Her tail had quit moving partway through its arc. That told him more than any report she could have filed.

"Someone is coming through the yard," she said. "Running. Small. And there is cold on her." A pause of exactly one breath. "The cold is hers. She is making it."

*Kokan.* Yoko's ears folded flat and came back up. *That is his father's signature under a child's output, and nobody in this column is ready for what happens in the next thirty seconds, least of all the man wearing the crown he has not put on yet.*

The bar came out of its brackets.

Sound carried it: a groan of dry ironwood in dry iron, the timber shifting its whole weight, then the double crack of the brackets releasing. The gate leaves were the originals. Sodoku knew them by the sound before he knew them by sight, because he had been eight years old the winter they were hung and he had stood in the snow watching four men fight a beam that was heavier than the four of them combined, and his father had watched too, and had said nothing at all for an hour, which was how Muken Moto praised anything.

The leaves came in toward the yard.

The two door-knights swung wide and stepped back and dropped, both of them, both knees, and it was not ceremony because ceremony has timing and this had none. They went down the way men go down when the floor tilts. Behind them the yard opened up: bare packed earth, a broken fountain crusted with ice, the old parade ground where the Banner Houses had once stood their contingents in six blocks of colour and where the frost was now unmarked except by cart ruts and one narrow track of small footprints running dead at him.

And at the end of the track, three paces inside the threshold, a girl stopped.

She was eleven and small for it. Wiry through the shoulders, all wrist and collarbone, built for the kind of growth that has not yet been fed enough to happen. Her hair was pale to the point of colourlessness, the white of birch shaved back to wet wood, and she had braided it herself and badly, the plait fraying at the crown where a maid would have caught it. Mail sized for a grown man had been cut down and re-riveted to fit her, the new rivet-work bright against old rings, and the hem still hung to mid-thigh where it should have hung at hip. Over it a wool overdress the colour of dirty slate. A fur collar worn bald in exactly one patch, on the left, where her fingers went when she needed somewhere to put them. Chilblain scars across the knuckles of both hands, old and healed and shining. A nick through the outer third of one eyebrow, white, a training scar, a blade that had been allowed to travel.

And the jaw.

Sodoku's chest did something that he had no instruction for. The jaw was Muken's, and the resemblance ran past family likeness into duplication: the same architecture, the same square set at the hinge, the hard little shelf under the ear that his father had carried into every negotiation he had ever won, transposed onto a face too narrow to carry it yet, on a child standing in a doorway in a dead capital with her fists closed.

Her eyes were grey. In the left one, a ring-line surfaced through the iris, thin and pale and turning, and she did not know it was showing.

Frost went out from her feet across the packed earth in a slow ragged bloom and reached the threshold stone and climbed it.

"Father," Hild said.

The word came out wrong. Too small, cracked down the middle, eleven years of preparation collapsing into two syllables that had clearly been rehearsed and had clearly not survived contact.

Then she ran.

*I said I would stand and I would ask him where he was and I would not cry and I would make him answer me standing up, and I am going to ask him tomorrow, I am going to ask him tomorrow, I am going to ask him tomorrow.*

She hit him at the sternum.

Arctic Lion took it, because Arctic Lion took everything, the Glacium plate transmitting the impact around the ribcage and out into the harness and away from him, and the practical result was that Sodoku felt eleven years arrive as a soft thud and a pressure and nothing else, and the injustice of that was so complete that his hands did not know what to do and went on their own. One at the back of her skull. One flat between her shoulder blades.

She was crying before her arms closed, and doing it ugly, the kind that comes up out of the diaphragm in whole sheets and does not care who is standing in the yard, and her face was buried in a breastplate that could stop a Titan's claw and her fists had a grip on the fur of his cloak collar that was going to leave marks in the pelt.

The cold coming off her ran up his gorget and found the exposed skin under his jaw and did nothing at all to him. Winter recognized winter. He stood in the middle of his daughter's involuntary Kokan output the way a man stands in his own house.

He did not say anything. There was nothing available. Every sentence that presented itself was either a lie or a wound, and he had spent eleven years learning the difference between a silence that abandons someone and a silence that holds still while they finish, so he held still, and let her finish, and set his chin down on the crown of her badly braided head, and stared over it at the ruin of the yard where his father had taught him to hold a sword.

Emira's boots came up on the gravel behind him and halted at a distance he had not needed to ask for.

The two door-knights were still down. So was one of the gate soldiers he had told to stand up ten minutes ago, who had apparently reconsidered.

And then a man came through the gate behind her, unhurried, at the pace of someone who has already calculated that running would not change the outcome and would cost him dignity he might need later.

He was tall and had been taller, narrow through the frame, stooped a few degrees at the neck from decades spent over documents in poor light. Fifty-three, and looking every hour of it, and wearing all of it in the two deep vertical lines that bracketed his mouth. Iron-grey hair receded to a hard widow's peak and cropped close enough to show the shape of the skull. Heavy lids, so that his eyes always seemed to be arriving at a conclusion. A charcoal wool coat, good cut, eleven years old and brushed to within an inch of its remaining life, with a fur collar and cuffs of wolf gone thin at the fold. A steward's chain with two links replaced in a cheaper metal that had not been polished to match, and had been left unmatched on purpose. Big hands for the frame. A burn scar riding the back of the left one, glossy and pink, an old scald from something that had gone into a fire and been taken back out again.

His right thumb was moving against the pad of his index finger. Counting.

Sodoku knew him before his brain produced the name. A younger version of that face at twenty-six with the widow's peak already starting, holding the far end of a survey line in ground so frozen the stakes had to be driven with a maul, in a wind off the plateau that had killed a mule that morning. His father walking the line. His father stopping at the third stake and saying *this one is wrong* and the young cadet saying *it is wrong by a foot and a half, Lord, and I put it there because the bedrock shelves and your wall will sit crooked in nine years if I put it where you want it,* and Muken looking at him for a long moment and then walking on and never touching the third stake again.

Edward Lambert.

One of the six who had driven stakes into the ground that the wall behind him was still standing on.

Lambert stopped at a correct distance, took the whole yard in with one pass of those heavy-lidded eyes, and bowed shallow, the bow of a man who has read a room and priced it exactly.

"Lord Commander," he said, and the address went past Sodoku's shoulder to the man standing there, and he held it a beat longer than he needed to.

Then he turned.

"Your highness." His voice had gravel in it and no warmth performed and no coldness performed either, every word set down in its exact position like coin counted onto a table. "Eleven years. It is good to see the two of you whole."

*Whole is the wrong word and I used it deliberately. He came back with a Titan, a Dawi smith, a Yukari warder and a wife, and he came back without the thing he went out with, and I will find out what that was before the week is out because the ledger does not balance until I do.*

Sodoku's daughter had not let go of his collar. He did not attempt to move her.

"Lambert." His voice came out lower than he intended, roughened by the thing sitting in his throat. He waited a moment and put it back where it belonged. "The third stake."

Something happened in Lambert's face that did not reach the level of expression.

"It shelved at four feet, your highness. Your father's wall is still square." The thumb stopped moving against the index finger. "Your father was wrong once and let me keep the proof of it standing in the ground for the rest of his life. I have thought about that decision more often than I have thought about most of my own."

The wind came across the parade ground and drove snow through the open gate in a long low sheet that broke around their legs.

"She has asked for you once," Lambert said. "In eleven years. Once. I keep an accurate count of everything in this house and that number is one, and I want you to hear it from me tonight rather than from someone who will tell it to you as a kindness." He let it stand. "She asked tonight."

Sodoku felt his daughter's fingers tighten in the fur.

Back at the gravel line, an old man came forward one pace and held there.

Heisuke had not spoken since Stormfold. Sixty-one years old, built out of the same short heavy timber the frontier built everything out of, with the sun-cured brown skin of a man who had spent forty years outdoors above the treeline and a face that had settled into an expression of patient refusal sometime around the assassination and had not moved since. His hand had come partway to his chest. It stopped at the sternum and stayed.

Lambert saw him. The heavy lids came up a fraction.

"Heisuke," he said.

*He is still alive. Of the men who drove those stakes there were six, and I had accounted five, and I have been carrying that count as complete for four years, and it was wrong, and I have never in my life been so pleased to be wrong about a number.*

Heisuke said nothing. He lowered his hand, and the set of his mouth did not soften, and he did not step back either.

Yoko came up beside Sodoku and did not touch him, because touching him in front of an audience was a thing she had decided years ago that she would not do.

"The output is climbing," she said, low, precise, offering exactly what was accurate and nothing more. "She has no control of it and no awareness of it. Whatever she is holding down, it is coming out through the temperature." Her nose tipped once toward the frost blooming steadily outward across the yard. "Four paces and growing. If she does not stop crying soon, the fountain will crack."

"Let it crack."

Sodoku got the fingers of his right hand under the gauntlet cuff, worked the strap with his thumb, caught the lip in his teeth and pulled the whole thing off. It hit the gravel with a flat dead sound. His bare palm was scarred across the heel and cold as the wall and he laid it against the back of his daughter's head, over the ruined braid, and the frost that came off her ran up his fingers and did not hurt.

He closed his hand.

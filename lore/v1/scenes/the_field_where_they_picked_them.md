# The Field Where They Picked Them

*The seventh hour of the second day. The flat ground east of the ridge.*
*POV: Ruvaen Aloreth.*

---

## I. WHAT IT SOUNDED LIKE BY THEN

Sound had stopped being information about eleven hours ago.

That is the thing nobody tells you and every man who has been in one knows. For the first hour you can hear where the line is. For the second you can hear where it has gone. After that there is simply a volume, flat across the whole of the ground, made of forty thousand men making individual noises that have stopped being separable, and the only thing it tells you is that it has not ended yet.

Under it, the other layer, which is the one that gets into the teeth. Iron on iron is a sound. Iron on a man is not, and forty thousand of the second thing at once produces something in the chest rather than in the ear, a low irregular thudding that arrives through the ground and the horse and the saddle and does not stop for eleven hours.

Then the smell. Opened men, and opened horses, which is worse and which nobody warns you about, and the particular ammoniac stink of a field where forty thousand people have been terrified in the same square mile for two days.

Sight was last and sight was almost nothing. He had a hundred and forty yards of visibility in any direction and had had since the middle of the first afternoon, because eleven hours of boots on dry ground produces a haze that does not lift, and above that the smoke, and above that a sky he had not looked at since the morning.

*I do not know if we are winning.*

*I have not known since the tenth hour of the first day and I have four hundred yards of read and I have spent almost all of it and the last honest thing I knew was that the Vessel Coast levies were still on the right and had not moved.*

*Nineteen thousand. Hers is more and I do not know how much more and I have stopped wanting the number.*

He got the helm off.

The air on his face was cold and stank and was the best thing that had happened to him in two days.

---

## II. THE HORSE

Pegasus had stopped.

Not refused. Stopped, four-square, head down, which is what a horse does when it has finished and is too well made to say so.

Ruvaen got down.

The animal had come through the whole of the first day and most of the second and had done everything asked and more than was reasonable, and there was a cut along the near shoulder about fourteen inches long that had stopped bleeding hours ago in the wrong way, and a pike had gone into the off hind above the hock and out again, and the whole of the white was not white any more anywhere.

It was shaking. Not from cold.

*She bred you.*

*She bred the line and she bred you for me and she was dead before you were backed and I have ridden you into two days of this and you have not once put a foot wrong.*

He put both hands flat on the neck under the mane, where the animal was warm, which was the only place on it that still felt like a horse.

"Rest," he said. "I will come back."

*That is a lie and he cannot understand it and I said it anyway, which is the whole of what a man is at the end of two days.*

He kissed the horse between the eyes, above the plate of the chamfron where somebody had painted the Sunroot mark eleven years ago and where it had almost entirely worn off.

Then he did the thing he had told himself all morning he would not do.

---

## III. WHAT IT COST

He cannot heal. That needs saying plainly because every ballad about this day is going to say otherwise.

There is no Wellspring in him that closes a wound and there never has been, and the only instrument he has ever owned takes readings and does not give anything back, and a man who has spent a hundred years learning to listen has not thereby learned to speak.

What he can do is the one thing the instrument does in reverse, and it is a poor thing, and it is what he had.

He closed everything. Every yard of range, the whole of the field, the read that had been running continuously since the first horn, all of it drawn down to nothing and compressed and then put into a body that was not his own, the way you put a coat on somebody.

It is not healing. It does not close anything. What it does is hold a failing architecture at the shape it currently has for a period, so that a thing which is about to stop does not stop yet, and the period is measured in hours and the hours are bought at a rate he had never had to work out before because he had never done it for anything but a man.

The shaking eased. The head came up about four inches.

That was all of it and it was enough.

*Yards.*

*I do not know how many and I will not know until I open again and that is the part I cannot afford, because I am going to open in about nine minutes and find out what I have left with a woman standing in front of me.*

*Four hundred was the number. It has not been four hundred since I did that.*

*I have spent the margin. The whole plan is one opening in one exchange read at the finest resolution I own and I have just given some unknown portion of it to a horse.*

He put the helm back on, because there were still men between him and where he was going.

He did not look at the animal again. He had looked at enough things for the last time in two days and had a limit.

---

## IV. THE WALK

It took him about a quarter of an hour and he does not remember most of it.

There were men.

Some were hers. Some were his. After the first two he could not tell at a glance any more, because two days does that to a surcoat, so he stopped looking and read instead, which is cheaper when you are this far gone.

Three came at him in the haze near the broken cart line.

He took the first across the thigh and the second through the gorget gap on the left. He did not touch the third. The third stopped four feet away and stood there with his spear up and did nothing at all, and Ruvaen went past him.

*He was nineteen. He had the Karreth mark on the shoulder and he was nineteen and he could not have been on that field for more than a day.*

*I do not know what he saw. I have not looked at myself since yesterday morning.*

A Vessel Coast man was sitting against a wheel with both hands in his own middle holding it together, and said something as Ruvaen passed, and it was not a request and it was not a curse, it was a question about whether the tide had turned, because a man from the coast will ask about the tide with his hands full of himself.

Ruvaen told him yes.

*I do not know. I have not known for a day and a half. And he is going to die in about eleven minutes believing a thing I made up, and I would do it again, and I would do it for every man on this field if I had the hours.*

---

## V. THE FLAT GROUND

Then the ground went open and the noise dropped off behind him the way it does when you come out of a fold, and he was standing on about four hundred yards of flat.

Nobody had fought on it.

That is the part he has never been able to explain to anybody afterward. Two days, forty thousand men, and this one stretch of ground had not been touched, and the grass on it was standing, and the whole of it was covered in the small white ones.

*Sallow-eye.*

*They come up in the third week after the thaw on any flat ground east of the ridge and they are gone by the fifth and they are worth nothing and nobody plants them and nobody picks them except children.*

*She picked them. Every spring until she was about eleven and then one spring she did not, and I noticed, and I did not ask, because I was nine and I thought it meant she had stopped liking them.*

*She had not stopped liking them. She had started being Empress-presumptive and somebody had told her what a woman of standing does not do in a field.*

He stood in it for a moment with two days on him.

*She would have hated every part of today. She argued four times at the yard that an animal in a war cannot be an enemy and lost four times. She would have hated the enclosures argument, and she would have hated the man who won it, and she would have hated me on this ground with this on my hands.*

*And she would have picked the flowers. On the way. In the middle of it. That is exactly what she would have done and everybody would have called it madness and she would have been the only person on the field who was right.*

---

## VI. THE WOMAN AT THE OTHER END

Ilthára Korvaeth was standing at the far side of it.

Alone. No Guard, which meant either that they were dead or that she had sent them away, and he did not know which and could not tell from where he was standing.

Three-quarter black lacquer, the whole of it gone red-brown down the front and dried in the folds, and the plates at the forearm scored in about nine places. No helm. White hair flat to her skull with sweat and something else. Fifty-one and looking every year of it and not spending a copper on appearing otherwise, the way she never has.

And the empty place on the left breast, four inches across, going right through the blood on it, because blood dries on plate and does not dry in an engraving, and there was no engraving.

He put his read on her.

*Nothing.*

Not the flat nothing of a man with nothing left. Not the hole Aeldros had gone to at the end. A clean, sealed, textureless absence about the size and shape of a woman, standing in the middle of a field, while every other object on that ground gave him what it had.

*She is suppressing.*

*At the end of two days, having fought both of them, on the ground, with no helm and no Guard and nothing left in any of us, she is holding it closed as tight as I have ever felt anything held, and she is not spending effort on it.*

*It is not a technique. It is the resting state.*

*Thirty years of a woman making absolutely certain that nobody on this continent could take a reading of her standing, because the one thing a yard can do to you is measure you and write the number down, and she decided at seventeen that nobody would ever get the number.*

*So I get nothing. I will get nothing at four hundred yards or at four feet, and I will get nothing when she moves, and the whole of my plan was an opening read at the finest resolution I own.*

*She has been building the counter to me since before I was a soldier and she did not know I existed when she started.*

---

## VII. WHAT HE SAID

He came out into the middle of the flowers and stopped at about twenty feet, which is further than a man stops when he means it and closer than a man stops when he does not.

"So this is what it has come to."

He got the words out and heard how tired they sounded.

"I am going to say the thing and I am going to say it once and I am not going to dress it up, because we are both past that and there is nobody here to perform for.

"Your madness has no bottom in it. I have watched it for eleven years and I have never once been able to find where it stops.

"And I do not want to kill you.

"Call it off. Pull the claim. Stand your houses down and take your Guard and go back under stone and I will hold every man on this field off you personally for as long as it takes you to get there, and I will do it in front of witnesses, and I will enter it in form so that no Court of mine can come after you later and say it was never said."

*And I have not thought this through and I am saying it anyway and Corvienne is going to have to write it down.*

*And I mean it.*

---

## VIII. WHAT SHE SAID

She did not move.

"Do you really think this ends in peace."

Her voice went the whole twenty feet without any effort behind it, which was the first thing about her all day that told him anything.

"After I killed your sister."

He did not answer that.

"And the rest of it. Do you think they would ever have it. An unnamed. Living and breathing beside them, in a hall, at a table, with a chair under her and a cup in front of her and every person in that room knowing that the yard looked at her three times and said no.

"I was outside it from the first day. Never respected. Never loved.

"And you are standing in a field offering me safe conduct back under stone as though the stone is where I came from and not where they put me."

*That is not true.*

*One person loved her and she knows it and she has known it for sixty-one years and she has spent every one of them building a continent on top of the sentence she just said, because the sentence only works if the one is not counted.*

*They were girls together. She asked my sister to come and hold her hand in a gallery at Rovann and my sister went and stood there and clapped for a name they gave to the wrong sibling, and she was there because Ilthára asked her, and nobody asks a person to come and hold her hand who is not loved.*

*She picked these flowers too.*

*That is the thing I have only just understood and it has taken me until this exact minute on this exact ground. Onawa picked sallow-eye in a field east of the ridge every spring until she was eleven, and she did not pick them alone, because a child does not pick flowers alone for six years running.*

*I am standing in the field where the two of them played and one of them is dead and the other one is twenty feet away with my men on her hands.*

*And if I say that out loud, one of two things happens, and I do not know which, and I have one guess and I get to make it now.*

Ruvaen Aloreth put his hand on the grip over his shoulder and did not draw it.

"Ilthára," he said. "Look down."

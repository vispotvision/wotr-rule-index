# The Fourth Letter

The fourth letter will not fit beneath the stone.

Tabitha draws the others out, knocks their edges square against the table and tries again. The night-stone is still warm from the hearth. A greasy print remains where her thumb has touched the paper; she turns that sheet inward, puts a clean one over it and settles the stone on the corner. There. Four letters, addressed to four factors in Nalūn, each asking the same thing.

Outside, a man is splitting wood. She has been listening to him since the light went from the window. He strikes twice to open a round, then drops the halves, then takes longer than he ought to fetch another. The woodpile stands too far from his block. Tomorrow she will tell somebody to move it.

The dispatch room has a hearth at one end and the door at the other, with a long table between them. Saddlebags hang from wooden pegs above a bench. Meltwater falls from their lower corners into a shallow tray, slowly enough that each drop sounds through the scrape of her pen. Beside the hearth, a pot of broth has boiled down to a dark ring. Nobody has remembered it.

Tabitha has kept her travelling coat on. Heavy brown wool bunches beneath her arms when she bends, and its road-stained hem brushes her calves through her skirt. She has broad shoulders, a thick waist, and hands that make the pen look borrowed. One wooden pin hangs loose. She pushes it back into her grey-streaked brown hair with the heel of her hand.

Her own letter lies beneath the four.

She has written: “Why was the grain bought in the first week?”

Below that: “The advance came in the fourth.”

She knows those lines without lifting the stone. Beneath them she has written the date on which she asked in council, then the dates of the two private refusals. The last entry is cramped against the fold. She should have allowed more room.

Someone tries the door.

“Come.”

Edward Lambert enters carrying a tied packet against his ribs. He shuts the door carefully against the draught, keeping the latch lifted until the wood meets the frame. Tabitha notices the packet first. Then the dark wool above it, smooth at the shoulder and polished where his wrist crosses his chest. His coat hangs loosely on a lean body that once filled it better. Close-cropped grey-brown hair leaves his crown bare beneath the lamplight; below it, his long, clean-shaven face has the dry creases of a man who spends the hours before dawn reading. His eyes settle on her letters.

She puts down the pen.

“The southern bag?” he asks.

“Leaves when the harness is dry.”

“It is dry.”

“Then when I have finished.”

He lays his packet at the far end of the table. The cord rasps. She can smell cold wool from his coat, over the fish-oil stink of the lamp.

“Those are for Nalūn.”

“You can read the addresses.”

“Yes.”

He pulls out the bench. Its feet catch on a warped board, and he lifts the near end clear before sitting. He reads without expression. She has spent enough evenings with him to resent the care she gives that fact.

“Four copies?” he asks.

“Four men.”

“Each will charge for searching.”

“Let them tell me what they want.”

“They will tell you what they think you can pay.”

“Then I'll know something about them.”

The wood-splitter strikes. A smaller knock follows as a piece hits the block on its way down. Lambert loosens the cord around his packet and slides a folded sheet towards her.

She leaves it where it is.

“What is that?”

“A release for the stores.”

“Which stores?”

“The northern depots. You have the quantities.”

“I have the quantities. I haven't signed the movement.”

“That is why I brought it.”

She takes the sheet.

There is work on it she recognises: destinations, loads, provisions to be held at the junctions. She checks the left margin first. One route mark is hers, copied by a hand that has made the lower stroke too long. She shortens it with her nail before remembering she is holding no pen.

“This means a hard turn,” she says. “Whoever copied it has made it a washout.”

“The driver knows the road.”

“And the next driver?”

Lambert turns the sheet towards the lamp.

She reaches for the pen, corrects the mark and signs beside the correction. The ink gathers at the bottom of the stroke. She touches the point to the edge of a scrap and leaves the release open to dry.

“There's your movement.”

“The signature at the foot, Tabitha.”

“I know where it goes.”

His right thumb presses against his index finger. Once, then again. She watches until he lays the hand flat on the table.

The broth has begun to catch. She can smell it now, scorched bone beneath the fat. Tabitha rises, crosses to the hearth and hooks the pot away from the hottest stones. Steam wets her face. At close range the room disappears behind it, and for the space of that movement there is a pot, a hook, her hand inside a fold of her coat.

She brings two bowls back.

Lambert receives the first because his place is nearest the hearth. She fills her own afterwards and sits. The broth has thickened enough to coat her tongue; it tastes of salt and marrow, with a bitter trace from the bottom. She drinks again. The warmth reaches the cramped fingers holding the bowl.

Lambert leaves his untouched.

“Drink it while it is hot.”

“I have eaten.”

“Then hold it. Your hands are white.”

He puts both hands around the bowl.

She takes the release again and follows every destination with a fingertip. The first calculation is sound. So is the second. Against the last, she returns to her own roll of manifests, unfastens the leather thong and finds the figure he has used. Correct. There is no bad sum for her to catch him in.

A long strip of windmeat lies wrapped in cloth beside her papers. She tears off a piece, softens it in the broth, and chews while she reads the release a second time. Lambert waits. Beyond his shoulder the window has become black enough to show the lamp's reflection.

“The grain,” she says.

His hands leave the bowl.

“I have heard your question.”

“You heard it in council. Then you heard it twice in rooms with the door shut.”

“Yes.”

“It's shut now.”

“I can see that.”

She sets the windmeat down.

There are sixteen thousand people in the southern reach who have eaten because Edward Lambert put grain where it would be needed. Tabitha has carried enough provision rolls to know what such a figure contains. Flour saved for a toothless mouth. A mother wetting meal on her finger to keep a child quiet until the pot boils, then licking what remains from the knuckle. Men who can shoulder a load again after food, though yesterday it took two to get them upright. Sixteen thousand is a number she can work with.

The first week is another.

“You bought before they advanced.”

“I bought grain.”

“In the first week.”

“You have the date.”

“And you have everything before it.”

Lambert folds the loose cord into his palm. There is a worn place where it has passed through the same knot many times. He draws that part straight with his thumbnail.

“What have you asked for?”

“To send the first instruction they received. Every change after it. The day they were paid.”

“The original papers?”

“Copies.”

“Made by whom?”

“Their own hands, or the hands that keep their accounts. With someone to say they watched.”

“And the originals remain with them.”

“Yes.”

He looks towards the night-stone.

“You have already written that.”

“Four times.”

“They will ask who wants them.”

“My name is there.”

“Your house is there.”

She turns the nearest sheet towards him.

Her name stands at the foot in full. Beneath it she has written where the reply must come. The letters are stiff from the slow work of a cold hand, but every one can be read.

“Say what you mean.”

“A man who sells to Hallenfeld will answer differently from a man who believes Hallenfeld is about to stop buying.”

“Have I said we will?”

“You have asked for the papers of a completed sale. You have given no reason.”

“Would you like me to give one?”

“No.”

He answers before she has finished speaking, with the sheet still between them and his thumb resting against the knot. She leaves it there.

Beyond the door, someone drags a sack along the passage. The sound stops at the threshold, starts again, and dwindles towards the stairs. Tabitha has to resist the old impulse to get up and tell the carrier to put it on his shoulder. Every dragged sack loses something eventually.

Lambert reads the letter through. She lets him.

“The grain came north,” he says.

“I know where it went.”

“Then you know what was waiting here.”

“I know what wasn't left down there.”

He puts it down.

She has walked a road after households have left it. Everyone speaks about what they take. Tabitha remembers what lies beside the ruts: the cracked pot carried until its handle parts, a door bar too good to abandon until the children begin to tire, bedding caught on a thorn where a load has shed it. A person can start with a household on a cart. By the fourth ford he has left the cooking pot and the spare harness on the bank to make room for someone who cannot walk.

There had been roads ready for those people.

Cleared roads.

Her house had done the work.

She rubs a dried bead of ink from the side of her forefinger. It comes away in a black curl and sticks to the table.

“What instruction did you have before you bought?”

Lambert draws her letter closer to the lamp, though he has finished reading it.

“That isn't in this letter.”

“No. I'm asking you.”

“You have asked.”

“[quietly] Then give me a different answer.”

He gets up. Tabitha remains on the bench, the heat from her bowl against the inside of her wrist. Lambert carries his broth to the hearth and sets it on the warm edge. When he turns back, his hands are behind him.

She knows the position.

For a moment she is in council again, with people waiting for the next matter and the next matter ready to hand. Someone has a timber complaint. Beyond him waits a man who needs a road opened before his load spoils, and others stand along the wall with matters of their own. She knows their faces. She could have stood there until her legs failed and he would have let the next person speak.

Here she has his unsigned release.

Her fingers move towards it.

He sees.

“If that waits,” he says, “the loads wait.”

“They're under cover.”

“The carts are. The men are going home.”

“They'll come back.”

“Some will.”

She thinks of the carrier dragging his sack. Of the wood-splitter taking too long between rounds. Men paid in food, or owed for work, or going back to households where a short stack has to be made sufficient by splitting every piece small. She knows exactly how many will return when summoned twice for one job.

Her hand stays on the release.

Lambert waits beside the hearth. He has given her the consequence in the fewest words that will serve. In another room, with another person's name at the foot, she would have said it herself.

*Don't make them carry this too.*

She takes the pen.

Her signature runs broader than the correction above it. The last stroke scratches because she has not dipped deeply enough. She goes over it, puts the pen down, and turns the paper so he can read.

“There.”

Lambert returns to the table.

He checks the signature. Then the corrected road mark. She lets him take the sheet without reaching for one of her letters in exchange.

For the first time since he came in, he looks at her bowl.

“You have barely eaten.”

“Neither have you.”

He carries his back from the hearth.

The wood-splitter has stopped. In the quiet she can hear the faint bubbling of fat along the lamp's wick. The room has cooled despite the fire; cold comes through the floor into her boots, and the damp hem of her coat weighs against the backs of her legs. She shifts the bench nearer the hearth.

He gathers his papers.

“You intend to send those tonight.”

“Yes.”

“On the provision route.”

“On a route already going south.”

“They will be opened beside orders for more grain.”

“Separate wrappers.”

“Same carrier.”

She studies him across the table. The skin beneath his eyes has darkened. At the mouth, a pale line shows where his beard would grow if he ever let it. She has watched him find food for people who could not feed themselves, find carts when there were none, find the exact man who would lend a horse to a woman he disliked. She would put a starving settlement into his hands tomorrow.

Her four letters lie between them.

“What do you want put in?”

“That the purchases continue.”

“They do.”

“Write it.”

“You can write it on your orders.”

“I have.”

“Then they'll have it.”

“They will have your request beside it.”

She pulls the nearest letter towards her and reads it as a man in Nalūn might read it, with his own winter to supply and his own creditors waiting. Her name. Her house. The first instruction, every alteration, the dates of payment. Nothing in the words says his next sale is safe.

She takes a fresh sheet.

Lambert drinks while she writes.

The added sentence takes longer than the rest of the letter. She crosses out her first attempt. A promise about future purchases would bind her to things she has yet to inspect. Grain can spoil. Roads can close. She will not purchase bad sacks to make a stranger feel safe.

She writes that the inquiry suspends no order already placed.

Lambert reads it.

“Existing obligations,” he says.

“Orders.”

“There are deliveries arranged under an agreement, before individual orders are issued.”

“Then write your agreements down.”

“They have them.”

“I don't.”

He sets the bowl aside.

Tabitha offers him the pen. He takes it by the upper end, away from the wet ink, and adds a narrow line beneath hers. His writing is smaller, each letter given the same allowance of space. She bends over it until her loosened hair touches her cheek.

Existing agreements remain in force.

She reads it again.

“Put your name to that.”

“It is a statement of fact.”

“Then it should be easy.”

He signs.

The mark sits beneath both sentences. She has expected him to choose another place, perhaps draw a line between his words and hers. He does neither. She takes the sheet back before the ink has dried and holds it level with the lamp.

“Four,” she says.

“I can count them.”

“Good.”

They work.

Tabitha lays a clean sheet against the table's straight edge, holds it with her left palm, and copies the first letter down to the request for witnessed copies. She leaves room beneath it for both sentences. Her pen catches once in the rough paper. She lifts it cleanly, turns the sheet towards the light and checks that she has not torn a hole through a date.

Lambert copies the added lines onto the next letter.

They exchange sheets. She checks every word he has written. He checks hers. When he reaches the address, he pushes the paper back and taps a letter whose lower curve could be mistaken for another. Tabitha closes the curve and checks it again.

Outside, a horse stamps.

She remembers the carrier.

“There's time,” Lambert says.

She keeps writing.

Her hand aches along the side that rests on the wood. The skin there has become hard through years of cold reins and wet rope, but the pen asks for a small, exact movement neither of those has taught it. By the fourth copy she has to stop and open her fingers.

Lambert pushes the night-stone towards her.

She puts her hand on it. Its warmth is nearly spent. Along one edge an old chip has left a hollow just large enough for the side of her thumb.

Across the room the tray beneath the saddlebags overflows. Water follows a seam in the boards towards the door.

“Boot,” she says.

He moves his foot.

Tabitha gets up, empties the tray into the hearth's ash bucket and sets it back. One bag hangs low enough to sit in the water. She shifts it to a higher peg, runs her fingers along its seam and finds the leather sound. It will keep the rain out.

When she turns, Lambert is testing the ink on the first letter with the edge of a scrap.

“Leave it,” she says.

“It is dry.”

“I'll fold them.”

He moves his hands away.

She gathers the four letters and reads each address aloud under her breath. The names have become shapes. Saying them makes her check the middles. She folds every sheet with the writing inward, makes the wrappers, and puts each letter down separately before beginning the next.

Lambert watches her tie them.

“Keep a copy of what you sent.”

“I have.”

“Of the additions.”

She points to the first sheet.

He checks his signature. Then he looks at the space beside it.

She brings the pen back.

For a moment she considers writing only her initials. There are already too many names here, hers on every request, his beneath the assurance, the factors' outside where anyone carrying the bag can read them. She has wanted his hand beside the work. It is there.

She signs in full.

The old question remains beneath the stone. She takes it out and puts the new sheet behind it, careful to leave the dated refusals visible when the two are unfolded. There is room for another entry at the bottom if she writes small. She leaves it blank.

Lambert ties the release into his own packet.

“What will you do when they answer?”

“Read it.”

“And if they send you exactly what you expect?”

She puts the packet of inquiries under her arm.

“I'll read it again.”

He stands aside at the door. The passage beyond smells of wet harness and broth carried up from the kitchens. Farther down, a man is bent over an open courier bag, feeding a strap through its buckle. His knuckles show pale.

Lambert holds out his dispatch.

She takes it.

The two packets fit together beneath her arm, his smaller one against the four she has written, their cords pressing through the wool. She carries them down the passage. At the bag she crouches, puts the provision release inside first, then lays her letters along its side where the leather will keep them straight.

The courier reaches for the flap.

“Wait.”

She settles the knot below the edge. A cord caught beneath a buckle can work loose on the road; she has seen a day's riding undo a thing tied well in a warm room. She draws the flap down herself, tests the first strap, then the second.

Behind her, Lambert still holds the door.

Tabitha gets up and takes her hand off the bag.

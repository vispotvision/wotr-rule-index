# The Holy Inquisition, Part 1B: The Empty Chair

The bread was three days old and Edward Lambert ate it anyway, because three-day bread was the bread that was available and availability was the standard, not freshness. He tore it with his fingers, the crust cracking in a line that followed the grain of the flour rather than the line of his hand, and he chewed slowly, and he counted the room while he chewed.

Eleven people. Fourteen chairs. Three empty.

The first empty chair belonged to Lord Bressan, whose gout had worsened since the autumn rains and whose gout was the excuse and whose excuse was the man's way of saying he did not wish to commit his household to a position until the position had been decided by the people in the room who would bear the cost of deciding it. The second empty chair belonged to the envoy from Thorngate, who had sent a letter of regret that arrived precisely on time and said precisely nothing. The third empty chair belonged to nobody. It had been placed there by the steward because the steward counted chairs by the number of legs at the table and fourteen was the number and the number was the habit and the habit was the thing Edward had not corrected, because an extra chair at a council table was a question that everyone in the room had to answer for themselves, and the answering told him who was counting.

The chamber was long and low-ceilinged, built for a purpose older than the council that now occupied it. Stone walls, plastered once and plastered again over the cracks of the first plastering, the second coat already yellowing at the joins where the damp came through. Two windows on the western wall, shuttered against the afternoon, the light entering in slats that crossed the table at angles and lit the dust that eleven bodies had stirred from the floor rushes. The rushes needed changing. Edward had noted this three days ago. The rushes had not been changed because the steward's attention was on the council and the council's attention was on the thing Ida was about to say, and nobody's attention was on the rushes, and the rushes were the kingdom in miniature: the thing that rotted while everyone looked at the thing that burned.

"Edward, what exactly do you plan to do about the talks of an attack?"

Ida Lambert stood at the far end of the table with her hands flat on the wood, the posture of a woman who had decided that the question was going to be asked and that the asking was not going to wait for the bread to be finished. She was forty-six, five years younger than Edward, built along the same Lambert lines but wider through the jaw and heavier through the arms, the specific architecture of a woman who had administered the eastern corridor's trade routes for nine years and who had done the administering on horseback because the eastern corridor did not have roads that accommodated carriages and Ida did not have patience that accommodated delay. Her hair was iron-grey, pulled back in a knot secured with a pin that was functional and not decorative. Her eyes were the Lambert grey. The same grey Edward carried. People called it iron. It was not iron. It was the specific absence of color that the bloodline produced in every generation, meaning nothing except that Lamberts had been breeding with Lamberts for long enough that the grey had become fixed.

She was looking at Edward. Edward was eating bread.

He looked at her.

The room held its breath the way rooms held their breath when Edward Lambert looked at someone, which was not through any quality of menace or authority but through the specific quality of attention that Edward's looking carried, which was: the look of a man who was reading your second sentence before you had finished your first.

Thomas stood by the window. He was Edward's youngest brother, thirty-two, the one the family had produced late and unexpectedly, the way families that had stopped expecting children sometimes produced one more, and the one-more had grown into a man who was tall where Edward was middling, broad where Edward was lean, and confident where Edward was patient, and the confidence was the thing that Edward watched most carefully, because confidence in a man of thirty-two was either the early form of competence or the early form of catastrophe, and Edward had not yet determined which Thomas was building.

Thomas had not sat down. Thomas rarely sat down in councils. Standing was the posture of a man who believed his contribution would be brief and decisive, and the believing was the thing Edward had not yet corrected, because correcting Thomas in public would cost more than the correction was worth and because Thomas's errors were still small enough to be educational rather than fatal.

"Why are we worried about a cult?" Thomas said. He turned from the window with the specific motion of a man delivering a line he had prepared. "Propagated by the Palatinate."

Edward clapped his hands at this. Once. The sound flat in the low-ceilinged room. Not applause. Punctuation. The bread crumbs fell from his fingers onto the table and he did not brush them away.

Ida shook her head. The headshake was small, controlled, the headshake of a woman who had watched Edward's mannerisms for forty years and who knew that the clap was not agreement but the specific sound Edward made when something was worth addressing, and the addressing was going to be unpleasant for the person who had prompted it.

Thomas did not read the clap correctly. Thomas read the clap as approval. Edward filed this.

"You are right, Thomas." Edward's voice had not changed from the voice that had asked for the bread twenty minutes ago. The same register. The same tempo. The words arriving in the order they had been counted into, each one at the distance from the last that the meaning required. "We shouldn't be worried about a religious organization."

He paused. Not for effect. For bread. He tore another piece and put it in his mouth and chewed and swallowed and the room waited because the room had learned that Edward's pauses were not pauses. They were the space between the first sentence and the second sentence, and the second sentence was the one that mattered.

"However." He set the remaining bread on the table. His right thumb pressed against his right index finger, once, and held. "Religions gain weight. Power in politics. I am not foolish enough to believe they are planning to purge more than a single village."

Ida's hands had not moved from the table. Her weight was forward, the balls of her feet taking the load, the posture of a woman who was listening with her body rather than her ears because her ears had already heard what Edward was going to say and her body was waiting for the part he had not said yet.

"However," Edward said again, and the repetition was deliberate, because repetition in Edward's speech was not emphasis but architecture, the second *however* carrying more weight than the first because the first had opened the door and the second was walking through it, "if we slaughter them all, kill them like dogs, what would that do?"

The question sat on the table between the bread crumbs and the empty cups. Nobody answered it. The question was not the kind of question that required an answer. It was the kind of question that required the room to understand that the answer was already known by the man asking it, and the asking was the teaching, and the teaching was: you have not counted far enough.

Oswin Lambert, who sat at the midpoint of the table, shifted in his chair. He was sixty-eight, the eldest Lambert in the room by a decade, built thick through the middle in the way of men who had spent their careers at tables rather than on horses, his hands broad and ink-stained from a lifetime of ledger work. His coat was finer than Edward's. His coat was always finer than Edward's, because Oswin's function in the family was trade and trade required the display that governance did not, and the display was the statement and the statement was: this man has capital, and the capital's presence is the capital's argument. He had been named Duke of the Alabaster Corridor upon Edward's retrieval of the seat, and the naming was the reward and the reward was the leash, because a man who holds a title holds the title's obligations, and the obligations were the thing Edward had counted before he granted them.

Oswin said nothing. Oswin was listening to the second sentence. Oswin was a Lambert.

Beside him, the men and women of the border houses filled four more chairs. Lord Renwyck of House Vellane, whose estates ran along the southern approach and whose southern approach was the road that anything marching on Alabaster from the exterior would have to use. Lady Edith Thorne, whose family had held the western grain stores for three generations and whose grain stores were the reason the western quarter ate and the reason anyone who controlled the western quarter controlled the council's patience. A younger man whose name Edward had filed but not yet categorized. Three councils attended. None spoken in. The silence was either deference or calculation.

And Aldric Marmont.

Aldric sat two chairs from Oswin, upright, his hands on the table in the mirror of Ida's posture, though Aldric's posture was not borrowed from Ida but was the posture of a man who had served in the field and who placed his hands where he could see them because hands he could see were hands he controlled and hands he controlled were hands that were not doing the wrong thing. He was fifty, lean, weathered across the face and neck in the way of men who had spent their middle years outdoors in country where the wind carried grit. His hair was dark, greying at the temples in two sharp lines that followed the bone structure underneath. His jaw was heavy. His eyes were brown and careful.

House Marmont held the northern border. The northern border was the border that faced the direction the Inquisition was coming from.

"We should be concerned," Aldric said.

The words were quiet. Not Lambert-quiet, which was mechanical and deliberate. Marmont-quiet, which was the quiet of a man who had learned that the things worth saying were the things that did not need volume to carry.

"Especially due to the fact they are acting upon the bequest of inquisition."

Edward heard the sentence. Edward heard the second sentence inside it, which was not spoken but which lived in the specific weight Aldric placed on *bequest*, because *bequest* was a legal word and a legal word in a military man's mouth meant the military man had been reading, and the reading meant the military man was worried about something more specific than a cult with torches, and the specificity was the thing Edward needed to find.

Edward's thumb counted against his index finger. Three. Four. Five.

He looked at Dabney.

The boy sat at the end of the table, in a chair that was too large for him, his feet not reaching the floor. He was six. His hands were in his lap, folded in the way that children folded their hands when they had been told to sit and watch and when the sitting and watching was the instruction and the instruction had been given by a man whose instructions the boy obeyed, not because the boy feared the man but because the boy had understood, at an age when understanding was not yet distinguishable from obedience, that the man's instructions produced results and the results were the reason for the obeying.

Dabney had not spoken. Dabney had not moved. Dabney had watched Ida ask the question. Dabney had watched Thomas give the wrong answer. Dabney had watched Edward eat bread and say the thing that made the room go still. Dabney had watched Aldric say *bequest* and Dabney's eyes had moved to Aldric's hands when Aldric said it, and the moving-of-the-eyes was the thing Edward noticed, because a six-year-old who watched hands instead of faces was a six-year-old who was counting without knowing he was counting, and the not-knowing was the stage before the knowing, and the knowing was the thing Edward was here to build.

"What do you think, boy?"

The room shifted. Not visibly. The specific interior shift of eleven adults recalibrating around the fact that the man running the council had just addressed a six-year-old, and the addressing was not indulgence and was not performance and was the thing that Edward Lambert did, which was: teach.

Dabney looked at Edward. His face was small and round in the way that children's faces were round before the bone structure underneath declared itself, and what the bone structure was going to declare had not yet been decided, but the eyes were decided. The eyes were dark, steady, and they held Edward's without flinching and without challenging, in the specific register of a child who had been asked a question by an adult he trusted and who was going to answer the question rather than perform the answering.

Dabney opened his mouth.

Then closed it.

Then opened it again.

"The chair," Dabney said. His voice was small. The room was quiet enough for small. "The one nobody's sitting in."

Edward's right thumb stopped counting.

Ida looked at the empty chair. Thomas looked at the empty chair. Oswin looked at Edward.

Edward picked up the bread.

"The boy's right," he said. He bit. He chewed. He swallowed. "We're not asking who's here. We're asking who isn't. The Palatinate sent a reading three days ago, and the reading's results are already moving, and nobody from the Palatinate is sitting in that chair, and the not-sitting is the answer to every question we've asked this afternoon."

Aldric Marmont's hands had not moved from the table. His jaw had tightened by a degree that only a man who watched jaws would have noticed.

Thomas was looking at the empty chair with the expression of a man who was reorganizing a conclusion he had already filed.

Ida straightened from the table. Her hands came up. She folded her arms across her chest, the fold not defensive but structural, the posture of a woman who had received the information she came for and who was now processing the information into the thing it would need to become, which was: a plan.

Edward finished the bread. He brushed the crumbs from his fingers, each finger wiped against his thumb in order, index to little, the gesture mechanical and complete.

"We will draft a response by morning," he said. "Ida, I want a count of every garrison between here and the northern border. Thomas, you will ride to Thornwell by the end of the week and you will count what you find there and you will bring me the count and nothing else. No opinions. No assessments. The count." He looked at Aldric. "Lord Marmont, your border is the border. I would like to know what your patrols have seen in the last two seasons, in writing, on my table by tomorrow evening."

He did not look at Dabney again. The not-looking was deliberate. The lesson had been delivered. The delivery was complete. Looking again would be praise, and praise was not the instrument.

He stood. The chair scraped against the stone floor, and the sound was the end of the council, because when Edward Lambert stood the council was over, not because standing was the signal but because Edward Lambert did not stand until the thing was done, and the standing was the proof that the thing was done, and the done was the only adjournment the room required.

Dabney's feet swung once beneath the chair, the small pendulum motion of a child whose legs were too short for the furniture and whose body had held still for as long as the holding required and was now releasing the held energy in the only direction available, which was down, toward the floor his feet could not reach.

The rushes still needed changing. Edward noted this for the fourth time and walked through the door into the corridor, where the light was thin and the stone was cold and his right thumb was already counting against his index finger again.

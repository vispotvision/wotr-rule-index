# The Judging Paw

*Aetherion Academy · the Crucible floor, Hiromi Mahuo's bout, after Cleave*

---

The dust came down over the Crucible like flour sifted through a sieve, slowly, settling on the broken slabs and the scored sand and the shoulders of the great golden thing crouched in the crater, and Hiromi watched it settle everywhere except one place.

Cleave had gone past. He had felt it go, the whole drawn line of the far mark pinched tight and the paw coming down along it with everything he had, and then the line had bent at its end like a reed in a current and the floor had burst several paces wide of where it should have burst. The Still Sea had broken the instant he threw it. That was the price of Cleave and he had known the price before he paid it: the field he had held folded inward for a year of dawns now came off him again in every direction at once, the way heat comes off a stove when the door is opened, and he could feel the room answering it. The lamp flames on the barrier posts nearest him leaned his way. The needles of the draw-gauges set into the near wall had swung toward him and stuck against their pins. Somewhere in the lower tiers a man was working his jaw against the pressure in his ears.

The blood ran from both nostrils now, warm over his upper lip and into the corner of his mouth, iron and salt, and he let it run. The coal in his chest had become a forge when he called the Lion and the forge had not banked. His knees wanted to fold. He kept them straight the way his mother had taught him to keep them straight in the thousandth push-up, by not consulting them. His hands had come open at his sides, and the half-moons his nails had cut into his palms stung in the dry air, small and exact, a pain he could locate.

The Lion was still his. It stood with its haunches among the talus of the broken floor, its light guttering where the edges of the slabs cut into it, its great head already turning back toward the far mark of its own accord, because that was where he had told it the line ended. First call of the day. The weakest it would ever be.

And in the dust, a boy-shaped hole.

Kain stood inside it exactly where he had stood at the bell. Hiromi read him the way he read every room, posture first, before faces and before gauges: weight even across both bare feet, arms hanging, the long black hair settling over the white cloth of his shirt, the shoulders carrying nothing. The purple eyes had not left him. A body that had not paid for anything yet. Hiromi had been reading bodies since he was six years old and he had never in his life seen one so entirely unspent in the middle of so much expense.

*Motion,* he thought. *What was it?*

He went to his father's shelves for the answer the way his hand went to a pen, without deciding to. The textbooks gave Wyther's art the name Kineris, and the books he had read before he could tie his boots said it read momentum and redirected it and amplified it until speed became a weapon. Motion, in that grammar, was the art of conversion: of taking what was stored and making it spent, potential into kinetic, kinetic into force at the moment of contact, the boulder poised on the ridge becoming the boulder in the valley becoming the crushed house. He had believed that. He had believed it for exactly as long as it had taken the Lion's roar to cross the arena and come apart around the boy like water around a pier.

Because what was force?

He had lain awake at nine over a question of the same shape, the night after the bullet train went by the platform in that impossible quiet, and he had worked it to the bottom then, alone, with a candle and a slate. Force was a transfer. It was the rate at which momentum passed out of one body and into another, and momentum carried two things at once: a size and a direction, a quantity and an arrow. Energy carried only the first. Energy was a sum, a scalar, a heap of coin with no face to it; momentum was the same coin with an arrow stamped on it, pointing somewhere.

And the boy had left every heap of coin exactly where it was.

Hiromi went back over it with the cold attention he gave to other people's summons, and found it whole: the roar had reached the far wards with its strength intact. The pinched place of Cleave had spiralled in around the mark and gone on spiralling, faster and tighter, with nowhere to finish. The paw had swept past at the full impetus of its fall, and the crater it had made when it landed was every bit as deep as it would have been had it landed on its mark. Nothing had been absorbed. Nothing had been blunted or bled away into heat. Every quantity he had put into the fight had arrived on the far side of the boy entire, and the only thing that had changed in any of it was the arrow.

He knew a thing that did that.

His own Trait was a magnet: the iron filings lining up toward the charge, the lamp flames leaning his way. He had read everything his father's shelves held about the lodestone because of it, and further, into papers no Academy tutor would have known how to set him. A magnetic field never did any work on a moving charge. It pushed, but it pushed always at right angles to the path, so that the push and the motion stood perpendicular to one another at every instant, and a push at right angles to motion could bend a path into a circle for the life of the world without adding one grain of speed or subtracting one. Put a charged mote into a field strong enough and it would never strike the magnet at all. It would wind around the lines of the field instead, in a corkscrew, a tightening helix, a gyration that kept every particle of its energy and spent all of it on going round.

He had watched that corkscrew happen. He had watched the whole drawn place of Cleave become it, a sheath of Aether whirling around the boy's body and finding no last inch, and he had not had the word for it while it happened. He had it now. The arena had shown him a figure out of a physics nobody in the tiers had a name for, and he named it to himself in terms that would not reach the Academy's lecture halls for a long time yet: a deflecting force orthogonal to velocity, everywhere within a boy-shaped boundary; the product of push and path summed to nothing; the work done, nil.

Two things followed from that, and he felt them follow the way he felt a binding settle, the small cold click of a thing locking into its seat.

The first was the oldest fact about magnets that there was. A field that only turns arrows has nothing to turn when there is no arrow; a charge lying at rest inside the strongest lodestone in the world feels no force from it whatsoever. Speed is the handle by which the field takes hold, and a thing with no speed offers it no handle.

The second was Wyther's law. Continuance through change. The Archon of Motion did not suffer a motion to end, and so every motion that came into the boy's jurisdiction went out of it still moving, still whole, still carrying every joule of what it had been given. That was what the Paragon of Motion was. It was conservation given a will and a face. And a power that obeys a law of continuance can stop nothing; by the terms of its own Archon it can only send things elsewhere. Every defence the boy had shown him was a defence by redirection, because redirection was the only defence his law permitted him.

Hiromi licked the blood off his lip and kept watching.

A Dominion was not doing anything like that. Hiromi turned this over slowly, because it was the root of everything he had ever made and he had never once been made to look at it from the outside. When he called the Lion he had not thrown it. He had not carried a single mote of its body across the sand. He had pulled the room's own Aether into the shape of a memory and the world had looked at the shape and recognised it, and in recognising it had agreed that mass was there, and weight, and claws the length of a finger joint. Recognition had no speed. It did not travel from a place to a place. It was a verdict, delivered everywhere it applied at once, and if it used kinetics at all it used them the way a judge uses the bailiff, afterward, to carry out what had already been decided.

That was the thing the textbooks had never said plainly. Recognition was no part of the physical world as the physical world was taught. It was a spiritual act, wholly, a construct of soul answering soul, and the Animatria constructs of every kind he had ever studied did not move the way a stone or a body moved because they were never physical objects to begin with. They were metaphysical, in the oldest and most literal sense of that word: after the physical, beyond it, standing behind it. Their bodies were arguments the world had accepted. When the Lion leapt, its leap was physics; when the Lion came to exist, that was something else.

And the boy had stopped every kinetic force aimed at him. Hiromi gave him that freely, with something that was almost delight under the blood. Every push, every pressure wave, every crushing ton, every grain of sand had been denied its last inch.

But the boy was breathing.

Hiromi watched it again to be certain. The white shirt rose and fell, slow and shallow. Air was going into him. Air, a fluid in motion, was travelling toward the boy's body and finishing that travel inside it, in his throat and his lungs, which was contact if anything in the world was contact, and the field did not turn it aside. The field had let the roar's pressure wave by on either side of him and had let his own breath straight in.

So the field chose.

Something inside that boy-shaped boundary was looking at every motion that approached it and deciding which motions threatened and which were invited, and a decision of that kind was a judgement, and a judgement was a recognition. The boy had dominion over what moved. The weight of the spiritual forces under the moving, the verdicts the world handed down about what a thing *was*, he did not govern at all. Those were never his. They had never been his Archon's.

Hiromi knew that shape too, and the knowing went through him with a jolt that he felt in his teeth.

AXIS. His own spinning top, the thing his will had become in the Arena when they told him to concede and he said no. Push Hiromi and the push went sideways into the room. The boy across the sand was AXIS grown tall and turned outward, laid over an entire body of space instead of a single stubborn will, and a hundred times more refined in its taste. And AXIS had a fault Hiromi knew better than anyone alive: a top has no grip along its own spin axis, so a push that agreed with what he already wanted went through him clean. The breath agreed with the boy. The breath went straight in.

He filed that. He did not yet know what else the boy agreed with, and a theory that could kill him wanted two more exchanges of evidence before he bet his body on it.

Wyther governed the Law of Continuance through Change. Hiromi's own Archon, the one whose name he had never once said aloud, not to his father and not to Geturo and not to Lily in the dark of the east gallery, governed the Law of Sovereign Will: that Essence obeys conviction. That was where hierarchy came from. It was the principle that set in place the order of every law there was, natural and otherwise, the ladder that decided which law governed where two laws met, and it lived in the glyph he had laid over this whole arena when the Lion first took shape. **[Dom].** *Here, all of this, is mine to arrange.*

What did that have to do with this fight?

Everything. Dominions were not affected in the same manner, because a Dominion was not an arrow at all. It was a slope. GRASP did not throw the room's Aether at him; it tilted the room, so that every draw within ten paces ran downhill toward him the way water finds the low corner of a floor, and a slope has no direction until something rolls down it. Height has no velocity. Rank has no velocity. The pressure he had carried all his life had none either, his Weight, the lid on the pot that kept small magic from boiling and the tea from steaming. Pressure pushed from every side at once, the way the sea pushes on a pearl diver at depth. Every push met its opposite. The sum pointed nowhere, and still it squeezed. Turn every arrow of a uniform pressure through any angle you liked and you had exactly the same pressure back, because it had been the same in every direction to begin with. It was invariant under rotation. A boy whose whole art was a rotation had no purchase on it whatsoever.

He could not reach that far. He knew his own sheet as well as he knew the stone lion at the Sum-gol gate. His field fell away with the square of distance, a quarter as strong at twice the span, and the far mark sat well beyond the ten paces where his Weight did anything worth having. Shouting it across the Crucible would be shouting a whisper.

But the Lion was close. The Lion stood a few paces from the boy with its haunches in the crater, and it was built out of the room's own Aether laid in line with his field under a glyph whose whole office was to stabilise a chain of command and stretch its reach. In his father's books of plates there was an engraving of the Dominion Spires, Irath's resonant towers that carried a commander's word across a battlefront. The Lion was his field, standing somewhere else.

The hard part would be the second call.

ECHO wanted rhythm. Each time a Mahuo called the same thing in a day it came back louder, but only if the call landed in time with the last, a push given at the top of the swing, and a call given off the beat built nothing at all. He was hurt and he was hurried and he had been spending since the bell, and all three put him off the beat. So he did the stupidest, slowest thing a boy could do in the middle of a fight on a floor where the crowd could see him: he stood still and breathed. In through the nose, through the blood, a count of four. Hold for four. Out through the mouth for four. Under the count he listened for the Lion's hum, the deep resinous drone that had come up out of it when it formed, and he found it, and he let his breath fall into time with it until the two of them pulsed together like a drum and the drummer's heart.

"Sit," he said.

He did not shout it. It might have been the first word he had spoken below a bellow in a public place since he was seven.

The Lion sat.

It did it the way the stone haetae sat at the gate of his father's house, the way he had watched it sit every morning of his childhood: the haunches settling down among the broken slabs, the forelegs planting straight, the chest coming up, the great maned head lifting until it looked level across the few paces of wreck at the boy in the boy-shaped hole. It did not crouch. Nothing in its body gathered or coiled. A pounce is an arrow and a seated guardian is the opposite of an arrow, and nothing whatsoever about it pointed at Kain except its eyes. In the old stories of Hon-guk the haetae did not hunt the guilty. It sat at the gate of the court and knew them, and when it knew them, it put its paw on them.

Then he unfolded his Weight and gave it away.

He had spent a year of dawns learning to fold it inward. Letting it out was easy, since it had always wanted out, and what was new was only the direction. He did not let it spill around himself in a blind ring the way it had spilled since the binding circle at six. He set it down on the Lion the way a man sets a full pail down on a stool, and the Lion, which had been made under **[Dom]** to carry his commands, carried it. Around its seated body the Aether grew heavy.

It showed in the room first. The dust that hung around the edges of the boy's clean pocket stopped drifting and began to fall straight down, all of it, in vertical threads, as though the air had thickened under it into something it could not swim in. The sand at the crater's rim lay flat. On the far barrier posts the lamp flames shortened to stubs and burned there, blue at the root. The draw-gauges set into the wall behind the far mark sank toward their stops and trembled. In the near tiers on that side a woman put both hands over her ears, and the fremitus of the overdriven ward-lines came up through the floor of the Crucible and through the soles of Hiromi's boots, a bowed note pitched too low for the ear.

It was a lid on a pot. Every working that wanted to boil inside it would need more heat to do it. Whatever it cost the boy to keep his field running, Hiromi thought, it cost more now, and the pressure that cost it more had no arrow in it for his field to turn.

That was the first layer. Under it went the second.

He let the Lion's right forepaw go dark.

The stars that made it went out one by one in the crater, from the claws up, until the seated haetae's leg ended at the wrist in a stump of guttering gold. And inside the boy-shaped hole, in the clean still air a hand's breadth above Kain's left shoulder, a point of light came on.

Then another, above the right shoulder. Then a third, behind his neck, in the hair.

The pocket was the stillest place in the Crucible, and the boy had made it so himself. His field had turned away every grain and gust and pressure wave that came at him, and so the Aether that lay inside the boundary of his clean silhouette was the only Aether in the whole arena that had not been touched, drawn, pinched, thrown or spun since the bell. It lay at rest against him, quiescent, as motionless as he was. Hiromi did not send the paw there. He did not move one mote of it across those few paces. He laid **[Ma]** over the still Aether that was already there, Recall, the root of Maelor whose stillness archives everything Wyther dissolves, and told it what it had been all along: *this one, exactly, as I have it.* The forepaw of the haetae at the gate.

The points of light became lines. They drew themselves short, from point to point, each line running toward its neighbour and none of them running toward the boy, a lacework of gold standing a finger's width off his skin and closing on itself the way frost closes across a pane. Every motion in the drawing finished on a star. Not one of them finished on Kain.

The Lion's hum climbed a note. Then it climbed another.

Second call. In rhythm. Louder than the first.

He felt the price arrive and was almost glad of it, because it meant the call was real. The forge in his chest went white. The blood that had been running from his nostrils came faster and he tasted it at the back of his throat, thick, and swallowed it rather than spit, because spitting would have broken the count. A heat-shimmer of diaphoresis broke across his scalp under the white hair and ran down behind his ears. The edges of the arena went grey and soft and began to close in at the rim of his sight, the penumbra that came before syncope, which he had felt three times on three practice floors and which Lily could have told anyone the dates of. His knees asked again. He did not consult them. Somewhere below his ribs the hunger he never admitted to sat up and made itself known, and he told it, as he told it every morning, that he was not hungry.

The grin rearranged itself around the blood. It did not drop.

The planes came. Between the lines the gold filled in, and the filling-in was a recognition and had no speed, so that there was no moment at which a sheet of light could be seen travelling; there was only a moment before which it was lines and after which it was a surface, the way a word on a page is a cluster of strokes until it is suddenly a word. The paw was forming the way the whole Lion had formed in the first minute of the bout: stars, then lines, then the beast filling in. A forepaw broader than the boy's shoulders. The pads, heavy as saddle-leather. The sheathed claws, each the length of a finger joint, curving over the white cloth of the shirt and not yet touching it.

There were three ways out of it, and Hiromi knew every one of them as well as he knew the trick itself. Step out from under the paw before the last plane closed, and the forepaw would rest on empty air at a mark the boy had stood on since the bell. Break the seated Lion, the relay, the Spire, and the Weight would lose its reach and the paw its record. Or put a hand on the one who held all of it, Hiromi himself, standing very still on the other side of the sand, bleeding from both nostrils, with the edges of the world going grey.

He wanted, with a hunger that had nothing to do with food, to see which one the boy chose. He had not moved once. Hiromi meant to find out what it cost him to start.

"Haetae don't pounce..." His voice came out low and a little thick around the blood. He found he did not mind. "They sit at the gate. And they judge."

A breath, in time with the hum.

"Of course."

The last plane closed. In the stillest place in the Crucible, the forepaw of the guardian of Sum-gol lay on the shoulders of the Paragon of Motion with no speed in it at all, and the world, which had agreed that it was there, set its weight down.

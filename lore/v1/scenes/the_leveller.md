# The Leveller

*Southbank. The Fourth Level, in the maintenance hours. Year 715 of the Imperial Age.*

*POV: Penn Lister. Explaining voices: the POV's own read, and Haldor Ivor.*

---

## I. THE CUT

The key went into the housing and turned twice. Penn got the plate in his back teeth before he heard it, iron seating on iron somewhere under the flags. Then the hum went out of him.

It went from the soles up. His boots were on stone and nothing else, and his knees gave a little, the way knees give on a stair that isn't there in the dark. His ears opened. The gauge-house got bigger. The drip off the tundish rang in its brass cup, and across the street a dog began to bark and would not stop.

Down Southbank the street lamps went out together. The whole row down to the river stairs, lit off one cock in this house, and put out by one iron disc. Nothing flickered. They were lamps and then they were posts.

Up the street the wire-office went deaf with half a word in its brass. The meters on forty stairs stopped their needles where they stood, and in every room the lamp-hum and the kettle-hum and the hum in the iron bedsteads quit at once, and the quarter lay down in the dark it had lain in before there was any Board, a wet bank of the river with ten thousand people on it and nothing under them at all. A cut district is deaf, the old women on the stair said. Penn had laughed at them, once.

Across the water the city stayed lit. The run went on past the tee under his feet and over the river, and on the hill over there something domed and rich sat in its own light the way a lamp sits in a window it has paid for. None of it reached this bank.

The ozone went out of the air. He had breathed it so long he had stopped knowing it, the clean scorched edge a live main puts on everything near it, and what came up in its place was river and wet brick and somebody's cabbage from the night before. Southbank stank of itself.

Doors opened up and down the street. Faces came to them, grey in the far light: women in shawls, a man in his shirt with his braces down, a girl holding a candle she hadn't lit. Every one of them looked up at the dead lamp-heads, and the ganger in the gauge-house door looked up, and Penn did, the way a street looks at a sky that has made a noise.

The old hand on the bench by the hatch did not look up. He had his waders across his knees and went on working tar into the knee patch with his thumb.

"Still on the main, Lister?" The chandler's wife, from her step, as she asked it every morning.

"Not tonight, missus."

"Archons take the Board, then."

"They'd send it back."

His own window was the third up over the chandler's, and it stayed dark. Willa slept through anything. Thea didn't. Children raised on a main sleep badly off it, any mother would tell you, and Thea's cradle stood hard against the meter wall where the main came into the room and the plaster stayed warm to the hand all night. A child put to sleep against that wall grows up gifted, the old women said. He had put the cradle there himself. He'd moved the bed to do it.

*She'll wake. A quarter hour of cold and she'll wake, and Willa will walk her up and down in the dark cursing the Board, and I'll be in the Four.*

The ganger struck a lucifer and put it to the oil lamp on the bench, and the gauge-house came back small and yellow. Lamp oil in the maintenance hours. Every cut Penn ever worked smelled of it.

The ganger reached him nose first: hot wax from the seal press warming in its strap-case at his hip. Iron-gall ink gone sour on the nib. Bay rum laid over the lard in his hair, and peppermint over all of it, there so that nobody would say gin. Ralf Sumner, Inspector-Collector in his own book, who said his name Rafe and let the men say Ralf and corrected nobody. His hair was cropped close and grey as a yard-brush and flattened with the lard. A broad red face, jowled, with burst veins across both cheeks and the nose pushed flat and a little left by some old argument. Eleven years at the book had hung a belly over his belt, but the shoulders were still a culvert man's, heavy and rounded forward, and his thighs pulled the Board serge tight across the knee. The frock coat was Board issue, the seal stamped on every brass button but one, where a horn button had been sewn on in black thread. A watch chain crossed the waistcoat. The watch on it kept the wire's hour from Stannvaard, and the plate key and the hatch key hung off the same chain. The last joint of his left little finger was gone. The pad of his right thumb had gone shiny pink with wax burns, and it worked the corner of the oilcloth on his book, lifting it and smoothing it down.

He opened the book on the bench and entered the cut, Southbank branch, plate in, the nib scratching.

"Two of you," he said, still writing. "I'd give you a third man if I had one to give. Holt was third on the Four till the spring quarter. Holt went live."

The old hand's thumb stopped on the tar. Then it went on.

"Orin?" Penn said. "The drink, you mean. He always had the shakes of a morning, did Orin."

"The physicians have a word for it. Tremens." The ganger blotted the line. "A physician will tell you what anything is for six copper, Lister, and tell it you in Latin so you know you've had your money's worth."

He took a copper off every man's day before the day was paid and called it the Board's. Nobody on the Four had ever seen the Board's receipt for it.

"As it stands," the ganger said, "you owe four silver and six on those waders. There's a cutter's place still going on the north stair, twice your day, and it'd have the waders off my book inside a turn."

"I'll not cut plates, Inspector."

"I'm told there's a notice on your own door."

"I've read it."

"Then give it a turn, and see how nice you are about plates."

A death-house may not be cut for arrears. The Board had cut his mother's plate the winter Penn was nine, and put it back on for the nine days his sister lay on the table, and he remembered the hum coming up under the floor while she lay there with no more use for the warm.

The ganger took out his watch and opened it and looked at it the way other men look at a face. "Forty counts, gentlemen. Collar clamped and lagged, and you're up the irons with your tools. The plate goes back on the hour."

"And if we drown?"

"Then drown quick, Lister. It's a form either way."

The old hand stood up off the bench, stepped into his waders and buckled the strap.

"Lamp," he said. "Gauge. Mind the Board lamp."

The Board hand-lantern hung dead on its hook by the housing. A brass barrel, a beryl lens the size of a crown piece in the end of it, and a bead of green wax across the bezel screw with the Board's seal pressed into it, so nobody could turn the bezel without breaking the Board. Charged, it threw a white light a man could read a meter by. Cut, it was tin and a stone.

"Board kit goes down with the gang," the ganger said, not looking up. "Lit or dark. It's on the chit."

Penn hooked it to his belt. The old hand took the oil lamp and the gauge. The ganger unlocked the hatch with the key off his chain, and the air came up the shaft at them, cold and wet and older than the Board.

The old hand went down first. Penn had him by the nose as he swung onto the irons with the lamp. Tar first, the black patch-tar he painted over his wader knees every turn, then wet wool and shag, then the clove he kept in his cheek against a bad back tooth. The lamp gave him up in lengths. Hair the grey of old mortar, thin on the crown and long at the nape, tied off with a bootlace. A narrow face, hollow under the cheekbones, the stubble along his jaw standing white as frost on a wall. Narrow shoulders, corded, under a guernsey coming undone at both cuffs. His chest had sunk the way the old culvert men's did, the belly under it flat as a board, the belt round it carrying three holes punched by hand past the maker's last. Knees knobbed from twenty-three years of brick. His thighs were nothing inside the waders. The Board's number was stamped on his wader strap in Chancery Hand. His hands were the biggest thing on him: knuckles swollen, nails ridged and yellow, a pale seam across the back of the left. On the irons he held them high, fingertips to the rungs, the way he always did.

"You look like the leech has been at you," Penn said down the shaft.

"Mind the irons."

Penn went down after him.

## II. THE FOUR

The cold came through the rubbered canvas at the thigh first, along the big muscle, then into the groin, then into the feet, which went before he noticed them go. The Four had been built by shorter men for shorter men. Penn bent into it and the crown of the arch knocked his cap back anyway.

"Lamp-post," the old hand said, without turning round.

"It's the brick that's wrong."

"Brick was here first."

The culvert was an egg stood on its narrow end. The invert at the bottom was barely wider than two boots, and Penn walked it as he walked every culvert, bent at the hips, knees soft, his neck always a hand from wet brick. Under the Board's brick, where the facing had fallen, older stone showed. Tool-marks in it. The Well's delvers had cut this gallery before anybody metered what came out of it. Then the river drowned it, and the Board lined it and laid a main through it.

Every twenty paces the brick carried its stamp. Upright letters, no joins between them, the same in any kingdom: LEVEL IV, and after it the grade mark for what the Board certified this length to carry. Penn could read it. It was the one line in the city that read the same to everybody.

The conduit ran along the left haunch on iron brackets, sealed pipe as thick as a man's thigh, and every length of it met the next in a Chainsalt collar, white-grey, hard as chalk when it was dry. In standing water they went soft. That was the whole of the trade, some nights. The water stood to Penn's thigh tonight and deeper at the fault, three days of rain in it and the river high against the drowned levels under their feet.

They passed the residue trap, a squat iron box slung under the conduit with its lid dogged down. The old hand tapped it with the small hammer off his belt and listened.

"Full," he said. "Slag to the lid. That's next turn's."

The sludge on the invert was black and gave under the boot, and where it gave it let up the smell of the river's bottom. Rot. Drains. A sweetness under it that Penn didn't want to name. He coughed and spat and coughed. The old hand breathed it like air. In a year and a month Penn had never once heard him cough down here, and nobody on the gang had either. Ivor went ahead with the lamp held low to the water, the way the trade tried an air, and the flame stood tall and yellow over the black.

Down here the cut was worse. Topside the hum had gone out of the ground; down here it had gone out of the brick all round him, and without it the Four was all edges. The drip off the crown landed with a tick he could place to the brick it came from. His own breath came back off the arch at him. The water lay still enough to take the lamp whole.

"Right," Penn said, to hear it.

They came to the fault.

The old hand held the gauge to the conduit wall below the bad collar. Brass case, slate face, three bands lettered round the scale, THIN and AMBIENT and ACTIVE, and a red line high in ACTIVE where drift had to be reported. On a charged night the needle down here stood a finger under that line and trembled. Now it lay down at the foot of THIN, where the residue in the brick held it off the stop, and didn't tremble at all.

"Two marks off the stop," the old hand said. "And a hair."

He passed it to Penn to hold while he got the hammer out. In Penn's hand the needle sat on two and no hair. Penn put it down to his own hand shaking.

The collar was gone. That was plain from a reach off. The white had gone grey and pitted like old cheese where the water had eaten it, and the iron ring under it had rusted to a lip, and all down the haunch below it the brick was stained where it had wept for weeks, and up in the gauge-house the tundish on the housing had been wet with it three turns running. It wasn't weeping now. With the plate in, the leg past the gauge-house stood full and slack, level with the water round it and no fall between them. A dead leg. The weep at the collar had dried to a bead that didn't drop.

The old hand tapped the sound pipe either side of the fault. It rang, clean, both lengths.

"A solid weight rings when struck," he said. He tapped the collar. It went dull as a boot on turf. "That one's cased."

On the collar, below the waterline, the grey lay in a row.

Penn had grown up on the river stairs, where a boy who waded came out with leeches on his shins and his mother went at them with a pinch of salt from the bar, and he knew a leech. These were bigger. A hand long, flat, the grey of a dead fire, and ribbed along their length, fine and even, like the scoring on the regulator bar in the gauge-house. Eight of them, near enough, laid close on the pitted salt where the weep used to come through. They didn't move.

"Meters," Penn said.

"Tax-men." The old hand didn't look at them. "They'll not trouble thee. Waders on, they'll not trouble anybody."

He took the gauge back off Penn and moved it along the pipe and back. Over the nest the needle climbed off two and hung at three, a whole mark higher than a dead leg had any business reading.

"Higher here," Penn said.

"Residue. Pools at a weep. Mind the collar."

Dry Ivor, the gang called him, out of his hearing. In twenty-three years not a man on the Four had seen his hands go into the water. He kept them off the brackets at a fault, and off the water always. Wellow habit, the gang said, and seniority, and let him have it.

So Penn did the wet work. Penn had done it for a year and a month.

The old hand set the gauge on the bracket face out and held up the lamp. Penn rolled his sleeves to the shoulder. In to the elbow. The cold took his hands the way a door takes a thumb. In ten breaths the knuckles ached down to the bone; in twenty the ache went dull and spread. He laid the tarred lagging round the collar under the water, and where the grey lay in the way he swept them off with the end of the canvas. They drifted off flat. Then they came back, slow, and lay down on the salt again, and none of them so much as turned toward his bare wrists.

"Twelve counts, gentlemen," the ganger called down the shaft, pleasant as a man reading out a bill of fare.

Penn got the two half-bands of the clamp round the lagging and the bolt through the lugs, and then the thread wanted finding, and his fingers had gone stupid. He could see them and not feel them. He found the thread and lost it. Found it. The nut went on crooked and he backed it off and it dropped and he caught it against the brick with his knee, and the old hand held the lamp and kept his mouth shut.

Then the warm came. It came up into his fingers all at once, pink and prickling, as if he had put them in a basin by the fire. He laughed out loud.

"There they are," he said. "Right. Right, there they are."

The old hand didn't laugh.

Round his waders the water stood in fine rings, close and trembling, the way water stands in a cup when a dray goes by in the street.

*Shivering. Cold gets the old ones first.*

The warm went out again as fast as it had come. After it the hands were worse. The grip went out of them from the heel of the palm first, and the key turned in his fingers and the nut didn't.

"Twenty-two!"

*Get it on. Get it on, get it on, get it on and get up the irons and home.*

"Twenty-eight, gentlemen!"

## III. THE TAX-MEN

"Out the road," the old hand said.

He hung the lamp on the bracket and shoved. Penn went sideways into the brick with his shoulder and his hands still in the water on the bottom jaw of the clamp, and the old hand pushed his guernsey up past both elbows and put his arms into the Four.

Penn had never seen it. The forearms went in white as peeled willow, roped with cord, and the old hand took the key off Penn's fingers as though they weren't there.

"Hold the jaw. Hold it."

Penn held it. The old hand set the key on the square head of the nut and put his weight through it and the thread took. One turn. Two. On the third his forearm came down across the broken lip of the collar, where the salt had pitted away and the iron under it had rusted to an edge, and the edge opened him from the wrist a hand's length toward the elbow.

It was a small cut, through the skin and no deeper, clear of the cords. Penn had had worse off a crate. It bled as small cuts bleed in cold water, slow, dark threads lifting off the skin into the lamp and going out into the still water like smoke into a still room.

Then the nest came off the collar.

They lifted in a row, flat, and came through the water in waves that ran down the length of them two and three to a breath. Penn's hands were in the water, his bare wrists a thumb from the collar. One went over the back of his hand, and the water moved and was still, and the dead hand told him nothing. They went past him as if he were brick.

The first of them lay down on the old hand's forearm, on the inside by the cut, and flattened there, and the next beside it, and the next, until the arm from the wrist to the crook was laid in grey, and then they were on the other arm as well.

They were on him before Penn's hands had moved.

The plate had left the Four slack. The leg stood level with the water round it, no fall between them to push anything through the Chainsalt, so whatever had fed on the weep had fed on nothing since the key turned. It had passed Penn's hands and lain down on Ivor.

The lamp on the bracket lit half a rod of the Four and no more.

*Blood. Blood in the water brings them.*

That was the river stairs. A boy with a scab on his shin came out with three on it and a boy with clean shins came out with none, and it was the scab they went for, every time. Ivor's arm was open. There'd be a split in his waders under that tar patch at the knee, where the water had been getting in on his skin all night. Penn's own skin was whole. His hands on the clamp jaw had nothing on them to taste, or he'd have known it.

"They're leeches, Ivor. Same as the stairs. You've a split in them waders and you've opened your arm and they've had the taste of you."

Ivor didn't answer. He was turning the key.

He turned it slower. Where the grey lay, the cords of the forearm that close a hand stood up under the skin and then lay down again, one after another, and the key stopped halfway through a turn because the fingers round it had nothing left to turn with. His mouth hung a little open. His lips had gone blue at the edges.

*The cold. The cold in him, and the blood going out of him to them. He'll not get up the irons on those hands.*

Round Ivor's thighs the water stood up in rings.

They had come back, finer, going out from his waders on every side at once, as if the brick under him had been struck and was still sounding. They came quicker when he swore. Ivor swore now, low, in the Wellow way, and the rings came so quick they ran together.

*He's shaking. It's the shakes.*

On the bracket where the old hand had set it the gauge had hung at three. Now it stood at four. As Penn looked it went to five. It went up as the grey lay down on the old hand, the way it went up on a charged night when the draw came through, and the old hand had his back to it.

Like goes to like, the leech-women on the river stairs used to say, the old ones go to the old. There was nothing older in the Four than Haldor Ivor except the brick.

They lay on the outside of his skin, where a thumbnail could get at them.

Penn let go the jaw.

He got a thumbnail under the head end of the nearest one, as you do on the stairs: never pull, slide the nail under the front and lever. It came away clean. No hold to it at all. No blood behind it, no mark on the skin, only a paler patch where the wet had been kept off, and the thing lay across his fingers flat and grey as a strip of wet felt. It didn't curl. It didn't try for his hand. He flung it and it went into the water a reach off.

It came back. Flat, in its waves, straight to the pale patch on Ivor's arm, and lay down in it again.

*They've not bit. There's no mark. Get them off before they bite.*

The ganger's voice came down the shaft. "Thirty, gentlemen."

Penn got his wet hand into his coat for the salt. It was the heel of a bar, grey and gritty, the size of his two thumbs together, and it was his dinner with the bread, and it was worth more than the bread. He opened his knife with his teeth. He got Ivor's right arm up out of the water by the elbow, and Ivor let him. He shaved the bar down the forearm over the grey backs so thick the salt gritted and caked in the ribs of them.

On the stairs salt took a leech off inside a count. It curled. It let go and dropped off the shin into the river, and some boy laughed.

These lay under the salt as if it were weather.

Blood brings them, salt takes them off, you never pull. He had used the nail. He had used the salt. He had nothing after that.

His mouth went on without him. "Right," he said. "Right. Salt's no good then, salt's no good, and I'll get them off with the knife, I'll have the knife under them and..."

"Leave it." Ivor took the arm back. He got the heel of his hand on the key and leaned, and the last half-turn went in. "Clamp's on."

## IV. FULL TO EMPTY

"Salt's for river leeches," Ivor said.

"They're leeches."

"Tax-men. They don't bite." His breath went, and came. "Tha's a flusher's lad. Two tanks. Pipe between. Which way does it run."

"Full to empty."

"Till."

"Till they're level." Penn's mouth was ahead of him. "Right. Till they're level."

"They're empty. I'm full. Twenty-three year stood in the pipe. I ring on its note, same as they do." He brought both arms up out of the Four and laid them along the top of the pipe, clear of the water, and the grey came up with them, flat to him. "Line engine. Lift the pick-up a span off the rail and it's lost near the lot. They've to touch. They'd have to come through thy waders, and a thumb's as good as a rod to them."

"My hands were in."

"Aye." For the first time since his arms went in, the old hand looked at him. "They went past thy hands."

Penn looked down. His own knuckles had split on the clamp, all four on the right hand, and from each of them a thread of his blood went out into the black water, the same as it went from Ivor's arm. He had not felt it go.

"Tha's bleeding same as me," the old hand said. "And thy hands were in first."

"Then why..."

Ivor very nearly laughed. "Board's stamped every twenty paces of this. They like stamped ground. They go to what carries most. There's nowt in thee for them to go to, lad. There's nowt in most folk. Me dad carried poles at Wellow thirty year. Stood in the drowned levels to his chest with the surveyors, and never had one on him. They'd go past him to get at a surveyor's boot. That's how we knew who'd stirred, at Wellow. You looked who they went to."

Ivor's teeth had started going. "Our Sira stirred at eleven. The screening had her on the roll inside a turn. A hunting hall bought her paper off me mam for the price of the winter, and the party took her down the flooded levels in front of them. First in. For the nest to go to. A lick, they called that at Wellow. The licensed men came on behind on the dry path and the tax-men came off the walls to her, and the porters killed them off her with spades."

"Ivor."

"After, I stripped her. Spent ward-stone in a bag. Tha presses it to them and they go into it, and drop." He stopped. The rings round his thighs had slowed. "I were thirteen. I carried the stone."

Up the shaft, in the gauge-house, the ganger shut his watch. The snap of it came all the way down to them.

*Full, he said. I'm full.*

"Then what's in you," Penn said.

The old hand took one arm off the pipe, leeches and all, and laid the flat of his hand on his own breastbone.

"I've gone live, lad."

Penn's mouth opened and nothing came. The words would not go into any shape he had.

"It hums," the live man said. "Here. Same as the main does."

"How long?"

"Since the spring quarter. Not before."

It came out of him smooth and whole, ready, the way the ganger's lines came out.

"I'll shout for him." Penn was already turning to the shaft. "I'll shout up and he'll send for a Guild man, they'll have somebody who knows what these..."

"Tha'll not." The live man's voice came up out of him hard, the first hard thing in it since the arms went in. "No one reads me."

Penn stood with the water to his thigh.

"At Wellow we called them levellers," Ivor said. "Same word as for a man that takes a copper off every wage."

Up the shaft the leveller called the count. "Thirty-two, gentlemen."

"Board lamp," the live man said.

"It's dead."

"Dead's what I want. Dead as a spent stone. Get the lens off it."

Penn got the lantern off his belt. He turned it to the lamp. The green bead lay across the bezel screw with the Board's seal pressed in it, the same seal as on the meter inside his own door, the same wax as on the cutter's notice outside it. A man could knock another man down a stair and be fined to his means for it. A broken seal was a tariff and a term, and the Board went after it with the whole of itself, every time.

*They'd cut our plate for good. They'd put Willa and the little one on the parish, and me in the Assize.*

He had his nail on the wax, and it stayed there.

"Break it," the live man said.

Penn put his thumbnail through the wax.

It was brittle with the cold and went in crumbs, green, into the water. He got the bezel between his dead fingers and turned it and it didn't turn, and he turned it again with the flat of both palms and it went. The lens dropped into the Four after the wax. Behind it the plate sat in its brass seat. A disc of dull metal, the size of the lens, with LUMINALIS round its rim in Chancery Hand and the Board's number under it. The inscription looked like scratches.

He held it to the nearest leech. A thumb off the grey.

Nothing.

"Touch it," the live man said. "Tha's to touch."

Penn pressed the bare plate flat on the leech on Ivor's forearm.

It went flat to the metal as it had gone flat to the skin. For a breath it lay across both of them, Ivor and the Board's plate, and in that breath Ivor's knees went, and he caught himself on the conduit bracket with the other arm, and hung there. Then the leech let go. It let go of the arm and it let go of the plate and it dropped into the water and turned over once, grey belly, grey back, and lay on the silt.

Meat.

In tune and touching the full one runs into the empty one, and nothing in the Four was emptier than the Board's plate, so the tax-man had run itself out into the plate and let go of the live man's arm and dropped. The thing had been full of Ivor. The plate had been dead since the key turned, and full ran to empty whichever way the pipe was laid. What held it to the skin was its own: some of what it took it spent keeping its hold, the way a pump spends some of its lift working its own valve, and once the plate had drawn off the rest there was nothing in it to hold with. Penn had the whole of it now.

"They go to whatever wants most," the live man said through his teeth. "They've the loyalty of water. A dead lamp wants more than a tired man."

Penn took the next.

That one cost Ivor worse. The breath went out of him as the leech lay across the arm and the plate together, and he sagged off the bracket a hand lower, and the leech dropped, and Penn wanted to stop and didn't. Every one of them was a bridge for the breath it lay there, and some of Ivor went across it into the plate, and there was less of him to go each time. He could not have stood now without the bracket.

In Penn's hand, in the scratches on the plate, a light came up.

Thin, at first. A wick turned right down. He took the third and it came up more. On the fourth the lantern was burning in his hand, white, the light a man could read a meter by. There were two of him on the arch. Two shadows off the one lamp, one over the other and a hand apart: two of Ivor hanging off two brackets, two grey leeches dropping into two waters. A Board lamp, burning in a cut district, on no feed from anywhere. It was burning on Haldor Ivor.

"Thirty-four, gentlemen."

The clamp work had stirred the black up off the invert, and the stink had come up round them while he worked. Rotten eggs. It had been thick enough to wet his eyes. By the sixth leech Penn couldn't smell it at all, and he took that for the air clearing, the way the air clears in a flush. The oil lamp on the bracket burned steady and yellow and tall.

*Lamp's fine. Air goes bad, the lamp tells you. Lamp's fine.*

He took the seventh. The eighth had crept up onto the soft of the arm, and when it dropped the live man made a sound Penn had never heard from a grown man. Small. A child's sound.

The last of them lay on the silt with the others, and the live man's arms were bare to the elbow, white, with the cut and the salt on them. Penn hung the lantern back on his belt.

The live man coughed.

Penn had never heard him cough in the Four. Nobody had. He coughed again and his eyes were streaming, and his breath had gone quick and shallow, quick as a dog's in summer. He was stooped over the collar with his face a hand above the water. Penn was standing, as much as the Four let him stand, his head up in the crown of the arch where it always was.

"Listen." The live man got hold of Penn's coat. There was no grip in the hand. "When he lifts the plate. It comes into the pipe full. It'll come into me the same, the way it fills the pipe. And it'll not stop at full." He coughed. "I seen it at Wellow. Get me out afore the hour."

"I'll get you out."

"Afore the hour."

He let go of Penn's coat, and the hand fell away. He dropped as if shot.

## V. THE HOUR

The face first. Penn got the guernsey in one fist and Ivor's hair in the other and hauled his face up out of the Four, and it came up grey and streaming with its mouth open. Penn had to get under him. He went down on his knees in the invert, and the water came over the tops of his waders and in, onto his belly, and the cold shut on his ribs and took the breath out of him and put it back in one great gasp, down there at the water with his face where Ivor's had been. His head swam. He got up.

The Four was in his mouth. He spat it and it was still in his mouth.

He tried Ivor under the arms from the front and his hands slid. He tried the collar of the guernsey and it stretched and the face went under again. He got it up again.

He went round behind. Got his arms under Ivor's armpits from the back, and reached across the chest, and got both his hands locked on Ivor's one forearm, his dead fingers hooked over his other dead fingers. Ivor's head came back against Penn's chest. Penn bent at the knees and walked backwards.

Nothing else had held. This did.

Ivor weighed nothing in the water. The weight was in holding the face up, and in the drag of the waders and the wet wool, and in Penn's heels, which went out from under him on the silt over the brick and came back, and went again. Every few steps the crown of the arch took him on the back of the skull. Lamp-post. He stopped counting it.

Behind them the oil lamp stood on its bracket and burned, steady, yellow, getting smaller.

The Board lamp swung on his belt. It threw its white up the brick and down the water and it threw everything twice. Two arches. Two of Ivor's faces, grey. Two of Penn's arms locked on two of his. The shadows swung apart and came together.

LEVEL IV went by him twice in the doubled light.

His grip was going. He had come down the irons with two hands. He had a third of that now, if he had that, and the third was going the way the warm had gone.

"Thirty-seven!"

The shaft. He got it over his shoulder, the square of it, the leveller's lamp at the top, a little yellow on the wet irons going up.

"Mister!" It came out of him the way it had come out of him at nine, to a Board man on the stair. "Mister, hold the plate! He's down! Hold the plate, mister, he's down!"

The leveller's face came into the square at the top. The keys swung out on his chain.

"The plate goes back at the hour, Lister," the leveller called down. "That's the Board's clock, not my hand."

"He says it'll fill him, mister, he says when it comes back it'll..."

"Thirty-eight."

Something knocked in the conduit.

Once, a reach behind Penn, iron on iron inside the pipe, the way a water main knocks when some fool upstream shuts a cock too fast and the column slams against it. Then all down the length of it, knock, knock, running away up the Four toward the gauge-house and the plate. The water on the invert shivered.

Penn's teeth hurt. There was metal in them, wrong, as though he had bitten down on a nail, and there was no nail. The wall across the culvert stood a hair nearer than it had, and then went back where it belonged. Something was coming back into the pipe from a long way off.

He knew before the watch knew.

"Rope," Penn said. Then he shouted it. "Rope!"

It came down the shaft in coils and hit the water beside him.

"Hoist's dead," the leveller said. "Everything's dead. You'll want to tie it well."

The hoist had a weight-plate in its drum that made a full trap weigh like a bucket, and it drew off the main, and there was no main. Penn got the rope round Ivor's chest under the arms. His hands would not make the knot. He made it with his hands and his teeth. The knot was a bad one. It would hold or it wouldn't.

"Take him!"

The rope came tight. The leveller hauled hand over hand up top, grunting, a culvert man's shoulders under the Board's coat, and Penn got on the irons under him and got his shoulder into his backside and shoved.

Ivor came up out of the water. The waders emptied down Penn's neck. His weight came back all at once as he left the Four, the whole dead weight of him and his wet wool, and his face, blue at the lips in the water, went grey on the rope. Grey as the mortar. His head hung.

*Don't. Don't, don't, not now, not up the irons.*

Ivor's boots cleared the water.

Up top the leveller made the rope fast round the hoist post. The watch came out and opened in his hand, and he stepped away from the hatch toward the housing, and the live man hung above Penn in the shaft, turning a little on the rope.

"Forty," the leveller said, to nobody.

The plate came up. Iron off iron, four seconds of it.

The hum came back.

It came up through the irons into Penn's palms, into his wrists and his elbows and his teeth where the metal had been, and the floor came back under the whole of Southbank where it had always been. Penn hung on the irons with his cheek on the rust. He shook.

## VI. THE BOOK

They laid him on the gauge-house flags.

Flat, Ivor came back. He had gone grey on the rope and he came back flat, and Penn didn't know why and didn't care. He coughed first. He coughed up the Four, and then last night's bread, and then bile, on his side with his cheek on the stone and his eyes streaming, and then the shivering took him so hard his heels knocked on the flags.

Penn knelt by him. His hands were two pieces of wood on the ends of his arms. His back had locked across the top of the hips, and his knees were open under the torn canvas, and his eyes stung as though somebody had rubbed soap in them, and the taste of the Four sat at the back of his throat and would not be swallowed.

The oil lamp had burned steady the whole time, down there on its bracket over the water. It had burned steady over the gas.

The hum was back under all of it.

Down Southbank the street lamps came up together, the whole row to the river stairs, off the one cock in this house. The doorways emptied, one after another down the street, and the chandler's wife went in last. Penn's own window stayed dark.

Round the live man on the flags the spilt water stood in fine rings, trembling.

The Board lamp still burned on Penn's belt. The plate stood bare in its brass seat, white, and threw its two shadows across the flags.

The leveller was looking at it.

"That burned all the way up my shaft," he said, "with the Four cut. I watched it come." He held out his hand. "Give it here. That's Board property."

Penn unhooked it. The leveller turned it in his hands, and his thumb, the pink wax-burned one, went to the bezel screw and found no wax on it.

"Sine sigillo," he said, soft.

"He'd have died, Inspector."

"I don't break a Board seal, Lister. Not for a man, not for a street. Not for my own mother." The thumb went over the bare screw and back. "My father broke one."

The thumb stopped on the screw and stayed there. Then he set the lantern on the bench beside his book, and it burned there on nothing, and he sat down beside it and opened the book.

"Now then," the leveller said. "As it stands. A spawn report stands the Four down, unpaid, the pair of you and me with you, till the Company's hunters have been down and been paid by the carcass. That is one. A spawn report brings a Guild clerk into my book. A Guild clerk reads every page of a man's book, and he will want a reserve reading off every man that was in the water tonight. The reading says what it says. And a man two years short of his twenty-five is read and cocked, and his pension goes where cocked men's pensions go. That is two." He wet the nib. "And a Board seal broken, entered against Lister. That is three, and it is a term and a tariff, Lister, before your waders are paid." He tapped the nib dry on the inkwell's lip. "As it stands, none of that is entered."

On the stone the live man's hands were jumping, both of them, on their own. Penn looked at them for a long time. Ivor's eyes were shut.

"It's meat now," Penn said. "What's down there. It'll be meat in the water by morning. A melted coin does not testify."

"No," the leveller said. "The book does."

He wrote. The nib scratched in the hum, a small dry sound, and Penn could read it upside down the way he could read the brick.

> LEVEL IV. Collar clamped. Clear. In registro relatum.

"Nobody on this gang has gone live," the leveller said. "That's entered."

On the flags Ivor opened his eyes.

The leveller blotted the line and turned the book round on the bench and held out the pen.

"Second man."

Ivor's name was already in, in the leveller's hand. Above it, in the same hand, Orin Holt was marked present for the night.

Penn signed with the pen in his fist. The feeling was coming back into his fingers. It came back as pins.

The leveller took the book back and closed it and put two pats on the oilcloth. Then he picked up the Board lamp and put it inside his frock coat, against his waistcoat, and buttoned the coat over it. It burned through the serge, a white smear at his chest. Two of him stood on the gauge-house wall.

"What has been said stands said."

---

Penn climbed his own stair at the end of the maintenance hours with the hum in the treads under his boots. The cutter's notice was still on the door, the Board's wax on its corner. He went in past it.

The room was dark and warm, and smelled of milk and damp wool and the cold ashes in the grate. Willa slept on her side with her hand under her cheek and her mouth a little open, the blanket pulled up to her ear, and she didn't wake when the door went. The meter hung on the wall by the door. Brass and slate and the Board's seal on it in green wax, and the needle creeping.

The hum was in the wall. It came through the plaster a span off his ear.

Thea slept in her cradle hard against the meter wall, where the main came into the room. One fist up by her ear. Her breath went in and out, small and even. He put the back of his dead hand to the plaster above her, and it was warm, the warmest wall in the room.

He stood there a while in his wet stockings.

Then he got his forearms under the ends of the cradle, because his hands would not close, and lifted it, Thea and all. He walked it backwards across the room, bent at the neck from habit under a ceiling he could have stood straight beneath, to the wall under the window where the frost had come in on the glass in the cut. He set her down against the cold wall.

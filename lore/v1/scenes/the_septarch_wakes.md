# The Septarch Wakes

*The Crucible tiers, under the darkened sky, as the relics speak*

---

Naori had seen his father angry perhaps twice in his life, and both times it had been a quiet thing, a palm laid flat on stone, a single phlegmatic word set down like a ward-peg.

This was something else, something chthonic. This was the Septarch, Ashura's hand, sempiternal.

It came up out of Niran in the seat beside him the way night comes up out of a valley at eventide, from the bottom first, filling the low places before anyone has noticed the light is going. A blackness poured off his father's shoulders, so stygian and so absolute that the eye slid off it the way a hand slides off oiled slate. Naori felt the Aether in the tiers around them undergo a phase transition. It condensed. The way a supercooled mist hangs in still air until a single cold point touches it and the whole cloud crystallises at once into rime, the ambient Aether of the stands found his father's darkness and fell out of the air into it: loose, common, unowned Aether turning into Essence on contact, translated by sheer proximity into the inner currency of a single soul. The benches nearest them furred with a hoarfrost that was not frost. A woman three rows down gasped as the warmth went out of her breath and her lips went cyanosed.

His mother had her hand on his father's arm with fierce solicitude.

"Niran." Mira did not shout; she never shouted. Her knuckles were wan on his sleeve. "Niran, look at me. Look at me. The children are here."

His father did not look at her, held in a torpor of rage.

Naori reached, as he had reached every quotidian morning since the Telling, for the threads. He reached for the web his father had taught him to read forward, the branching filaments of what might happen next, and for the slower, sere strands of what had already been. He found nothing. Where his father sat there was only a hole in the web, a tenebrous lacuna with smooth black edges, as though somebody had taken shears to the fabric of time and cut his father out of it. No future. No past. Naori could see the futures of the woman three rows down, of his mother, of the man selling cloying cordial in the aisle. He could not see one evanescent moment of the man beside him.

He had never once feared his father. Now his knees had gone to a shaking ague, and his mouth was dry.

---

Niran rose out of the stands, inexorable.

He did not climb. The blackness lifted him, and the tiers fell away under his feet, and the wind of the dark sky moved in the long sable feathers that had opened behind his shoulders: wings of the Septarch, the hand Ashura had called back from beyond the Veil and that he had told her, then, she was not owed the whole of. Every crow in the arena's roof-trusses that had not yet fled went up in a cacophonous screaming.

He saw only the third figure, at the back like a recusant.

It stood a little apart from the others on the dark air, behind the man in incarnadine silk, with its hand raised to its own hair. It had pushed its reading-lenses up into its hair. Niran had watched that diurnal gesture ten thousand times. He had watched it across a breakfast table, across a ward-circle in brumal snow, across the long room in Kushara where the Telling was kept, and then a quarter-hour later he had watched the same man pat every pocket of his coat looking for the lenses on his own head.

"Father..."

It left him before he could stop it. It came out cracked and plangent, the voice climbing on the one word the way his voice had climbed only once before in his adult life, and he forced it back down, as he always forced it down, and it would not stay.

"Father."

No. This could not be; the thread had snapped. He had *heard* it snap, four months late, far away, a filament of his own life going slack in the dark, and he had received the loss as information because that was what a Yukari did with loss, and he had done the work in order, and he had gone back to his father's sayings and to the words on Senri's stone, and he had laid his palm flat on the rock in place of anything said. The thread had snapped. This was not, could not be, the man he had mourned with such obdurate quiet.

This was Naiser. The long dark seams of the chains still at his throat and wrists, preserved like specimens in an ossuary. The grey hair, the patina of a long life. The lenses. And under the lenses, eyes that looked at Niran with recognition and stopped there, the way a page stops at its last line.

Niran's head turned with a slow torsion toward the man in crimson.

"You." It came out of him low and quiet and absolutely flat, and all around him the condensing Aether shivered with a faint sibilance. "You monster."

He wanted to kill. The wanting was so vast and so elemental that it left no room in him for compunction. He wanted to take this thing that wore his father's face and cleave it from the fabric of the world, every thread of it, back to the place where it had been knotted on, so that nothing of the obscenity remained in the web for his son to read. And he wanted more than that. He wanted the source. He wanted the man in the crimson coat, the hand that had reached down into his father's rest and pulled, the prime author of this whole charnel chain of misery. He wanted to find the hinge in that man's life and sunder it.

The crimson deepened in his eyes to a sanguine black. The Kurotana stirred behind them, damascened in black.

---

Sodoku came up out of the lower tiers with his brother at his side and stopped at the barrier's revetment, and looked up, and did not breathe.

He had braced for many things since the corridor: for the Tenrai's assassins at every postern, for the High Council's writ of attainder, for his daughter's face. He had not braced for this. There was no stance for it. The white hair tied back for a crown, the scars, the terrible saturnine stillness of the shoulders. The old fracture-scars glowed faintly, fulvous, at his throat. Sodoku's hand had gone to his hilt without his asking it, and stayed there, recalcitrant.

He could not, by any casuistry, make it his father.

The figure on the dark air came forward through the crepuscular gloom. It did not walk; it simply moved, drifting down and forward through the gloaming of the false night until it stood a little above the barrier and a little ahead of the others, and its gold eyes found Sodoku, and found the man beside him.

"My sons."

Muken's voice. The exact voice, the one that had ended arguments in the hall at Kharven-Seat by being the last word, the knell on every quarrel.

Sodoku shook his head, slowly, once, with the lugubrious finality of a man who has looked at a lamed horse and knows it will have to be put down.

"Don't call me that."

His own voice came out of him the way it always came, slow, ferrous, each word set in its place with a silence after it. He made himself look into the gold eyes and hold there with all his sangfroid. The Moto read through the eyes, and so a Moto who meets another Moto's eyes has opened his door. He opened it with a kind of temerity and let the thing read him, all of it.

"You are not my father." A silence. "You can't be. He died."

The corridor had taken him, him and Tomuka and Ezo, in one night of perfidy. And then he and the man beside him had ridden out on the Tenrai main-branch settlement and burned it to calcined ground, every house, every granary, women and children included, because the man who had taught them both that ruin always has a ledger someone pays had been murdered, and somebody had to pay it. That ledger had been the whole of Sodoku's life since, its every rubric written in Tenrai ash. His exile, his ignominy, his name: the Scourge of the Moto. It was the price of a dead king.

And the dead king was standing on the air in front of him with refulgent gold in his eyes.

"You..." His jaw worked. Something acrid rose in his throat that he would not let out. "You have no idea what you've done. Do you."

It came out flat, without the smallest equivocation.

For the first time in years, the old intent came back to him out of desuetude. He had put it away at Kharven-Seat when he met his daughter. He had abjured it, folded it away, a blade in oilcloth at the bottom of himself, and had not unwrapped it since. It unwrapped itself now. It came up through him from the soles of his feet, a pure, cold, adamantine wish to end something, and the barrier rail under his hand frosted white and then cracked with a sound like a bone going. The people nearest him moved away with a pusillanimous haste they did not know they had chosen. It was the purest thing he had felt since the corridor, purer than grief, purer than any benison. It was murderous, and it was clean.

"Come now, children."

The man in crimson spoke from the dark air with the mild, dulcet warmth of a schoolmaster at a prize-giving. The pressure pouring off the tiers did not appear to trouble him at all; the Septarch's blackness at one end of the oval and the Sovereign of Kushara's at the other, the great twin wells of grief and refusal rising off the stands, and he stood between them with Borin Ironheart's heart in his hand as though he were standing in a vernal breeze.

"That is no way to treat your elders. After all, they have been so looking forward to meeting you."

Across the arena the Sovereign said something, low. Sodoku did not catch the words. Malphas did.

"You will try," Malphas said, quite kindly.

He turned slowly on the air, taking them all in, and his new eyes glowed in the dark, candent as coals under a bellows.

"Do you imagine I am afraid of any of you? It will take a good deal more than threats to unmake what I have started. I have been at this work since before most of you could read." He smiled. "No. Why don't we handle this in proper fashion? It would be so messy to do battle this early. The fun is only beginning. It would be a terrible pity to kill you all before the games have properly begun."

Muken was looking past Sodoku now, past his shoulder, into the stands where Sodoku's own people sat and his brother's. The gold eyes moved from face to face along the row with a slow, antediluvian patience. They rested a moment on each small face turned up toward the fuliginous sky.

"Those must be my grandchildren."

Something happened in the dead king's voice that Sodoku had never once heard in life. It softened, into a tenderness its living owner had kept under strict mortmain.

"I'm proud of you. Both of you. That you made families for yourselves." The gold eyes came back to his sons. "That you did not let what I left you make you into nothing but what I left."

Sodoku's hand tightened on the hilt; the wrappings creaked.

On the dead king's right, Rengai Zettari was looking down into the far tiers too, at the Sovereign of Kushara, in his drifting snow of scoria. The sardonic curl had gone out of his mouth. He looked, for a moment, only tired, a lassitude no living Zettari would have shown.

"You were always so happy," Rengai said. His voice was dry and quiet and it carried, the way a voice carries over a tarn at dusk. "When you were small. I never saw a boy laugh so much at so little." He tipped his head, that small, familiar motion of insouciance. "It must have been hard. Without me."

He said it as a man offers a viaticum, meaning to console. It landed like a blade.

---

Sodoku watched the third relic move.

Naiser Yukari came down through the dark air toward the black-winged man hanging over the stands with a scholar's diffident slowness, the way a man crosses a room to a sleeping child. The Septarch's blackness did not stop him. There was nothing in that revenant for it to read but the palimpsest of a man. He came to within arm's length of Niran and stopped, and lifted his hand.

He laid it on Niran's head.

Any son would have known the gesture. It was the whole of a father's joy, put into the flat of a hand. Naiser did it now exactly that way. His palm rested on the crown of his son's head, very lightly, and the black feathers behind Niran's shoulders shivered from root to tip with a fine fremitus.

"Oh, my dear son," Naiser said. His voice was a scholar's voice, mild and a little absent, a voice made for marginalia. "How much you've grown."

He took his hand back. He looked at it a moment, as though not certain whose it was.

Then his head turned, and his gaze went past Niran to the woman who had come down out of the stands onto the arena floor with her light coruscating off her like sparks off a grinding wheel, and whose blade he had turned aside a moment before.

"I don't want to kill you," Naiser said to her.

He turned back to Niran. His eyes, behind the lenses he did not need, were wet.

"I don't want to kill you."

---

Muken looked at his two sons across the plumbeous dark.

"You know what you have to do."

He said it the way he had said everything that mattered, rarely and with sepulchral finality, and then he let the silence carry the verdict for a while before he went on.

"Even if it is hard for you to accept. My sons. We are no longer on the same side." The gold eyes did not waver. "I will kill you if I have to."

Sodoku said nothing; there was no apologia in him that wanted saying.

Rengai had not spoken again. He stood in his ash with his mouth working once, as though he were chewing on something rancid that would not go down, and when he finally spoke the words came out of him slowly, one at a time, each one seeming to cost him. Sodoku saw his body flinch from them. The ash around him thickened into a dry, astringent storm. The red of his one eye brightened. Every line of that lean, scarred body leaned away from what his mouth was doing, the way a hand pulls back from an incandescent stove before the mind has registered the heat.

"I'm sorry, my son," Rengai said. "But to make sure of this new order..."

The ash spun. His jaw locked.

"There can only be you. Or me."

---

Malphas began to laugh.

He could not help it; it came up out of the new lungs in a long rich peal, a tintinnabulation of pure delight, and it rolled out across the dark Crucible and over the tiers. They were magnificent, an encomium written in living grief. All of them. The dead king and his sons. The ash-son and the ash-father. The Yukari and his Yukari. Every thread he had knotted was humming in the air in one harmonic, taut with grief and recognition and refusal, and every one of them ran back to his hand. He had never heard anything so beautiful, not in all his immemorial work.

The relics came to him in their cerements of borrowed flesh. Naiser drifted back from the black-winged man, Muken from the barrier, Rengai from his storm of ash, and they took their places at his sides on the dark air like mourners at exequies.

He looked down at Borin's heart.

It lay in his left palm, still now, its last stroke spent, the great Dawi muscle gone slack and livid. He closed his hand on it. He let Dissolution into his fingers and the heart went to powder in his grip: muscle and valve and the thick fibrous rings of the great vessels, all of it unbound in an instant into a fine, friable ash, which ran out between his fingers and fell away through the dark air in a long thin stream toward the floor of the Crucible like the sand from the bottom of a glass. The tiers made a sound, a low threnody of horror. He let it fall.

Then he breathed in.

He drew the air of the Crucible down into the Zettari lungs, and with it, through the Yukari nerves, he drew in everything else. The threads of fate, every one of them in the whole arena: the futures of thousands, a susurrus. The spirit world laid over the physical like a second skin of opalescent glass. All of it went into him, into the vast vat of his cupidity, and in that vat it was all one thing, all matter for transmutation, all mash for the ferment.

He breathed out.

The floor of the Crucible split.

It went with a long grinding roar from one end of the oval to the other, a fissure opening down the middle of the arena floor and running out under the barriers into the foundations of the tiers, and the whole raked oval of cyclopean stone shuddered on its bed like a ship striking a reef. Then the rot came up, rank as a tumulus opened in summer.

It came up out of the fissure in geysers. Black Ground, the floor of the world gone to ferment, a black, bubbling, mephitic muck that stank of rotten eggs and swelled like rising dough, and it did not ooze. It *erupted*. Great columns of it shot up out of the cracks and arched over the barriers and came down into the stands in a putrescent rain, and where it touched it ate.

A man in the lower tiers raised his arm against it. The muck took the arm to the elbow. Malphas watched the flesh go: the skin sloughing grey, the muscle under it slumping to a necrotic slurry, the bones of the forearm showing through for an instant like the ribs of a sunken boat before they too went soft and fell. The man looked at the stump, at the white gristle in the black. He did not scream at once. Then he screamed.

The stands broke. People went over benches, over each other, the black rain coming down on them, the crowd pouring up the stairways toward the gates in a precipitate crushing tide. Spikes of crystal grew up out of the puddles where the rot had landed, hollow and gleaming, and whomever they transfixed, the wound went to gangrene before the victim had finished falling.

One geyser rose higher than the rest. It climbed out of the fissure in a black, unctuous arch over the northern tiers, toward the section where the Yukari had been sitting, where the woman with white knuckles still stood with her two children behind her and her face turned up, and it bent at the top of its arc, as a thing bends when it has been aimed.

Malphas watched the black-winged man see it.

---

Niran went down.

He did not decide to. The wings decided. They folded back along his spine and drove, and the air of the dark sky cracked under them, and he careened out of the sky like a stooping hawk. The condensed Aether streamed off the trailing edges of the feathers in long vortices, a fulgurant wake. He went down so fast that the wind tore the breath from his mouth and the tiers smeared into one vertiginous grey wall beside him, and below him, very small, getting larger, was his wife's face turned up toward the black arch coming down on her.

He reached the arch before it reached her.

He did not strike it. There was no time to strike it. He went through the space between the arch and his family with his wings spread to their whole span, and the wind of his passage, the great percussive downdraught of a dive pulled out at the last instant, tore the geyser sideways. The black rain came down across the empty benches to the left of where Mira stood, and sizzled there, and ate the wood to mouldering pulp.

He had a heartbeat. Less. He had turned his head to find her face, to see that it was whole, and he saw it, white and whole and staring at him, and then the world hit him from the side.

Naiser.

The dead man had come down after him through the dark without a sound, and he did not strike with a hand or a blade; he slewed his whole body into his son. The psychic force that had turned aside the Yuno woman's sword came off him in a dense, concussive wall and he drove it into Niran's ribs like a battering ram, and the two of them went tumbling sideways through the air over the northern tiers.

Niran twisted. Even then, even as the impact punched the air from his lungs, the old drills, annealed into him over thirty years, took him, and he turned in the air, wings snapping half-open to catch himself, legs coming round under him. It was not enough. The dead man had all the weight of a cadaverous body that did not care whether it broke, and he did not let go. They came down together into the rot.

The black ground took Niran across the back and shoulders, as though he were being flensed.

It burned. It burned like lye and like frost at once, the muck clinging to his wings, eating into the feathers, and he felt the Septarch's blackness rise against it at every point of contact, the condensed Essence of his soul grinding against the ferment like two millstones turning the wrong way. He felt the rot try to enter him, and the dark refuse it with a refractory violence. Feathers came away in sodden, carrion-black clumps. The skin of his shoulders blistered and wept sanies.

And still they were moving, because the dead man had not let go, and the momentum of the collision carried them both out of the puddle of rot and on, and they smashed together into the stands, into the empty benches of the northern tiers where the crowd had already fled, splintering three rows of seats into kindling and gouging a long furrow through the stone of the tier beneath, and came to rest at the bottom of it in a talus of broken wood and grit.

Niran lay on his back with his father's weight on his chest.

He could feel the hand that had rested on his hair a moment before. It was pressing him down now, flat against his sternum, as gently as it had touched him, with the whole psychic force of a dead man behind it. Above him, the face he had mourned at the obsequies was looking down at him. The lenses had slipped from the grey hair. They lay on Niran's chest, beside the pouch that rode against his sternum, warm and unburned.

"I don't want to," Naiser said again, from very far away.

---

Sodoku saw his father raise his hand.

It was the gesture of a king calling for a cup, with all the hauteur of the north, small and certain, palm up, the fingers half-curled. Muken held it there on the dark air above the ruin of the Crucible, beside the man in crimson, with the miasma of the geysers still roaring up out of the fissure below him and the crowd screaming up the stairways, and he did not look away from his sons.

And the sky answered.

It began a long way off. Sodoku felt it before he saw it, the way a man feels thunder through the soles of his boots before he hears it, a deep tremor running through the world from the south and east, from the direction of the Empire. He knew the direction. Every Moto knew the direction. The blade had hung there, in the Empire's keeping, since the year the king fell, a trophy and a hostage in one, half a world away.

Hundreds of thousands of miles. It did not matter.

Sodoku felt it come. The Plane of Fate trembled like a struck web, and through the tremor, threading across the whole span of the world in less than a breath, something passed that was not a thing travelling. The blade did not fly. Nothing so heavy could have crossed so far so fast and left the air of the world unburned. It translated. Its iron and its edge, its weight and its temper, its whole physical matter, came apart into the threads of the Plane of Fate, each grain of it, by a kind of tunnelling, unravelled into a strand of probability, a chance of being here instead of there. The chance that it hung on its pegs in the Empire's vault thinned, and thinned, while the chance that it lay in its master's palm swelled to meet it, the way water finds its level between two linked cisterns. It levelled. For one instant the king's blade was nowhere and everywhere along a single line drawn across the world, a superposition of pure possibility, humming.

The sky over the Crucible split open along that line.

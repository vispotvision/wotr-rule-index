# The Stands Before the Bell — Aetherion Arena

Sodoku had decided to travel with Bram and Edward, and Bram and Edward, somewhere on the road, had acquired Darius.

He did not understand how the four of them had come to be one party, and had stopped asking. He had tried to work it back on the coach south, and given up. It must have happened some time after the war with Kujo, in the months when men who had stood on the same field kept finding reasons to stand near each other again, and nobody said why. Bram rode with his shield slung at his back though no one in the south was going to hit him. Edward kept a ledger on his knee and wrote in it at every stop, the cost of the fodder, the cost of the rooms, the hour they left and the hour they arrived. Darius said little and took up most of the bench. Sodoku watched the country go from frost to mud to green through the coach window and kept his own counsel, which was what he did with most things.

They had come in under the Academy's gate the evening before, with the lamps lit along the conduit trestles and the draw-hum thick in the air like the drone of a hive in a wall. Now it was morning, the morning of it, and the Crucible Field was filling.

The arena did not look like much. That was the first thing Sodoku noticed, and he noticed it because he had expected otherwise. A great bowl of tiered grey stone, open to a pale sky, eight entrances spaced round its circumference like the hours on a clock face. The floor was pale sand, raked smooth. A thin line of silver-red ran round the edge of the ring, no wider than a thread, and the sand inside it lay as ordinary as a riding school's. He looked at that line a long moment. He had stood on ground with law laid into it before, and he knew the feel of a floor that had been told what to do. This one had been told a great deal. It said nothing about it.

The stands were already loud. Families had come up from four quarters of the world for this, and they had brought their noise with them: a Mahuo grandmother arguing with an usher in a voice that carried three tiers, a cluster of lowland merchants passing round a paper twist of sugared almonds, a boy somewhere selling hot chestnuts out of a brazier on a pole and burning his fingers on them and swearing about it. Somebody had brought a dog. Somebody else was objecting to the dog.

Yoko had come by another road, and she had not come alone.

Sodoku saw her ears first, as he always did. They came up the eastern stair above the crowd, red-gold and pricked forward, turning a little at each step toward whatever sound interested them, and then the rest of her, the long coat and the narrow face and the tail held low and still behind her in a crowd, because a tail in a crowd gets stepped on. She had her nose up. She would have known he was in the bowl before she reached the top step. She did not look for him. She did not have to.

Beside her walked the heir to the Empire of Whitemere.

Kaiyo Moto was thirteen and carried herself like a woman of thirty who had been handed an empire and told to keep it clean. She had her brother's skin, the deep Moto brown, and the Moto white in her hair. Where his hung loose and unbrushed to his shoulders hers was pulled back hard and bound at the nape in a court knot, every strand of it accounted for. She was a head shorter than her twin. She had their father's frame, close-built and economical, the frame of a fighter made by technique rather than by size. Her travelling coat was Whitemere grey, cut in the court fashion, high at the collar and fitted close through the shoulders, and there was dust on the hem of it from a road she had plainly not enjoyed. Her eyes were wide open. Everything her brother's face kept shut, hers kept awake. She came up out of the eastern stair, swept the whole bowl once with them, and found what she had come for in the time it took her to breathe out.

She was looking for her twin brother. Of course he was asleep on the bleachers.

Rikudoku had stretched himself out along the third tier of the southern stand as if it were a bed laid on for him, all six foot three of him, one arm over his face and one long leg hanging off the edge of the bench. His Academy coat was unbuttoned and rucked up under his back. His white hair, black at the tips, had spread out across the stone round his head, and there was lint in it, and a dry beech leaf, and what looked like a fragment of straw from somewhere nobody would ever learn. He had fallen asleep a few hours before the tournament, and he had done it completely. His chest rose and fell. A first-year two benches down had been watching him for the last quarter-hour with an expression of deep spiritual unease.

Kaiyo walked down the tiers to him without hurry. The crowd let her through without knowing why. She stood over him a moment, looking down, and then she crouched, and put her fingers into his hair, and began to pick the lint out of it.

She did it the way their mother groomed, quick and exact, a strand at a time. The leaf came out. The straw came out. A burr she had to work at with a thumbnail. He did not stir.

"Wake up," Kaiyo said. "Wakey, wakey, idiot." She flicked the burr away. Her voice dropped, so only he would have heard it, had he been awake to hear anything. "It's me."

He breathed in, and out, and did not wake.

---

Sodoku had come down the western stair to the rail of the second tier, where the view of the ring was clean, and was looking for Sonzai.

He did not see him. He looked along every entrance in turn, the eight of them, and along the shaded rows under the overhang where a man who did not want to be counted would sit, and along the high rim where a man who wanted to count everyone else would stand. Nothing. That did not mean Sonzai was not there. It meant only that Sodoku had not found him, which with Sonzai was a different thing.

He turned his head a little toward Edward, who had settled on the bench beside him with his ledger closed on his knee for once and his hands folded on top of it.

"I heard your nephew draws Xhem's boy in the first round," Sodoku said. "Taesyn." He kept his eyes on the silver-red line round the ring while he spoke, as though it might answer instead. "Your nephew trains under Darius."

Edward said nothing at once. Darius, two seats down, said nothing at all, and went on saying it with the whole of his considerable weight.

"What do you make of it," Sodoku said.

Edward took a breath through his nose and let it out slowly, the way he did before giving a figure to a council that was not going to like it. "I make it a bad draw for a first round," he said. "For both of them. Two boys who have each been told all their lives they are the most dangerous thing in any room, put in the same room before lunch." He tapped the closed ledger once with a finger. "I don't know enough about Xhem's boy to price it. I know my nephew hits harder than he thinks, and thinks less than he should, and Darius has spent a year trying to reverse those two numbers." He glanced past Sodoku, at the big man down the bench, and back. "I would not put money on it. I would put money on it lasting longer than either of their teachers would like."

Sodoku considered that, and nodded once, slowly, which from him was a long speech.

---

Niran was sitting a tier below the Greymane bench, on the shaded side, with Mira.

Mira had the children. She always had the children; it was a thing her hands did without consulting her. Neraveth sat on her right, swinging his heels against the stone and eating something sticky out of a paper cone with great concentration, and every so often Mira's hand went over without looking and steered the cone back upright before it emptied into his lap. Akira sat on her left, close against her side, with her face turned up toward the open sky as if it were a lamp she could feel on her skin.

Akira could not see the ring. She had never seen any ring. She sat as she always sat in a crowd, very still, her head tilting a little this way and that as the noises reached her: the chestnut-seller's swearing, the dog, a gong being tested somewhere below the stands and stopped halfway through its note. She knew where the ring was by the shape of the sound round it. She knew where her brother was not, yet.

She tipped her face up toward her mother, smiling, and then turned it, unerringly, toward her father's voice where he sat murmuring something to Mira about the gates.

"I can't wait to see big brother fight," Akira said. "He's so awesome." She bounced. "He's almost as cool as you, Dad!"

Niran looked down at her.

Something happened in his face that he did not let go any further than his face. He put out his hand and rubbed her head, slowly, his palm flat over her crown and his fingers moving through her hair, and it was the exact same gesture Naori used on her, the same weight, the same unhurried circle, so that Akira went still under it and smiled wider and said nothing, because she knew whose hand that was in the habit of doing it, and whose it had been first.

"You're right," Niran said. "He is extremely talented." He left his hand where it was a moment longer. "But so are the others in this tournament. Every one of them. It'll be interesting to see how he matches up."

"He'll win," Akira said, with total serenity.

"He might," Niran said. "That's what makes it worth watching."

Mira, without looking, righted Neraveth's paper cone again.

---

Naori was not in the stands. He was sitting alone in one of the waiting rooms behind the northern entrance, a bare stone cell with a bench, a jug of water and a slit window high in the wall, meditating before the tournament.

He had his back straight against the cold of the wall and his hands open on his knees. The crowd came through the stone as a long low weather of noise, rising and falling, and he let it rise and fall and did not follow it. He let the gong-tester's broken note come and go. Somewhere above him, through a tier of masonry, his sister was saying his name to someone with enormous confidence. He did not hear that. He would have smiled if he had.

---

In the practice area under the eastern stand, Xanelor simply stood still.

It was a long low hall with a sanded floor and straw targets down one wall and rack after rack of blunted practice weapons, and every other fighter who had come through it that morning had used it the way practice halls are meant to be used. A Mahou girl was working the same three-step entry against a post over and over until the post rang. Two boys in Accord colours sparred and called the touches. Someone was shooting at the far targets, and the arrows went in with a sound like a fist into a sack of meal.

Xanelor stood in the middle of the floor with his weight even on both feet and his arms at his sides and did nothing at all.

His forearms were bound from wrist to elbow in clean linen under the sleeves. He did not look at them. He was going to try something new in this tournament, and it was not a move. He was not drilling some new form into his shoulders. He was not rehearsing a sequence. He stood with his eyes half open on the sand and let the ring build itself behind them, exactly as it was: the pale sand, the eight entrances, the silver-red thread at the boundary, the open sky and the angle of the sun on it at this hour and at the hour after. The weight of the air. The sound a body makes crossing sand at a run, and where the eastern stand's shadow would lie by noon, and how fast it would move after. All of it, as it was, and himself inside it, and nothing added.

The girl at the post looked over at him twice. The second time she stopped, and watched him for a while, frowning, and then went back to her post and hit it harder than before.

---

Rikudoku finally woke.

He did it by degrees, the way a cellar floods. One leg drew up. The arm came down off his face, and his eyes opened, both of them, about a third of the way, which was as far as they ever opened, and found his sister's face hanging over him upside down, and stayed on it while the rest of him caught up.

"Wuh..." His jaw worked. "Oouuuu." He blinked, very slowly, once. "Kaiyo." Another blink. "Guess you finally made it." He yawned, enormously, the whole long length of him stretching along the bench until his heels knocked the tier below and someone down there said *hey*. He did not take his eyes off her through any of it. "I'll try to win," he said. "Or whatever."

Kaiyo had the scolding ready, and had since the eastern gate. It was a good scolding, built on the road, with a beginning and a middle and a section on what their mother had said about his letters, and it was in her mouth and behind her teeth.

She had missed her brother.

She let it go. She grabbed his face instead, both hands, a cheek in each, and pinched, and pulled them out sideways from his face until he looked like a startled frog.

"You'd better wake up before the tournament," she said. "Wouldn't want you falling asleep during it."

"OUCH!" Rikudoku came up off the bench all at once, six foot three of him unfolding at a speed nobody in the southern stand had believed he owned. "THAT HURTS!" He pulled free and sat clutching both cheeks, rubbing them, his eyes for one moment more open than anyone at the Academy had ever seen them. "That *hurts*, Kaiyo. What is wrong with you."

"Plenty," Kaiyo said, and sat down beside him, and straightened his coat.

Up on the eastern stair, Yoko's ears had turned toward the noise. Her tail moved once behind her, a single slow sweep, and was still again.

---

Bram looked down the tiers at his son.

Lance was sitting with Emilia near the rail at the front of the stand, the two of them side by side and a hand's width apart, the way children sit when they have been friends long enough to stop needing to touch to prove it. Emilia was talking. She was always talking. Her hands moved as she did it, sketching something in the air that might have been a working or might have been a bird. Lance was not talking. He sat with his elbows on his knees and his eyes on the pale sand of the ring, on the thin silver-red line, on the eight entrances, as if he were reading terrain for a shield-wall.

He had Bram's brow. He had his great-grandfather's jaw. Bram watched him and saw Godfrey's face coming up through the boy's like a stone coming up through a field after frost.

Lance did not say anything. He did not need to. He knew this was his chance and Emilia's both, the one chance, to stand on that sand in front of every family in four quarters of the world and prove that the two of them were Class X material, and that nobody had put them there by mistake.

Below the stands, the gong sounded again, and this time it was not a test, and it finished its note.

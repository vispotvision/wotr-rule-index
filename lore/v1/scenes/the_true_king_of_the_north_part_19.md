# The True King of the North, Part 19

Sonzai’s mouth tightened.

“Annoying.”

The word fogged faintly in the cold. Footsteps approached behind him. He knew who they belonged to without turning.

Selvara had a different sound now that he knew what she was. Or perhaps he only imagined she did. A softer weight than a human woman should possess, movement almost too controlled, pauses where ordinary people allowed their clothing to rustle. The Blood Hound listened to her cross the garden and wondered how many signs had been there from the beginning that he had refused to arrange correctly.

She came close. Too close. Her breath touched his ear before her voice did.

“I’m glad you’re keeping my secret.”

Sonzai’s eyes narrowed.

Her arms slipped around him from behind, crossing over the front of his cuirass as though the revelation inside his old room had somehow earned her greater familiarity rather than significantly less. Selvara pressed herself against his back, cheek near his shoulder, completely comfortable inside the anger rolling from him.

And that anger responded. Not toward her. Through her.

Sonzai felt it again, that subtle theft, that feeding. His irritation sharpened and her presence seemed to strengthen beneath it, as though his emotional heat were warmth after a long winter.

His hand closed around her wrist.

Selvara had barely enough time to register the movement before Sonzai peeled her arms from around him and shoved her away. Not with enough force to hurt her, but with enough to establish several feet of distance immediately.

“Don’t.”

One word. The garden became very quiet.

Sonzai turned fully toward her, and there was none of the uncertain awkwardness she had managed to drag from him on the ice. That Sonzai had been annoyed by a beautiful stranger refusing to leave his shelter. This Sonzai knew exactly what stood in front of him.

“You being alive in this garden does not mean I trust you.”

His purple eyes traveled over her face.

“And me not telling my brother does not make us conspirators.”

The anger remained under every syllable, controlled but unmistakable. He could feel Calamity moving with it, answering emotional pressure the way it always had. That alone made looking at her more difficult now. She was connected to the source of the thing that had invaded his childhood. Connected by blood.

“You told me what you are because you decided I should know.” His jaw hardened. “I decided no one else needs to know yet. Those are separate decisions.”

Selvara remained where he had pushed her, watching him.

Sonzai stepped closer by one pace, though not enough to surrender the distance he had created.

“If Malakar sent you, I will know.”

Another step.

“If you are lying about why you were on that ice, I will know.”

His eyes sharpened further.

“And if you use what he put inside me against my family, Kusho, being his daughter will become the least interesting thing about you.”

There was no theatrical menace in it. Sonzai spoke the way a commander stated weather conditions before a march. Calmly. Precisely. As fact.

Then his gaze lowered briefly toward the place her arms had been around him.

“And stop hugging me.”

That carried considerably more irritation than the threat had.

Sonzai turned from her and continued down the garden path, boots grinding lightly over frost. He still did not call for Sodoku. He still did not summon guards. He still did not expose her.

Selvara’s secret remained exactly where she had placed it.

With him.

And that, more than anything she had revealed in his old room, was beginning to bother the Blood Hound.

"Is it wrong to not allow them to have their own beliefs?" She asked Sodoku, her eyes looking with confusion on Lamberts request. "That feels quite cruel to determine how the beliefs of a selected group will go." She said, her arms crossed as she directed her stern gaze to Lambert. The same kind a woman would give in disappointment for the actions of a man's foolish actions. Her head shook softly.

"Would it not come off as aggression if the house decided how the Church would believe? Is that not the same action that Malakar did when he tortured our family? Would you really want to tell a devoted priest that his beliefs are nothing but delusions?" She asked, her eyebrow raised as she stepped in front of Sodoku now getting close, too close for comfort. Her disapproval shining through the glare in her eyes as she kept the tension high between them.

"I'd just thought I'd point out my concerns, because it seems a little bold of us to do that." She said, her eyes shifting back to Sodoku now as she moved away from Lambert, a sight leaving her lips as she shook her head softly.

"I'm not hungry, and besides you'd probably poison my food if I even said it was good." She said, her gaze shifting over to Yoko's as she looked over at her with a softer gaze, this wasn't pity, but something more curious. "I mean seriously... you changed so much... what happened to the more cheerful side of you, Yoko?" She asked her, seemingly unappreciative of her much more serious and blunter tone compared to Emira's laid-back and teasing one.

Rye and pork fat and the iron smell of a range plate that had been hot for six hours.

Underneath it the sourness of the room itself, wet wool on the drying rail giving off eleven days of the polar shelf, and under that the mineral cold coming up through the flags where the floor met the north wall and would not be warmed by anything. Somebody in the scullery was scraping a pot with a wooden spoon and had been for a while. Out in the passage two of Lambert's people were arguing about a door hinge. A cart went past on the low street and the wheels found every rut.

The food went down the table on a plank.

Rye, the heel end, four days old and cut thick. Salt pork boiled soft and grey with a rind on it that had gone translucent. A dish of onions fried in the pork fat until the edges went black. A pot of hot water with rinds in it, which is what the north calls broth when it is being honest. No butter. No greens, because there were none in the second month. No rice.

Sodoku looked at the plank for a moment longer than the plank required.

Eleven years ago there had been rice on this kingdom's high table twice a week, hauled up the corridor at a cost that made the Thornwalls audibly unhappy, and every grain of it had been a sentence spoken in a language the Banner Houses could not read: *we came from somewhere, and we can still reach it.* His father had never once said that out loud. He had simply eaten rice in a country that grew none and let six houses watch him do it.

He stood up from the bench.

The room noticed and did not understand. Yoko's ears came around. Garret stopped with a knife halfway to a rind. Lambert set his cup down without a sound, which was itself a kind of comment.

Sodoku put his feet together and bowed.

It took under two minutes. Fourteen inclinations, shallow, from the waist, each one held for about the length of a breath and released without ceremony, in an order nobody at that table could have recited. He did not speak while he did it. There was nothing to speak. A bow contains no request, and a man who adds one to it has stopped bowing and started bargaining, and his mother had explained that to him exactly once, in a corridor, while she was on her way somewhere else.

On the fourteenth, Hild bowed too.

Half a beat behind him. Same depth. Same release.

Sodoku sat down and picked up his bread and could make nothing of it at all. She had not copied the first thirteen. She had copied the last one, and she had copied it the way you copy something you have watched before and never been told the name of, and there was nobody in this ruined city who should have been able to teach her that, and he did not ask.

*He is going to think Edward taught me.* Hild pulled the bench in with her heels. *Edward did not teach me. I am not going to say who did, because the last time I said a true thing at this table in front of six people, Pietro used it.*

"Heresy is not a matter of taste."

Sodoku said it to his bread, and then he raised his head, and the room understood that the conversation had started some time before the sentence.

"I will not accept heretics of any variety and I am not going to pretend that position is fashionable. Do not forget yourself, Emira. You were not raised by our parents. That is not an insult and it is not your fault and I have never once held it against you, and it means there are things you were never in the room for. I was in the room. I intend to uphold what was put into me there."

He tore the heel and gave half of it to his daughter without looking at her.

"Look at the history and stop looking at the manners. We were successful because we were correct, and correctness and piety are two separate trades. Four thousand years the world brought its questions to my family and went away with answers that held, and a contract sealed at the white shelf held anywhere, and a war stopped at the white shelf because both sides had already been told how it ended. That is the whole of the record. We did not conquer anyone. We did not need to. A polity that requires the Moto's determination in order to function has already been annexed and simply has not been told."

Lambert's thumb moved once against his index finger.

"And the Works are not a preference," Sodoku said. "Fourteen sovereigns hold fourteen meanings and every one of them exists to stop the other thirteen from eating the world. Take one out and address it alone and you have said, out loud, in the posture of devotion, that its principle runs without the others. That is a false statement about the structure of reality. Call it what it is. The offence is inaccuracy. I have no interest in whether a man's heart is in the right place while he is being wrong about the load-bearing member."

He ate. He chewed and swallowed and set the bread down.

"And do not put Malakar's name next to mine at this table."

The scraping in the scullery had stopped.

"Malakar broke the continents. My family held the ground he broke. Understand what that sentence costs before you repeat it back to me. The Kōkan stayed. Every other line went north with their registers and their retainers and whoever they could carry, and the Kōkan stayed with everybody who would not leave, and they put a frame around a landmass and held it while the world came apart, and they bought the crossing with their own bodies and named the working nothing at all. Every Moto drawing breath in this room and in this kingdom and in the Tenrai's houses is alive on time those people purchased. Including the ones who called the working unlawful. Including me."

Somebody at the far end of the table reached for the rind-water and thought better of it and put their hand back in their lap.

"And you want to know whether we did to anyone what he did to us. Ask it plainly next time and I will answer it plainly, because the answer is yes."

He said it without any change in pitch at all.

"At the shelf the house read children at delivery. It established what they carried, what their line owed, and what lawful function they were fitted to perform, and then it put the determination into the body. Covenant Scar. You have one of the shared arts sitting in your own kit and nobody ever told you what it was built to do at scale. A scarred labourer who walked off an assigned function was not hunted. He was simply not recognised anywhere else, by anyone, ever again, because recognition was the family trade and the family had declined to extend it. That is what we did. For four thousand years. And the house would have been offended to hear it called slavery and the offence would have been sincere, because every single determination was accurate."

He let that stand in the room with the pork fat and the wet wool.

"A man told he is unfree by a tyrant has a grievance. A man told he is unfree by an instrument that has never once been wrong has a diagnosis. I know exactly what my family is. I am not defending the Long Reading. I am telling you that Malakar broke a world and my line held the piece of it he broke, and those are different acts, and the fact that both of them left bodies does not make them the same ledger."

He turned his hand over on the table.

"Four thousand years of Reading soaked into that shelf and the shelf is still there and it is still warm. That is what testimony is. That is where it came from. It is not a doctrine somebody had in a library. It is residue. It is what is left when a house spends four millennia determining what things are on one piece of ground and then dies on it."

Yoko had put her hand on Emira's shoulder somewhere in the middle of that, and Sodoku registered the gesture and filed it as a side being taken.

"So no. I will not be gracious about it. There is an order sitting on that shelf that files my mother's rites under a heading she would not have recognised and consults one of the fourteen and calls the other thirteen an assortment, and it does this on ground my blood paid for, and it has never once been asked to show the deed. I refuse to be laughed at over it. I will not have this name dragged through the mud by a man in clean robes who has never gone a winter on two thirds."

He picked the bread back up.

"That is all of it. Eat."

Lambert let the silence sit for three full seconds, which was his way of declining to be hurried.

"You should not be so poised, my lord."

The old man turned his cup a quarter turn on the boards.

"That is not a rebuke. It is a description, and you may take it or leave it standing where I put it. Poise is what a man has when he already knows the shape of the room he is walking into. You are about to walk into rooms whose shape has changed while you were away, and I would rather you went in slightly off balance and looking than upright and certain."

*He is his father to the bone and I do not know whether to be relieved or to start counting.*

"I agree with your sister on the substance and I will say so plainly, because agreeing with you quietly and disagreeing in the corridor afterward is a habit I have watched destroy three men in this hall." Lambert's burned hand flattened on his knee. "We cannot be rude. We cannot be ill-mannered. Not because manners are a virtue, but because we are, at this moment, three thousand two hundred people on two thirds of a ration asking strangers for grain, and there is no version of that conversation that survives us opening it by informing the other party that his religion is a category error."

"I did not say I would open with it."

"You said it here, at a table, in front of nine people who will repeat it, in a house with a scullery." Lambert did not raise his voice at all. "Understand something about those people, your highness. Most of them do not know. The tenancy, the shelf, the working, what the Kōkan did and what it bought. It is not withheld from them out of malice. It was simply never written into a document anyone outside the house has ever been permitted to read. You are proposing to be furious with a priest for failing to know a thing your own family has spent eleven hundred years declining to publish."

Sodoku's jaw set and he did not answer immediately.

"Be frugal," Lambert said. "In grain, in coin, and in the number of people you tell they are wrong. All three ledgers run out."

The onions had gone cold in the fat. Somebody down the table took the last of them anyway.

"The priest," Yoko said.

She had not moved her hand off Emira's shoulder.

"I want to be exact about him, because exactness is the only thing I am useful for at a table." Her tail moved once against the bench and stilled. "If he is devoted, then his devotion has a shape, and the shape has consequences, and one of the consequences is that a granary in the south stays shut while children in this city go to bed on a rind. I have no argument with what he believes. I have an argument with what his belief costs, and I would like somebody to explain to me at what point a man's convictions become expensive enough that we are permitted to call them a problem." A pause. "That is a real question. I do not have the answer to it. I have been carrying it since the shelf."

She looked down the table.

"And I would never poison you."

Sodoku closed his eyes for a moment.

"I want that said, because it was said the other way and I do not want it left standing," Yoko went on. "I would not poison your food. I would not poison anyone's food. I understand it was a joke. I am telling you plainly anyway, because we are in serious times and I have found that jokes about poison have a way of being remembered as something other than jokes by people who were not in the room."

*She thinks I have gone cold. I have not gone cold. I have a son on the third sledge and a city of three thousand two hundred and a husband who bows fourteen times to nobody, and cheerfulness was a thing I could afford when the worst outcome available to me was embarrassment.*

"You are not a child," Yoko said. "That is the whole of it. I am not asking you to stop being who you are. I am asking you to accept the nature of the world we walked into, because it does not adjust for anybody and it has never once made an exception for good intentions."

Hild had been eating steadily through the entire exchange, working through her ration with the mechanical thoroughness of somebody who had been told to finish it and had learned it was faster to comply.

She put the crust down.

"Do they have grain?"

The table went quiet in a different way.

"The Holy Sea," she said. "Does it have grain, or does it have ships. Because Edward says it is poor in grain, and if it is poor in grain then all of this is an argument about a man's beliefs when what we need is somebody's boats." She wiped her hand on her mail hem, which was a habit somebody was going to have to break her of. "You can be right about the shelf and about the working and about all fourteen of them and it will not put one bushel through the north gate. So. Grain, or ships. Which is it, and who signs for it."

Lambert set his cup down again.

*Eleven years old. Eleven. Her father spent four minutes on four thousand years of history and she stripped it down to a manifest question in nine words, and I did not teach her that either.*

"Ships," Lambert said. "And grain moving through the Malani system that they do not grow and do not own, which they can broker and will not gift. Somebody signs for it in Kushara or nobody signs for it at all."

"Then that is the letter." Hild picked her crust back up. "The rest is for after we have eaten this winter."

Sodoku looked at his daughter for a long moment.

The frost had not come up off her at all through any of it, not at one point in four minutes of her father telling a room that a house had died on a shelf. Whatever had been leaking out of her at the gate had gone somewhere and shut, and he had no idea where a child learns to do that, and he was not going to find out by asking her at a table.

He reached across, took the pot of rind-water, and filled her cup before his own.

Sonzai had eaten very little.

He sat farther down the table with one forearm beside the untouched broth, crimson hair falling around a face that had remained increasingly unimpressed as the conversation stretched from heresy to the Fourteen, from the Long Reading to the white shelf, and eventually to whether history justified the shape of the present. Most of it he allowed to pass without comment. Religion had never interested him enough to become angry over someone practicing it incorrectly, and dead institutions only mattered to him when their corpses were still obstructing something useful. Grain mattered. Stannvaard mattered. Three thousand two hundred people eating two-thirds rations mattered. Those were problems with bodies attached to them.

Malakar was different.

The first time Sodoku spoke the name, Sonzai’s eyes shifted sideways automatically, not toward anyone in particular, but toward the empty space beside the room’s western doorway before he caught himself. The movement was small enough that most of the table would have dismissed it as another one of the Blood Hound’s constant environmental checks. It was not. Malakar had been dead for years, but the name had acquired new weight in the last few hours, enough that hearing it now struck against information Sonzai was deliberately carrying alone.

His daughter.

The thought returned immediately.

He remembered Selvara standing in his abandoned room. Remembered bone replacing beauty, the additional arms unfolding, the tail, the red-white anatomy that had made argument unnecessary. He remembered the calm way she had admitted whose blood she carried and the kiss she had left on his cheek afterward as if Malakar’s name were not one of the few names capable of reaching backward through Sonzai’s adulthood and putting hands around the throat of the child he had once been.

Malakar had touched his Soul Crystal before Sonzai had been old enough to understand what consent meant. Calamity remained because of it. Every time his brother said the demon’s name, some part of Sonzai’s attention sharpened involuntarily.

Nobody at the table knew why. He intended to keep it that way.

By the time Hild reduced several minutes of philosophy into the far more useful question of whether the Holy Sea possessed grain or ships, Sonzai finally looked fully engaged. His purple eyes settled on his niece for a moment, and something near approval entered the severity of his expression. Eleven years old, and she had already learned that a debate which did not alter a manifest was something that could be resumed after winter.

“We are using a remarkable amount of breath on priests and dead people while the Dawi are the ones presently holding everyone’s throat.”

His voice was rough, low, and utterly lacking in ceremony.

Sonzai tore a piece from the rye, looked at it as though the bread itself had disappointed him, then ate it anyway. “Stannvaard did not forget how trade works. They stopped commerce across the Inner World because they believe everyone else will suffer first and negotiate second. That is leverage, not shortage.”

He chewed, swallowed, and leaned back slightly.

***“Maybe we need to make the Dawi monkeys remember why reopening trade is healthier for them.”***

There was no smile to soften it. Lambert’s thumb stopped moving.

Sonzai continued before anyone could decide whether the sentence deserved correction. “They need customers. They need timber. They need foreign food. They need caravan traffic. They need armed states between their walls and whatever decides to come through the frontier next. Their forges are impressive, but iron does not eat iron and Dawi do not survive on ingots.” His finger tapped once against the boards. “So we identify what they receive from everyone they have decided to starve, and we start calculating which of those things can stop moving south.”

Sodoku would recognize the change immediately. Sonzai had stopped speaking as the irritated younger brother at dinner. The Commander had begun dissecting the problem.

“Not war,” he added. “Not unless they insist on becoming stupid. Pressure. Escorts disappear from routes we control. Timber contracts become unavailable. Intelligence sharing becomes slow. Border cooperation becomes expensive. Every dependency they assumed was permanent gets priced again.” His eyes narrowed. “Something in Stannvaard hurts when squeezed. We find it.”

Then he looked toward Hild.

“You are right. Kushara is the practical answer before any of that becomes necessary.” His gaze moved to Sodoku. “Dawn Guard has access to an entire continental network independent of Stannvaard. Malani grain. Jomari shipping. Zembari caravan infrastructure if anything can be routed north from another direction. Kusharan purchasing power behind it.” He picked up his cup. “We ask for commerce, not charity. If the Old World can open even one dependable artery into the Inner World, Stannvaard loses the luxury of believing every hungry kingdom must eventually crawl back to them.”

He drank the rind-water and immediately looked offended by it.

“We can recover our pride after everyone has eaten. Pride has very little nutritional value.”

A few breaths later, Malakar’s name entered the conversation again.

Sonzai’s cup stopped halfway back to the table. His eyes lifted. Sharp.

There was no Selvara in the room to look toward, no visible connection for anyone else to follow, only the sudden hardening of Sonzai’s face and the subtle pressure of Calamity stirring beneath his skin. The reaction lasted barely a heartbeat before he controlled it, but that heartbeat carried years: a child’s corrupted Soul Crystal, a dead demon whose influence had survived his body, and a daughter of that demon currently somewhere inside the same ruined capital as his family.

He set the cup down slowly. Dead. He reminded himself of it again.

*Malakar was dead.*

The name should have been lighter because of that. It was not.

Sonzai rubbed his thumb once along the rim of the cup, eyes lowered while the rest of the table continued. He was beginning to understand that death did not necessarily reduce a person’s weight. Sometimes it concentrated it. Malakar no longer occupied the world physically, so everything associated with him had collapsed inward into consequences: continents split, bloodlines displaced, Calamity lodged inside Sonzai, and now Selvara.

His jaw tightened.

“And perhaps,” Sonzai said eventually, voice returning to its usual dry register, “we can manage the remainder of supper without saying Malakar’s name every few breaths.”

It sounded like irritation. That was intentional.

Nobody at the table needed to know it was the closest thing Sonzai currently had to asking them not to press on a fresh wound.

He reached for another piece of rye and returned his attention to the matter that could actually be solved tonight.

“Write Kushara first,” he said. “Then we decide which Dawi finger to bend if they say no.”

"Correctness and Self-Righteousness aren't the same... some religions require heretics to separate what is truly believeable. That is the balance that is required to keep it stable, if we keep continuing to say we are right, then who is to say that we are wrong? The balance shifts and then everything else seems to feel... null. I believe that is heresy, defying what stands around us, rather than embracing the diversity. After all... it is our world that wasn't created on a single pattern." She said, her eyes shfited over to look at Sodoku, her views seemingly just as strong as his, but the interpretations much different from the way he chose to believe it.

"I am not speaking on their part, I am not a believer of their religion, but I am knowledged in my own to understand that there is a reason why they exist. Urion presents us a conflict, and we must settle it, but not as animals." She said, her eyes looking over at her brother with an insightful gaze. 

"I believe you missed that part, Sodoku. It's not about which ways are true, it's about what exactly sets us apart from them. To understand opposition is to understand where one's footing, I believe Urion is testing us. After all, one side cannot stay balanced without there being weight on the other side. Let them have their faith, and let us not interfere. We treat them as harmony, not as alien, it's best we make a powerful ally then a powerful foe." She said, her words insightful of the view she has been given, she let out a soft sigh as she looked over at her brother.

"As for you..." She shifted over to Yoko, her eyes laid onto her. "I accept the nature of this world clearly, but that does not give an excuse to live like suffering is an absolute. I laugh, I cry, but I do not open my heart to the grim possibility. I have survived many winters, and yet I am not as fragile of a heart... no offense of course. I believe we are leading off the wrong foot, rather than inviting those of the Sanctum to a hearing."

The fire had burned down to the stage where it gave more smell than heat, resin and old ash and the particular sourness of green wood that has been forced. Somebody had banked it wrong. The heel of Sodoku's boot found a nailhead standing proud of the flagstone and he pressed on it twice without deciding to, and out in the passage a woman was telling a man that four ricks of green would not see him to Ice-out and that he should have said so a month ago.

"My beliefs will not move."

He said it into the cup and then set the cup down.

"That is the first thing and I am not going to soften it. The second thing is that I understand your approach, sister, and I am not pretending to. You have made an argument about weight on both sides of a scale and you made it in the vocabulary of the thing you are defending, and that is more work than most people do."

He turned the cup a quarter turn, which was a gesture he had picked up from a man sitting four feet away and had not noticed he was copying.

"I am not going to answer it tonight."

Lambert's eyes came up. Only the eyes.

*He has just declined the argument in front of the whole table and called it deferral, and his father used to do the identical thing, and his father did it because he had already decided and did not want to be talked out of it.*

"I have an answer. The answer costs an evening, and we have a courier nine days out, and I would sooner spend the evening on the thing that can still be changed." Sodoku pulled the plank of bread toward himself and then pushed it away again, untouched. "And because my brother is right, and he has been right for the length of this meal, and everyone in this room has been arguing about priests while the hand around our throat belongs to somebody else entirely."

He put two fingers on the boards.

"Sancta Lux does not hold Stannvaard. Say that out loud and hear how obvious it is. The Grand Church sits at Altherion and it has jurisdiction in four worlds and no writ over an ore quota. It cannot open a granary. It cannot close a corridor. It cannot instruct a factor house to hold a shipment for two seasons and it has never once tried, because the day it tries is the day the Ore Council remembers whose mountain the church is standing on." His fingers moved half an inch across the wood. "Twelve guild masters. A council chair. The River Crown's republic underneath all of it. That is where the decision lives, and it has lived there for two hundred years, and no theology I have ever been furious about has moved one wagon."

"Then why have you spent four minutes being furious about it," Lambert said.

"Because I am capable of both." Sodoku did not look up. "Write it down if you like. Under acceptable losses."

Yoko's ear flicked once and settled.

"Consider what the pattern actually is," Sodoku said. "Grain cut by three quarters over two years. Inscription substrate delivered in full, on schedule, every quarter, for eleven years, through the collapse, through two winters when the corridor was shut to everything on wheels. Four letters from this house asking for an explanation and three courteous replies with no fact in them." He let that stand. "That is not a trade posture. A trade posture has a price attached to it. This has no price attached to it, which means somebody is paying the price somewhere else and it is not showing up on our side of the ledger."

"Blackmail," said Lambert.

"Blackmail. Or something with a longer word." Sodoku's jaw moved once. "A council of twelve does not act as one for eleven years without either an agreement or a hand. If it is an agreement then there is a document and Tabitha can find the document. If it is a hand then the hand is on somebody's family, and there is a Dawi guild master somewhere in that mountain who has been voting the same way for a decade with his stomach turning over every time."

He picked up the cup again and found it empty.

"So we make it expensive. War is off the table, and nobody sitting at this one wants it or could pay for it. Pressure. We stop being convenient." His thumb went along the rim. "Kharven ironwood goes into Dawi tool handles because nothing else in the four quarters holds an edge-shock the way it does, and they have been buying it at a friendly rate for two centuries because they have never once had to imagine buying it at any other rate. That stops. Escort cover on the northern corridor stops. The border reports we pass to their commission for nothing, and have passed for nothing since my father signed it, stop." He set the cup down. "And the Mandate's western approaches are watched by our mountain companies and by nobody else, and Stannvaard has never once had to garrison that gap, and they should be made to think about the gap."

The room got quieter than the fire.

Lambert took a slow breath in through the nose.

"Say the last one again, your highness."

"I said what I said."

"You said you would consider ceasing to hold the western approaches in order to make a point to a mining consortium." Lambert's voice did not change register at all, which was how everyone at that table understood he had stopped being agreeable. "I want it stated plainly and not gestured at, because gestured at it reads as leverage, and stated plainly it is Bram Greymane's ridge and eleven hundred people who live behind it."

"I said they should be made to think about it."

"They will think about it. So will the Mandate." Lambert flattened the burned hand on his knee. "And then there is the ironwood, which I will take separately, because you have proposed to shut off the one export this kingdom still has in order to injure the only customer who buys it. Brackenhold eats because the ironwood moves. Whitmoor eats because the ironwood moves. Cold Cross eats because the ironwood moves. You will be cutting the throat of the only three towns in Kharven currently on a full ration, and you will be doing it in the second month, and the Dawi will feel it in their tool inventory in about a year."

Sodoku's fingers stopped on the wood.

"How long."

"Ten months before a guild master notices. Fourteen before it reaches the Council floor." Lambert did not enjoy saying it and did not disguise saying it. "Kharven will have starved the timber towns for a year in order to make a point that arrives after the point stops mattering. This is the correct instrument at the wrong scale, my lord. It is a lever built for a kingdom with two years of grain, and we have one hundred and sixty days at two thirds."

The nailhead was still standing proud under Sodoku's heel. He pressed it a third time.

"Then it goes in the drawer and it stays in the drawer until we have eaten," he said. "And we build it anyway. I want the whole of it costed. Every dependency they have on us, what it is worth to them, what it costs us to withhold, and how long each one takes to bite. We build it in order to know it and we do not touch it. A man who has not costed his own leverage is a man who will use it badly the first time he is frightened."

"That I will do tonight," Lambert said.

"And the letter goes first." Sodoku pushed the bench back and stood, and the whole room heard the joints in him. "Kushara. Grain that does not come through the corridor, ships that do not answer to the Ore Council, and a courier who arrives before the Thin Weeks close over the roads. Robin left nine days ago on a fast horse and I have been assuming nine days is enough, and I would like somebody in this house to tell me whether that assumption is worth anything."

"It is not," said Hild.

She had her chin propped on the heel of her hand and her eyes on the fire.

"The southern road is out past the fourth ford. Tabitha walked it. He will have gone the long way through the Ironwood March and that is four days added and nobody told him it was four days because nobody knew when he left." She did not move her chin. "So it is thirteen days, not nine, and you have been counting wrong since the equinox."

*And now he will look at me and work out that I have been reading Edward's road reports, and Edward will work out that I have been reading them before he redacts them, and I will lose that.*

Sodoku stood there and did the arithmetic and found that his daughter had done it first.

"Then we send a second rider on the second road," he said, "and we accept that we are paying twice for one message, and we stop pretending that is wasteful."

He put his hand flat on the table and pushed himself off it.

Salt, wet stone, and hot wax.

Robin Ice had been smelling all three for six hours and had run out of anything to compare them to. The Alabaster Court was built downhill and open to the water, and every corridor in it carried a draught that came off the harbour with the tide in it, and the white stone held that damp with the obstinacy northern granite reserves for cold, so that a man walking through it was never dry and was never quite cold either. The floors were worn into shallow troughs down their centres. Four hundred years of feet. Somewhere ahead a bell rang twice and stopped.

The Lord Knight Lieutenant walking him in had not spoken since the gate.

Ser Marten Quay was a wide grey man in the Court's harbour-blue with a surcoat that had been let out at the sides and taken in again at some point in the last decade, and he walked half a pace ahead and slightly to the left, which put his body between Robin and every doorway they passed. He had asked for the seal at the gate. He had looked at the seal for four seconds. He had then given it back without comment and had been in a foul temper ever since, and Robin, who had spent his whole life reading which adults were angry and which adults were angry at him, could not work out which this was.

"Ser."

"No."

"I have not asked anything yet."

"You are going to ask whether I dislike you," Quay said, without turning around. "I do not. I have never met you. Save it."

They came through an arch and the air changed.

Robin felt it in his chest before he understood it. Something in the neighbourhood of cold and of pressure and exactly neither. Like the first four feet of a dive, that sensation of the world putting a hand flat on your sternum and holding it there without malice. His ears did not pop. His breathing changed anyway, went shallower and higher and he had to reach for it and pull it back down.

*It is a room. It is only a room. Get through the room.*

The meeting chamber was small and had one long table in it and no chair at its head. There was a chair standing against the wall, alabaster white, clearly heavy, clearly ceremonial, and clearly not in use, and there was a second empty chair placed at the table itself with nobody near it and nothing in front of it.

Two people were waiting.

The woman was sixty and built square, in the grey of the Court with a First Auditor's black cording at the cuff, hair iron-coloured and pinned back hard enough to pull at the temples. Broad through the shoulder, thick through the wrist, the hands of somebody who had done a trade before she had done this. Her mouth was set in a line that had clearly been there before Robin arrived and was not going to be moved by his presence.

The man was standing.

Tall and gone narrow with it, shoulders forward over a chest that was too deep for the rest of him. Grey wool to the instep. A stole down the front worked in raised gold coils the width of a thumbnail, hundreds of them, thousands, stacked in overlapping ranks from the belt to the hem, and Robin looked at them for a moment too long before he understood that they were not decoration and that somebody had put each one there for a reason. The cap was in his hands. He had taken it off. His lips carried a faint blue cast at the edges, and there were two vertical lines drawn deep between his brows that had not relaxed in some years.

"Say it at depth," said Verinus VII, "or do not say it."

Robin had a speech. He had carried it nine days on a horse and thirteen days in total and had said it aloud to himself in the dark at four separate way-stations, and he opened his mouth and delivered it.

"My Lord Palatine. I come with word from the True High King of Kharven, Sodoku Moto. He sends notice of distress and requests the support of the Holy Sea in his endeavours, specifically naval and grain support, and asks that you consider the ancient friendship between our houses in your deliberation."

It weighed nothing.

He felt it go. That was the horrible part, and nobody had warned him, because nobody in Kharven had known to warn him. The words came out of his mouth and went up instead of down, the way chaff goes up off a threshing floor, and the room registered them and did not register them, and Deacon Barrow's jaw moved once at the corner in a way that told him she had heard a thousand of those and had stopped being irritated by them around the four hundredth.

Verinus tilted his head slightly down and to the left.

"That is a correct message," he said. "Whose question was it?"

"My Lord?"

"You have brought me an accurate account of what a king wants. I believe every word of it and it has told me nothing, because nothing in it cost you anything to say." He turned the cap once in his hands. "You rode thirteen days. What did you see."

Robin's throat closed and opened.

"We will absolutely not do this," said Barrow.

She said it to Verinus and not to Robin, which Robin noticed and filed.

"The Grand Church has a standing position on the northern corridor and we have a chapter hold in this city that reports past this table to Altherion, and the moment a Holy Sea keel goes north with grain in it we are a party to a quarrel between a mining council and an exiled house, and we are a party to it with our name on the manifest." Her hands stayed flat on the table. "I have watched you weigh four hundred of these. You are going to consider it. You consider all of them. I am telling you now, in front of the courier, so that nobody afterward can say the Curia was not warned."

"Noted, entered, and I am considering it," Verinus said.

"Then hear the rest of it. You would be moving against the expressed will of the High Church of Sancta Lux." Barrow's voice did not rise, because nobody's did in that city. "That is not a censure. That is a stripping. You would not be the Palatine by the spring convocation and you know it, and whatever you think you are buying, you would be buying it with the seat and not with your own coin, and the seat is not yours to spend."

*Nineteen years and he still thinks the chair against the wall means he is not sitting in it.*

Verinus did not answer her. He looked at Robin.

"What did you see," he said again.

And Robin Ice, who was seventeen and a bastard out of a bastard's line and had been chosen for this errand precisely because nobody would miss him, discovered that the speech was gone and there was nothing behind it but the road.

"There is a death-house at Coldbeck with forty in it," he said. "They cannot bury them. The ground is shut until Ice-out and the man who reads the names is a cooper because the last one who could do it properly died four years ago and the liturgy went with him. I stopped there on the sixth night. A woman gave me half a rind because I was on the Crown's business and I ate it and I have thought about that rind every day since, because she had four children in the room behind her and she gave it to me anyway, and I did not have anything to give her back except a sealed pouch I was not allowed to open."

He stopped.

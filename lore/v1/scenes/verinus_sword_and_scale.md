# The Sword and the Scale

*The Grand Cathedral of the Alabaster Court. Continuous.*

---

The knee going down on hardwood made a sound the whole nave took and passed along.

Four hundred years of oath into the same stone, and the stone kept every word, and it kept the small ones too. Verinus heard it come back off the vault a half-second late, doubled and thinned, and behind it the suppressants shifted their note for the length of two breaths and settled again. Somewhere up in the clerestory a pigeon went along a ledge and stopped.

He let her finish.

Then he came down off the step, and he did it slowly because the pelvis had been complaining since the second hour, and he stopped at a distance where nobody had to raise their voice.

"Stand up."

He said it without heat.

"I am not going to make a lesson of it and I am going to say it once. This order does not kneel and it is not knelt to. That is the first clause of the Catechism of Equal Breath and it comes before everything else we have ever written, and the reason is not modesty. A man who is knelt to begins, within about four years, to believe that the weight in the room is coming off him. It is not. It never was. It comes off the thing being measured and I am only the instrument that reads it, and an instrument that has been knelt to has been quietly recalibrated by everybody who did the kneeling."

He put his hands behind his back.

"Call me Palatine. It is not a courtesy and I do not mind it, because it is a job and that is the word for the job. I was Osric Peyser for thirty-one years and I answer to that in exactly two houses on this water and neither of them is this one."

*Nineteen years and I have never seen him take that tone with anybody who was not already standing.*

Charles Lambert had not moved from the front of the pew.

"You asked me to lend you my faith as Darius did." Verinus's voice did not change. "I am going to refuse that too, and this one matters more than the kneeling, so I want it heard properly.

"Darius gave you faith because faith is what the Will has to give. The Will decides. It looks at a thing entire and it commits, and commitment without faith is paralysis, and I have watched that man carry the weight of committing for six years while the investiture sat unsigned in a drawer and nobody in this Court asked him how he was managing it.

"I hold the Scale. The Scale does not have faith to lend. It has never had any, at any point, about anything, and the day it develops some it stops being able to read." He let a breath out through the nose. "So no. You will not get my faith and you should not want it and Darius would tell you the same if he were standing here, probably less kindly.

"What I will give you is the reading. Every time. Accurate, whether or not it flatters you, whether or not it agrees with what you have already decided, and delivered to your face rather than into a file. That is worth more than faith and it is considerably less comfortable, and you will hate it inside a year."

The light through the south clerestory had moved another finger's width across the boards.

"Now the other thing," Verinus said.

He walked four paces down the nave and stopped with his back half to her, looking at the empty fourth row.

"A sword pointed at all who defy the light."

He turned the sentence over the way he turned a coin over, without any visible reverence for it.

"I want you to understand something about my Domain, because you are going to be near it for one more day and then not again for a long time. Everything said inside the Sounding acquires weight. Density times depth. A rehearsed speech weighs nothing. A true thing said at cost sinks the floor. I have read four thousand statements in forty-one years and I can count on one hand the ones that surprised me."

He turned around.

"That sentence weighed nothing."

He held up a hand before anything could be said into the gap.

"I am not calling you a liar. Attend to the distinction, because it is the whole of what I have to teach you. A statement weighs according to what it claims about the world and what it costs the speaker to claim it. *There are forty in the death-house at Coldbeck and they cannot be buried* sank this floor a foot last week, out of the mouth of a seventeen-year-old courier who did not know what he was doing. *I am a sword pointed at all who defy the light* makes no claim that can be weighed at all, because there is no arrangement of the world in which it turns out to be false."

He came back two paces.

"Every creed I have ever put on the Scale has read the same way. Weightless, and true for all I can tell, and the Scale has nothing whatever to say about the difference. And I have never told anybody that, and I am telling you, in front of a bishop, because you are going to spend the next year in rooms where men say things of exactly that shape and mean them absolutely."

*He is doing it now. He is putting the whole of it on her at once and he is doing it because he will not see her again before she sails, and he has decided that one uncomfortable morning is cheaper than a comfortable letter.*

"So here is the question and I do not want it answered today. I want it carried."

Verinus stopped in front of her.

"Who holds the hilt."

The suppressants hummed and went under hearing.

"A sword is an object that does exactly what it is aimed at and has no opinion about the aiming. You said the sentence as though the pointing were settled. It is not settled. It has never once been settled for anybody who has said that sentence, and I have sat in this building long enough to read the accounts of four of them." He did not look away. "Darius knows the answer for himself and it cost him six years in a drawer to get there. Aurelian knows the answer and he is currently somewhere in Altherion not answering his correspondence, which I suspect is the answer being paid for. You are nineteen. Nobody expects you to have it. I expect you to have noticed the question exists."

He stepped back.

"That is the whole lesson and there is not another one. Take it north."

---

Charles Lambert cleared his throat, which in that family was a considerable event.

"On the matter of sour tones."

He came round the pew and stopped at a courteous distance, hat still in his hand, and he had put the pleasant face back on and it did not quite sit level.

"You were correct and I am not going to dress it up. I looked at you and said a word about you before I said one to you, and my brother would have described exactly what I did in exactly those terms, and he would have written it down, and the fact that he has not spoken to me in nine years does not make him wrong about my habits." He turned the pectoral chain one link. "So. Withdrawn, and not asked to be forgiven, because you have already told me what you think of being asked and you were right about that too."

He put the hat on the pew back.

"One thing in return, and then I will take the instruction and be useful.

"You said you have travelled Kushara and can read the routes as well as a local. I believe you. I have read the same maps and I will not pretend to your ground knowledge." His voice went flat in the way the family's voice went flat. "What I have and you do not is the room. Nine years on that bench, and every single week of it spent watching men decide something and then spend two hours constructing the reason they will give for it afterward. I can tell you which one of the twelve has already decided before he sits down. I can tell you when a courtesy is a delay and when it is a refusal that has not been drafted yet. That is not a virtue and it is not wisdom. It is a trade, and I have done it for nine years, and it is the only thing I have that is worth carrying onto that ship."

He picked the hat back up.

"You do not need my solace. Agreed and understood. You may find you have some use for my ear, and I am offering it as a tool and not as comfort, and if you never take it up I will not raise the subject again."

*And I am about to spend four months on a small ship with a sword pointed at everything that defies the light, and there is a four-month-old in the cabin below whose eyes went gold in a chapel this morning, and I have not told her, and I am going to have to.*

*Anyu first. Tonight. Before the tide.*

---

She turned for the doors.

Verinus let her get three paces and then spoke to her back, which was a thing he did on purpose and had done on purpose for nineteen years, because a man's shoulders answered before his face did.

"One more."

He waited until the boots stopped.

"You said you are not doing this out of goodness. You said it as though it were a confession and you expected me to be disappointed." He put his cap on and settled it. "I am not. I would have been considerably more worried if you had told me you were doing it out of goodness, because goodness runs out around the fourth month of a bad winter and duty does not, and every person I have ever watched fail in a long errand failed at the point where the warm reason ran dry and there was nothing structural underneath it.

"You have something structural underneath it. Hold on to it and stop apologising for it."

The stained glass had gone another finger across the floor.

"And when you find the thing in the north that makes you want to move immediately," Verinus said, "which you will, and it will be obvious, and every instinct you have will be correct about what it is. Write to me first. Permission does not come into it. You do not require mine and I would not presume to offer it. Write because putting a thing into a sentence for somebody else forces you to say what it actually is, and half the disasters I have read in the Archive were men who never once had to write it down before they did it."

The north door mechanism turned over, two hundred years of maintenance in the sound of it.

Verinus stood in the middle of his own cathedral with his hands behind his back and listened to the suppressants come back up to full and settle into inaudibility.

"Bishop."

"My lord."

"How much of that did she take."

"All of it." Charles was still looking at the door. "And she will do the opposite of at least one of it, because she is nineteen and because you told her four difficult things in eleven minutes and the human ear takes three."

"Which one will she drop."

"The hilt." Charles picked his hat up again and turned it round by the brim. "She will carry the weightless-creed lesson because it was an insult and insults keep. She will carry the writing-first because it is a procedure and she strikes me as a woman who respects a procedure. She will drop *who holds the hilt*, because it is the only one of the four that requires her to consider that she might be being used, and she has spent this entire morning telling us with her whole body that she has already worked out who she serves."

Verinus was quiet for a moment.

"That was an extremely good answer, Bishop."

"It was my brother's answer," Charles said. "I have simply been carrying it around for thirty years without permission."

Charles looked at him for a moment, and then he did a thing that only somebody raised in that household would have thought to do, which was to say nothing at all and wait.

"I have spent nineteen years," Verinus said eventually, "being extremely careful about what this Court aims at anything. I have declined four requests for a Reading that would have ended careers and one that would have ended a house, and every single time I declined it I entered the reason and I slept afterward.

"This morning I attached a nineteen-year-old to a legation, sent her into a country I have never walked, and told her four things she will remember badly. And the arithmetic I did to justify it was sound and I could set it out for the Curia tomorrow and win. And I would like it recorded somewhere, Bishop, that the arithmetic being sound is not the same as my having done a good thing, and I know the difference, and I did it anyway."

"You want me to hold that for you."

"I want somebody to be holding it who is not me. That is the entire office and nobody explains it to you before you take it."

Maud Thole was still at the north door. She had let it come to behind the departing boots and had not gone out after them, and she had her hands folded in front of her buckle where everybody could see them.

*He has told a bishop the true reason out loud in a room that weighs things, four paces from a Lord Knight Commander whose job is to write down what he says. Which means he wants it written down. Which means he is frightened.*

"Commander."

"My lord."

"Add a fifth name to your four. Somebody who can read and who will not be missed from the muster, and who reports to you and not to the Bishop and not to me."

"Watching which of them?"

"I have not decided," said Verinus, "and I want you to notice that I have not decided, and to enter that as well."

Thole's chin came down half an inch.

"Entered."

Verinus walked down the nave to the fourth row on the left and sat down in the seat at the pillar, in the worst acoustic in the building, where nobody could see him from the door.

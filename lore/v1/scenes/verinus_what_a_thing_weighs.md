# What a Thing Weighs

*The Grand Cathedral of the Alabaster Court. Late morning, the same day.*

---

The suppressants were still humming when she finished speaking.

That was the detail Verinus took first and would think about for the rest of the week. The stabilizers in the hall's understructure ran continuously and inaudibly and had done for two hundred years, and the only time any living person heard them was in the four or five seconds after they had been asked to do actual work. He had heard them audible eleven times in nineteen years. Four of those had been Darius. Two had been the Primate. One had been a thing brought up out of the harbour in a crate that the Court did not discuss.

They were audible now, coming down slowly, and the air in the nave had that scoured quality it gets after a storm has gone over and taken the dust with it.

Verinus was aware that his own chest had opened. Some cage in it that he had stopped noticing had been unlatched and set down for the length of the time she had stood there, and putting it back on was going to be work.

He was seventy-one. He had been told what she was and he had read the file and he had privately assumed the file was generous.

*Nothing in that file was generous. Somebody in the Archive was being careful, and being careful is what people are when they are describing something they have not yet found a category for.*

Twice in one morning.

He set that down where he could reach it later and did not examine it.

The stained glass along the south clerestory threw its colour down across the floor in four long panels, and the panels had shifted a hand's width since he came in, and he used them to tell the hour more often than he used the bell.

"It is peaceful," he said. "It is also the loudest room in this city, which is a thing people find out about it slowly. Four hundred years of oath, testimony and sentence, all of it into the same stone, and the stone kept every word. The suppressants are not there to keep you quiet. They are there so that the building does not answer you."

He came down off the step.

"And you are correct about the evenings. Darius sat in the fourth row on the left, at the pillar, where the acoustic is worst and where nobody could see him from the door. I asked him about it once. He told me the seat was chosen for the acoustic and I have never believed him and I never will."

Behind him, chair legs went back over hardwood.

Charles Lambert stood.

He came around the front of the pew slowly and stopped at a distance that a soldier would have called correct and a bishop would have called courteous, and he looked her over from the boots up in a single unhurried pass, and he did not disguise that he was doing it.

"Hm."

He turned the pectoral chain one link.

"I see this is the girl you would have accompany me."

"She is nineteen," Verinus said. "She holds the Blade of Judger. She walked through a door in this building that requires two of your Curia to open together, and the understructure of this hall came up to meet her and then had to sit back down. Call her what you like in your own house, Bishop. In mine you will find the word costs you accuracy, and accuracy is the only currency I have ever been able to spend here."

Charles did not flinch. That was interesting too.

"Noted, my lord, and taken." He inclined his head a fraction toward her, which was more graceful than the apology he had declined to make. "I withdraw the noun and I keep the question underneath it, because the question is a fair one and I would like it answered in front of her rather than after she has left the room."

"Ask it."

"You are attaching a Blade of Judger to a trade legation."

"I am."

"Then the legation is no longer a trade legation." Charles's voice had gone flat in the way that Verinus had begun to recognise as the family's actual register underneath everything else. "My lord, I have spent nine years learning to walk into rooms where nobody is entirely sure what I am there for, and that ambiguity is the whole of my usefulness to you. A bishop and a clerk on a Kusharan keel is a delegation. A bishop, a clerk and a Blade of Judger is a statement, and every party we meet in the north will read the statement before they read my face."

"Yes."

"You are agreeing with me."

"I am agreeing with you and doing it anyway, and I would like you to hear the reason before you decide how angry to be." Verinus put his hand on the pew back and leaned some of himself onto it, because the pelvis had opinions about standing and had been expressing them since the second hour. "A quiet legation is ignored. That is not cynicism, it is nineteen years of watching quiet legations be ignored, courteously, in writing, with a seal. Kharven has written to the Ore Council four times and been answered three times and has not received a single fact. Do you understand what that means? It means that a polite request from a starving kingdom is the cheapest object in the four quarters."

He straightened.

"So we will not be sending a polite request. We will be sending something that has to be looked at. And while every party in that room is looking at her, Bishop, you will be doing the work I actually need done, and nobody will be watching you do it, because there will be a considerably more interesting object in the room."

*He has just told me, in front of her and without a flicker, that she is the lamp and I am the hand in the dark. And she will have understood it too, because she has not stopped reading this hall since she came through the door, and I do not know whether he intends her to have understood it or whether he simply does not lie in this building.*

"That is a use of a person," Charles said.

"It is. And she is going to be used by somebody in the next eighteen months, and my preference is that it be somebody who tells her so out loud in a room that weighs things." Verinus turned. "Which brings me to what you asked me, and I am going to answer it plainly and you are not going to enjoy the answer.

"You asked when your regime begins. I have thought about the request since it was sent ahead of you and I have arrived somewhere that will disappoint you.

"I cannot train you, and I want the sense of it exact before you argue. Whatever you carry, it is not mine and it does not run on my principles, and a man who instructs outside his competence is a man producing confident errors at speed. Darius could teach you because Darius held the Will and the Will and the Blade are the same instrument at different tempers. I hold a Domain that measures. There is nothing in my art that will make your art sharper by so much as a hair, and I would be lying to you inside my own Sounding to suggest otherwise, which is a thing I am physically capable of doing and have not done in forty-one years."

He let that sit long enough to be understood as final.

"What I can teach you is what a thing weighs before you cut it.

"That is not a lesser discipline and it is not a consolation. Every person I have watched destroy themselves in this office destroyed themselves at the same place, which was the six seconds between recognising a thing and acting on it. They were not wrong about the thing. They were wrong about the weight. A man who knows exactly what he is looking at and does not know what it costs will do the correct thing at the wrong hour and spend forty years finding out why nothing improved."

Verinus's hand came off the pew back.

"So. Your regime begins now, and it is not conducted in this cathedral, and I want you to hear the whole shape of it before you agree to it."

Charles's head came up.

"My lord."

"You will go north with the Bishop. That is the regime." Verinus did not look away from her. "You will travel with the Kusharan party, you will be present in every room he is present in, and you will not be given an errand of your own, and that will be the hardest part of it and I am telling you so now. You will be asked to stand in rooms where you are the most dangerous object present and do nothing, repeatedly, for months, while men who are worse than you talk. And every night the Bishop will ask you what happened in the room and you will tell him, and he will tell you what you missed, and after four or five months you will begin to miss less."

He paused.

"And at some point in that country somebody will hand you an obvious answer that requires only that you act, and you will feel the whole of yourself lean toward it, and what I want from you is that you do the arithmetic first. Once. Every time. That is the entire curriculum and it will take you the rest of your life."

The suppressants had gone under hearing again.

At the far end of the nave, forty paces back beside the north door, Maud Thole had not moved since the doors opened.

She was forty-four and built like a piling, short and thick through the trunk with a neck that went straight down into the shoulders, in the Court's harbour-blue with the sleeves off at the elbow because she had never once in her career worn a garment she could not fight in. Grey coming through the crop at the temples. A broad flat face with a nose that had been rebuilt badly and left that way on purpose.

Her hands were folded in front of her belt buckle. Deliberately, and where everyone could see them, and nowhere near the weapon on her hip.

*Eleven times I have stood in a doorway and told him no. This is not one of them and I want the record to show I decided that in the first four seconds, before the stabilizers finished, when it would have been easy to decide the other way and be praised for it.*

"Lord Knight Commander."

"My lord Palatine."

"Kit them both. Everything the Bishop asked for and everything he did not think to. Cold-weather issue at the northern grade and none of the Court's, because the Court's is designed for a harbour and they are going somewhere that kills people. Three months of preserved rations on the assumption that they will be feeding themselves and possibly others. Two sets of papers each. Coin in three denominations and none of it in Alabaster mint."

"Physicker's chest?"

"Two. One that travels with them and one that stays on the keel." Verinus turned his head. "And the Bishop's household goes with him. Wife, child, and whatever the wife says the child needs, and she is not to be asked twice about any of it or argued with about any of it. If she wants something that is not in stores, buy it."

Thole's expression did not change and Verinus, who had worked beside her for eleven years, watched her decide not to ask.

"Sixth hour tomorrow, my lord?"

"Sixth hour tomorrow."

"Then I will have Fend running until midnight and he will enjoy it, which is the part I resent." Thole unfolded her hands. "My lord. One thing, in front of them, so it is said once and not privately afterward."

"Say it."

"If she goes north under this Court's colours and something happens to her out there, it will not be the Curia that answers for it. It will be you. Personally, by name, and for the rest of it." Her voice was flat and unhurried. "I am not objecting. I have already decided not to object and I decided it at the door. I am putting the cost where you can see it, because that is what I am for and you have never once thanked me for it and I have never once needed you to."

"Entered," said Verinus.

"And the second half, my lord, since I am spending the coin anyway." Thole's hands came back together. "You have three people going onto one keel and every one of them is carrying something that would be worth killing them for. That is not a legation. That is a cargo. I would like leave to put four of mine aboard as crew and not as escort, in ship's clothes, on the manifest as hands, chosen by me and not entered in any book that leaves this building."

"How long have you been holding that."

"Since the doors opened. It took me the length of the stabilizers coming down to work out the shape of it and the rest of the conversation to decide whether to say it in front of the Bishop." A pause. "I decided in front of. He is going to be living with them for four months and he should know what he is living with."

"Do it," said Verinus. "And Commander. Choose four who can be lost."

"I was going to choose four who can swim, my lord, but I take the instruction and I will hold both criteria."

"Entered," said Thole, and went out.

The north door came to behind her with the small settled sound of a mechanism that had been maintained for two centuries by people who took it seriously.

Charles Lambert stood in the middle of the nave with his hat still in his hand and looked at the floor for a while.

"I want one thing," he said.

"Name it."

"If she is going to be in every room I am in, then she is going to see things about my household that I have not told her, and I would prefer to tell her myself, badly, at a time of my choosing, than to have her work them out in a corridor." He looked up. "I am asking your leave to do that."

"You do not require my leave. You require your wife's." Verinus picked up his cap from the pew and turned it once in his hands. "Ask her tonight. And Bishop, when you do tell it, tell all of it. A secret told in halves is a debt with interest on it, and you are about to spend a great deal of time on a very small ship."

He put the cap on and settled it.

Then he walked down the nave to the fourth row on the left, and stopped at the pillar where the acoustic is worst, and put his hand flat on the back of the empty seat.

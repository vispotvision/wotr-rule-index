# What the Plate Would Not Take

*Vaelmarr. The drill ground above the sea gate. Year eleven of the Landward Suppression.*
*POV: Ruvaen Aloreth. Explaining voices: the POV's own read, and the Measurewright to her clerk.*

---

## I. THE THIN GROUND

The drill ground smelled of chalk dust and old rope and the particular rankness that comes off oak that has been soaking in a harbour boom for six years.

Wind off the water, hard from the south-west, pushing the dust in flat tongues across the packed ground and driving it into the corners of his mouth. Under his boots the chalk had gone to a grey paste at the centre where nine hundred years of men had turned on the same eight feet of it, and the paste had dried, and it took the weight with a chalky give. Behind him the sea gate winch was working. Two hundred and forty links of chain coming over an iron drum, and the drum had a flat spot on it, so the sound was not a rhythm so much as a rhythm with a limp in it.

Ruvaen Aloreth stood in the centre of the packed ground with the blade point down and his eyes shut, and read the field.

It came in the way it always came in, which was as water.

The Aether over this headland ran shallow. Chalk holds nothing and the sea shears whatever settles, and the Density on the drill ground had never in eleven years matched the Density in the lower yard forty feet below it, which meant everything he did up here cost more per unit of work than it would have cost anywhere else in the city. He had chosen the ground for that reason. A man who trains where the ocean is thin does not find himself surprised by a thin ocean.

Over still objects the flow ran laminar. Smooth, layered, stacked in sheets that slid across each other without mixing, and the post and the rack and the water butt each sat inside a skin of that stillness with their shapes legible in it. Over moving objects the sheets broke. A body passing through the Aether sheds a wake in it the way a hull sheds a wake in water, and the wake is not decoration. The wake is the object's mass and its velocity written out in turbulence, and the structure of it holds for as long as the flow takes to close.

He did not see the men behind him. He saw seven wakes, six standing and going still, one walking left, and he could have named the walker by the length of the disturbance he left in the sheets.

*Fifty-two feet. Coming to sixty. He has gone to the rack.*

"Sergeant."

Behind him, Hesh Orovain grunted. "My lord."

"Take the oak off the top of the stack today. The bottom course has been under the tarp since the Thin Weeks and it has taken water and I am not going to learn anything off wood that weighs three pounds more than it looks."

*Eleven years. Eleven years and it still gets up the back of my neck.*

Hesh Orovain was ninety-one and built like a bollard and had two fingers off his left hand at the second knuckle, taken at Ollavaeth in the fourth wave, and he had thrown wood at the first prince of Eresse four days a week for a decade and had never once got used to being told what was under a tarp by a man facing the other way. He took the top course off the stack. Spar-ends, sea-gate boom oak, cut to the length of a forearm and squared, and they came out of the pile with the dry knock of seasoned timber.

"Count, my lord?"

"Whatever you have. Start when you like and do not tell me when."

Which was the whole point, and Hesh Orovain had understood that since the first year and Lunn Aeveth, nineteen years old and four months on the Landward Wall, had not understood it yet at all.

"But how does he know when to," Lunn began.

"Shut up and throw."

---

## II. THE INSTRUMENT

Forty feet off, under the lee of the rack shed where the wind broke, Neyra Corvenn of Velthaeir set her plate on its tripod and levelled it against the chalk.

It was a good plate, brass rimmed, eleven inches, with the ring scored at quarter-marks and a needle of drawn Materia wire standing off a jewelled pivot at the centre, and it had cost the Archive more than the shed it was standing under. She had carried it eleven hundred miles to point it at a man who had not asked to be pointed at and had not refused either, which she had found more unsettling than a refusal.

"Ossa. New sheet. Head it with the hour and the ground."

Her clerk crouched with the board on his knee and his back to the wind. "Hour and ground."

"Ambient first. Always ambient first, because a reading with no floor under it is a number and not a measurement." She bent to the ring. "Aetheric Density, drill ground, taken at the lee of the rack shed, forty feet from the subject at a bearing of, call it, east by south." The needle settled. She looked at it for a while. "Write it as nominal."

Ossa's pen stopped. "Nominal, Measurewright? On chalk? Over the sea?"

"Nominal."

*That cannot be right and I have no reason to think the plate is wrong.*

She had levelled the thing on a Velthaeir dock, on a barge in a swell, and twice inside a Wellspring-dead pocket in the Reach where the needle had gone flat and stayed flat, and it had never lied to her. The drill ground at Vaelmarr sat on four hundred feet of chalk with a south-westerly running across it and it should have read thin. It read the same as the lower yard. She wrote it down and did not explain it, because the office is to record the floor, and reconciling the floor with what the floor ought to be is somebody else's fifty years.

"Now the subject," she said. "And Ossa. Whatever the plate gives me, you enter the figure and you do not repeat it aloud on this ground."

## III. THE FIRST PASS

The first spar-end came off Hesh Orovain's right hand at the top of the sergeant's shoulder turn, rising, from behind and to Ruvaen's right.

He had it as a wake before it had crossed eight feet.

*Oak. Four and a quarter pounds. Rising at about eleven degrees. Twenty-two feet per second and losing the tail of it to the crosswind, so it will come in flatter than it left.*

The turbulence off a tumbling billet is not the turbulence off a thrown one. Something spinning end over end carries a wake that opens and shuts as it turns, and the frequency of that opening is the spin rate, and the spin rate against the forward speed is the whole of the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the throw. Hesh Orovain threw with a wrist-flick that put eleven and a half rotations on a forearm-length billet across thirty feet and had done since the fourth year, and Ruvaen could have picked the sergeant out of a hundred men by the wake alone.

He stepped through off the rear foot.

Not a lunge. A passing step, the *Zutritt*, left foot coming past the right and the hips arriving before the hands, and the blade came up out of the low guard in an *Unterhau* that took the spar-end across the grain about two thirds of the way along its length. Oak that has been six years in salt water splits along the vessels and not across them. It came apart with a wet sound closer to a bone crack than to a snap, and the two halves went past his shoulder spinning at a rate he could still read.

The second was already in the air.

That one came flat at his torso from his left and slightly behind, thrown underhand and hard by Garrick Dole, who was a Vessel Coast contract crossbowman and had no business on a drill ground and had been invited anyway because he threw at the ribs and meant it.

*Six pounds, no spin worth the name, nineteen feet per second, and he has put it a hand's width inside my elbow because he wants me to have to move my feet.*

He did not move his feet. He turned at the waist into the *tutta volta* and took the thing with an *Absetzen*, displacing the line with the flat of the strong while the point stayed forward, and the billet went off the blade with a clack and a shiver up his forearms into the elbows and out through his shoulders, and he felt the impact land in the joint of his left thumb where it always landed.

The third and fourth came together.

---

## IV. WHAT IT COSTS

Two at once is a different problem from two in sequence, and a different problem again from two at once when the two are seventeen inches apart at the moment of arrival.

A wake stops being legible when another wake is running through it. Turbulence does not politely stack. Two objects passing within a certain distance of each other produce a single combined disturbance that carries neither of their shapes cleanly, and the only way through is to read them before they interfere, at the range where the flow is still sorting them, which is a resolution problem and not a speed problem.

So he opened.

He had been holding his own resonance field compressed the whole morning, the way he held it every waking hour, and he let a third of it go.

The effect on the ground was immediate and physical. Lunn Aeveth's arms came up over his own head without any decision being taken in the boy's skull. Garrick Dole sat down on the chalk, hard, and swore in a coastal dialect nobody on the ground spoke. The gulls that had come back to the rack shed roof in the last hour went off it in one body. Hesh Orovain, who had been standing inside this for eleven years and had built a tolerance the way a man builds a tolerance to cold water, felt his jaw lock and his back teeth grind and threw the fifth billet anyway.

*He has never once done it without warning us first. Not once.*

And the field lit.

A compressed resonance field is silent in the Aether and reads like nothing. An open one floods the local volume with a known quantity, and every disturbance in that volume now stands against a reference he had made himself, which is the difference between listening in a dark room and listening in a dark room you have just shouted into. The two billets came out of the interference at nine feet with their spins and their masses and their seventeen inches of separation as legible as two lines ruled on a plate.

He took the upper one with a *Zwerchhau* that crossed his hands high and short and drove it down and left into the chalk. The lower one he let past his hip at a distance of about two inches, because it was going to miss, and he had known it was going to miss since it was twenty feet out, and a man who cuts what is going to miss him is a man who has learned to answer a question nobody asked.

Behind the rack shed the brass plate's needle went over hard and struck the pin at the edge of the scored ring and stayed there, trembling against the stop.

"Measurewright."

"I see it."

"It has no more plate to give you."

"I said I see it." Neyra Corvenn did not raise her voice. "Enter it as unentered. Do not enter an estimate and do not enter the word exceeded. Unentered."

*Perception at a grade the Archive does not carry a mark for, running continuously, with Forecast and Acuity feeding off the same read, and he is doing it in a Density the plate calls nominal while he plainly believes the ground is thin.*

*And his Vigilance is gone. That is the finding. That is the thing I came eleven hundred miles to write down and cannot write down, because to write it I would have to say how I know, and how I know is that a man reading at that grade would have felt the plate on him from the moment I levelled it, and he has not once looked this way.*

---

## V. THE BLINDING

The fifth and sixth and seventh he took without opening further, which is the part the men never saw and the part that mattered.

An open field is expensive and it is expensive in a way that does not show for an hour. The Shell runs hot converting at that volume, and what does not convert comes off as waste, and the waste has to go somewhere, and where it goes is into the lattice as heat. The first hour after a long open read, the resolution comes down. The second hour, the wakes go to a general blur with mass in them and no shape. By the evening bell he would be reading the world through a body and nothing else, and on a night when the Under-Houses came up the Ollavaeth line in the dark, he would be exactly as blind on that wall as Lunn Aeveth.

He had budgeted for that since the first year and had never once been surprised by it.

The thing he had not budgeted for was the other one.

There is a faculty that reads the room and there is a faculty that reads your own architecture, and they are not the same faculty and they draw on the same reserve, and eleven years of spending everything on the first has left the second thin enough to see through. He no longer felt his own ceiling coming. He used to. At forty he could have named the moment he was eight minutes from overdraw the way a man names the moment he is eight minutes from needing to piss, and now the warning arrived at the same time as the event or slightly after it, and both of those are the same thing as no warning at all.

Nobody had told him this would happen. He had worked it out himself in the eighth year, standing in the lower yard, by noticing that he had stopped noticing.

*I have traded the instrument that reads my own collapse for the instrument that reads everyone else's approach, and it was the correct trade eleven times out of eleven, and it will be the wrong one exactly once.*

He closed the field.

The ground came back. Garrick Dole got off the chalk. The gulls did not.

And in the second after he closed it, while the local volume was still settling out of his own reference and going back to whatever it had been, he caught something on the southern bearing that he had no shape for.

Not a wake. A wake has a front and a tail and a body between them and this had a front and no tail he could find, four leagues out over open water and high, and it was not moving the way mass moves through a medium. It was moving the way a drain moves. Something out there was taking the Aether in rather than pushing it aside, and the sheets over four leagues of the southern water were bending toward it, and he had never in a hundred and forty years of doing this read anything that did that.

He stood in the chalk paste with the blade point down and the wind coming across, and did not know what he was looking at.

"My lord."

He opened his eyes.

---

## VI. THE FIGURE NOT SAID

Hesh Orovain had the last billet still in his hand and had not thrown it.

"There is a Measurewright behind the rack shed," the sergeant said. "Forty foot. She has had a plate on you for the whole of it."

"I know."

*You did not. In eleven years I have watched you name a man by his wake at fifty feet with your eyes shut, and you did not know a brass plate was pointed at you from forty, and I am going to think about that tonight and not enjoy it.*

"Will you have me put her off the ground?"

"No. She came eleven hundred miles and the Archive will only send another one." Ruvaen wiped the blade down the outside of his thigh, twice, which was a bad habit he had never lost. "Tell her the plate will not take it, so she can stop pretending she is going to get a figure, and tell her the ground is thin here and always has been, so she can stop writing down a floor that is wrong."

Behind him, Ithuran Melloran came up the chalk from the lower yard one tread at a time with the knee doing what the knee did, and stopped at the edge of the packed ground, and watched the last of the dust go across it in a flat tongue and pile against the water butt.

He had come up to report that the Cliff Watch had counted three more before dawn, all southbound, all high, and that Ashvenn Lorreth had crossed out the word *nine* on the tally sheet and written *eleven* and then crossed out *eleven* as well and written nothing.

He did not report it yet. He stood at the edge of the ground and looked at the two halves of a split spar-end lying eleven feet apart in the chalk with the cut face of each one turned up and clean.

*He came back from Ollavaeth with the whole front of him red and the harness never has a mark on it, and I have watched him cut wood out of the air for a decade, and in ten years I have not seen him take a single billet cleanly on the second pass without opening.*

*He opened today on the fourth throw. Last winter it was the ninth.*

Lunn Aeveth, nineteen and four months on the wall, bent down and picked up one of the halves and turned it over in his hands and looked at the cut face, which was smooth as a planed board across grain that should have splintered.

*I am going to be dead within the year and I do not think I mind, if some of it is standing next to this.*

"Boy," said Hesh Orovain. "Stack it."

Ruvaen Aloreth took the last billet out of the sergeant's hand, hefted it once for the weight, and threw it himself, underhand and flat, forty feet into the lee of the rack shed, where it landed at the foot of the tripod and put the levelled brass plate over into the chalk.

# Xanelor: Dallae

The trench was still ticking when Kwon Mu-jin came down off the rail.

He heard it before his boots found the sand, a dry, uneven crackle out of the clay where the arrow's poured remains had gone through the floor. Kilns made that sound on the second night, when the walls began handing back the heat they had taken. The air over the channel shook. It stank of hot iron and charred blackwood and scalded clay, and under all three sat the thin sour bite that comes off a ward when its lattice splits and the district draw starts bleeding out through the seam. He looked up once. The crack ran across a third of the dome, a crooked white line from the eastern standpipe housing to somewhere above the judges' box. The draw-hum in the conduit had dropped half a note and stayed there.

Somewhere up in the tiers a bench scraped. Two porters were already arguing at the service door about who signed for the floor. A pigeon that had lived in the rafters since spring beat twice against the inside of the ward and settled again on the gauge-housing, affronted.

Hiromi lay on his back a body's length short of the trench.

The gauntlets had fallen open at his sides, palms up, fingers loose in the grit. Sand filled the collar of his black coat. Blood had dried in two tracks from the septum to the lip, and a third had started fresh over the old ones, bright against skin so pale it held the arena lamps. His red eyes were open. They moved when his father's shadow crossed them.

Mu-jin counted that first. Then the breath, shallow and quick but even on both sides. Then the lips, which had colour. He did not let himself count anything else until those three were in.

He knelt. The sand was warm through the knees of his trousers, heat seeping sideways out of the trench wall, and the smell of the boy's blood came up copper under the iron.

"Don't sit up."

The flask came out of his inner coat. It was thumb-long ribbed glass, the stopper sealed in blue wax with his ring pressed into it, and it had ridden in that pocket since the first morning of term because he had known which of his children would find a way to empty himself in the first week. A Distillatio draught, three passes through the still over the winter: pine resin and iron salts and a bitter bark to tighten the small vessels, and at the bottom of it, because this was Hiromi, two fingers of honey he had never once admitted to putting in. The boy skipped breakfast. The boy had skipped it this morning. Mu-jin had watched him do it from the refectory door with his own spoon halfway to his mouth.

He cracked the wax with his thumbnail. He slid his right hand under the back of his son's skull, lifted it off the sand, and set the glass to his mouth.

"All of it. Slowly."

Hiromi's throat worked. Some ran out of the corner of his mouth and cut a clean line through the grit on his cheek.

His left hand was already moving.

He did not look at it. He held it low, palm down, a hand's breadth over the sand between his son and the other boy, and he said a name. He said it the way his grandmother had said it, with the second syllable pressed a fraction flat, because that was the version she answered.

"Dallae."

The room was generous tonight. He felt that first, as ease under the sternum. The arrow had come apart inside the ward's field. Every joule the shot carried had gone somewhere, most of it into the trench and the stone, and a measurable remainder into the Aether over the floor as Residue, hot and disordered and thick. The crack in the dome was letting the district main pour more in behind it, until the air above the sand carried more than it could hold. A supersaturated medium does not want to stay that way. It wants a seed.

His Crystal held the seed. That was all Mahuo summoning had ever been, under the ceremony and the six hundred years of teachers telling students that nothing could be compelled: a pattern kept on the Attraction Layer, a thing the summoner had been recognised by and had learned to recognise back, and a place to put it. He put it a hand's breadth over the sand. The Aether did what saturated things do around a nucleus. It came out of solution.

She came up from the ground. Feet first, grey stockings and flat black shoes worn pale across the toes, then the hem of a grey wool dress, then an apron, and the air around her warmed by a degree he could feel on the back of his hand as the latent heat of the precipitation went into the room. The pull on him arrived a breath later. It was a small, steady draw out of the Core, a thread fed off the Attraction Layer and paid out through the Shell at the efficiency he had spent twenty-four years making good. Most of what he spent reached her. Every breath she stood on the sand he would pay again, and the room would not always be this kind. His left nail beds went white and stayed white.

*Cheap,* he thought. *The room is paying half of her. Don't get used to it.*

She was a little under middling height, square through the hips and narrow through the shoulders. Her forearms were thick, a laundress's forearms. Her hair was black with a blue cast to it, the colour of the inside of a mussel shell, pinned flat under a white cap that had been starched and then worn soft. Her face was a long oval with flat cheekbones and a small closed mouth. At the hairline on the left side, running down past the temple to the corner of the jaw, a seam showed where two different skins met and did not quite agree: one warm and olive, the other paler and faintly freckled. The seam carried into the eyes. The left was amber. The right was grey-green, river ice with weed under it. The dress fitted her badly across the bust, taken in by someone who had been guessing, and the apron over it was clean but worn thin and shiny at the knees from years of kneeling on floors. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow. Along the inside of her right forearm, from the wrist crease to the crook, ran a line of small punctures, closed and pale and evenly spaced.

She looked at Mu-jin first, as she always did, and then at the boy.

Xanelor was on his knees at the lip of the trench with the broken bow still hanging from his right hand. The mask was askew. Both forearms had swollen, the left worse, the skin over the radius gone tight and shiny and dark along its length. Three fingers of the right hand were cut to the bone where the string had bitten at the overdraw. His palms were raw. His boot soles had scorched, and the stink of burnt leather was his own.

Dallae went to him with her hands out, open, low, where he could see them.

"Please don't be alarmed," she said. The voice was soft and warm and carried no further than it needed to. "I'm here to help. It'll hurt, and then it'll stop hurting, and I'll tell you before each part."

Mu-jin had given her none of those words. She had come with them.

She knelt in the sand beside the boy without looking at where she knelt and took his left forearm in both hands. Her thumbs went along the radius from the wrist, slowly, pressing, and stopped a third of the way up. They moved again and stopped again. She was reading the fracture through the swelling. Hairline, transverse, running a little oblique across the shaft, the break that comes from a load driven along a bone instead of across it. Mu-jin had watched it happen from the rail. The boy had set his own skeleton between two opposing forces and let it carry the difference, and bone is magnificent in compression and poor at everything else.

"The radius first," Dallae said. "Then the ulna, and then the other arm once this one has gone quiet. Breathe out for me."

The stylet came out of the third puncture on her forearm. It was bone-white and hollow and about as fine as the barrel of a quill, and it grew more than it extended, lengthening a finger's breadth, then two. She laid her forearm along his and angled it, and the point went into the skin over the dorsal border of the radius without a sound. Xanelor's fingers curled on their own. The forearm muscles jumped under her grip and she held them.

The white came through the stylet slowly. Mu-jin could see it under the skin as a faint pale tracking where the swelling was thinnest.

It was his own work, most of it. The paste was a calcium phosphate slurry carried in a collagen sol, the same mineral the bone was already made of, ground finer than flour and held in suspension so it would run. A hairline crack is narrow enough that a thin liquid will climb it unaided, drawn along the gap by nothing but its own wetting of the walls. That was why she went in slowly. The sol found the fracture line and followed it, filling the crack from the inside, and when it had filled it, the Coagulatio closed. It was the one working of his she carried in her body, and she carried it because he had put it there: a law of setting, sol to gel to solid, the suspension giving up its water to the mineral in a hydration that ran warm as it went. What had been liquid in the crack became stone in the crack, locked to both faces of the break. The radius held.

It would take load for now. Bone had to grow into it before it was bone, osteoblasts climbing the mineral bridge from both sides and laying down their own, and until they had, the repair was a patch of very good cement on a living beam. Six days, if the boy ate and slept. He had seven.

Xanelor's breath came out of him in a hiss through his teeth. The heat of the setting showed on his skin as a flush along the line of the bone.

"There," Dallae said. "That was the worst of the first one. The ulna's easier, I promise."

She drew the stylet back into her arm and moved her thumbs across. The ulna fracture was cleaner. The stylet went in at the subcutaneous border near the wrist, the sol ran, the setting flushed warm, and she was done in half the time.

Then she did something Mu-jin had not asked for. She went back to the radius and laid two fingers flat over the place where the paste had gone in, and held them there, and her head tilted.

He saw it before he understood it. The flush along the radius stopped short. It had run a hand's length from the entry and stopped a finger shy of the far end of the crack, where the fracture ran toward the elbow, and yet the swelling at that end had already begun to settle. The setting had arrived there before the sol had. Something had met it halfway.

Dallae's amber eye moved to the three raised ridges on the boy's left cheek, where the mask had slipped. It stayed there a long moment. Then she went on.

Mu-jin found the explanation in the time it took to breathe once. There was stored heat in the bone. The boy had shoved an arrow through the sound barrier with his own arms as the bridge, and the load had to go somewhere, and a good part of it had gone into the radius as heat and stayed there. Hydration runs faster warm. The far end of the crack had been hotter than the near. That was all.

He put it away and did not look at it again.

She took the right arm. The fractures there were finer and the swelling less, and she worked it in the same order, radius, ulna, and told the boy each time before the stylet went in. When she came to the right hand she stopped with her thumbs resting either side of the three cut fingers, and her small mouth went tight.

"The arms," Mu-jin said. "Only the arms."

She did not move her thumbs.

"He'll draw with those," she said.

"He'll draw with those in six days, and I want him feeling every one of them until he does."

The seam at her temple creased. She had been an unfinished thing once, left in the margins of the house's Book of Summons half-written, two sets of hands on her and neither of them done, and he had spent a winter at nineteen finishing her. She did not like leaving work open. It was the only thing about her he had not put there.

She took her thumbs away from the fingers. She wrapped them instead, with a strip of clean linen from her apron pocket, quickly and very well.

At the lower tunnel mouth, past the judges' box, a girl in Academy grey stood with a silver cord on her shoulder and a pencil moving on a card. Class S. Mu-jin had seen her there since Dallae knelt. When he turned his head she was already turning away, and then the tunnel had her.

He let his breath out and stood.

His knees told him about the sand. So did the small of his back, which had been telling him things since he was thirty. He held his left hand closed at his side, the nails still white, the thread still paying out. Standing, with both of them in front of him, the other thing cost more: nine Stages of distance folded down small and held there so two children could stand in the same air as him and not be sick in it. He had been holding it all afternoon and his jaw ached with it. He did not let it out.

Hiromi's colour had come back. The draught had closed the fresh bleed; the old tracks on his lip were drying brown.

"Both of you," Mu-jin said, and stopped, because the next words had been *did well* and they were the wrong words. He started again. "No. That was excellent, both performances, and I'd put either one in front of the Division and not be embarrassed by it, and I'm embarrassed by most things I put in front of the Division."

He looked at the trench. A last bead of iron had cooled black on its lip.

"And you both took a gamble you couldn't cover. Both of you. You each made a bet with nothing behind it and then stood there and let the other one build a scheme around you while you waited to get paid." He turned his hand over, palm up, empty. "If I hadn't been at the rail, I'd be... one of you would be dead. Or beaten so badly it comes to the same thing in a week. I'd be writing to somebody."

He looked at his son.

"Hiromi. You were cocky." He let that sit. "You were so sure the well was going to finish it that you stopped watching what it was doing to him. You put the weight on him and you never asked where the weight would go. He found where. He put it in the bow." Mu-jin's mouth moved without quite making a smile. "You loaded his weapon for him and then you stood in front of it. You underestimated him from the first exchange, and I watched you do it, and so did he."

Then the other boy.

"Xanelor." He took his time with the name. "You held back. You've still got most of what you know how to do sitting behind your teeth. I counted three times today you had something and chose not to use it, and I'd guess it's more than three, and I'd guess the reason is you were afraid of showing too much. Of giving away..." He shook his head once. "Whatever it is. Too much."

He looked down at his closed left hand and then up again.

"Neither of those holds up. Not on a battlefield. Not when you work for contractors either, and some of you will, whatever the prospectus says. A man paying you for a job doesn't care what you were saving it for. He cares if the job got done. You hide a thing, you've capped yourself at whatever you were willing to show that day, and that's your ceiling now, and you picked it." A breath. "And the thing you're hiding, whatever it is, is a gate. You left it open. Somebody who pays more attention than your classmates is going to walk through it with a knife."

Dallae had not moved from her knees. Her hands were folded in her apron. Her amber eye was on the boy's scars again.

Mu-jin bent, picked the broken bow up off the sand by its riser, knocked the grit off it against his thigh, and held it out to Xanelor grip first.

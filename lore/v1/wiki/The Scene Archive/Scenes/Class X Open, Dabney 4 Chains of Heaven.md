*Aetherion Academy. The Crucible Field, the opening match of the Class X Open Tournament. The second exchange.*

The question comes across the white floor in the other boy's voice, and the light comes with it.

It is no bigger than the first, a marble of violet-white with two small rings of script turning round it on separate axles, and it hums. That is the first thing Dabney notices, before the shape of it, before the glyphs: the hum. The first light sang a thin doubled note, one glass in two rooms. This one hums low and rising, the sound a kettle makes in the moment before it decides to boil, and the hum climbs as the light comes on into the place where his weight leaned on the field a breath ago. The residue of that lean is still there. He can feel it the way you feel the warmth left in a chair. The slope has not finished relaxing, and the light is drinking from it.

*Weight,* the voice said, *but nothing to weigh.*

He opens his Judgement.

He kept it shut for the boy. He made himself that promise at the eastern mark, and he keeps it now: he does not turn the scale on the person across the floor, the heart behind the teeth, the thing Darius said only the field would show him. But a working is a made thing. It has a builder and a design and a foundation, and a thing with a foundation can be put on a scale and broken down, item by item, for every flaw and every inequity in it. That is all the Judgement has ever been. It is the oldest office he has, older than his name. He uses it now the way a mason uses a plumb line, on the work and nothing else, and he lets it open the width of a single hair.

They called it the Assize in Darius's books, after the court that rode the circuit of the Palatinate parishes and heard every case in a county in a day. Dabney has never called it anything. It simply happens, the way reading happens once you know your letters.

The light goes onto the scale, and the scale starts to itemise.

**[Oth]**, Path, again: Zhaeren's root, the road. It sets the direction and nothing else. Beside it, on its own turning ring, a glyph he did not see in the first sentence: **[Th]**, Foundation, Thalen's root, the Geometer's word. Its office is to impose geometry and reinforce load paths. It runs on Petralon's law, the law of stone that bears weight, and its domain is mechanics, the plain science of forces and supports. Here it has been given one job. It gives the light a floor to stand on, a reference state, and the floor it has been nailed to is the arena itself: the long stone body of the Crucible, the anchor arrays under it, the whole fortified ground. The local slope Dabney made can pull on the light all it likes. The light measures every pull against the arena's own level.

And **[Ur]** is gone.

He notes the absence the way Edward taught him to note an empty line in a ledger, as information. The Balance clause carried the first sentence, and he weighed it down to the floor with it. The other boy saw that and struck the clause out. It was quick learning, and Dabney likes it. A good classmate learns that fast.

Then the inequity at the heart of the thing, the place the whole design is built to stand.

The light has no body. It has no mass. There is nothing in it that can resist being pushed, nothing for a push to move. And it has been given weight. It has been handed the full recognised weight of the figure carrying it, so that the field reads it as heavy and pulls on it as on a heavy thing.

Every child who has dropped two stones off a wall knows they land together, the big one and the small. The old natural philosophers spent three hundred years asking why, and the answer turned out to be one of the deepest facts in the world: the thing in a body that is pulled and the thing in a body that resists being moved are the same thing, measured twice. Weight and inertia are one quantity. The pull grows with the mass, and so does the resistance, and they cancel exactly. That is why everything falls at the same rate. A Concord instrument-maker proved it to one part in twenty million with a beam hung from a wire as fine as a hair in a sealed case. He called it a torsion balance, and Dabney has always found it fitting that the proof that everything falls alike was made with a scale.

The light breaks that law on purpose. It is heavy with nothing to resist. Divide a real pull by no resistance at all and the answer is no number: it runs off the edge of the sum. A real body built that way would have to do something impossible, which is why no real body is built that way. This light does not do the impossible thing. It cannot accelerate, because there is nothing in it to accelerate, so every pull the field puts on it piles up instead, unspent, measured against the arena's floor, stored as a height the light has been lifted to and never allowed to fall from. And the more the field pulls, the higher the pile.

It is a bank, and it takes deposits only from him.

The scale delivers its verdict in the same instant, the way a sum delivers its last figure. It is built well, inside its own terms, and it has one assumption under the whole design that it has never been made to pay for. It assumes him. It assumes that his weight will go on being there to draw on, that the slope will keep feeding it, that he will answer heavily because the last time he answered heavily. The whole of its power is a debt written against his account. And the second assumption hides under the first, quieter: it assumes that the floor it measures from, the arena's long level, reaches unbroken from the stone under his feet to the light itself. Every unspent pull is stored as a height above that floor, and a height means nothing without a floor under it.

The idea that his weight would become its fuel. That is the flaw in its verdict.

Now the possibilities, because the Plane of Fate does not wait while he admires a design.

They fan open in front of him the way they did at the first exchange, each one with its consequence already tied to its foot, and he weighs them in his soul one by one. The weighing is not a thought. It is a sensation, low under the breastbone, the scale tipping inside him toward the heavier outcome before he has words for it.

He could lean. Let the plates slip the way they slipped for a hand's width, put real weight on the field, and drag the light into the stone the way he dragged the first. The scale tips hard against that one, so hard it hurts. Every grain of weight he adds is a deposit. The light would take it, and take it, and climb, and whatever he hurled at it would come back at him with interest when the debt fell due. And somewhere at the top of that climb the plates come off their bolts in front of every person in the Crucible.

He could hold perfectly still and give it nothing, let the plates seat hard and stop being heavy. The light would starve slowly. Slowly is the word that sinks that one: the slope is already there, the residue of his first answer, and the light is already drinking from it. Whatever it has banked, it keeps.

He could cut the light, which is the first thing his hands want. One stroke, the edge through the marble. The scale nearly throws him off his feet on that one. A stored height is a fall that has not happened yet, and cutting the thing that stores it lets the fall happen all at once, at the length of his own arm.

He could go for the source. The voice in the air and the figure walking behind the light, stepping round the eastern side of the floor without crossing the floor in between, and every sense he owns that is not his eyes insisting that the figure is the boy he spoke to. He does not know where the boy is. He knows where every magical sign says the boy is, and he has a feeling, cold and certain as the stone under his heels, that the boy has arranged for every magical sign to be wrong in exactly one direction. To aim at the source he would have to weigh the person, and he will not. And this was built to punish whoever aims.

He could do what comes to him most easily of all, which is Balance. The Work of Balance, Baransu no Shigoto, his own pan of the scale: reach into the working and equalise it, give the opposed loads a path through the middle and let them argue there. The scale tips against that one too, more gently, and he understands why before the tipping is finished. The light is a contradiction built to store. Balance would honour both sides of it. It would equalise the stored height against the pull and give the whole debt a lawful road to travel. He would be lending the light his own office.

So he weighs the other pan.

There are two in a scale. Every child who has watched a grocer knows it. The Balancer's root sits on every plate at the head of the five, and every plate puts the same root opposite it, its partner in the oldest pair the world has: Disruption, Uurgath's word, the Shatterfather's. Urion and Uurgath are a dyad. Darius made him learn the word at nine. A dyad is two things that are each the measure of the other. And the light is standing on Thalen's Foundation, and Uurgath is the one power the Geometer has never trusted.

Instead of answering with a working of Balance, Dabney chooses Ruin.

---

Darius taught him Ruin last, and badly, on purpose.

He was ten, and the yard was hot, and the white stone threw the light up under his chin. Darius had set a clay brick on the rim of the dry basin, an ordinary fired brick from the pile behind the chapter house, and stood beside it with his bad shoulder low and his hands folded over the head of his stick.

"Break it."

Dabney broke it. He reached into it the way he reached into everything and found the strands that held it to itself, and pulled, and the brick came apart into red dust with a sound like a slap, and a little of the basin's rim went with it.

"No," Darius said. He did not sound surprised. "That was strength. A boy who breaks things with strength will spend his whole life paying for every break at full price, and run out of money by thirty." He took a second brick off the pile and set it in the place of the first. "Look at it."

Dabney bent until his nose was nearly on the clay and looked at it as hard as he had ever looked at anything.

"Not at the brick. At what it is doing. Whatever it looks like, a brick lying there is working. Every grain in it is holding on to the grains beside it, every moment, and the holding costs. The kiln made it pay, the frost has been making it pay every winter since, and there is a crack in it there," he touched a corner with the ferrule of his stick, "where it stopped being able to afford the whole of itself and started pretending. Everything that stands is paying to go on standing. Everything is already ending. Any fool with a hammer can make an ending. Ruin finds the one already running and stops paying for the pretence."

He tapped the corner once. The brick fell into two clean halves along a line Dabney had not been able to see a moment before, with no sound at all.

"Hametsu," Darius said. "The Work of Ruins. The cheapest of the seven to perform and the most expensive to perform wrongly. You may only pronounce collapse on a thing that is already hiding its exhaustion. Pronounce it on a sound thing and you are no longer doing labour, you are only enjoying yourself, and Ruin that is enjoyed does not stop." His pale eyes held the boy's. "There is a kind of man who goes that way. He begins by ending what ought to end and finishes by ending everything, because ending has become the only thing that feels like work. I have met two. I killed one. Do not make me meet you."

Dabney had asked how you were supposed to make sure it stopped.

Darius had been quiet for a time, long enough that the gulls on the ridge had settled and lifted again.

"The men who first worked the Works," he said at last, "before anyone had words like Domain, before there were Orders or Courts or Academies, worked them on ground. They would measure a piece of ground and fence it, and let a Work loose inside the fence and nowhere else. The fence was the important part. The fence was what made it labour. The man who taught me called it by their word for it, which was the word for the broken stone you find heaped at the foot of a cliff where the face has come down: Talos. A field of rubble with a wall behind it. Ruin with a fence round it." He tipped his stick toward the brick. "The first form of Talos they taught their children was the one that keeps you honest. The fence holds the Ruin. Nothing else. They called it the Chains of Heaven, because the chains are not for your enemy."

"Who are they for?"

"You," Darius said, and went in to see about lunch.

---

Out on the white floor of the Crucible, the light is ten paces off and climbing its hum, and Dabney does what he has never done in front of anyone.

He lets.

The physicians of the Palatinate used the word for opening a vein to take the pressure off a man whose blood was too much for him, and Darius borrowed it, because no better one existed for what the old man taught him to do with a soul that has more in it than its walls were built for. **The Letting.** Under everything Dabney is there is the Essence, the soul's own water, the deep store that the Crystal holds and the Shell answers for. The Temperance plates sit on it the way a lid sits on a pot. When he leans, the lid lifts and the weight comes out as pressure, as a bend in the field, as lamps tugging on their chains. That is what the light wants. That is the deposit.

He gives it somewhere else to go.

He opens the Shell. A Class Two Shell is a Harmonic one, the Echo-shell, the first rank at which a soul's outer skin stops merely containing what is inside it and starts ringing in sympathy with the room. It is a throat as well as a wall. He opens the throat and lets the Essence through it, and on the way through, it changes state.

It is a phase change, the plainest thing in natural philosophy. Water held under pressure in a boiler is water; let it through a valve into open air and it comes out as steam, the same stuff in a different state, and the boiler's pressure falls as it goes. Essence is a soul's, belonging to him, shaped like him, carrying his weight. Aether is the world's, the common medium every working draws on, the stuff the arena's anchor arrays drink and the Guild meters sell by the hour in the streets outside. Essence let through the Shell comes out as Aether. It stops being his. It stops weighing what he weighs.

It comes out as a mist.

It pours from him quietly, from the whole surface of his skin at once and from nowhere in particular, the way breath pours from a horse on a cold morning, and it hangs. A dense cloud of fine particles, so many and so close that the lamp-light catches in them the way the sun catches in flour in a mill-race, and the air in front of him turns the soft grey-gold of an autumn morning over water. It drifts. It floats out over the white floor at the height of his chest and spreads, aimless, wandering the way smoke wanders in a room with no draught, turning in on itself in slow curls. It does nothing. It does absolutely nothing at all. Aether with no instruction in it is only weather, and this is a fog of it, thick enough that the edge of it eats the far tiers to a blur.

He knows what the tiers will make of it, if they make anything: a nervous boy fogging the field to hide behind. Let them.

What matters is under his own skin. The plates stay seated. The lid does not lift, because the pressure that would have lifted it has a valve, and it goes out through the valve as mist instead of down through the plates as weight. The lamps over the Crucible hang straight on their chains, and the shadows on the white floor keep their length. The chalk at his boots stays where it crawled. And the slope, the residue of his first answer that the light has been drinking, begins at last to flatten, because nothing is feeding it any more. He is still holding the half. If anything he is holding it more easily than he was a minute ago, because for the first time since the tunnel the thing he is holding has somewhere to go.

It costs. The Letting always costs, and it costs in the coin it spends: Essence let out as Aether does not come back. His fingers go cold first, from the tips inward, as if he had been holding them in the basin in winter. Then a taste of tin at the back of his tongue. Then a faint lightness behind the eyes, the feeling of standing up too fast after a long sit. He has let perhaps a tenth of what he carries out into the air, and the air has it now.

The hum of the light dips a half-tone as the slope under it eases. It does not stop. Whatever it has banked, it keeps.

Dabney breathes in.

Darius's breath, the one from the basin: only what he needs. He takes in exactly the air the next moment will cost and no more, and on the in-breath he lays the sentence down inside himself, all four parts of it, without moving his lips. He does not need a mouth for Parun. Nobody who has read it as long as he has does. An Archmagus holds his chains internally and never lifts a finger, and Dabney has been reading the language of law since before he could ask what the words meant. The sentence of the form goes down into him whole, like the ballast that goes into a ship's hold before she sails.

**Authorization** first, because a Work of Ruins speaks only with leave, and the leave is the verdict. The Assize has weighed the light and found the flaw in it, the assumption it never paid for, the place it is already hiding its exhaustion. Collapse may be pronounced on that, and on nothing sound. That is the whole of the permission and he holds it like a warrant.

**Boundary** next: the fence. Talos is Ruin with a fence round it, and the fence is cut with steel.

**Direction**, inward. Ruin in this form does not travel out to find its object. It runs inward from the fence onto whatever is inside it.

And the **Sealing**, which is the part Darius made him say aloud every night for a month: **[Vorn]**, End, Uurgath's fourth root, whose office is to force a termination state and collapse what is unstable. In this form it is pointed back at the Ruin itself. It is the clause that makes the work end its own labour. It is a chain.

He reaches up over his right shoulder and draws Aequitas.

All of it, this time. The full length comes out of the scabbard in one long pull, cross and blade and the plain round pommel, and the grain down the fuller lies flat under the lamps, and the lamps do not lean. That is the proof, and he feels it like a held breath let go: the plates hold. Every grain of what would have come out of him as weight is going out as mist instead, and the sword is only a sword. He brings it round and down in front of him with both hands on the grip, the point low and toward the floor, his right foot drawn back half a step, his weight settled into his hips, and the fog parts round the blade and closes again behind it.

He does not look for the boy or for the light. He looks at the ground in front of him, which is the only thing he means to touch.

He cuts the fence.

The first cut is high. The blade comes up from his right hip and runs flat across the air at the height of the top of his head, left to right, a long level stroke that lays a line through the fog as straight as a mason's rule. The second drops from the end of the first, straight down the right side to the stone. The edge stops a finger above the floor with a jolt he feels in both wrists. The third runs back along the floor, low and level, the blade skimming the white stone so close that the chalk dust lifts in its wind. The fourth climbs the left side and closes the square where it began.

Four cuts. A frame in the air in front of him, a little wider than his arms can spread and as high as he is tall. Where the edge went, the fog has been parted and stays parted: four bright clean lines through the grey-gold, as if somebody had drawn on a misted window with a fingertip. And because a frame faces something, the ground it faces runs away from him along his line of sight, out across the white floor, eight paces deep into the fog, a long box of air and stone with its mouth at his sword and its far end somewhere in the drifting gold. He did not aim it at the illusion. He did not aim it at anything. He has fenced the whole of the ground in front of him, every place the light can come from and every road the walking figure might take to reach him.

"Chains of Heaven," Dabney says.

He says it quietly, out of habit, the way he said it every night for a month to an old man in a chair. The Scriptia carry it to every tier at the same polite volume as everything else.

The Ruin goes in.

It goes in through the fence and runs inward, and it finds the mist.

This is what the fog was for. A plate of glass is strong. The bonds between its grains, counted one by one, could hold up a cathedral. And yet any glazier will tell you that a pane breaks under a blow a child could give it, because no real pane is perfect. It is full of flaws too small to see: scratches, bubbles, grains that do not quite fit. A crack never begins where the glass is strong. It begins at a flaw, where the stress gathers to a point. Once it begins it runs, because a running crack releases more of the glass's stored strain than it costs to make the new surface behind it. The glaziers have a saying for it, and every gunsmith and bridge-builder in the Accord lives by it whether he has heard the saying or not. Flaws decide whether a thing breaks, and strength has very little say in it.

The mist is flaws. Every one of the uncountable grains of Aether hanging in that box is a seed, a place where the lattice of the world, the fine invisible weave that the Aether rides on and space is measured by, does not quite fit around a foreign thing. **Semina ruinae**, Darius called them, the seeds of ruin: the ending already present, placed by hand. Dabney filled the ground in front of him with them while the tiers watched a boy make fog, and the fog did nothing because nothing had yet asked it to.

Now Ruin asks.

**[Uur]**, Disruption, the Shatterfather's first root, Fractura's law running under it: its office is to break lattice coherence and sever threads, and its domain is the same mechanics as Thalen's Foundation, read from the other side, the science of what holds turned into the science of what fails. Inside the fence it does exactly that. At every seed the weave gives. A crack in the world starts at each grain of mist and runs, and each one runs until it meets another running toward it, and where two meet they stop, because there is nothing left between them to break.

The box shatters.

It shatters aimlessly, in every direction at once. Inside the long frame of the fence the air divides into cells: hundreds of them, then more than he can count, each a few fingers across, irregular, many-sided, packed against one another like the stone columns that form when a lava sheet cools and cracks from the outside in, the ones the Guild geologists draw in their surveys of the old coasts. Each cell is whole inside itself. The space in it is sound. But between each cell and the next is a joint, a face where the weave has parted and not come back, and the joints catch the lamp-light and throw it back in thin edges of silver. The whole box, eight paces of it, hangs in front of him, a wall of cracked ice that is not ice, glittering at every seam.

And the cells hang from each other. That is the part the old practitioners named. Each cell touches its neighbours only at its faces, edge locked to edge, so that the whole field is a mesh of linked pieces with nothing whole running through it, link through link through link: a chain hung in the air with no hook at the top. The far tiers will see light going strange in a box in front of a boy with a sword. Dabney, inside his own reading of it, sees a chain of a thousand links holding the Ruin exactly where it was put, and the **[Vorn]** seal set at the far end of it like a lock.

The sound is the sound of a river in the week the ice goes out, a long, dry, many-voiced crackling that runs back and forth through the box and does not stop. The smell is ash and cold iron and wet slate, the smell of a quarry face after rain. The mist thins as he watches. Each seed is spent in the crack it started.

He knows what some of the tiers will call it, the ones who know enough to call anything. They will say the boy has laid a Domain: a working of Ruin let loose over an area, the way a Domain lays its owner's law across a room. They will be wrong in a way it would take an afternoon to explain. A Domain is a soul's law imposed on a place. It reaches out of its owner and covers ground, and it stays as long as the owner's will holds it there, and it is his, the way his face is his. Dabney has no Domain. There is none on his card and none in him. Talos is older than the word. It is a Work, the world's own labour, let loose on ground he has measured with his own sword, inside a fence he has cut, under a seal he has set against himself. The law belongs to Ruin, borrowed on a warrant, chained, and given back.

Here is the law of the ground, as Darius gave it to him to recite, in the order the old practitioners set it down:

Nothing continuous crosses a joint.

A road drawn outside the fence does not run on inside it; a road needs ground that goes on, and the ground stops at every face. A slope does not cross a joint. A slope is a difference between one place and the next, and across a joint there is no next place, only another cell with a floor of its own. A height measured from a floor outside the fence means nothing inside it. Measure the height of a hill across a river and you need the riverbed to agree with both banks, and inside the box every cell has its own riverbed, and none of them agree. A boundary condition laid down outside, any glyph's boundary, any lens or Path or Foundation, holds right up to the first face it meets and no further. The working itself is not touched. It is simply asked to go on being true across a gap, and true across a gap is the one thing a borrowed frame cannot be.

Plain matter crosses. A stone thrown through the box would come out the other side a stone. A hand put in would come back a hand, though its owner would feel a wrongness in it like the moment a ship drops off the top of a swell, and his ears would pop, and the hair on his arm would stand. Matter holds itself together by its own cohesion, Materia's law, carried inside every grain of it, and it brings its own frame along. So does a working rooted wholly in the body that makes it. The form was built for tournaments in the old days as much as for war, and nobody is cut by its joints. Only the borrowed things break: frames hung from somewhere else, roads drawn on ground that is not theirs, debts written against an account outside the fence.

He did not aim at the illusion. He did not need to. The figure walking round the eastern side of the floor was walking down his slope, settling from place to place along it without crossing the space between, and there is no slope inside the box to settle along, only a thousand separate floors. The light coming toward him is measuring every pull it has banked against the long level of the arena. And the arena's level stops at the fence.

He does not know what happens next. He has weighed it, and the scale leans one way, and the scale has been wrong before about things it had never seen. He stands at the mouth of his own ground with the sword point-down in front of him. He finds that he very much wants to know.

The cost arrives all at once, the way it always does with Ruin, because Ruin is paid for on delivery. The tin taste at the back of his tongue turns to ash. The flexors of both forearms ache from the elbow down as if he had cut through four oak posts instead of air, the small muscles at the base of his thumbs are cramped hard round the grip, and a fine tremor runs into his fingers and stays there. The Letting has taken a tenth of what he carries and the form has taken about as much again, and the Shell's throat feels the way a real throat feels after an hour of shouting: raw, open, unwilling. He could not do that again before the box closes. He could not pour another Letting through that throat without tearing something. And he cannot follow his own work into the ground he cut, because he would be one more borrowed thing crossing it: anything of his he sent through, any reach, any read, any weight, would stop at the first face like everything else. The Chains of Heaven hold the Ruin, and they hold him out of it, which is what Darius said they were for.

The box will not last. **[Vorn]** is already pulling at the far end, quietly, the lock drawing the chain in. A handful of breaths, he thinks; the old men counted it in breaths, and it is a sealed thing, built to end its own labour before it can learn to enjoy it. When the seal closes, the joints will heal from the faces inward and the cells will run back together into one ground, and the field will be the field again.

The one word that could walk his ground before then is the one the other boy struck out of his sentence. Balance equalises across a gap. A clause of **[Ur]** laid down on both sides of a joint would stitch it, and a road with Balance in it would cross the box cell by cell. He knows it as plainly as he knows the glyphs. It is written in the law of the form for anyone to read, and if it is found, it is found.

He keeps his breath to what he needs.

Out in the drifting thinness of what is left of the mist, the violet-white light comes on humming toward the first face of the box, and the silver edge of the nearest joint catches its colour and holds it, and the whole chain of cells, every link of it, rings once, very faintly, like a struck bell heard under water.

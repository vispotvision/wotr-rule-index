# The Muster, the Breach, and the Road North

*Four places, the same morning.*

---

## I. THE MUSTER FIELD, EIGHT LEAGUES SOUTH OF THE TENRAI CAMP

Six hundred men breathing in the cold made the sound of a tide coming in under a pier.

That was the first thing on the field and it stayed the first thing. Under it, the pressure of them, which a man feels in the chest before he counts anything, six hundred bodies packed at four feet and putting out heat that hung over the whole block in a haze and went up. Then the smell, which was horse and wet felt and old grease and the particular sourness of men who have slept in mail for eleven nights. Sight came last and came late. Wren Greymane could see about a third of his own muster from the saddle and had to take the rest on faith.

Seiji Tenrai Moto sat his horse beside him and did not look at the men.

He was twenty-nine and had the sort of face that agrees with whoever is nearest. Black hair, oiled and pulled back, cut level at the nape by somebody good. A jaw that came to a point without narrowing. Skin kept out of weather. He wore lacquered plate in a red so dark it read black until the sun found it, and the fit of it was perfect, which only ever means it was made twice.

"They are ready," Wren said.

"They are." Seiji's breath went up in a thin column and did not disperse. "The messengers went out at the second hour. Four riders, four routes, all four southern corridors. By tonight the Seat will have three separate reports that we are moving on the Stormfold road and one that we have already taken the Brine crossing, and all four will be signed by men whose hands their clerks know."

"And they will believe them."

"They will believe two of them, which is better. A man who believes everything reconsiders. A man who believes half of it commits." He turned his head. "We march directly. We put ourselves into their hands and we slaughter the last of a dead line."

*He says it the way a factor reads a manifest.*

*I have been thirteen months in this man's company and I have not once heard him raise his voice, and I have watched him give an instruction that ended a village, and the instruction was four words and one of them was please.*

"The Ice have no claim," Seiji said. "That is the whole of it and it has been the whole of it for nineteen years. A man does not become King of the North because he held a ridge and because a girl sat on a broken chair and nobody stopped her. The Tenrai recognise one heir. The Amagiri recognise one heir. The Akagane recognise one heir. Miku Tenrai Moto, trueborn to Azure Tenrai Moto, who was High King of the North while these people were still deciding what to call themselves."

Wren said nothing to that.

He had asked, once, in the fourth month, where the boy was kept. He had been answered pleasantly and at length and had come out of the conversation understanding that he had been answered pleasantly and at length and told nothing whatsoever, and he had not asked again.

He looked at his men.

He had chosen every one of them himself and he had chosen them for faith, which was the word he used in his own head and had never said aloud to any of them. Skill he had in quantity, and he had passed over men who could have held the left of any line in the North. He had taken the ones who would go on doing a thing after the thing stopped making sense.

They had played traitors perfectly. That was the part nobody would ever write down. Sixteen months of being spat at in their own villages. Eleven of them had wives who had stopped writing. Cass Holloway in the third rank had buried his mother in the autumn and had not been permitted to attend, and had asked once, and had been refused, and had said *aye* and gone back to the horses.

Three of them had been killed by their own kin and Wren had entered all three in a book he kept in his saddlebag and had told nobody about the book.

*Bram would understand the book. That is the obscene part. Of every man breathing, my brother is the one who would open it and read four pages and hand it back and say nothing and never mention it again, and Bram is at the southern reach this morning with three hundred men and a wall that has already gone.*

*I smiled at him across two hundred paces of dead men. I have never been able to account for it. I have gone at it eleven different ways and there is no version where I did not smile.*

He put his heels in and took the horse out four lengths in front of the block so that the whole muster could see him, and stood in the stirrups.

The tide-sound went out of the field.

"We rally today," Wren Greymane said.

Six hundred men waited.

"Under the banner of Greymane. The true Greymane. The true Shield of the North." His voice came off the frozen ground and went back through them. "Every one of you has been called a traitor to your face by somebody you loved. Every one of you took it. You are going to be called it again this afternoon by men on the other side of a shield wall and it is going to mean nothing at all, because they will be saying it about a thing that ended sixteen months ago and you will be looking at the end of the work."

He drew.

"RALLY."

Six hundred throats, and under them the horses going up, and under that the pressure changing across the whole block as the mass of it shifted forward one step in unison, and the ground took it and gave back a sound that Wren felt come up through the horse and into his knees.

Seiji Tenrai Moto watched him do it with his hands folded on the pommel and did not shout.

---

## II. THE SOUTHERN REACH

The wall had gone in the night, forty feet of it, on the fourth day of them working at it.

They had not thrown themselves at it. That was the thing the Seat's own officers would not believe when it was reported. They had surveyed it, and they had gone at the mortar course two feet above the footing where the frost had already been working for eleven winters, and they had done it in shifts, in silence, under a shed roof of their own making, and on the fourth night the face had come away in one piece and had taken the rampart walk with it.

Bram Greymane had three hundred men.

They had come through at the second hour and they were still coming through at the fifth, and there were two thousand two hundred of them, and they were the most disciplined thing he had ever seen in his life.

Sound first. Boots on rubble, all of them landing at the same time, which is a noise a man's body objects to before his mind gets to it. No shouting anywhere. A horn on a two-count, twice, and the whole front rank of them stepping off together.

Then pressure. The gap in the wall was twenty-two feet at its widest and everything on the far side of it wanted to be on this side, and Bram could feel that as a physical fact standing sixty feet back.

Then the smell, which was hot iron and lamp oil and something mineral and dry that came off their skin and that nobody in Kharven had a word for.

Then, last, what he could actually see, which was eleven feet of a breach with eight of them abreast in it and the geometric etching on their forearms catching the fire behind them.

That was the whole plan and it was the only plan.

Twenty-two feet. Eight abreast. Two thousand two hundred men arriving eight at a time, for as long as three hundred could hold a line eight wide and rotate it.

"THIS IS WHERE WE PROVE WHAT WE ARE."

Bram had a voice that carried without effort and he had never once been glad of it until this year. He rode the length of his own front at a walk with his shield on his arm, the same shield, the one he had thrown down at the ridge and picked back up, and the crack in the boss was still there and had been rebound twice and everyone in the line knew what it was.

"PROTECTORS. STEWARDS. DEFENDERS. THOSE ARE WORDS THEY PUT ON A CHARTER IN A WARM ROOM AND THIS MORNING WE FIND OUT WHAT THEY COST." The line was watching him and none of them were shouting yet. "WE DIE HERE OR WE LIVE TO TELL IT. THERE IS NO THIRD THING."

He got to the end of the line and came back.

"RAGE. LET THE NORTHERN COLD GUIDE YOU."

They started then.

"FOR THE GLORY OF KHARVEN."

He put the horse forward.

The first of them came out of the breach at a controlled advance with the shields overlapped and Bram took the horse in on the oblique, which is the only approach that works against a locked front, and came across the end of the line at the shoulder rather than the face, and brought the axe down across and back on the return.

The head came off the second one in the file at the base of the neck, through the trapezius and the cervical column together, and the body took two more steps in formation before the man behind it walked into its back.

The line closed. The breach kept producing.

After that it was fast and it was filthy and Bram Greymane had no idea whether it was going well.

That was the part nobody ever tells you. He could see thirty feet. He knew what was happening on his own left because he could hear Renard's voice in it, and he knew nothing at all about his right for a period he later worked out at eleven minutes and experienced as either a moment or an afternoon. Men went down in front of him and he did not know whose. The horse took something in the near hind at some point and kept going for a while and then did not.

The cold did the work that three hundred could not.

Their skin ran mineral, and the mineral ran slow, and at thirty below on a fourth night with no fires and no shelter the slow got slower. They were not weak. They were three-quarters of a beat late, all of them, and the lateness was the only currency Bram's men had. A Kharven axeman who has spent his whole life at this temperature swings at his own speed on the coldest morning of his life. An Expanse man drilled to the count of a horn does not, and a formation that cannot keep its count stops being a formation and becomes two thousand two hundred individuals in a hole in a wall.

Bram's men held the gap for four hours.

They rotated on a whistle every eighty count, front rank to the rear through the gaps in the second, and it worked for the first nine rotations and got ragged at the eleventh and by the fifteenth they were rotating men who could not lift the shield to head height and were being put back in the front anyway because the alternative was a hole.

Egil Vald went down at the twelfth rotation with a hooked blade under the cuisse and the femoral opened and he was dead in the time it takes to fill a bucket, and he made no sound about it, and the man beside him stepped across the gap without being told.

Sound. Pressure. Smell. Sight, last, and less of it as the light came up and the fires went out.

The men of the Expanse were still coming through at the ninth hour, and Bram Greymane, on foot by then with a dead horse behind him and his shield strapped to a forearm he could no longer feel, put his boot on the chest of something that had stopped moving and pulled his axe out of it and turned round to face the gap again.

## III. THE ROAD

They took the first village at the eleventh hour and Wren Greymane understood by the second one that he had misread what he had joined.

He had expected a Northern war. Two claims, two hosts, a field somewhere flat with a wood on one flank, the thing decided by whoever held together longest, and afterward the arguing and the burials and a treaty that everybody would break in nine years. He had joined a Northern war. He had spent sixteen months on his own name for it.

Kujo's generals rode at the head of the column.

There were four of them that Wren could name and two that he could not, and none of them consulted Seiji, and Seiji did not appear to expect it. They set the pace. They chose the road. Twice they went through ground that no cavalry officer alive would have taken and the ground let them.

Kaalabad rode at the front.

The horse was not a horse. It stood nineteen hands and its barrel was plated along the flanks in a pattern that was under the skin instead of on it, and the head was long and wrong and the nostril was a slit and it did not blow steam in the cold. It made no sound on frozen ground. Six hundred men behind it made the noise of six hundred men and the thing at the front of the column travelled in silence, and the silence was ahead of the noise, and that is a thing a body cannot stop noticing once it has noticed it.

He had the halberd across his back. He had not spoken to anyone since the muster.

The first village was called Ashgate and had eleven houses and a mill.

They did not fight there. Nobody in the column drew. Kaalabad turned the draconic horse off the road at a walk and went up the length of the village street once, and by the time he came back the mill was on fire from the inside without anyone having entered it, and four of the eleven houses had come off their footings and gone over sideways in the same direction, and there were people in three of them.

Wren rode past a woman standing in her own doorway with a child on her hip. She was not screaming. She was looking at the mill.

*She has seen the banner. She has seen it and she cannot read it and she is going to describe it to somebody tonight if she lives that long, and what she will describe is a grey wolf, and my grandfather is under a stone eight leagues from here.*

Beyond the second village, off toward the west where the ground fell away toward the coast road, the horizon lit.

It came without sound for two full seconds. A white swelling under the cloud base, and then a second one, and then a third further out, each one throwing the shape of the hills up in silhouette, and the sound arrived afterward as a flat concussion that came through the frozen ground into the horses' legs and made every animal in the column check at once.

Nobody in the column looked at it except Wren.

The generals did not turn their heads. Seiji had his eyes on the road. And it went on ahead of them all morning, out past the range of anything Wren understood, opening the country between the column and Kharven-Seat as a man opens a road with a plough.

## IV. KHARVEN-SEAT

Lorn Stark had been on the outer wall since the fourth hour and had watched the west go white three times before anyone came up to tell him what it was.

Nobody could tell him what it was.

He sent two runners and got back two men who had run four miles in the cold to say that the villages on the Ashgate road had stopped answering, which he already had. The third came back at the seventh hour and reported to him with his hands on his knees that a rider had come in on the Brine crossing road claiming the enemy was moving south along the Stormfold and would be at the crossing by dark.

"That is the third one of those," Lorn said. "And they do not agree."

"No, m'lord."

"Then two of them are being paid and I would like to know which two." He turned his back on the west. "Get warm. Eat something. You will be going out again."

The yard below him had filled while he was not looking at it.

They had mustered everything. That was the plain description and it did not take long to say. Every man in the Seat who could hold a line, every one of the Ashwell tenants who had come in off the hill farms in the last four days, the Stormfold party, Osric's household, the whole of the gate guard less twenty. It came to a number Lorn had already worked out twice and did not intend to work out a third time.

Sodoku came down the inner stair into the yard in full harness.

Lorn watched him from the wall.

He had been armoured since before the light, which meant he had put it on in the dark and by himself, and the mail skirt under the plate had been rebound at the left hip in a way that said somebody competent had done it recently and that it had needed doing. He crossed the yard at his own pace. The men in the yard went quiet in a rolling wave that started where he entered it and went out to the walls, and it was not the quiet of respect exactly, and Lorn had been trying to find the word for it since the boat.

Hild was at the foot of the stair with Tabitha behind her and the broken circlet in her hand instead of on her head.

He stopped in front of his daughter and stood there a moment.

Then he put a hand on the back of her head and kissed her hair, and let go, and turned away, and did not say anything that carried as far as the wall.

*Nineteen years.*

*I stood in a room with her mother and I made an arrangement about that girl and I have kept every part of it and I would like it recorded somewhere that keeping it has cost me more than any oath I have sworn to anyone with a title. And he comes up out of the sea after nineteen years and puts his hand on her head, and she lets him.*

Sodoku said one word.

"Tōga."

The thing came over the north curtain wall.

It did not climb it and it did not jump it. It came up the outside of the wall in four movements and put its forelimbs on the parapet and brought the rest of itself over, and the parapet took the weight and shed frost in a sheet, and every horse in the yard went sideways at once and two of them went down. The cold came off it in a front. Lorn, forty feet away along the wall, felt his eyes dry in the space of a breath and felt the inside of his nose freeze on the inhale and put his forearm up in front of his face without deciding to.

It came down into the yard and lowered its head and Sodoku got up onto it.

The men who had never seen it before made a sound. Not a cheer.

Edward Lambert was at Lorn's shoulder without having arrived there, which was a thing he did.

Fifty-three. Grey at the temple and nowhere else, a long dry face, a collar done up to the throat, gloves on. He had a wax tablet in his left hand and had not written on it in some time.

"He wants Sonzai," Edward said.

"Where is Sonzai."

"That is the question I have been given." Edward looked out west, where the cloud base had gone the wrong colour and stayed it. "The last confirmed sighting is nine days old and it is from a man I do not trust, and every channel I have running north of the Ashgate road stopped reporting between the fourth and the fifth hour this morning, which is either the weather or it is not."

"So you have nothing."

"I have four names and a direction." He put the tablet inside his coat. "I have been ordered to go and find him. I am fifty-three years old and I intend to do it on the Stormfold road with six riders and no banner, and if I am not back in five days you will assume the road is closed and act accordingly."

"Edward."

Lambert waited.

Lorn found that the thing he wanted to say would not come out in any arrangement that was not embarrassing, so he put his hand out instead, and Edward Lambert looked at it for a moment with an expression that gave nothing away at all, and took it, and gripped once, and let go.

Then he went down the stair and got on a horse and left through the postern with six men, and Lorn watched the seven of them out until the ground took them.

The gates came open below.

The column went out under them at a walk, the horse first and then the foot, and the thing with Sodoku on its back went out ahead of all of it and did not slow at the threshold, and the cold that came off it laid a white line down the middle of the road that the whole muster then marched along.

Lorn Stark stayed on the wall until the last of them was through. Then he went down the inner stair to the yard, and picked up the broken circlet where Hild had set it on the mounting block and forgotten it, and put it in his belt.

## Notes (author-facing, not canon)

**PC handling.** Sodoku is rendered entirely from outside, in Lorn Stark's POV. No interiority, no dialogue beyond the one word you gave me, no thought attributed to him. **One line I could not use as written.** Your paste has *Sodoku looked at Kujo*. Kujo is at the root of the wound in the Void and Sodoku is in a yard at Kharven-Seat, so I gave him the west horizon and the wrong-coloured cloud instead. If you meant something else by it, tell me and I will put it back.

**Mass Combat Guide is in force for section II.** Sensory hierarchy inverted to sound, pressure, smell, sight. Bram never knows how the battle is going: he sees thirty feet, hears Renard on his left, and has no information about his right for eleven minutes that he experiences as either a moment or an afternoon. Three ordinary soldiers named across the whole piece and no more: Cass Holloway, Egil Vald, and Renard by voice only.

**The breach is the tactic.** Twenty-two feet of gap, eight abreast, so two thousand two hundred arrive eight at a time for as long as three hundred can hold eight wide and rotate. Whistle every eighty count, front to rear through the gaps in the second. Nine clean rotations, ragged at eleven, and by the fifteenth they are putting men back in the front who cannot lift a shield to head height, because the alternative is a hole.

**Hobgoblins as canon has them, which is scarier than a horde.** They are the disciplined ones.

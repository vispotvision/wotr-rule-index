# The Undamped Thing

*A keel north of the Sound. The ash at Sum-gol. The same hour.*

---

## I

The boy had been asleep for two hours and eleven minutes and Alys Fenwick had been counting because that was what she did with the quiet parts.

Tar and wet rope. Herring in the bilge from the load before this one, four days out and gone sour in the cold, and under that the ammoniac smell of a clout that wanted changing and would want it for another hour because she was not going to wake him for it. The keel ran flat and slow with the tide under her and the boom creaking on every third swell, and the whole boat smelled of a working life and nothing in it belonged to anybody important.

Dabney Lambert was four months old and lay in the fish crate she had lined with her own second petticoat, and he had his fists up beside his ears the way they all do.

He came awake at once.

There was no fretting first. That was the thing she would tell the Bench afterward, eleven times, in the same words, because it was the only part of the night she was certain of. A child stirs. A child works up to it, a whinge and a kick and a false start, and this one went from sleeping to a full cry in less than a second and the cry started at the top.

Phonated first, and she knew that sound as well as her own name. Two hundred and fifty to seven hundred cycles, the ordinary distress of an ordinary infant, cyclic on the breath, expiration and inspiration alternating the way a bellows works.

It went through the ceiling in about four seconds.

Hyperphonation. She had no name for it and she had heard it twice in her life, both times in a room she had left afterward.

The pitch climbed past anything a four-month-old chest ought to produce and went on climbing, and the register broke, the way a fiddle breaks when a hand drives it above the string, and the harmonic structure widened until the source and the throat carrying it stopped being separable things. A thousand cycles. More. Dysphonic at the edges, turbulence laid over the tone where the folds had stopped closing cleanly, and the amplitude jumping from one breath to the next in a way that put her own pulse rate up before she had decided anything about it. That is what the sound is built to do. It is tuned to be unbearable, at exactly the frequencies an adult ear cannot ignore, because an infant that could be ignored would not have descendants.

Alys Fenwick had buried three of her own and had nursed nine others and had heard colic and hunger and croup and the cry a child makes over a broken arm.

*This is grief.*

*He is four months old and he cannot know a single thing and that is a grieving. I have made that sound myself. I made it in a doorway in the third year of the Thin Weeks and my husband had to hold me up.*

She got him out of the crate and against her shoulder and he arched away from her, which they do not do, and his whole small body went rigid from the crown to the heels and stayed rigid.

*Thousands of miles.*

She had no way to think that thought and it arrived anyway, whole, out of nowhere and in nobody's voice, and she would never be able to account for it.

---

What had happened to him had happened in a valley he would never see.

An anchor at the Attraction Layer is a bound thing. It sits in the Crystal at a lower state than the two souls it joins would occupy apart, and the difference is what holds it there, and holding costs nothing because a bound state is the cheaper arrangement. That is the whole of why love does not tire anybody. It is downhill.

Break the binding and the constituents do not simply stop. They go up into the continuum, unbound, free to take any value at all, and everything that was holding them at the low state comes out.

Charles Lambert had gone into the continuum at some point in the last minute.

The strands that had been him came apart out of the bundle and expanded into the Plane at large, unfiled, unweighed, with no record anywhere to say which of them had been which. And at the other end of an anchor that had never been written down in any book, in a fish crate, on a keel north of the Sound, a four-month-old took the entire release of it.

He had no idea what it was. He never would.

He only had the amount.

Every joule of it. The binding energy of a bond that had been carrying itself downhill for four months, released in one direction into the only thing still attached to the other end.

---

The lamp went out.

Nothing blew it. It went out with the flame standing straight and the wick untouched, and the cold that came into that little cabin came in from every side at once and had a grain to it, and Alys Fenwick, who had never in her life stood within a mile of a Well, felt the air get thick enough to lean on.

Aetheric Density in that cabin went past anything the Guild meters into a district on a feast day, in a place with no standpipe and no main and no sealed conduit within forty miles, and the Residue of it would still be readable off the timbers in nine years by anybody who thought to look.

Because there was nothing to stop it.

A practitioner is a damped instrument. Sixteen Stages of Temperance exist to put resistance into the line, so that what comes out of a soul comes out at a rate the body chose and stops when the body stops driving it. Every hour of training any Moto or any Mahuo or any Court practitioner has ever done is the installation of friction. It is why a trained man can be furious in a room and the room survives it.

Dabney Lambert had no Stage. No Temperance floor, no suppression, no trained resistance anywhere in him, nothing between what he felt and what came out of him.

An undamped thing has no ceiling of its own. Its amplitude is set entirely by what is driving it, and it will keep going after the driving stops, and what was driving this had been a man's whole life coming off its anchor.

Coherence held anyway. That was the part nobody would believe. A soul that size taking that input should have come apart along its own Shell in under a second, and his did not, and there is only one class of thing on record that does not, and the Court has had a word for that class for four hundred years and has never once had to use it.

The threads in that cabin went visible.

Alys saw them and had no word for them and afterward described them to the Bench as wet hair in water, which the Bench recorded without comment. They stood out from the child in the crate and from her own hands and from the boom and the crate and the herring in the bilge, thousands of them, each one a small unique thing that was nothing on its own, and they were all leaning.

Toward him. Bending, over their whole length, out to the walls of the cabin and through them and past that.

She did not know how far past.

---

He got his hands up.

That was the part that broke her. He was rigid and screaming at the top of a register that a body that size is not built to reach and he had been at it for eleven seconds without a proper breath, and he got both arms up off his chest and out and open, high, the way they do when they want lifting, and it was hard.

She could see it was hard. His whole frame was locked and the arms came up against all of it, shaking, and stayed up.

And his eyes opened.

They had been brown when Charles put him into her arms in the lower chapel at Altherion and told her the sum and did not tell her the reason. They had gone gold once, for about four seconds, and the old man in the doorway had seen it and had written nothing down.

They were gold now and they stayed gold, and there was a ring inside the gold, and the ring was turning.

Fate bent around a four-month-old child on a cargo boat and the boat came up level under her feet in a swell that should have put her against the bulkhead.

*Whatever this is, it is nobody's fault and there is nothing in it that he did.*

*And every single person who finds out about it is going to want him.*

Alys Fenwick sat down on the deck with her back against the crate and her arms around the fish box and the child inside it and put her body between him and the door.

---

## II

Eleven hundred miles away and a plane sideways from any of it, the ash went still.

Darius Ignatius Sanctus Sanctorum had been standing in it for a stretch of time he had stopped numbering, and his right hand had been open in front of him for the last four seconds of it, and there was nothing in the hand.

He looked at the hand.

*Something has just come out of me.*

He could account for the sensation precisely, which was the horror of it. No fatigue in it anywhere, and none of the burn of the seventh Fist held too long. A subtraction, clean, made somewhere he could not reach, of a quantity he had never been shown the figure for, and it had left through a channel he had carried since he was thirty-five years old and had never once been told the far end of.

The ash around his boots had frozen into a ring, eleven feet across, and the frost on it was going outward and not inward.

"Aye," said Gimbzo.

He was sitting on nothing with Tsuiji across his knees and the linen unwound to the beard of the blade, and he had not moved, and he was not looking at the axe.

"That was it."

Darius said nothing for a while.

"The trial."

"Aye."

"I was not told."

"No."

"Because I would have prepared a position."

"Because you'd have prepared a position," Gimbzo agreed, "and the whole of the twelfth is that a man in your office does not get to. HOO." He scratched the side of his jaw with a thumb. "You've spent it. You didn't choose the hour, you didn't choose the amount, you didn't know the man's face and you couldn't have stopped it if you'd put both hands on it. That's the trial. There's nothing to pass. There's only whether you're still standing after."

*Ten years.*

*They put a chain over his head and walked us out through different doors and I signed second, and I have thought about him perhaps forty times in ten years, and I have never once been permitted to ask after him, and the sum of my knowledge is a face across a table for eleven minutes and a hand I did not shake.*

*And he has just spent me, and he did it correctly, and he did it without knowing whether there was anybody on this end.*

"He is dead," Darius said.

"Aye."

"Then the office is one man."

"Aye. And a hand wants two." Gimbzo turned the axe over on his knees. "You'll find out what that costs. Not today."

The old man got up.

He did it without weight going anywhere, which he always did, and standing he stopped resembling a man who happened to be enormous.

"You're ready to go back."

Darius looked at the frozen ring in the ash and did not answer.

"Take the teachings with you," Gimbzo said. "All seven and the matrix under them and the reason the six agree to hold still. You don't get to keep them. That was never the arrangement, and if you thought it was, you weren't listening in the fourth month."

"To whom."

"The Paragon of Urion."

The ash under Darius's boots let go of both feet at once.

"There's a child," said Gimbzo. "Somebody's child, somewhere, and he's already opened and I felt it open about four seconds before you did, which is why I'm on my feet. You'll teach him the Ways. You'll teach him what a Work is and what a Work costs and why the seven stand in the order they stand in, and you'll do it while he is still small enough to be taught anything at all, because the alternative is a Paragon who learns it from the world instead."

"And the protection."

"That's the other half and it's the harder half." Gimbzo looked at him properly. "The Regent will come for that child, and fear will be no part of it. He'll come because a Paragon of Balance is the one arrangement in four worlds that can't be argued into being void, and he knows it, and he has been assembling the conditions for one all season without meaning to. It's possible he already has the name. It's possible he had it before you did."

"Then I am already late."

"You've been late since the day they gave you the seat and you refused it." No heat in it. "You're the Archpaladin of Sanctum Lux and the Sun of the Judger whether you'll be invested in it or not, and you've spent eleven years writing the apparatus that finds the Paragon and refusing to be the man who uses it. Well. It's found. You've no apparatus to hide behind and no seat to refuse."

Kizami came down the column with too many joints going at once and landed without disturbing the ash.

*He is going to ask why the Judger gave the Ways to a man who will not take an office, and Gimbzo will not tell him, and I have read the subsection and I am not going to tell him either.*

"The Judger put the Ways of Hataraki in your hands," said Gimbzo, "and you have never once asked me why. I'll answer the question you didn't ask, and then you'll go."

He put Tsuiji over his shoulder.

"Because a man who wants the seat teaches to make more of himself. You don't want it. You'll teach it and hand it over and walk off, and that's the only kind of teacher a Paragon can survive."

The gate opened behind Darius without anyone opening it.

He turned his head and looked at it, and the light coming through was the wrong light, and the sound coming through was rigging and cold water and a child at the very top of his register.

Darius Ignatius Sanctus Sanctorum put his right hand flat on his own sternum, where the subtraction had gone out, and held it there a moment, and then stepped through.

# The Second Law, Held Open

*The narrows. Continuous.*

---

The leak did not stop when he closed his hand.

Kwon Mu-jin understood that about four seconds after he had congratulated himself on stopping it, which was the correct interval, because that is how long it takes a man to notice that a thing he has taken credit for has gone on happening quietly behind him.

It came off him in the seams. Out of the right shoulder where the arms stood, out of the sternum, out of the sockets of both eyes and the hinge of the jaw, and it went into the road as vapour and as light and as nothing at all, and it was not one kind of thing. Several hundred bolts of another man's essence had gone into his eye and had not been digested, and what came out of him now carried their signatures alongside his own, and there were others in it that he did not recognise and had not put there.

Every practitioner on that road who could read anything at all was looking at the richest field any of them would see in their lives.

*Saturation. That is my fault and it has been my fault for ninety seconds.*

*Aetheric Residue from the aurora, from the shield, from four hundred paces of men dying with their workings half made, from whatever the old man did in the sky at the ninth hour, and now from me, and the density in this valley is a hundred times what the district main at Sum-gol carries on a feast day. I have flooded a room and I am standing in it with a man who has spent his whole life short of a drink.*

Down the road behind him, at the ditch bank, the shield with the arm still strapped in it had begun to rime over.

Kaalabad stopped pulling.

That was the tell and Mu-jin caught it in the first half-second, because a working that has been running for eleven hours leaves a hole in the room's texture when it stops, and the hole arrived before anything came to fill it.

The centre went out. The pull went out. The spiral the bound arms had turned it into unwound and dissipated with no ceremony whatsoever, and the man let go of the whole architecture he had been holding since dawn and did not attempt to hold anything else.

*He has stopped fighting me.*

*A man who stops fighting the man in front of him has decided the man in front of him is a feature of the ground. He is going to work on the ground.*

He had time for that one sentence and then the air went white.

---

The water came out of it first.

No fog anywhere, and nothing settling on a surface. It went straight from vapour into ice without visiting liquid on the way, in the middle of the air, at a height of six feet, all across the narrows at once. Crystals seeded on nothing. They came up in plates and columns and long six-armed things, hanging where they had formed with no wind to move them, so that the whole four hundred paces of road filled from the ground to the height of a man with a slow-turning suspension of ice that caught what light was left and cut it into pieces.

Deposition. Vapour to solid, no intermediate, and every gram of it that changed state gave up its latent heat at the moment of crystallising.

It went into Kaalabad.

Every crystal that formed handed him what it had been carrying. A pound of water changing to ice in open air surrenders enough to hold a kettle at the boil for a quarter of an hour, and there were tons of it in the narrows, and it was all arriving at one address in the same second and it went on arriving.

Mu-jin watched him take it. That was the mechanism and it was in front of him and it was elegant enough that some detached part of him wanted to write it down. The law was not making cold. Cold is the absence of a thing and no law has ever been written that produces an absence. The law was closing the ways in which heat is permitted to be stored in a volume, one at a time, until motion had nowhere left to sit and had to leave, and every joule that came out of the air came out of it into the only object present that had been shaped to accept it.

Heat death, with a hand on the arrow.

That was the whole of Liminia and Mu-jin had read the entry twice as a first-year and had understood exactly none of it. Absence, the paper said, and void, and the end state. He had pictured emptiness. What was standing on the road in front of him was the second law with somebody holding it open at the correct page and refusing to let it turn back, and the reason it looked like emptiness was that it was the shape everything takes on the way to the same temperature.

The black came in behind it.

It amassed from the road upward, and it had no interior, and it had no edge that Mu-jin's eye could find twice in the same place. Light going into it did not come back out. Light going past it came out wrong. The remaining daylight bent as it crossed the boundary and separated as it bent, so that the shapes of two thousand men lying in the mud stood in the ice at three different offsets in three different colours, red furthest, blue least, every man on the road duplicated into a spectrum of himself.

*Refraction across a density gradient. The whole valley has become a lens and I am standing inside the glass.*

The temperature went past the point where a body reports it.

Mu-jin's fingertips stopped sending. The left hand first, then the face, then the ears, and he knew the sequence because he had grown up eleven miles from a mountain and had helped carry men down off it. Under the skin it would be running the same way it always ran: extracellular water crystallising first in the tissue spaces, drawing the water out of the cells behind it by simple osmotic difference, the cells shrinking and concentrating and dying of thirst in the middle of an ice field.

It was already too late in three fingers of his left hand and he knew that too. Once the water inside a cell has gone, nothing brings that cell back, and the pain of finding out arrives four to eight hours after the warmth does, and half the men lying in the ice on that road would lose something in the night from what was happening to them right now while they felt almost nothing at all.

The right arm went first and he did not feel any of it.

No circulation to withdraw, because it had never withdrawn anything since the summer, and no sensation to lose. It hung at his side and froze from the fingertips to the elbow while the arms of light stood out of the shoulder above it, and he found out about it later, and by then it did not matter.

Four hundred paces up the road, where a man in Moto harness had been getting eleven hundred men onto their feet and moving them south, the ice reached the back of the column and the column stopped being able to see. Mu-jin registered that and could do nothing with it and put it down.

Kaalabad tilted his head back.

*Oh.*

*Oh, that is what it was. Forty years and a hundred sites and every one of them a mouthful, and I had begun to believe the thirst was simply what a man of my kind is. It is not. It was the district. It was every district. I have been a lamp on a starved main since I was nineteen years old and this boy has opened every standpipe in the world in one place and stood in the middle of it.*

The gauntlet on his right hand came apart.

The swelling that had been building since the ditch bank reversed inside two seconds. The plate that had stood off the wrist by a finger's width let go entirely and fell in pieces into the ice, and the hand under it was a hand again, and he opened it and closed it twice and looked at it.

He said something then that Mu-jin heard as three syllables and did not catch, and there was nothing triumphant in the delivery of it. It had the flatness of a man confirming a figure he had been given years ago and had privately doubted ever since.

Ardency came up in him.

Mu-jin felt the figure move and had no instrument to put a number on it and did not need one. Every practitioner alive carries a reading on that axis and most of them go their whole lives without it changing by a Grade, and whatever Kaalabad had carried onto this road at the ninth hour, he was not carrying it now.

*Depth. It is Depth and it is Flux behind it, and I have been reading the wrong number all summer.*

*He was borrowing. That is the whole of what he was doing in the orchard and it is why I lived through it. A man borrowing from a Wellspring has to carry what he draws and the carrying is the ceiling. In this field he is not borrowing. He has stopped translating and started speaking, and the ceiling is now whatever is in the room, and I put what is in the room here.* The corona around him stopped being violet and became a colour that the ice took and multiplied and threw around the whole valley in facets, and every crystal hanging in four hundred paces of air had a small purple man standing inside it.

Then the ground.

---

The Ashgate valley sits on a basalt shelf and the shelf is full of iron.

Mu-jin did not have it in those words. He had the working leaving the air and going down, and the note in the ground he had walked eleven miles toward changing key, and the whole continent under his boots choosing that instant to move.

The rock went below the point where the iron in it changes its mind.

Every grain of it, across the shelf, in the same instant. The crystal system folded from cubic to monoclinic and the easy line of every grain swung off its old orientation onto a new one, and the rock twinned against itself as it went, ridging and buckling along a million internal boundaries at a scale no eye could see and every boot could feel. The conductivity of the shelf fell through two orders of magnitude in about a second.

The ley in that valley went dark.

That was the part that mattered and Mu-jin found it out through his shoulder.

The nine arms standing out of his right side lost their line. An Animatria form lives as long as the line to its caster holds, and the line had been running through the ground because everything in that valley ran through the ground, and the medium had stopped carrying. The letters began to drift. They came off the surfaces of the arms in ones and twos and then in whole clauses, and the hands came apart at the knuckles first, and Mu-jin felt each one go as a specific thing, the way a man feels a rope part strand by strand in his grip.

He had held four before, once, in a bad month, at a cost he had entered in his own record as *not to be repeated*. He had held nine here for less than a minute and the ground had taken them off him.

Then Kaalabad took hold of all of it.

The influence had never been on the ley alone. It went into every grain of iron in the shelf, in the ore in the ground and the nails in the carts and the mail on two thousand men, and it pulled, and the ground of the Ashgate valley came apart in waves.

Nothing cracked. It rolled. The road and the fields on both sides of it lifted in long rolling ridges eleven feet high and moving, coming out from under the black in ranks, throwing frozen mud and buried stone and the corduroy of split logs and the dead, and the ridges reached the ditch and filled it and went on across the fields toward Kharven-Seat, and where they passed the ground did not come back down flat.

Kaalabad roared.

The sound of it and the colour of it arrived together and the ice took them both, and the whole hanging suspension across four hundred paces rang at one pitch and shattered inward.

He cut.

Diagonal, left shoulder to right foot, the whole of Zarakal in a right hand that worked again, and the cut did not travel through the distance between them. It closed the distance and arrived, and the space it had crossed had not been consulted. Compression, refined past the point where the working needs a path, and the cost of delivering it at four hundred paces and at four feet was now the same cost.

Mu-jin went sideways.

He did it without his feet, because there were no feet available. The ground under him was a wave in motion and the ridge was already lifting his left boot off the ground when the cut came, and there was nothing to push against in any direction.

He had perhaps a fifth of a second and he spent all of it on the only asset he had, which was the thing he had spent the last ninety seconds being ashamed of.

So he stopped holding the leak.

He opened the seams on his right side all the way and let everything that had been coming out of him quietly come out of him at once, all of it, in one direction, out of the shoulder and the ribs and the hip. Several hundred bolts of another man's essence and his own and the ones he had not recognised, vented in a hard jet from one flank, and every ounce of it that left him going one way put him the other.

There was no elegance in it whatsoever. It is what a squid does. It is what a pump does. Mass out of the back, and the body goes forward, and the only thing it asks is that you have something to throw and that you do not care about getting it back.

He went eleven feet across the front of the cut with the ridge still rising under him, and the vent tore his coat off his right side from the collar to the hem and took the skin off the back of his hand where the sleeve went, and he did not slow down and could not have.

Zarakal went through the place he had been and through the wave behind it and through the ground under that, and the ridge it was riding split lengthwise for a hundred and forty feet and both halves fell away from the line of the cut, and everything inside that line simply had no further account.

Mu-jin came down on the far side on one knee in broken ice with the jet still venting off his right hip, and turned his head, and his eye tracked the whole length of the cut in about a quarter of a second and took the black at the end of it and did not blink.

He put the frozen right hand flat on the moving ground to steady himself, and it made a sound, and he set his left and stood up.

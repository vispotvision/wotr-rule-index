Her face, round and dark-eyed and carrying the particular expression of a nine-year-old who has learned that the world contains more information than she has categories for, turned far enough to catch the crowd's attention passing over them like a weather system. The hostility was not aimed at her. She could feel that. Children parsed intention at an animal level, below language, below reason. She was not the target. But the person who was the target was walking behind her, and the person who was the target was also the person whose hand had been on her head that morning, whose coat she had gripped in the dark, whose leg she had clung to when the world produced a fox-eared stranger in her bed.

"Why are they mad?" she asked.

The question was not rhetorical. It was not performing innocence. It was the genuine interrogation of a child who had observed a phenomenon and wanted the causal mechanism explained, the way children ask why is the sky blue or why do dogs bark at strangers — not because they expect a satisfying answer but because asking is how nine-year-olds process the fact that the world contains things they cannot yet categorize.

Yōko hesitated. The hesitation lasted 1.2 seconds, which was long enough for Temür to notice and short enough that Emira did not.

*Because they know what he is,* Temür thought.

But Yōko only said, "Because people are ugly when they're scared."

That made Temür's eyes shift to her for half a second.

Not because the answer was wrong. Because it was incomplete.

Fear was part of it. Fear was always part of it. The crowd's hostility ran on fear the way a furnace ran on fuel — fear of what he represented, fear of what he had done, fear of the proximity of someone who had crossed a line that most people needed to believe was uncrossable because the belief in uncrossable lines was what allowed them to sleep in houses with unlocked doors and send their children to play in streets where no one was calculating the force required to break a five-year-old's collarbone.

But fear was not all of it.

They hated him because the stories were true enough.

He had killed them. Men. Women. Children.

Not in madness. Not in confusion. Not in some heat-blind frenzy that dissolved the border between action and memory and produced the kind of violence that survivors described as I don't know what happened, it was like I wasn't there, like someone else was using my body. He had been there. He had been present for every verdict. He had ranked every target. He had compressed every strike. He had felt the Coagulatio engage and the Fractura guide and the Judicium confirm, and the confirmation had been correct, every time, technically correct, structurally correct, and the correctness had been the scaffolding on which he hung the thing he was actually doing, which was killing people because killing people felt like the world owing him less.

He had wanted it.

That was the part he never bothered lying to himself about.

He had built his reasons afterward. Crafted them carefully, the way an architect crafts load-bearing walls — not to hide the structure but to make the structure look intentional. Bloodline guilt. Historical debt. Inherited atrocity. Corrective violence. The old arithmetic of the world: if one branch rotted deeply enough, the tree could not be spared. He had told himself their deaths were structure, not cruelty. Necessity, not hunger. Verdict, not indulgence.

But beneath every justification was a truth uglier for how simple it was.

He had wanted them dead.

He had wanted the line ended. Wanted the blood punished for carrying the wrong name. Wanted their cries, their fear, their pleading, because somewhere in the fracture of his grief — somewhere between the corridor and the settlement and the exile — he had decided that pain moved cleanest when pushed outward, that grief was a pressure and pressure needed a direction and the Tenrai were the direction, and the direction had included people whose only crime was being born into the same family tree as the man who had planned Muken's assassination, and the crime of birth was not a crime at all, and he had known that, and he had killed them anyway.

So when the town glared at him with hatred, what exactly was he supposed to feel?

Injustice? Wounded pride? Shame that the world had dared to judge something he himself had chosen?

Nothing in him reached for any of that. The reaching mechanism had been disconnected. The circuit that connected external judgment to internal response had been severed somewhere around the third year of exile, when the fifth township had thrown the fifth round of stones and the fifth set of whispers had called him the same names and the repetition had filed the neural pathway smooth until the pathway stopped conducting.

A rotten strip of vegetable — cabbage, by the smell — struck his boot and stuck to the toe cap with the adhesive quality of something that had been decomposing for long enough to develop structural properties.

Someone from the crowd shouted, "Murderer!"

The word traveled across the street and reached every ear at approximately the same time, and the effect was catalytic — the first shout gave permission to the second, the second to the third, the accumulated social pressure of a crowd that had been holding its hostility behind its teeth now releasing it in a cascade.

"Monster!"

"Butcher!"

A third voice, cracking with the righteous disgust of someone who had been rehearsing this sentence since the whispers started and had finally found the timing: "And he drags a beastkin and a child with him. Disgusting."

At that, one woman near the well stepped forward.

She was middle-aged, maybe forty-five, with the weathered hands of a laundress and the squared shoulders of someone who had been carrying things heavier than laundry for long enough that her skeleton had remodeled to accommodate the load. Her face carried the particular expression of moral urgency that arrived when someone's conscience exceeded their caution by enough of a margin that the body moved before the survival instinct could file an objection.

"You there," she called to Yōko. Not quite able to look Temür in the eyes. The words aimed at the beastkin because the beastkin was the available surface — the person in the trio who might respond to concern without turning the response into a threat. "Girl. You don't have to go with him."

Yōko stopped.

Temür did not.

He only slowed enough that the space between them did not break, the distance contracting from six feet to four with the ease of a man who had been managing group proximity in hostile environments for years and could modulate the distance between himself and his charges without conscious thought.

The woman swallowed. The swallowing was visible — the throat moving, the jaw adjusting — and the visibility was the tell that said her courage was a finite resource and the resource was being spent faster than it was being generated. "If he's forcing you—"

"He isn't," Yōko said.

The answer came flat. No warmth. No gratitude. No performed relief at being offered an exit. The flatness was not rudeness. It was the specific vocal register of a woman who had been offered rescue by strangers before and had learned that rescue offered by strangers came with terms, and the terms were never stated at the point of offer, and the terms were always worse than whatever the stranger thought they were rescuing you from.

*She thinks she is being brave. She thinks she is saving me. She cannot look at him. She cannot even say his name. She is speaking to the beastkin because the beastkin is the safe one, the approachable one, the one whose ears and tail make her look like something that needs rescuing, and the assumption that I need rescuing is the same assumption that put me in a pen, because the world has two categories for beastkin — victim or animal — and neither category allows for the possibility that I am standing here because I chose to stand here, because a boy broke my lock and bought me bread and called me a tool and meant please stay, and staying was my decision, and my decision does not require this woman's permission or her pity or her carefully performed moral courage that evaporates the moment she has to look at the person she is actually afraid of.*

The woman frowned. The frown carried the specific quality of someone whose narrative had been contradicted by reality and who was now recalculating. "You don't need to protect him."

Yōko's tail gave one sharp lash behind her. The lash was involuntary — the limbic system expressing irritation through the channel it controlled — and the sharpness of it made the woman take a half-step backward before she caught herself.

"I'm not," Yōko said, and the two words carried enough finality that the conversation's structural integrity collapsed, and the woman's mouth opened and closed and opened again and nothing came out because the rebuttal she had prepared required Yōko to be grateful and Yōko had refused the premise.

Emira pressed closer into Yōko's arms. The nine-year-old's face was turned toward the woman, watching, processing, cataloguing the interaction with the silent intensity of a child who had spent five years learning to read adult conflicts for the information they contained about whether the world was about to get worse.

The woman looked at Temür then. Finally. Truly looked.

Her face went pale.

The transition was physiological — blood retreating from the dermal capillaries of the cheeks and forehead in response to a threat signal that the body had processed before the mind finished interpreting it. The threat signal was not violence. Temür was not moving. Temür was not speaking. Temür was standing six feet away with his hands at his sides and his blade at his hip and his face carrying the absolute zero of a man who had reduced violence to function so thoroughly that the violence no longer needed to be displayed because the potential was ambient, the way heat was ambient around a forge that had been burning for years.

There was no rage in her after that. Only the sudden helplessness of someone who realized too late that the thing she meant to confront was not built like other people.

Temür held her gaze.

No words. No threat spoken aloud.

The woman stepped back first.

"Keep moving," Yōko muttered under her breath.

They did.

The road toward the gate sloped downward through the lower quarter, past shuttered homes and low market stalls where gossip moved faster than trade. More people had heard by then. The whispers came in waves as they crossed through the district, each wave louder than the last, each wave carrying more specific accusations as the rumor network refined its data and the facts — real and imagined — achieved consensus.

"That's Temür Büri."

"The prince?"

"No. Not a prince. A curse."

"I heard he butchered a whole house."

"They say he didn't even spare the little ones."

"Then why's the child with him?"

"Why's the beastkin?"

The questions were not asked to be answered. They were asked to be performed. The asking was the point. The asking said: we see what is happening and we are naming it, and the naming gives us the feeling of having done something about it without requiring us to actually do anything, and the gap between naming and doing is the gap where most moral courage lives and dies.

Yōko clicked her tongue. The sound was sharp, percussive, the beastkin equivalent of an eye-roll, and she lowered her face closer to Emira's. "Ignore them."

"I'm trying," Emira said. Quieter than before. Smaller.

They passed a bakery. The baker — a man with forearms like hams and a face permanently flushed from oven-heat — stood in his doorway and watched them pass and said nothing, but his hand found the door's edge and pulled it two inches closer to closed, and the two inches said everything the mouth did not.

They passed a cobbler's shop. The cobbler's wife, a thin woman with pins in her hair and pins in her apron and the harried expression of someone who was always in the middle of something, looked up from her work and saw Temür and dropped a shoe.

The shoe hit the cobblestone. The sound was small. The woman did not pick it up.

Emira finally spoke loud enough for Temür to hear. "Are they talking about him?"

Yōko was quiet for a moment. The moment lasted three steps. Three steps was a long time when a child was waiting for an answer that would determine how much of the world she was allowed to trust.

"Yes."

"Did he do it?"

Yōko's steps slowed. Not a stop. A deceleration. The deceleration of someone whose body was trying to buy time for a mouth that had not yet decided which version of the truth to dispense.

Temür looked ahead. Expression unchanged. Stride unchanged. The back of his coat still carrying the bruised-plum stain. The cabbage strip still adhering to his boot. His breathing even. His hands at his sides.

*She is going to answer. She is going to answer honestly or she is going to lie, and either way the answer will do something to the architecture of a nine-year-old's understanding of the world that cannot be undone. If she lies, Emira learns that the people who hold her will not tell her the truth when the truth is large enough to matter. If she tells the truth, Emira learns that the person whose leg she gripped this morning killed children, and the learning will sit inside her and grow and eventually produce a question that no answer can satisfy, which is: how can the same hand that rests on my head be the hand that did that.*

Yōko surprised him.

"Yes," she said. Slowly. Carefully. The way someone sets down something fragile — not dropping it, not placing it carelessly, but lowering it with both hands and both eyes and the full weight of her attention to ensure that the setting-down did not become a breaking. "He did terrible things."

Emira looked down. Her feet on the cobblestone. The mud at the edges of the road. The distance between one step and the next.

"Then why are we with him?"

Yōko's voice softened. Not into false comfort. Not into the saccharine register that adults used when they were trying to make ugly information sound palatable. Into something harder and more honest — the voice of a woman who had stopped believing in palatable years ago and was now offering the only thing she had left, which was the truth served without dressing.

"Because terrible people and hurt people are not always separate. And because sometimes someone can still choose what they become next."

Temür almost spoke.

Almost.

The impulse arrived at the base of his throat and sat there for 0.6 seconds — a sentence forming in the dark behind his teeth, pushing against the closure, testing the seal. The sentence was going to be a correction. The sentence was going to say: I have not chosen anything. I am not becoming anything. I am the verdict that was delivered and the verdict does not change.

But the impulse died before it reached his tongue.

He did not know whether he disliked what she said because it was naïve, or because some splinter of him — lodged deep in the place where the Zasag could not reach because the Zasag had not been built to reach the places where hope lived — understood that he did not entirely want it to be.

The gate appeared ahead of them at the bottom of the quarter's slope.

Four guards. Too many for routine. Too few for certainty.

One rested his hand near the shaft of his spear — a man in his late twenties with the thin mustache and wide-set eyes of someone who had been assigned to gate duty because he was not quite good enough for patrol and not quite bad enough to dismiss, the perfect middle of military mediocrity. Another kept glancing between Yōko, Emira, and Temür with the rapid eye-movement of a man who was trying to construct a narrative from the available information and finding that the available information refused to organize itself into any of the narratives he had rehearsed.

The oldest guard was the one who mattered.

Fifty. Maybe older. A face like a knuckle — all ridges and compression and the particular quality of skin that had been weathered past the point where weather could damage it further. His armor was town militia standard — half-plate over leather, maintained but not polished, the maintenance of a man who respected his equipment without loving it. His eyes were dark, flat, and carried the specific weight of someone who had been told what was coming and had spent the last fifteen minutes deciding how to handle it and had arrived at the decision that handling it required him to not die.

"Halt."

Temür stopped.

Yōko stopped too, turning her body so that Emira remained behind the line of her.

"Name," the oldest guard said.

"Temür Büri."

The man's jaw flexed. The flexing was the only movement in his face. Everything else locked — the eyes, the mouth, the muscles of the brow. The lock was not fear. It was the controlled immobility of a man who was processing information that carried significant personal risk and was managing the processing through physical stillness because the alternative was physical reaction and physical reaction in the presence of this particular individual was a coin flip between survival and the kind of report that gets filed posthumously.

Another guard — the one with the thin mustache — muttered, "Gods."

The oldest one ignored him. "We've heard things."

"That makes you like everyone else in this town," Temür said.

The guard's frown was professional. Managed. The frown of a man who had been frowned at by his superiors enough times to have developed a competent imitation. "The beastkin and the child. Are they with you willingly?"

Yōko opened her mouth, but Temür answered first.

"Yes."

The answer came without pause, without hedging, without the verbal cues that people produced when they were constructing a lie or qualifying a truth. Just the word. Flat. Complete. Occupying the entire available space without leaving room for follow-up.

The guard looked at Yōko. "Is that true?"

Yōko stared right back at him. Her amber-brown eyes held steady. Her ears were up. Her tail was still. "Would I still be standing here if it wasn't?"

One of the younger guards gave a nervous huff that might have been a laugh if he were not too afraid to commit to it. The sound came out of his nose and died in the air between his face and the spear shaft he was gripping.

The captain did not smile.

He looked at Emira. The nine-year-old shrank behind Yōko, and the shrinking was not fear of the guard but the instinct of a child who had learned that adult attention was unpredictable and the safest response to unpredictable attention was to become smaller until the attention moved somewhere else.

"And the girl?"

Temür saw the question forming before the man asked it. Saw the shape of his doubt — not genuine concern but the performance of concern, the moral arithmetic of a man trying to find a configuration that would let him feel righteous without requiring him to do anything that might produce a report that included the phrase and then he drew his weapon.

The guard took a breath. "If either of them are under duress, we can—"

Temür stepped forward.

One step. Just one.

Not enough to close distance. Not enough to constitute advance. Just enough to change the geometry of the conversation by four feet, which was enough to make every guard at the gate go rigid simultaneously, which was the point, because the step was not movement, it was language, and the language said: this conversation is approaching a boundary that you do not want to cross.

"No," Temür said. Quiet. "You can open the gate."

The silence that followed was heavier than shouting would have been.

One of the younger guards tightened his grip on his spear. The knuckles whitened. The leather wrapping creaked. Another guard looked at the captain with the expression of a man waiting for an order he desperately hoped would not come — the expression that said please do not make me find out what happens next.

They all understood the same thing in the same moment: if they pressed this, someone would die. None of them believed it would be him.

The captain held Temür's eyes for three heartbeats. The heartbeats were long. The heartbeats were the kind that carry weight — the biological ticking of a clock that is measuring something more expensive than time.

Then he exhaled. The exhale was surrender and relief and the specific self-forgiveness of a man who had decided that living was more important than the story he would tell himself about why he had not acted. He stepped aside.

"Open it."

The gates creaked open on iron hinges that had not been oiled this season.

Yōko said nothing until they were halfway through the gate's shadow, where the stone walls created a brief tunnel of cool air that smelled of mortar dust and the iron-oxide residue of old hardware.

Then, quietly, without looking back, she said: "You could try not doing that every time."

"They asked a question they did not have the strength to finish."

"That doesn't make it less awful."

"No," Temür said. "It doesn't."

Emira looked up at Yōko. "What did he do?"

Yōko gave Temür a sidelong glance — the kind that carried more communication in the angle of the iris than most people put into full sentences. "He reminded them that living is often the better choice."

A pause. Then, lower, to herself: "That's a terrible answer."

The world changed past the walls.

It changed the way terrain changed when you crossed a line that the people who drew maps had decided was the boundary between known and please don't go there: suddenly, completely, and with a commitment that suggested the landscape had been waiting for the road to run out before it started being honest.

The last signs of town life fell away behind them. Wagon ruts. Trampled grass. Chimney smoke. The dull comfort of fences and fields and the human habit of pretending that order was the natural state of things. Ahead: harsher country. Dry earth split by roots. Iron-colored stone jutting through the soil at angles that suggested the stone had been trying to escape the dirt rather than the other way around. Wind moving low through brush that looked too thorned to belong anywhere gentle.

This was not Temür's terrain. He knew it the moment his boot met the first patch of ground that did not feel like ground but like the lid of something.

He had been raised in different severity. Kharven's surrounding territory was desert forest — harsh heat, long-reaching sightlines broken by ancient stone and thornwood. Predator country, yes, but honest predator country. You could see where death might come from if you had the training for it. You could learn the angles of danger. Learn what the sand held, what the trees concealed, what the silence meant before it changed.

This place was different.

The air was wrong. Too damp beneath the surface. Too rooted. Too dense with things growing over one another in secret, competing for light and water and space with the quiet aggression of organisms that had been fighting the same war for centuries without resolution. The shadows looked less like absence and more like concealment. Branches twisted low and thick. Ground cover shifted in clotted patches. Broken trunks lay half-rotted beneath living ones. There was no clean geometry.

He disliked it immediately.

*Ambient Aether density: increasing. The air here is thick with free Essence — not the controlled, channeled Aether of a warded settlement but the wild, diffuse saturation of an environment that has been marinating in raw metaphysical energy for centuries. The local fauna will be cultivated on this. The things that live here are not beasts. They are beasts plus time plus Essence, which means they are ecosystems with teeth. Encounter density will increase with depth. Threat level will scale with the ambient concentration. The deeper we go, the more the environment owns us.*

Yōko looked ahead at the tree line. The ash-grey ears rotated forward, scanning the acoustic landscape, and what they found was not silence but the specific quality of not-silence that indicated a soundscape in which things were present and choosing not to announce themselves.

"So this is it," she muttered.

"Hm?" Emira asked.

Yōko's ears flicked. "Hell's Scourge."

Emira whispered the name like she was not sure if saying it too loudly might wake something. "That sounds bad."

"It is bad," Yōko said. "I've heard stories."

"Stories are mostly exaggerations," Temür replied.

Yōko snorted. The sound came out through her nose with the percussive sharpness of someone who had just heard something so manifestly incorrect that the body elected to respond before the brain could compose a more diplomatic reaction. "And the part where everyone dies?"

"Also exaggerated."

"That was not comforting."

"It was not meant to be."

She gave him a look over her shoulder. The look was the one she had been developing over the past twelve hours of knowing him — the one that started at his jaw and traveled to his eyes and arrived at the conclusion that she was dealing with someone who had been alone with his own verdict for so long that the verdict had replaced his personality. "You know, normal people try to reassure children."

"Then it is fortunate I was not selected for normality."

To his mild surprise, Yōko laughed.

Not because he had intended humor. He had not. Humor was a resource he did not allocate. But the sound came out of her anyway — quick, disbelieving, the involuntary exhale of someone who had been expecting a wall and found a door and was surprised by the door and then surprised by her own surprise.

And Emira, hearing the laugh, smiled. Just a little. The corners of her mouth lifting by maybe four millimeters, the muscles performing the motion without permission from the mind, the smile arriving and departing in the same second like a bird that had landed on a branch and found it insufficient and moved on.

Temür noticed.

He said nothing.

They walked deeper.

The road surrendered itself to the forest-desert border that gave the region its name. The soil grew darker in patches, then pale and dusty again, as though two dead landscapes had been forced into one living argument and neither had conceded. Thorned vines clung to black-barked trunks with the tenacity of organisms that had made a philosophical commitment to the act of holding on. Clusters of dry grass hissed in the wind beside roots thick as knotted wrists. Overhead, the canopy closed in stages — not the gradual dimming of a normal forest but the deliberate, architectural narrowing of a space that was being designed, by something or by entropy, to produce a specific quality of enclosure.

Birds did not sing. Whatever winged things lived here knew better than to waste sound.

Yōko's voice dropped. "How far in are we going?"

"Far enough."

"That is not a distance."

"It is the one that matters."

She stared at the side of his face. "Do you ever get tired of sounding like a funeral inscription?"

"No."

"I figured."

Emira tugged faintly at Yōko's sleeve. "What's a scourge?"

Yōko blinked, then softened. The softening was physical — a micro-relaxation of the muscles around her eyes and mouth that converted her face from alert-mode to Emira-mode, the mode she had developed in twelve hours and already wore more naturally than the alert. "It means a place people are scared of."

"Because of monsters?"

"Yes."

Emira thought about that. The thinking was visible — a furrowing of the brow, a downward cast of the eyes, the particular head-tilt that children performed when they were processing information that required more cognitive real estate than their current allocation allowed. Then she looked toward Temür.

"He'll kill them."

The certainty in the statement was absolute. Not hope. Not faith. The flat, factual certainty of a nine-year-old who had watched her brother do exactly one thing consistently for five years and had drawn the only conclusion the data supported.

Yōko's eyes narrowed a little. "Maybe. But that doesn't mean we want them close."

Temür's gaze stayed fixed ahead. "If they come close enough to matter, they have already made their decision."

There it was again. That way he spoke. As if everything in the world moved toward verdict. As if choice existed mainly to lead people toward the consequence they had earned.

Yōko heard it.

"Do you talk like that because you believe it," she asked, "or because if you stop, you'll have to think about what it means?"

Temür looked at her then.

For once not as a threat. Not as a warning. Not as an evaluation of competence or a measurement of utility or a ranking of relative importance to Emira's survival. Just a look. Cool. Measuring. But the measurement was not tactical. The measurement was the specific kind that people performed when someone had said something that reached a part of them they thought they had sealed off, and the measurement was trying to determine how much damage the reaching had done.

"You ask too much."

Yōko shrugged. The shrug was small, one-shouldered, the physical rhetoric of someone who was not intimidated and wanted the not-intimidation to be visible. "Maybe you answer too little."

Emira, caught between them, looked from one face to the other. Her dark eyes moved like a spectator at a match whose rules she did not fully understand but whose stakes she could feel. "Are you fighting?"

"No," Yōko said.

"Yes," Temür said at the same time.

Yōko groaned. The groan was genuine, produced by a diaphragm that had decided verbal communication was insufficient and had elected to express itself through the more honest medium of subvocal protest. "See? This is why no one likes you."

"That has never been a useful metric."

"Maybe not. Still true."

The wind shifted.

All three of them felt it. Not because it grew colder. Because it stopped moving naturally. The breeze had been blowing southwest at a consistent 8-10 kph for the last twenty minutes, carrying the smell of dust and dry bark and the faint ozone of ambient Aether, and then it stopped, the way a breath stops when the body has detected something that the conscious mind has not yet processed — the involuntary suspension that mammals performed when the environment produced a stimulus that required the sensory system's full attention.

The brush ahead gave one long rustle. Then another.

Somewhere deeper in the trees, something stepped on wood heavy enough to break it cleanly without effort. The sound was sharp, percussive — not the crack of a branch giving way under the weight of accumulated age but the snap of living wood being destroyed by a mass that exceeded the wood's yield strength, and the snap carried through the still air with a clarity that suggested the thing that had produced it was not concerned about noise, which meant it was not concerned about predators, which meant it was the predator.

Emira froze. The freeze was total — every muscle in her small body locking simultaneously, the way prey animals froze when the predator-detection system fired and the body elected to become invisible rather than mobile.

Yōko's tail stiffened. The ash-grey fur rose along the dorsal line, the piloerection response that beastkin shared with their animal relatives, the body's ancient method of appearing larger to whatever had just entered the sensory field.

Temür's hand moved to Khar Ild's grip with the smooth, practiced certainty of an action that had been repeated so many times the neural pathway between intent and execution had been polished frictionless. His fingers closed around the worn leather wrap and his weight shifted to the balls of his feet and his breathing slowed and the Zasag's ring-lines brightened from passive scan to active assessment, the amber-gold bands expanding in his irises until the banded structure was visible from three feet away.

No fear. Not in the simple sense. Not in the sense that the word fear was typically applied to human experience. What occupied the space where fear would have been in a normal person was something more specific and more functional: adjustment. The recognition that the environmental parameters had changed and the operating system needed to update its resource allocation to match.

He stepped in front of them. Placed his body between the path ahead and the two behind him. The placement was not heroic. It was geometric. He was the combat asset. They were not. The geometry dictated that the combat asset occupied the forward position. The geometry did not care about heroism.

"Stay back," he said.

Yōko shifted Emira behind her and lowered her center of gravity, dropping her hips and widening her stance in a movement that was not trained but was instinctive — the beastkin crouch, the pre-flight posture that predated training and language and operated on the same hardware that made her ears rotate and her tail stiffen. "What is it?"

"Too heavy for a scavenger. Too deliberate for wind."

Emira whispered, "Monster?"

That should have been enough to raise terror in a child.

And maybe it did. But Emira only looked at him for a second — the same look she had given him in the corridor five years ago, the look that poured every remaining scrap of hope into the identification of the one person who might be able to keep her alive — and then she hid behind Yōko without screaming.

Trust, Temür thought.

Or ignorance.

The difference was sometimes hard to rank.

The brush moved again. Heavier this time. Closer. The Zasag read the displacement pattern — the way the branches moved, the timing of the movement, the weight distribution implied by the angle of deflection — and constructed a silhouette: quadrupedal, approximately 400 kilograms, low-slung through the shoulders, wide through the jaw. Crag Stalker or something in the same behavioral classification. D-tier territorial. Aggro-locked to a specific hunting range.

The creature's Aether signature registered as a low, sullen pressure — the ambient hum of a body that had been absorbing free Essence from the environment for long enough that its tissues had been permanently altered, bone density increased, muscle fiber cross-section widened, sensory apparatus expanded beyond the original species template. Not intelligent. Not strategic. Just big and old and saturated with enough wild Aether to make its threat level exceed what its physical dimensions alone would suggest.

Then it stopped.

The brush stilled. The pressure receded. The creature's Aether signature faded from the Zasag's active-scan range — not because it had moved quickly but because it had moved slowly, deliberately, the withdrawal of a predator that had assessed the available prey and calculated the energy expenditure against the caloric return and determined that the three signatures at the road's edge were not worth the investment.

The B-tier signature at the center of the group had tipped the calculation. The creature had read Temür's Aether the way territorial predators read the presence of a larger predator in their hunting range: not as prey, not as competition, but as information. The information said: the energy cost of engaging this target exceeds the caloric value of the other two targets combined, and the engagement would need to pass through this target first.

"Yes."

The creature left.

Yōko let out the breath she had been holding. The exhale was long, controlled, the kind of breath that a body releases when the threat-detection system stands down and the muscles that had been allocated to flight begin returning to baseline. "I hate this place."

Temür's hand left Khar Ild's grip. "Good."

"Good?"

"Hatred sharpens weak caution into something usable."

"That might be the most Temür thing you've ever said."

The kid hadn't drawn a weapon. Hadn't even squared his stance. He'd simply *looked*, one slow drag of the eyes from boot to crown, and in that single glance Kroathar felt something in his own Essence flinch, like a hound catching the scent of something bigger further up the trail. No flare of power. No posturing. Just *calculation*, cold and total, the kind that only came from a soul that had already measured worse things and lived.

*A real monster,* Kroathar thought, rolling the word in his mouth like a stone he wasn't sure he wanted to spit out or swallow. *By every measure that matters.*

He rubbed his chin with a hand the size of a dinner plate, knuckles scarred white over old burns, and hauled the serpent's bulk off the Ridge with a grunt that barely registered as effort. Five tons, easy, maybe more with the way its coils kept trying to slither back toward gravity, slung over one shoulder like a damp coat. The Ridge groaned under the absence of its weight, loose scree clattering downhill in a soft avalanche of dust and broken stone.

"Hey, Saiya, catch."

Saiya was already three strides up the hillside, having long since learned to read the particular tilt of Kroathar's shoulders before he did something *Kroathar-shaped*. She didn't even turn fully, just pivoted at the hip, ears flattening, and let the motion carry her clean out of the serpent's shadow as it sailed past where she'd been standing a heartbeat before.

"Mreow, you absolute *dummy*!" Her tail lashed once, sharp and indignant. "I am not nearly as strong as you, you overgrown slab of muscle! Throw that thing at me again and I'll make you regret having arms!"

Kroathar grinned, all teeth, unbothered. "Reflexes say otherwise, cat."

"Reflexes say I'm *smart enough not to be under it,*" she shot back, hopping down off the rise she'd scrambled to. "There's a difference."

Temür had already turned back toward Yoko and Emira by then, his voice dropping into something quieter, steadier, the tone of a man who'd said this exact sentence a hundred times and meant it every time. "We can go. Stay close to me."

"Of course, Temür." Yoko's reply came soft, automatic, the kind of agreement that wasn't really agreement so much as trust worn smooth with use. She didn't crowd him exactly, just drifted a half-step closer, angling her body so that Emira walked the safe channel between her legs and Temür's shadow, small hands fisted in the fabric of her skirt.

Temür moved a pace ahead of them, eyes on the road, on the treeline, on anything that might think Emira looked like an easy thing to want.

That was when Saiya looked down, really looked, and went still.

A beastkin. Her own kind, standing among humans like it was nothing at all, like the world hadn't taught her that such things ended in chains more often than friendship. Her ears pricked forward, pupils narrowing to vertical slits as she took in the line of Temür's shoulders, the careful, deliberate way he'd positioned himself between the world and the child at his heel.

Kroathar hadn't been told. That was the problem.

He stepped into the road in one unhurried motion, the kind of motion that didn't need to be fast to be absolute, and Temür's path simply stopped existing in front of him.

"You didn't tell me you had a beastkin." Kroathar's voice had lost its earlier warmth, gone low and flat, a tone Saiya recognized, the one he used right before things got complicated. His eyes didn't leave Yoko. "Is she your slave?"

The air went tight. Even the serpent, half-conscious, seemed to feel it, coils twitching against Kroathar's shoulder.

Temür didn't reach for a weapon. He didn't need to. He simply turned, slow and deliberate, putting his full body between Kroathar and the woman behind him, close enough now that Emira's grip on Yoko's skirt had gone white-knuckled.

"Why do you care?" Temür's voice was quiet. Quiet in the way a drawn bowstring is quiet.

"Because I do." Kroathar didn't blink. "So answer me."

Saiya's tail had gone rigid, every line of her body caught between *step in* and *wait and see*, because she knew Kroathar well enough to know that underneath the bluntness, underneath the casual cruelty of the question, there was something that mattered to him more than he'd ever admit out loud. He didn't ask about beastkin chained to humans because he liked the answer. He asked because he'd seen the *other* answer before, and it had cost him something he'd never fully gotten back.

Yoko's hand found Temür's sleeve without thinking, fingers curling into the fabric, not pulling him back, just *anchoring*, the way you hold onto a doorframe in a storm.

"She's not my slave." Temür's words came out even, but there was an edge underneath them now, something that hadn't been there a moment ago. "She's under my protection. There's a difference, and you'd do well to learn it before you ask that question again."

Kroathar studied him for a long moment, studied the set of his jaw, the way his weight had shifted forward onto the balls of his feet, the way his free hand had drifted, casual as anything, toward where a blade would be if he carried one.

Then, slowly, the tension bled out of the giant's shoulders, and something that might have been respect flickered behind his eyes.

"...Good answer."

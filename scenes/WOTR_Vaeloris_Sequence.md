- **The culling** is defined only by date, authorship and instrument. Its wider place in canon is undecided.

# The Sounding Walk

The wind came off the harbour carrying tar and cold salt and, beneath both, a thin iron edge that had not been in it at dawn. Verinus stood at the sea wall with his hands folded into his sleeves and let the air move across his face and did not move to shelter from it. Alabaster underfoot, wet from the night, the great flags of the Sounding Walk gone the colour of old bone where four centuries of feet had worn the polish off them. He could feel the grain of the stone through the sole of his left shoe, which had been resoled twice and was due a third time, and he had been meaning to send it down to the cobbler for eleven days.

There were no gulls.

That was the first thing the city had noticed and the last thing anyone had been willing to say aloud. Eight days of an empty sky over a working harbour, eight days of fishwives glancing up between gutting strokes and looking down again, and the absence had a sound to it, which was the sound of the water against the mole with nothing laid over the top. Rope creaked. A hull knocked against its fenders somewhere below and to the left. Under all of it the sea made the noise it had always made and it had never been audible before, not once in nineteen years, and now it was the loudest thing in the capital.

Far out, past the light, past the shoal markers, past the point where the channel opened into deep water, the horizon carried a wound.

It had no colour that the eye could hold. Verinus had been looking at it for six days and could not have described it to a painter. It occupied a wedge of sky and sea perhaps two fingers wide at arm's length and it did not flicker, did not pulse, did not resolve into anything with edges. Around its margin the air behaved incorrectly. Ships had stopped going that way. The Ebb Court had issued a routing instruction on the second day and the fishing fleet had ignored it and then obeyed it on the fourth day without being asked twice, which told Verinus more about the harbour's assessment of the situation than any of the four reports currently sitting unopened on his working table.

The barometer in the Court's east gallery had fallen sixteen millibars in a week and had not come back up.

He breathed in through the nose and held it and let it go, and did that again, and did it a third time.

Behind him, at the top of the stair, a man had stopped.

Verinus knew before he heard the footsteps, because the man had come up onto the Walk carrying a sentence and the sentence had weight. It arrived across the flags ahead of its bearer and settled against the small of Verinus's back with the particular downward pressure of something that had cost the carrier a great deal to bring. Not the pressure of a lie. A lie was buoyant and had a lightness that the untrained mistook for confidence. This was dense and it was deep and the man had been carrying it up out of a harbour boat and along the mole and up ninety-one steps and he had not put it down once.

*Somebody has died,* Verinus thought, *and he is going to make me ask.*

"Lord Palatine."

The honorific hit the stone and did nothing. It always did nothing, in the Sounding, and visitors always spent their first hour discovering it and their second hour resenting it. Verinus had watched three crowns and a High Concordant go through the sequence and had never yet found a gentle way to shorten it.

"Come to the wall," he said. "You will be able to see it from here and you will not be able to see it from there, and you have come a long way to be given a report you could have had from the harbour master."

Footsteps. Uneven, and slowing near the end, and stopping a full two paces further back than a man of that rank would ordinarily stand.

"Arbiter-Lector Halric Peld," the man said. "Seconded from Arbitration to the chapter hold. We have met, sire, at the Weighing, the year before last. You will not remember."

"You sat on the left and you asked the Measurewright a question about drift compensation that she could not answer and she has been irritated about it ever since." Verinus kept his eyes on the wound in the horizon. "Say it."

The pause that followed had its own density.

"We have come with news of the Archpaladin." Peld's voice did the thing that voices did when they had been rehearsed on a boat and then abandoned at the last moment in favour of the plain version. "Darius is dead."

The words went into the Walk and the Walk took them.

The reading arrived in the chest first and the jaw second and behind the eyes last of all, as every reading had arrived for forty-five years. He did not turn. He stood with his hands folded into his sleeves and his weight even on both feet and he let the pressure come up through the flags and into his sternum and he took the number off it the way a man takes a depth off a knotted cord, one knot at a time, without hurrying and without hope.

It was heavy. It was very heavy. It was among the heaviest things that had been said to him inside his own Domain in nineteen years and he had taken the testimony of a woman who had buried three children in one season.

And it told him nothing at all.

*He believes it,* Verinus thought. *That is the whole of what I have. He believes it at a depth I could not fake and would not want to, and I have spent forty-five years of my life trying to make grown men understand that this is not the same as it being true, and I have failed at that so completely that I am now standing on a wall being told the world has ended by an instrument that cannot tell me whether it has.*

He breathed in. He breathed out. He tested the sentence again, sounding the same patch of channel twice on a falling tide, and it came back the same.

"How."

"He executed a second Halt." Peld's words came faster with the first ones out. "The vessel was killed. The clause held, sire, it held exactly as written, the Division has the transcript and the scope was clean, and the scope was clean and that is the difficulty, because the scope included his own body, and when the clause resolved it resolved against everything inside it." A breath. "He was drawn into the Plane of Fate. The breach was open eleven seconds. The thing that came through it did not stay and did not resolve and we do not know where it went."

"No," Verinus said. "You do not."

Out at the horizon the wound sat and did not move.

*Sheep in wolf's clothing.* The line came up out of the old text unbidden and out of order, as it had been coming up for six days, and it had been misquoted for so long that the correct version had stopped sounding correct. The great corruption. They hide beneath us. Every commentary the Alabaster archive held had read that passage as a warning about disguise, about the wicked wearing the shape of the good, and every commentary had been drafted by a man who had never once considered the other arrangement. A wolf in a sheep's coat had something to gain by the exchange. A sheep in a wolf's coat had only ever been trying not to be eaten.

Verinus had read that passage aloud at forty and had believed it was about deceit. He was seventy-one now and it had begun to look very much like it was about fear.

"The prophecy is being read again in the Court," he said. "You will hear it quoted before you leave. When you do, listen to which half they quote."

"Sire?"

"They will quote the disguise. They will not quote the reason for it." He let that lie on the stone. "Something has been under us a long time, Lector, and the common reading is that it has been hiding to hunt. Consider the alternative and consider what it means that the hiding has now stopped."

Peld did not answer. Verinus had not expected him to and had not asked him to; the sentence had been laid down for the man to carry back, not to solve, and Arbitration would spend a month on it and reach the wrong conclusion and then, eventually, reach the right one.

"The instrument was honest," Verinus said, more quietly. "And the question was purely mine."

"I do not understand."

"No. Nobody has, in forty-five years, and I have begun to accept that the failure is in my drafting." He shifted his weight, and his left knee registered the movement with the flat unglamorous complaint of an old joint on a cold wall. "You are shaking."

The sound behind him stopped, which was the sound of a man who had been shaking and had just tried to stop.

"Do not correct it on my account. The Sounding does not care and neither do I." Verinus finally moved, turning his head a fraction, not enough to bring the man into view. "You have carried a thing up ninety-one steps that you were not senior enough to be given. Somebody in that chapter hold decided that the news should be delivered by whoever could be spared. Is that a fair reading."

A long silence.

"Yes, sire."

"Then you have been treated poorly and you have done it well, and I will say so in writing to Altherion, and it will do you no good whatsoever." He turned back to the horizon. "We have lost the joint that held the frame square. You are correct to be frightened and everyone who tells you otherwise this week will be lying at a depth you can measure yourself if you stand still and pay attention. But the balance has not tipped. The Scale is not a thing that Darius was holding up. He would have been the first to say so and he would have been unbearable about it."

"How can you be certain, sire?"

"I am not." Verinus said it without any change in tone at all. "Certainty is a claim about the world. I have made a claim about myself. I will not be forsaken, and I say that in Urion's name, and the reason I shine at all is that the light is borrowed and the lender has not yet called it in."

*He talks like the archive,* Peld thought, standing two paces back with his hands wrapped around the strap of a satchel he had not been asked to open. *They warned me. They said he answers in soundings and you have to pay the line out yourself. Nobody said it would be worse than being shouted at.*

"The Grand Church will convene," Peld said. "The Will's seat is vacant, sire, and it has been vacant six years, and now it is vacant differently. There will be a convocation. There will be, forgive me, there will be argument."

"There will be a great deal of argument and none of it will be about the seat."

"Sire?"

"They will argue about the seat because the seat is the thing they know how to argue about." Verinus lifted his chin very slightly toward the horizon. "Ask them what draught that has."

Peld looked. He had been avoiding it, which the whole capital had been doing for six days, and now he looked, and Verinus heard the man's breathing change and did not comment on it.

"It is closer than it was in the reports," Peld said.

"It is not closer. The channel is deeper." Verinus's voice remained exactly where it had been. "I sounded the Ebb Channel on the ninth of the month, as I have sounded it on the ninth of every month for nineteen years, and it read four fathoms and a hand. It read four and a hand for nineteen years, Lector. It has silted twice in that time and both times the harbour dredged it back to four and a hand because that is what the charter requires. I sounded it three days ago and paid out nine and did not find bottom."

"That is not possible."

"It is measured." Verinus let that one sit. "You may tell Altherion that the Palatine's position is that possibility is a question for the Grand Church and depth is a question for the lead line, and that if the two disagree, the line is not the thing that has been mistaken."

The wind came round and brought the iron smell stronger and something turned over in it, faintly, a sweetness that had no business in sea air.

"Sire." Peld's voice had gone thin. "You are not proposing to go out there."

"I am not proposing anything. Proposals are for men with colleagues."

"But you would. You would actually engage them. The creatures out of the breach, sire, you would stand against them personally, at your age, with your, forgive me, with your record."

"Say the rest of it."

"Sire?"

"You have got half a sentence out and you have swallowed the half that has any weight in it. You want to say that my Ardency is the lowest ever assayed in a practitioner of my Stage and that the Measurewrights ran it three times because they assumed the instrument had failed. That is in the public register. You may say it on my wall." Verinus turned, at last, fully, and looked at him. "Say it at depth or do not say it."

Halric Peld was perhaps thirty-two, narrow through the shoulders, with the pale over-scrubbed look of a man who had been in libraries when he should have been in weather, and his Arbitration greys had been packed wet and it showed at the hem. He had a good face. It was doing badly.

"I think you will die," Peld said. "Sire. I think you are seventy-one and I think you have not expressed force since the harbour, and I think the men who send you out there will write a very beautiful account of it afterward, and I think you know that, and I think you are going anyway, and I do not understand why and I have been trying to understand it since they woke me to send me and it is the only thing I have thought about on the boat."

The sentence went into the flags and the flags took it and it went down a long way.

Verinus felt it arrive and did not blink and did not soften his face, because softening his face at that moment would have been a lie about what the reading had cost the man to produce, and he had never once in forty-five years told a lie about a reading.

"That is the first honest thing anyone from Altherion has said to me in nine years," he said. "Thank you. I will not answer it, because you have earned an answer and the answer is bad."

"Sire."

"Do you expect me to stand on a wall and watch my men be slain in a channel I sounded myself?" He turned back toward the water. "It is the weight of the Palatine to act. That is not courage and I would be obliged if nobody wrote it down as courage. It is an occupation. It is the same as a man who weighs salt bars on a dock at Coldwater Steps, and it happens that the thing I have been given to weigh is heavier, and it happens that I am the one standing at the scale when the load came in. My life is a tool. A tool that declines to be used is scrap, and scrap is honest about what it is, and I have never yet met a man who could sleep as scrap."

"And if the tool breaks."

"Then it will have been the correct tool for exactly as long as it lasted, and somebody will forge another, and the forge is not mine and never was." Verinus paused. "You may write that down. They will hate it in Altherion."

Peld made a sound that was almost a laugh and was mostly not.

Then his face changed.

Verinus did not see it happen, because he was facing the sea, but he felt the change come across the flags, because the man had begun to shape a second sentence and this one was not like the first. It came up out of him slowly and it was already, before he had said a word of it, heavier than the death of the Archpaladin of Sanctum Lux.

Verinus's hands came out of his sleeves.

"Sire," Peld said, "you must already know about Vaeloris."

The name landed.

It went through the alabaster and through the sole of Verinus's shoe and into the arch of his foot and up the long bones of his leg and it kept going, and the pressure of it in his chest was the pressure of a column of water standing over him, and there was no floor under it. Forty-five years. Four hundred and sixty-one testimonies taken personally with both hands. The woman who had buried three children. The confession at Ebbmouth that had taken him nine hundred kilopascals and put him on a stretcher. All of them had a bottom. Every reading he had ever taken had come to a stop somewhere, and the stopping was the measurement, and the measurement was the whole of what he was for.

This one did not stop.

He put his hand out and set it flat against the parapet and the stone was cold and gritted with salt under his palm, and he kept it there, and he breathed on the count the Court physicians had drilled into him across eleven letters he had never answered, and the blue at the edge of his lips deepened by a shade that nobody was close enough to see.

"Palatine."

Deacon Ilse Barrow had come up the stair without being announced, as she had come up every stair in the Alabaster Court without being announced for twelve years, and she took the whole of the Walk in with one sweep of her eyes and arrived at the correct conclusion before she had finished crossing it.

"Lector, step back from him."

"He has done nothing," Verinus said. "He has told the truth in a bad order and he did not know what he was carrying. Do not put it on his record."

"I will put on his record what the record requires." Barrow reached the parapet and stood beside him, sixty years old, square, grey wool, no ornament, her sleeves shoved to the elbow because she had come from the archive and had not stopped to be presentable for anyone including him. She did not touch him. She had never once touched him and it was, he had always thought, the most considerate thing about her. "How far did it go."

"It did not."

"That is not an answer to the question I asked."

"It is the only answer the question has." Verinus straightened by degrees. "The line ran out, Ilse. All of it. I paid out every knot I have and there is more below."

*Nineteen years I have argued with this man about corroboration,* Barrow thought, *and I have been right every single time, and I have just watched him take a reading that has no bottom, and the argument I have been winning for twelve years has stopped being worth a single one of these flagstones.*

"Then we corroborate," she said.

"There is nothing to corroborate it against."

"Then we find something." She did not raise her voice, because nobody raised their voice in the capital, and the flatness of it carried better than shouting ever had. "You published the flaw. You stood in the pulpit and you told four hundred years of courts that the Sounding reads conviction and calls it testimony, and you were right, and half the Curia has never forgiven you. So do not now stand on this wall and act on a weight because it was heavy. That is precisely the thing you have spent your life saying is not permitted."

"I know."

"Then say what you intend to do and say it plainly, because I am too old to do arithmetic on your riddles this morning."

Verinus looked at her for a moment.

"The tide," he said, "does not consult the harbour master."

"That is a riddle."

"It is an instruction. Read it again on the stair."

Barrow's jaw moved. She had been on the receiving end of that particular manoeuvre perhaps two hundred times and had solved it perhaps a hundred and eighty, and she solved this one on the third step down and stopped dead on the fourth with her hand on the rail, because it meant close the harbour, and it meant close it without a Court order, and it meant close it before the Curia sat, and it meant that the Palatine had just told his First Auditor to break the charter and had done it in a form she could deny under oath.

She went down the rest of the stair very fast.

Peld was still standing two paces back with the satchel strap wound twice around his fist.

"Lector." Verinus did not look at him. "Go back to your boat. Tell Altherion what you saw and do not improve it. And when they ask you whether I seemed frightened, tell them yes, and tell them I said the reading was accurate."

"Sire, what is Vaeloris."

"You said the name to me. I did not say it to you. Consider what that means about which of us was told." Verinus reached under the left sleeve, past the stiffened gold of the case cuff, and worked the plaited flax cord free of its keeper, and it came out in a long dry rasp of fibre. The lead weight at the end of it swung once and clicked against the parapet. "Go."

He heard the man leave.

Then he leaned out over the wall with the old cord in his hands, and paid the lead down toward the black water of the Ebb Channel, knot after knot, four fathoms and a hand and five and six and seven and eight and nine, and the cord ran on out of his fists into the dark, and he stood there paying it out until there was none of it left.

# What A Fender Is For

The fitting hall smelled of neatsfoot oil and wet wool and, under both, the mineral grey stink of whetstone slurry going off in a bucket that somebody had failed to empty on Tuesday. Maud Thole had her right forearm bare to the elbow and a strip of soft leather laid across her knee and she was rewrapping the grip of a boat hook, over and under, thumb pressed hard into each turn, and the hemp squeaked against itself in a small regular rhythm that had been going on for forty minutes. Rain moved across the high windows in long unhurried passes. Down in the yard somebody was caulking a hull and the mallet on the iron came up through the flagstones and into her boot heels every second and a half, and had been doing so since before dawn, and she had stopped hearing it around the time the light came up.

The rack behind her held nineteen white surcoats over grey, hung by the shoulders, all of them dry and none of them clean, because the Guard washed its working colour and did not bleach it, and the doctrine on that was two sentences long and had survived four centuries of people finding it inconvenient.

The door at the far end opened without anyone announcing anything.

She did not look up. There were perhaps six people in the Alabaster Court who came through that door without a herald and she could tell most of them by the gait, and this one walked with a slight favouring of the left knee and set both feet flat before shifting weight, and had done for about three years.

"You have taken the cap off," she said. "You have been indoors four seconds."

"I have been indoors four seconds and my neck has already thanked me twice." Verinus came down the length of the hall past the racks. He had the grey working vestments on and the gold coils on the front panel caught what light there was and threw none of it back, and the case cuff on his left forearm had been cut off at some point in the last hour, because the sleeve hung loose over the wrist and he kept forgetting and pushing at fabric that was no longer stiff enough to push. "Lord Knight Commander. We are going to depart within the day. I have arranged for all matters to be dealt with by my council."

Thole finished the turn she was on. Over, under, thumb.

*He came here himself,* she thought. *He did not send Barrow and he did not send a clerk and he did not put it in writing, and the last time he did that he was forty-four years old and he came back without the mole.*

"Within the day," she said.

"On the ebb. Six hours."

"And the council knows this."

"The council will know it in about two hours, which is the point at which the Curia's fastest man reaches them, and I would be obliged if you did not shorten that interval." He stopped four paces off and folded his hands into his sleeves. "You have nothing to worry about."

The sentence came off him and went nowhere.

Thole had served in the Sounding for nineteen years and had been Lord Knight Commander for eleven of them, and one of the first things anyone learned inside a testimony Domain was the specific sensation of a statement that carried no load. It did not feel like a lie. Lies had a buoyancy to them, an active upward push, the sense of something in the room straining toward the ceiling. This had nothing. It sat in the air at chest height and stayed there and had no more presence than a remark about the weather.

She set the boat hook across her knees and looked at him for the first time.

"Say that again."

"You have nothing to worry about." He said it without hesitation and without any change in his face, and it floated exactly as it had floated the first time, and both of them stood in the hall and listened to it not sink.

"That is twice."

"It will be three times before I leave this room and probably five before I leave this building." Verinus's mouth moved very slightly at one corner. "I am aware. I have been aware since the stair. It is the only thing I have that fits in a sentence and I intend to keep using it until somebody stops me, and I would prefer that person not to be you, because you will make it stick."

"It weighs nothing."

"It weighs what I have paid for it, which is nothing, because I do not believe it." He said this in exactly the tone he used for reading out channel depths. "I am not going to insult you by pretending otherwise in a room you can measure me in. What I am telling you is that I have decided to behave as though it were true, and that a man who behaves so is a different animal from a man who believes it, and that the second animal is far more dangerous to have in command of anything."

"That is a riddle."

"It is a reassurance. It is simply a poor one and I would ask you to accept it in the spirit it is offered, which is the spirit of a man who does not have a better one."

The mallet came up through the floor. Once. Twice.

Thole put the boat hook on the bench and stood, and she came up to a hand's breadth under his height and about half again his width through the chest and shoulders, forty-four years old, forearms corded from a childhood spent setting planks before she was ever set to a sword. She had the white over the grey with the sleeves rolled and the surcoat had a tar stain at the left hip that had been there for two years and had outlasted three formal complaints.

"Then tell me what you are doing," she said, "and I will decide how much of it I am allowed to know."

"I have a theory."

"Palatine."

"I have a theory, Lord Knight Commander, and it is not a heavy one yet, and I am not going to put it in your head at half weight because you will then have to carry it and it will be the wrong shape." He walked past her to the window and looked down into the yard where the caulking crew were working under a canvas that was doing nothing useful. "I have left Ilse to sit on the Court. She has the seals and she has the vaults and she has the pieces, and she will keep them locked and she will keep them separate and she will keep the inventory in her own hand, which is the arrangement I would want whether or not I trusted her, and I do."

"The pieces."

"Yes."

"You have never said the word pieces about them in my hearing in eleven years. You say *the holdings*. Once you said *the material.*" She did not move from where she was standing. "Pieces implies there was a whole."

Verinus was quiet for a moment.

"You are very good at this," he said, "and it is the reason you have the post and it is occasionally the reason I take the long stair."

"Answer it."

"There was a whole. That is not new information and it is in the founding archive and any Auditor above the third grade could tell you as much over a drink. What is new is the arithmetic." He kept his eyes on the yard. "Six days ago the channel read nine fathoms and no bottom in a cut that has read four and a hand since the Long Reckoning. The glass has dropped sixteen millibars and stayed down. The gulls have gone and they have not gone inland, Maud, I have had riders out, they are not in the marsh and they are not on the flats and they are simply not anywhere. And there is a hole in the sky over the deep water that every Curia in four kingdoms is currently describing as an incursion."

"You do not think it is an incursion."

"I think an incursion comes in." He turned around. "That thing is going out."

The rain moved across the windows.

"Say more."

"No. That is the whole of it and it is enough to be wrong about." He came back down the hall. "I have a theory and I need to go and prove it for myself, because I will not send another man to take a reading I would not take, and because I do not currently trust any instrument in this kingdom that I am not standing inside. I have an interest in seeing this void corruption with my own eyes. And you have nothing to worry about."

"Three."

"Three," he agreed.

Thole crossed to the rack and stood in front of nineteen hanging surcoats and did not take one down.

"We should take a regiment," she said. "Small. Two hundred. Ward-fitted, a Sealwright, two field surgeons, and the Ebb Court's own boats so we are not begging hulls off the fleet. I can have it standing on the quay in four hours and I can have it provisioned in five and it will cost you the last hour of your tide and nothing else."

"Do not bother."

"Palatine."

"If our true enemy shows his face it will not matter in the smallest degree how many men you bring." He said it plainly and without weight, which was the worst part, because it meant he had not needed to reach for it. "They will simply kill them all. Two hundred is a longer afternoon. That is the entire difference between two hundred and none, and I have done that arithmetic every night for six nights and it comes out the same every time and I have stopped hoping that it will not."

Thole laughed.

It came out short and hard and entirely genuine and it bounced off the stone and startled a pigeon out of the roof beams, and she put the back of her wrist against her mouth for a second and then took it away again.

"You sure do know how to set the mood," she said.

"I have been told."

"By whom."

"By a woman who is dead, and by you, twice, and by a Concordant who thought he was being funny and discovered he was not." Verinus almost smiled and it did not reach anything. "Maud. If I am fortunate enough to be used for the thing I was made for, I will go out there and I will gladly try to take this malformation apart by myself, and I will do it badly, and it will be the last useful afternoon of an otherwise administrative life. I would take that. I have wanted it for a long time and I am not going to pretend to you that wanting it is healthy."

"And if you are not fortunate."

"Then I will have looked at it, and I will have measured it, and I will have come back and told the Curia something they will spend four years refusing to enter." He paused. "But I have a feeling, and a feeling is not a reading and I am flagging it as such, that this malformation is nothing at all compared to what is happening underneath the surface. The hole in the sky is the part that is allowed to be seen. I would like to know what it is exhaust from."

Thole stood in front of the rack with her back to him for a while.

Then she reached up and took down a surcoat, and turned around with it folded over her forearm, and her face had changed into something he had seen on her four times in eleven years and had never once enjoyed.

"Charter of the White Guard," she said. "Founding provision, article the fourth, clause two. The Palatine may travel beyond the Court without escort in one circumstance only, which is that he refuses the escort himself." She let that sit. "At depth."

Verinus did not move.

"Ah," he said.

"You wrote the ratification note on that clause. Nineteen years ago. In your own hand. I have read it perhaps forty times and I have never needed it before this morning." Thole took two steps forward. "So refuse it. Stand in your own Domain, in front of your own Lord Knight Commander, and tell me that you do not want my people with you, and mean it all the way down, and I will put the boats away and I will open the gate for you myself and I will not say another word about it as long as I live."

The hall was very quiet apart from the mallet.

Verinus opened his mouth and closed it again.

*She has had this loaded for six days,* he thought, *and she has been waiting for me to walk into the room, and I taught her how to do it, and I taught her out of a doctrine I wrote to stop exactly this kind of manoeuvre being used against people weaker than me, and there is a lesson in that which I do not have the time to learn properly.*

"I cannot," he said.

"No."

"I do not want to go alone. I want very badly not to go alone and I have wanted that since the stair and it will not weigh any less for my having decided it is the correct thing to do." He looked at the floor for a moment, and then up. "That was cleanly done."

"It was your clause."

"It was my clause and I have just discovered what it is for, which is the fourth time this month that a thing I drafted at thirty-one has turned round and explained itself to me." He drew a breath and let it out. "Six."

"Two hundred."

"Six. That number is not a negotiation and it is not a compromise and I want you to understand why before you argue it, because you will argue it." Verinus stepped closer. "Two hundred people on that water is two hundred people whose deaths I will have arranged in advance for the purpose of not being lonely. Six is a boat's crew. Six can be pulled off the water. Six is a number I can look at."

Thole's jaw worked.

"Six," she said.

"And you."

"Obviously me."

"I was going to argue that."

"You were going to lose it." She turned back to the rack.

*Seven,* she thought. *He has moved me from two hundred to seven in ninety seconds and I have just thanked him for it, and the worst part is that he is right about the two hundred, and the second worst part is that I am about to go and choose six people, and there is no version of that where I choose fairly, because a fair choice would be a lottery and I am not going to run a lottery, I am going to pick the ones I can spare.*

She was still standing there when the side door banged.

"Commander." The voice arrived before its owner, which was normal, and about a second and a half ahead of him, which was also normal. Sub-Serjeant Elber Fend came round the end of the rack at a pace that was not quite a run and was in no sense a walk, twenty years old, red to the ears from the cold, hair plastered flat, carrying an oar in one hand for no reason that was going to be immediately apparent. "Commander, the yard is saying we are taking the launch out on the ebb, and the yard is also saying nobody has told the yard anything, and I have told the yard that if nobody has told the yard anything then the yard should possibly stop saying things, but the yard is not listening to me because I am twenty."

"Fend."

"Commander."

"Put the oar down."

Fend looked at the oar with the expression of a man discovering it for the first time and leaned it against the rack with great care, in case it turned out to be evidence.

Then he saw who else was in the hall and every part of him locked.

"Lord Palatine."

The honorific hit the stone and did nothing at all, as it always did, and Fend visibly heard it not land and visibly did not know what to do about that, and stood there going a further shade of red.

*They told me his name does not work in here,* Fend thought, standing at attention with the rain running out of his hair and down the back of his collar. *Nobody told me what you are supposed to do with your mouth afterward. Four seconds. Four seconds in the room and I have already invented a new way to be wrong.*

"Sub-Serjeant." Verinus inclined his head very slightly. "The yard is correct and you were right to bring it. Do not stop telling your Commander things that the yard is saying."

"Sire." Fend's chin came up. "Am I on it?"

Thole closed her eyes.

"You have not been told what it is," she said.

"No, Commander."

"You do not know where it is going."

"No, Commander."

"Or for how long, or against what, or whether anything is coming back."

"No, Commander." Fend's voice did not waver and his heels stayed together. "Am I on it?"

And the weight of it came up through the flags and into Verinus's chest, and it was heavy, and it was dense, and it went down a long way, because the boy meant it entirely and had meant it before he came through the door and would have meant it if the answer had been the sea itself, and there was not one gram of performance anywhere in it.

Verinus stood in the fitting hall of his own White Guard and took the honest measure of a twenty-year-old's willingness to die and found it true.

*That,* he thought, *is the flaw. That is it, exactly, standing in front of me with wet hair. He is sincere and he is entirely wrong and my instrument cannot tell the difference and it never could, and I have known that for forty-five years and it has never once been this loud.*

"Commander's decision," he said. "Not mine."

He turned and went back down the length of the hall, and the door closed behind him, and the fitting hall was left with the rain and the mallet and a young man holding his breath.

Thole opened her eyes.

"Go and tell the yard six pins," she said. "No. Do not tell the yard anything. I will tell the yard."

"Commander, am I on it?"

"Get out, Fend."

He got out.

Thole stood alone in front of nineteen white surcoats for a while and did not take any more of them down. Then she went out through the side door and down the wet stone steps into the yard, past the caulking crew, past the canvas that was doing nothing useful, to where the Court launch sat cradled with her gunwales bare and her oars stacked alongside, and she picked up the mallet that the crew had left on the thwart and took the box of new-turned pins out of the locker under the stern bench.

She set the first pin in its hole and drove it home in three strokes and put her thumb on the head of it and rocked it hard against the grain and it did not move.

Then she set the second.

# The Count At Vaeloris

Nothing had rotted.

That was the first thing, and Verinus had been standing at the boundary stone for eleven minutes working out why it bothered him more than any of the rest of it. Three weeks since Vaeloris had stopped being a place. Late summer, warm days, standing water in the drainage cuts, and there was no smell at all. No sweetness, no gas, no ammonia, none of the flat greasy stink that got into wool and stayed. A dead village three weeks in the sun should have been announcing itself from a mile off and the wind coming down the valley carried nothing but dry dust and a faint scorched mineral edge that had no source he could point to.

The ash was not ash. It broke under his shoe with a sound closer to shell than cinder and it did not smudge, and when he crouched and took some between finger and thumb it had no grease in it and left no mark, and he rubbed his fingertips together for a while and then wiped them on his sleeve for no reason he could have defended.

Under it all, silence. Not quiet. Quiet had insects in it and had wind moving through things that were still standing. This had a floor to it.

And he could not weigh any of it.

Three days out from the Alabaster Court and the Sounding had thinned at fifty kilometres exactly, as the charter said it would and as he had never once had reason to test, and it had gone out of him somewhere on the second afternoon between one field and the next. He had felt it leave. Nineteen years of standing in a room and knowing what everything in it cost, gone, and what replaced it was a species of deafness he had not been prepared for in any of the ways he had prepared. Men spoke to him and he heard words. He had no idea what any of them were worth. Fend had said something about the horses on the first evening and Verinus had spent an hour afterward trying to work out whether the boy had been frightened, and had failed, and had gone to bed feeling seventy-one for the first time in a decade.

"Sire." Fend was six paces off with his boots at the edge of the ash and his weight back on his heels. "Sire, permission to say a thing."

"You do not need permission and you have never once waited for it."

"That is fair." Fend swallowed. "I have been trying to work out what is wrong with the light. It is not the light. It is that everything is casting the wrong shadow, and I have been looking at it for a quarter of an hour, and I have just understood that the shadows are correct and the sun is where it ought to be and it is the *ground* that has gone the wrong colour, and now I cannot stop seeing it, and I would like to formally register that I would prefer to be somewhere else."

"Registered."

"Thank you, sire."

*He is not going to tell me to be quiet,* Fend thought, standing with his hands locked behind him because they had started doing something else. *Commander would have told me to be quiet an hour ago. I do not know which of them is being kinder and I do not want to know before lunch.*

Verinus straightened and looked out across what had been Vaeloris.

Perhaps four hundred structures once, arranged along a single street that followed the contour, with the byres uphill and the tannery placed downwind at the far end by somebody who had been thinking about their neighbours. The frames were still there. That was the part the Accord's report had failed to convey and the part every man in the escort had gone silent at, because a burned village fell down and a sacked one was pulled down, and Vaeloris was standing. Roof timbers in place. Doors on hinges. A drying rack at the third house with the pegs still in it. Everything grey through to the core, everything light, everything giving under a boot heel like a fungus that had grown into the shape of a barn.

The void had not destroyed it. The void had subtracted it, slowly, from the inside, and had left the shape.

"This was the work of something from beyond our world," Verinus said. "And there is nothing surprising in it, which is the part I want written down. Every commentary you will read this year is going to open with astonishment. Astonishment is a way of declining to have expected something. We were told. We were told for four hundred years and we filed it."

"Sire, I do not know what to do with that."

"Neither do I, Sub-Serjeant, and I have had eleven days."

He walked in.

The Zettari camp sat two ridges east, and Maud Thole had come up the last of the track on foot with her horse led behind her and the white surcoat rolled and stowed in her saddle bag, because a white surcoat on a ridgeline above a war camp was a way of getting an arrow into a conversation before either party had opened it.

The sentries picked her up at four hundred paces and did not pretend otherwise.

By two hundred there were six of them on the path and none of them had drawn, which she noted and respected, and the one in front had the tall carriage and the deliberate stillness of somebody who had been doing this since before the war and would be doing it after.

"Far enough."

"Far enough," Thole agreed, and stopped, and put both hands out from her sides where they could be seen.

"Name."

"Maud Thole."

Nothing. Not a flicker. She had expected nothing and had got exactly nothing and was aware, distantly, of a part of her that had wanted a flicker and was going to be teased about it later by nobody, because the only person who would have teased her about it was three weeks dead in a way that had not yet been fully explained to her.

"That is not a name I know," the sentry said.

"No."

"Whose."

"The Holy Sea of Alabaster."

The stillness in front of her acquired a texture. Somewhere behind the six, further down the slope, a horse was being walked and the walking stopped.

"You are a long way east, Alabaster."

"Three days and a bit." Thole kept her hands out. "I am Lord Knight Commander of the White Guard and I have come ahead of the Palatine, who is at Vaeloris as of this morning, with six of my people and no request for hospitality and no commission from the Accord. I have come to say that to somebody with the standing to hear it, and I would prefer to say it once."

*They are going to make me stand here,* she thought, *and they are entitled to, and the entitlement is the whole of the problem, because we spent eleven weeks being entitled to things and this is the far end of it.*

The sentry considered her for a long moment.

"Nuru Zettai," he said. "Camp warden. You will come with me, you will keep your hands where they are, and you will not speak to anyone we pass."

"Understood."

*Alabaster,* Nuru thought, walking her down through the lines with his shoulders square and his pace deliberately unhurried. *Three days east with six riders and no commission and no request for hospitality, and she has come to say it once, and the eleven weeks she did not come are standing behind her the whole way down this slope and she knows it. Good. Let her carry it as far as the court.*

They took her down through the lines. The camp had the smell she had been expecting since the ridge and had not smelled once at any point in three days of riding through this country, which was woodsmoke and horse and boiled grain and men who had been in the field a while, and it went into her chest and did something to her that she was not going to examine standing up. Faces came up as she passed and went down again. Nobody spoke to her. Twice she saw the specific arrangement of a field surgeon's tent and the specific arrangement of what got carried out of one.

At the centre of the camp the ground had been trodden into a hard flat court and there was a tent on it that was not larger than the others and was pitched with more care than all of them together, and Nuru Zettai stopped ten paces short of it and turned and looked at her.

"Wait," he said.

Thole stood in the middle of a Zettari war camp with her hands out from her sides and her horse three hundred paces behind her and every single person in her field of view pretending to be busy, and she waited, and she thought about what she was going to say and discarded four versions of it, and settled on the true one, which was short.

Verinus went in as far as the well.

It had been a good well, stone-collared, with a windlass and a rope and a bucket still hanging, and the rope had gone the same grey as everything else and the bucket had a bloom of pale fibre growing over the outside that came away as powder when he touched it. He stood with one hand on the collar and looked down and there was water, and the water was clear, and he looked at it for a while.

Then he took his hand off the stone and pushed the sleeve back from his right forearm.

"Sub-Serjeant. Take the escort back to the boundary stone and put them behind it and keep them there."

"Sire, the Commander's instruction was that I do not leave you within twenty paces."

"And the Commander is not here, and I am going to ask you to disobey her, and I am telling you in advance that she will approve it afterward and that you should not let that comfort you." Verinus did not look round. "There is going to be noise. I would rather it were not heard by seven people who will have to sleep in a tent tonight."

"Then it should not be heard by one, sire."

"That is a fair objection and I am overruling it."

Fend went. Verinus listened to him go, and to the escort moving back, and to the specific quality of the silence that came in afterward and stood around the well.

Judicium had always been the third of his harmonizations and the one he had spoken against from the pulpit for nineteen years, on the grounds that a Wellspring which measures and then decides is a Wellspring that will eventually stop showing its working. He had held it thinly and deliberately, at a depth that embarrassed the Curia, and he had turned down two offers of tutelage in it before he was fifty. It was, standing here, the only thing he had brought that was of the slightest use, and he registered the joke and did not enjoy it.

He put his palm flat on the ground.

"*Ardeat quod verum est.*"

The discharge came up out of the earth.

It was not fire. Anyone in the escort who had been close enough to feel heat would have felt none, and the pale colourless working that ran outward from Verinus's hand behaved along the ground the way charge behaved, seeking points, climbing the corner of a step, gathering at the iron of a hinge and standing off it in a fine hissing brush. The ash lifted before it went out. The hair on his forearms rose and stayed risen. Ozone came up sharp and clean and it was the first honest smell in Vaeloris in three weeks.

The mechanism was not complicated and the Curia had been mistaught it for four centuries. Judicium did not consume. It read the local accounting and it asserted the true entry over the false one, and the void's work here had been the entering of a zero rather than the taking of a life. The hollow was a claim that nothing had ever stood here, filed against the Continuum in a hand the Continuum had accepted and kept on the books for three weeks.

The flame contradicted the claim.

The ground gave the first one back at nine paces from the well.

She came up out of the grey mid-scream, a woman of perhaps thirty in a working apron with her arms above her head and her mouth wide and her weight going backward, exactly and precisely at the instant she had stopped, and the sound that came out of her went into Verinus's ears and did not stop going. She was not alive. She was the last true statement she had made and the flame was reading it out. The sound had a hard edge to it that a living throat does not produce.

Then the light went past her and she was not there.

"One," Verinus said.

The second came up on her left. A man, older, still holding a door frame that was no longer there.

"Two."

The third and the fourth came together and the fifth was a child and Verinus's hand did not lift off the ground.

"Three. Four. Five."

He counted in Coldwater, which he had not spoken in fifty-one years and had not intended to speak now, and the numbers came out of him in the flattened coastal vowels his father had used on the dock at the salt scales, one two three, over and over, because you did not count a stack in your head, you counted it aloud so the man across the table could hear you doing it, and if you were honest you did not stop when it got bad.

The working spread. It went down the street at the pace of a man walking and it took the byres and the tannery and the drying rack with the pegs still in it, and Vaeloris gave its people back in the order they had died in, and every one of them was screaming, and every one of them lasted the length of a breath and went out.

"Forty. Forty-one. Forty-two."

The child at eighty-eight was holding another child.

"Eighty-eight. Eighty-nine."

At two hundred and thirty he stopped being able to keep his palm flat and put his knuckles down instead and kept the contact, and the pale brush of the discharge climbed his wrist and did nothing to him and he watched it not do anything to him and kept counting.

At four hundred and ten he understood what the arrangement of them meant, which was that they had gone toward the eastern end of the street and had been going in a body and had almost got there, and he did not stop counting.

At five hundred and sixty-two he began to be afraid of the number.

It came out at six hundred and six.

The flame reached the boundary of the subtraction at the tannery wall and found nothing further to contradict and went out along its whole length in the time it took him to draw one breath, and the ozone hung, and the ash settled, and Vaeloris was standing there in the sun with its doors on their hinges and nobody in it.

Verinus knelt in the grey with his knuckles on the ground and said nothing for some time.

Then he said, "Six hundred and six."

The parish roll of Vaeloris, entered at the Alabaster Court eleven weeks ago in the hand of a deacon who had been requesting a Sanctum Lux chapter detachment and a Sealwright and forty men, gave the settled population of the village as seven hundred and forty-one souls.

He had read that roll. He had held it. He had put it down on the working table and he had written on the docket, in his own hand, in the formulation he had spent forty-five years defending, *deferred pending corroboration*, because a single deacon's account of an incursion was one reading and one reading was not a finding, and he had scheduled the corroborating survey for the second week of the following month.

Six hundred and six.

One hundred and thirty-five short.

And the flame did not lie, and the flame had contradicted every false zero in the valley, and it had not produced them, and it had not produced them because they were not here to be produced, and there was one honest reading available from that and he took it kneeling.

*They were taken,* he thought. *A hundred and thirty-five people were taken off this ground alive and they are somewhere, and everything I have said for six days about the hole in the sky being an exhaust is now heavier than I can hold, and there is not one person within fifty kilometres of me whose word I can weigh, and I am going to have to decide what is true using nothing but my own judgment, which is the precise arrangement I have spent my whole life legislating against.*

He got his breath under control on the count the Court physicians had drilled into him.

Behind him, at the boundary stone, the escort had not moved and had heard all of it, and Elber Fend was standing at the front of them with his fists closed and his face wet and nobody was looking at him about it.

Verinus knew what came next and did not want it.

A single reading is not a finding. He had written that. He had written it at thirty-one and he had put it in the corroboration provision and he had defended it against Barrow for twelve years and against the Curia for nineteen, and eleven weeks ago it had gone onto a docket in his own hand and six hundred and six people had died on the far side of it, and the doctrine had not stopped being correct simply because it had killed them.

Six hundred and six was one count. Made by one man. Under strain. Outside his Domain. In a language he had not spoken since he was twenty.

If he carried that number back to Altherion as a finding, and the finding turned out to be one out, then the thing he would have done to a hundred and thirty-five people was worse than what had already been done to them, because it would have been done in a register.

He took his knuckles off the ground and turned his hand over and set the palm flat again.

The ash lifted.

"*Ardeat quod verum est,*" Verinus said, and the pale light came up out of the earth a second time, and at nine paces from the well a woman of perhaps thirty in a working apron came up out of the grey with her arms above her head and her mouth open, and the Palatine of the Holy Sea of Alabaster knelt in the ruin of Vaeloris and began, in his father's dialect, to count again.

# One Reading, Taken Twice

The Sounding came back at a milestone.

There was nothing at the milestone. It was a squat weathered post at the top of a rise with the parish mark cut into it and a lichen crust over the numerals, and the road went past it the way roads do, and Verinus came up the rise on a borrowed cart horse with his knees complaining and the oilcloth over his shoulders shedding four days of rain, and between one hoof-fall and the next the world acquired weight.

It arrived in the chest, which was where everything arrived. The carter ahead of them said something to his lead animal and the something had a value, small and warm and entirely uncomplicated, a man telling a horse it was doing well and meaning it. Somewhere back down the column two of the escort were arguing about a debt and the argument came up through the road surface with a light peevish buoyancy that told Verinus inside two seconds that neither of them believed a word of it and both were enjoying themselves. A woman in a field four hundred paces off called to a child and the call went down a long way and Verinus had to close his eyes.

Four days of hearing words and not knowing what a single one of them cost. He had thought of it as deafness on the way out. It had been closer to living behind glass, and he had not understood how much of himself was the instrument until he had been carried outside it and left there.

He sat on a cart horse in the rain with his eyes shut and let the world be audible again.

"Sire." Thole brought her mare up alongside and did not comment on any of it, which he appreciated more than he was going to say. "The Zettari have struck the camp. They went the day before I reached them and the outriders confirmed it. On their present rate they will make the Rimward approach within a day either side of us."

"We missed them."

"By about eleven hours."

"Unfortunate." Verinus opened his eyes. "I would have enjoyed the voyage."

"You would not have been on the voyage."

"I am aware."

"I want to be clear that I am not being difficult." Thole's tone did not change at all. "There is no arrangement in which I put you on a hull I have not fitted, crewed by men I did not pick, out of a harbour I did not close. You may write that down as obstinacy. It is not obstinacy. It is that a ship is a box with one exit and I do not put the thing I am responsible for inside a box with one exit that somebody else is steering."

"You would have refused a Zettari deck."

"I would have refused my own mother's deck and she built it."

*He is going to sulk about the boat for a week,* Thole thought, watching the side of his face, *and he is going to sulk about it because sulking about the boat is easier than the other thing, and I have three days of road left in which to decide whether I let him.*

"Six hundred and six," Verinus said.

"Sire."

"That is the other thing, and you have been waiting four days to be told and you have not once asked, and I want that noted somewhere it will be read."

"It will not be noted anywhere, and you will not thank me in front of the escort either." Thole shifted her weight in the saddle. "Say it when we are inside the walls and say it to Barrow first, because she is going to want to hear it before the Curia does and she is going to be right about that."

"She is going to do considerably more than want to hear it."

"Yes."

Three days later Deacon Ilse Barrow came through the door of his working chamber at the hour she always came through it, which was the hour before the Curia sat, and she came in with a wet cloak over one arm and no announcement and a docket board under the other, and she did not sit.

The chamber smelled of lamp oil and old paper and the specific damp-plaster cold of a room on the seaward face of a stone building in the second week of a wet autumn. Rain on the shutters. A gull outside, the first in twenty-two days, and neither of them had said anything about that.

"We have visitors entering at the Rimward," Barrow said. "Zettari, under the Sovereign, with wounded. The gate wardens have them at four hours out. Harbour Health wants to quarantine."

Verinus was standing at the working table with both hands flat on it. He had been standing at it for most of an hour.

"How many wounded."

"Enough that they have asked for water and clean cloth before they have asked for anything else, which tells you what condition they are in and tells you something about their discipline as well."

"Then the wounded are healed first and the procedure follows. I want that order in that sequence and I want it in writing and I want it to be the first thing anyone at that gate is told." He straightened. "As to the quarantine. I have no objection in principle and I have a considerable objection in fact, which is that I have been to Vaeloris and I have knelt in the middle of it with my hands on the ground, and the damage there was a localised subtraction with a hard boundary at the tannery wall. Nothing propagated. Nothing spread. If soul-sickness or plague of any ordinary kind were going to attach to anyone in that valley it would have declared itself inside a week, and it has been three."

"Six days," Barrow said. "Standard."

"Six days is more than adequate and I have no qualms with six days. Enter it. And I would like to meet the Sovereign myself, personally, inside those six days." He said it in exactly the tone he used for reading channel depths. "If I am carrying whatever they might be carrying, I acquired it three weeks ago with my palm on the soil, and no gate in this kingdom has kept it out of anywhere since."

Barrow put the docket board down on the table.

"Forty," she said.

"Ilse."

"Forty days. That is the number in the founding provision and it has been the number for four hundred years and the reason it is forty rather than six is that six is the incubation of the illnesses we already know, and we do not know this one, and we have no case series, and we have no presentation, and we have no idea what the interval is between exposure and expression because there has never been an exposure to this before Vaeloris and every single person who was exposed at Vaeloris is dead." She let a beat go. "Except one."

Verinus did not answer immediately.

"That is a fair construction," he said.

"It is not a construction. It is the situation." She had not raised her voice, because nobody in the capital raised their voice, and the flatness of it landed harder than volume ever had. "You want six because six ends before the Grand Church convenes on the Will's seat and forty does not. You have arrived at a medical finding by working backward from a diary, and you have arrived at it honestly, which is worse, because you believe it."

"I do believe it."

"I know you do. I can feel that you do. That is precisely the problem and you wrote the pamphlet about it." Barrow's jaw set. "The Sounding reads conviction and calls it testimony. You published that. Half the Curia has never forgiven you for it. And you are now standing in your own Domain being sincere at me about incubation periods, and being sincere is the one thing that has never yet been evidence of anything."

The rain went across the shutters.

"Say the rest," Verinus said.

"You know the rest."

"I would like it said aloud in a room where it will weigh something, because I have spent four days in country where nothing weighed anything and I have discovered that I do not trust myself out there at all."

Barrow picked up the docket board again and turned it round and set it in front of him, and it was the Vaeloris return, written in Thole's hand from his dictation on the road, and it said six hundred and six.

"Two readings," she said. "By one man. Using one instrument. Standing on the same ground, in the same hour, in the same state of strain, in a dialect he has not spoken since he was twenty." She tapped the page once with two fingers. "That is one reading, taken twice. It is not corroboration and it was never going to be, and you knew that while you were doing it, and you did it anyway, and I want to know why."

*He has aged four years in eleven days,* she thought, *and I am going to have to say all of it regardless, and the fact that I would rather chew glass is not a consideration that has ever appeared in the provision.*

*She has had this ready since the road,* Verinus thought, *and she has waited three days to bring it because she wanted me inside my own Domain when she said it, which is exactly what I would have done, and I taught her that as well, and one of these days I am going to run out of things I taught somebody that are being used to take me apart.*

"Because I could not stand up," Verinus said.

"Sire."

"That is the answer and it is not a good one. I knelt in the middle of that street and I finished at six hundred and six and I could not make my legs work, and the alternative to doing it a second time was standing up and walking back to the boundary stone past seven of my own people with a number in my mouth." He looked at the page. "So I did it again. It bought me eleven more minutes on my knees and it bought the finding nothing whatsoever, and you are correct, and put it in the record in those words."

Barrow closed her eyes for a moment.

"I am not going to put it in those words."

"You are."

"I am going to put *finding unsupported, single instrument, single observer* and it will do the same work without handing the Curia a sentence they can read aloud."

"They will read it aloud anyway. They always do." He almost smiled. "Do it your way. You are better at this part than I am and you have been for twelve years."

"And the hundred and thirty-five."

"Enter it as an unexplained shortfall against the parish roll and enter the roll's provenance and enter that the roll is eleven weeks old and may itself be wrong." Verinus set his palm flat on the table. "And enter the docket."

Barrow went still.

"Which docket."

"The one in the second drawer. The deferral. Eleven weeks old, my hand, my formulation." He did not look up. "Enter it alongside the return. Same file. Not appended. Alongside."

"Palatine, that will end you."

"It will end the corroboration provision, which has needed ending or amending for nineteen years and which I have defended against you personally through every one of them." He paused. "It will probably also end me and I have thought about that and I find I do not weigh very much against six hundred and six."

"That is not humility. That is arithmetic performed by a man who wants a particular answer."

"Yes." He met her eyes. "It is both. I have never once claimed the two were separable and I am not going to start in front of you."

Barrow stood in the middle of the chamber with the rain going across the shutters and did the thing she had been doing for twelve years, which was weigh him, thoroughly and without affection, in a Domain where his rank and his office and his seventy-one years contributed precisely nothing to the reading.

"Fourteen days," she said.

"Six."

"Fourteen. I will come down from forty and I will come down to fourteen and I will not come below it, and the wounded are treated from the first hour regardless, and any Zettari showing a presentation we do not recognise goes to forty on my signature and not yours."

"Fourteen puts me past the convocation."

"Yes it does."

"Ilse."

"You want to be sealed in with the Sovereign of the Zettari for a fortnight and then walk out and go straight to Altherion and stand in front of the Grand Church having already spoken to him." Barrow folded her arms. "I am giving you what you asked for and I am charging you the convocation for it. You will be inside the Rimward house for fourteen days. You will not receive the Curia. You will not receive Altherion. You will not receive me. And the Grand Church will sit on Darius's seat without you in the room, which they will do whether you are there or not, and you know that and you have been pretending for nine days that you do not."

Verinus was quiet for a while.

"You are giving me the meeting."

"I am giving you the meeting."

"And taking the seat."

"You were never going to get the seat. You were going to go and lose it in person, at seventy-one, eleven days after Vaeloris, in front of men who have been waiting nineteen years to watch you do exactly that." Barrow's voice did not shift by a hair. "I am arranging for you to lose it in your absence and to lose it having done the only useful thing available to you this month. Take the trade."

"You always twist my hand."

"Yes."

"You could allow me to be gracious about it."

"I have never once allowed that and I am not starting in a fortnight I have to write up." She picked up her cloak.

Verinus smiled, and it was a small thing and it reached slightly further than most of them did.

"Call it my expertise," he said. "I am your elder, after all."

"By eleven years. And the post of First Auditor is sixty years older than either of us and it does not care which of us was born first, and you drafted the clause that says so." Barrow paused at the door. "That was a cheap move and you are better than it."

"I am seventy-one and I was enjoying myself. Make sure it is done."

"It will be done."

She went.

Verinus stood in the empty chamber for a while with his hands folded into his sleeves and listened to the rain and to the gull, and then he went round the table and opened the second drawer and took out a single sheet that had been in there for eleven weeks under two other things, and he brought it back and laid it beside the Vaeloris return.

The parish deacon's request. The forty men. The Sealwright. Seven hundred and forty-one souls.

And across the foot of it, in the small square upright hand that had not changed since he was thirty, *deferred pending corroboration.*

He set the two sheets square to one another on the table and lined the edges up with the flat of his thumb, and stood there looking down at the same handwriting on both.

# Three Poppy Heads

At the fourth hour the ink had gone thick in the well and Verinus had to stand it in the candle's heat for a while before it would run, and the room was cold enough that his breath showed against the lamp, and the nib made a small dry scratch on the laid paper that was the loudest thing in the seaward wing of the Alabaster Court.

He had ruined two sheets already. The first because he had opened with the office and had looked at the words and had understood what they would weigh. The second because he had opened with an apology, which was worse, and which he had blotted out so thoroughly that the paper had furred.

The gull was back on the parapet outside. Twenty-four days now, and it had brought two others with it, and nobody in the Court had commented on that either, and the collective decision not to comment had itself become a thing the building was carrying.

He wrote the third sheet without stopping.

> *To the Sovereign of the Zettari, at the Rimward house, by the hand of the physic detail.*
>
> *I was at Vaeloris on the eleventh. I knelt in the middle of the street with my palm on the ground and I counted six hundred and six, and I counted them twice because I could not stand up, and the parish roll says seven hundred and forty-one.*
>
> *You were there before me. I was not there when it happened and you were, and I have read four accounts of it written by men who were also not there, and I have found all four of them accurate and none of them worth the ink.*
>
> *The quarantine is fourteen days and it is real and it was not my number and I did not win the argument. I am inside it with you. I will not receive my own Curia and I will not receive Altherion and I will not receive the woman who set the number, which is a considerably heavier sentence than it appears on paper.*
>
> *I do not want your account of the engagement. Every court in four kingdoms will want that from you and you will be giving it for the rest of your life. I want to know what you smelled.*
>
> *There is a thing scheduled for the ninth. I will speak plainly about it when we are in the same room and not before, and you may take that as evasion, and it is.*
>
> *Say it at depth or do not say it.*
>
> *Verinus, Palatine*
>
> *Postscript, in the Coldwater hand: I am seventy-one and I have not slept properly since the eleventh and I am writing to you at four in the morning like a boy, and I would rather you knew that from me than worked it out across a table.*

He read it once and did not read it a second time, because reading a thing twice was how a man talked himself into hedging it, and he folded it and sealed it with the plain white and pressed the ring into the wax without any of the ceremony the office prescribed.

*She will read this,* he thought. *Ilse will read it before it goes and she will read it because I have not forbidden it and she will notice that I have not forbidden it, and she will let it through, and the letting through will be a message of her own, and by the ninth I will have had three conversations with that woman without either of us opening our mouths.*

He put it in the physic detail's satchel himself and went back to bed and lay on it for two hours without closing his eyes.

The Rimward house had been a wool store before it had been a quarantine house, and it smelled of it still under the vinegar and the lye, a deep animal lanolin sweetness in the beams that four centuries of scrubbing had not shifted. The physic detail came through the outer gate at the seventh hour with two carts, and the second cart was clean linen and the first was everything else, and the Zettari wounded had been brought down into the long hall on trestles at first light because the light in there was better than in the cells and somebody had made that call without asking anyone.

Captain Zamari Kesh met them at the inner door with her sleeve rolled to the shoulder on the left and a fresh dressing on the forearm underneath it that she had put on herself at some point in the night.

She was perhaps thirty-five, broad through the back, with the specific forward set of the shoulders that came from a decade of carrying a shield rather than of lifting anything, and her hair was shorn to a finger's length on both sides and left long across the crown and she had not had time or water to do anything with it in nine days. Two thin scars across the right cheekbone, old, parallel, the kind that came from a rim rather than an edge. Her surcoat had been red once and had been through a river.

"Count," she said.

"Fourteen." The senior of the physic detail was a woman of sixty with a Court badge and a wooden case and no interest whatsoever in ceremony. "Two surgeons, four dressers, eight bearers. Every one of us is inside the fourteen days with you from the moment I cross this line and none of us goes back out. You may inspect the carts. You may inspect the case. You may inspect me."

Kesh looked at her for a long moment.

"You are inside it too."

"That is the arrangement. The Deacon would not sign it otherwise and I would not have taken it otherwise." The woman set the case down. "Where are your bad ones."

"Far end. Six I would like looked at before the light moves off that wall."

"Then stop talking to me."

Kesh stood aside and let them past and did not smile, and did something with her jaw that was adjacent to it.

*They walked in,* she thought, watching fourteen people carry a quarantine over the line and shut it behind themselves. *They walked in knowing. Whatever else this kingdom is, and I have heard a great deal about what this kingdom is, and most of what I have heard was said by men who were angry about salt prices, fourteen of them just walked in.*

The letter came last, out of the satchel, in the hand of the sixty-year-old woman.

"For the Sovereign," she said. "Palatine's hand, Palatine's seal, and he asked specifically that it come to Captain Zamari Kesh and go on from there, and he asked it in a way that made it plain he expected to be asked why."

"Why."

"He did not say. I have known that man forty years and he does not answer that question when it is asked by me either."

Kesh took it. The wax was white and plain and the impression in it was a rough uncut stone, which was not what she had expected and was, she found, the first thing about the Holy Sea of Alabaster that had told her anything.

She turned it over once and went to find her Sovereign.

The royal garden of the Alabaster Court had never been royal and had never been much of a garden, and both of those facts were older than the current arrangement of the walls.

It was a physic ground. Four long beds under the south wall with a gravel walk between them, a low hedge of clipped rosemary along the seaward side taking the salt so the rest did not have to, and a shallow rectangular tank at the centre that the Court called the fish pond and that had held no fish in living memory and existed to settle rainwater for the beds. Wormwood. Comfrey. Rue, which nobody would eat and everybody grew. A long bed of white poppies gone over now to seed heads on grey stems, standing in ranks with their crowns dry and rattling in the wind off the water.

Verinus came out into it at midday with the cap off and under his arm and the sun on the back of his neck for the first time in eleven days.

Behind him, at the arch, four of the White Guard.

At the far end of the walk, two more.

On the roof of the east cloister, where a man could see the whole garden and both approaches and the seaward wall, one more, sitting down, elbows on knees, entirely unhurried and entirely unhidden.

And Maud Thole came out through the arch three paces behind him and stayed there.

"Seven," Verinus said.

"Nine. You cannot see the two in the orchard and you are not meant to."

"Ilse."

"The Deacon signed the standing order the night you came back and I countersigned it inside the hour, and if you are going to be aggrieved at somebody you may be aggrieved at me, because she asked whether it was necessary and I told her it was and she took my word for it." Thole fell in at his left shoulder. "You may also thank me, at your convenience, in writing, which I will frame."

"How generous of her."

"It was not generosity. She has read you for twelve years and I have watched you for eleven and we compared notes." Thole's tone did not shift. "You are being kept off a boat, off a road, and off a plan. Two of those are mine. One of them is hers."

Verinus walked. His left knee did what it did on gravel and he let it.

The wind came up over the seaward wall and moved through the rosemary and the poppy heads rattled, four hundred of them, dry and light and going all at once.

"There is a thing on the ninth," he said.

"There is."

"You have known for six days."

"Nine. I was told before you were, because I had to arrange the escort, and I have said nothing because you did not ask and because you were counting." Thole put her hands behind her back. "The culling is on the ninth. It is not yours. You could not stop it if it were, and I have watched you try to find a way to stop it anyway for six days and I have watched you fail, and I would very much like you to stop that now, because a man who is failing at a large thing tends to compensate by succeeding at a small one, and the small one is the part that frightens me."

"That is unusually direct."

"You are inside a fourteen day seal with no Curia and no Altherion and no Barrow, and you have four clear days before the ninth, and this morning you sent a letter." Thole stopped walking. "So let us do it out loud, in your Domain, where you cannot dress it, and then it will be said and neither of us will have to carry it separately."

Verinus stopped as well and turned and looked at her.

"Go on then."

"You want the Sovereign of the Zettari at your side on the ninth. Not for his sword. You have said four times in nine days that a man who was present is worth every account written by men who were not, and you meant it every time, and I have weighed it every time." Thole's eyes did not leave his face. "He cannot leave. He is inside a fourteen day seal that ends five days after the ninth, and the seal is the Deacon's and the Deacon does not bend, and you have already spent everything you had on her to get the fourteen in the first place."

"That is all correct."

"So you intend to make him fit. Which is a medical judgment and not yours, except that there is exactly one working in your kit that moves a thing off one body and onto another without lessening it by a single gram, and you have never once used it in the other direction, and the Register would show it if you had." Thole took a step closer. "Oblation. The Constriction. You are going to take that man's wounds into your own body so that a surgeon will sign him fit to travel, and you will call it mercy, and the arithmetic will be honest, because it always is, and it always is because the total never goes down."

Verinus said nothing.

"And then," Thole said, "you will present at the gate on the ninth as a seventy-one-year-old man carrying somebody else's field injuries, which is a presentation nobody in this kingdom recognises, which under the Deacon's own signature is forty days, not fourteen. You will have sealed yourself in for six weeks. You will have handed her the thing she has wanted since the day you came back, which is you in a room where you cannot do anything, and she will not even have had to ask for it."

*He has not blinked once,* she thought. *He thought of that. He thought of it before I did and probably on the road, and he has been sitting with it for nine days, and he is going to do it anyway, and the reason he is going to do it anyway is the reason I took this post.*

"You have done that cleanly," Verinus said. "Twice in a fortnight. I am going to have to start meeting you somewhere I have less of an advantage."

"There is nowhere you have less of an advantage. That is the entire problem with you."

"Maud."

"Say it at depth or do not say it, sire, since you are so fond of it."

Verinus looked past her at the poppies for a while.

"Two things are true," he said. "The first is that I do not know whether he will accept it, and the working requires acceptance aloud, at depth, inside my Domain, and it cannot be given to a man who has not said yes with his whole weight behind it, and I have never once in forty-five years found a way around that clause and I built it. So the entire scheme you have just laid out in front of me rests on a decision that is not mine, has never been mine, and will be made by a man I have not met."

"And the second."

"The second is that if he says no, I will accept it, and I will go to the ninth without him, and I will be very much worse at it." He put his hands into his sleeves. "You have my word on the second. I am not able to give you my word on the first and I would not insult you by trying."

Thole let out a long breath through her nose.

"That is the most honest thing you have said to me in eleven days."

"It weighed something, did it."

"It weighed a great deal. Do not look pleased about it."

They walked the rest of the gravel in silence. The gull was on the cloister roof beside the guard and neither of them appeared to mind the other.

At the end of the poppy bed Verinus stopped and stood for a moment looking down the ranks of dry grey stems with their crowns rattling, four hundred of them, seed heads gone hard and pale and ready.

Then he crouched, with his left knee complaining the whole way down, and took the small folding knife out of his belt, and cut three of them off close under the crown, and turned them in his fingers to shake the seed out, and put all three into his left sleeve.

Thole watched him do it and said nothing at all, and when he put his hand out she took hold of it above the wrist and pulled him up off the gravel.

# The Sermon He Was Not Allowed To Give

The reply came back out of the Rimward house stinking of vinegar and juniper smoke, and the folds were wrong.

That was the first thing Verinus noticed, sitting up in bed at the sixth hour with the shutters still closed and the sheet the colour of weak tea in the lamplight. Somebody at the fumigation table had opened it, laid it flat over the coals in a wire frame, held it there the prescribed count, and folded it back along the wrong creases because they had not paid attention to the original ones, which meant a Court servant had handled a letter from a sovereign the way you handle a laundry bill. He found that entirely correct and made a note to say so to nobody.

The paper had gone slightly brittle at the edges from the heat. The ink had not run. The hand was not what he had expected, and he sat with that for a moment before he read past the first line.

Then he read the whole of it.

Then he laughed.

It came out of him without any warning at all, one hard bark of it in an empty room at six in the morning, and it hurt his chest in the specific place that everything hurt his chest now, and he put the back of his wrist against his mouth and laughed a second time anyway.

*Well,* he thought, *that is considerably more charming than I had any right to, and I have spent nine days building a man out of four field reports and a casualty return, and I have built him wrong in every particular, and I am going to have to throw the whole thing out before midday, and I have not been this pleased about being incorrect since I was thirty-one.*

He read it a third time more slowly, and stopped laughing somewhere in the middle of it, and sat with the page on his knees for a long while after he had finished.

The gulls had started up outside. There were eleven of them now.

His mother and father had come out to the Alabaster coast as frontier people, before the seaward parishes had walls worth the name, and had cut peat and set nets and buried two children in ground they had cleared themselves, and had never once in their lives been inside a court of any kind. His father had weighed salt bars on a dock and had counted them aloud so the man across the table could hear him doing it. His mother had told him, when he was nine and had come home crying about a boy from further up the Steps, that a person was not what they had done to you and was not what they had been born into and was not what the parish said about them, and that if he wanted to know what somebody was he would have to weigh them himself and he would have to do it honestly and he would probably not enjoy the result.

He had been Palatine for nineteen years and had never found anything in the founding archive that improved on it.

That was the whole of the doctrine, in his reading, and the Grand Church at Altherion had put four hundred years of scaffolding around it and had occasionally lost sight of the wall. A person came into your reach and you accommodated them. Not because it was expedient. Because the alternative was to decide in advance what somebody was worth on the basis of where they had come in from, and the instrument did not permit that, and the instrument had never permitted it, and every regime in four kingdoms that had gone bad had gone bad at precisely that hinge.

He had met a great many criminals. He had weighed thieves and had found the reading light and hollow, and had weighed other thieves and had found something underneath that went down further than the magistrate's whole bench put together. He had taken testimony from a man who had killed three people and had come out of it understanding, with total clarity and no comfort at all, that the man had been eleven years old when the world had explained to him what it required. Drifters. Children on the road who had learned a set of behaviours because the behaviours worked and nothing else had. The world was violent and it made people into the shape it needed and then punished them for holding it.

And under all of that, the other thing, which he had watched take four people he had loved.

Obsession did not arrive as wickedness. It arrived as a grip. A person found something and held it, and held it harder, and the holding became the whole of them, and by the end they could not have said what they were holding or why, only that letting go had stopped being available. Attraction answered when it was recognised. Obsession compelled because it could not bear to wait. Verinus had spent forty-five years trying to tell people, in language they would tolerate, that everything comes into recognition eventually and that all things will be done, and that the entire content of a good life was the willingness to stop squeezing.

He had never once got that across from a pulpit. He had got it across twice, both times to one person, in a room, holding their hands.

He put the letter inside his robe, against the chest, where it would be all day.

He went down to the quarantine quarter at midday on foot with Maud Thole at his left shoulder, and the city came out.

It came out the way it always did, which was slowly and then all at once. Shutters first. Then doorways. Then the street. By the bottom of Cooper's Rise there were perhaps three hundred people along the frontages and the guard had gone from nine to fourteen without anybody telling him, and Thole's jaw had acquired the set it acquired.

"You could have taken the carriage," she said.

"I could have taken the carriage."

"You are going to be four days out from the ninth and you are walking two miles downhill on that knee, in front of everybody, at seventy-one."

"Yes."

"I am recording the objection."

"Record it. Then walk on my left where you are, because you have been on my left since the arch and you have not once got in front of me, and I have noticed, and I want you to know I noticed."

A woman held out a child at the third corner and he stopped and put his hand on its head and asked how old and was told, incorrectly, four, by the child itself, who was three. An old man on a doorstep at Netter's Lane called out that he had known Verinus's mother, and Verinus went over and crouched down on the step beside him with his knee complaining and stayed there for a full two minutes while the entire street pretended to be elsewhere, and when he got up he said, "He knew my mother," to nobody in particular, in the Coldwater vowels, and did not explain further.

A pastry seller with a barrow tried to give him something for nothing and he refused it and paid, and then, because the man's face fell, bought a second one and gave it to the nearest child.

The nearest child was a boy of about six called Pol, who had wormed to the front of the crowd on the strength of being small and shameless.

"Are you actually the Palatine," Pol said, "because my nan says you are the Palatine and my uncle says you are just an old man in a hat and my uncle is usually wrong but he is not always wrong."

"Both," Verinus said.

"That is not an answer."

"No. It is the truth, though, and you will find those come apart more often than anyone tells you at six."

*He gave me a pastry and did not answer the question,* Pol thought, entirely satisfied, *and Nan is going to lose her mind, and Uncle Deb is going to be furious, and I am going to tell it wrong on purpose.*

They went on down the hill.

The crowd got quieter as the street got steeper, and by the time the lime-washed wall of the quarantine quarter came into view there was almost no noise at all, which Verinus registered and understood and did not enjoy, because a silent crowd was a crowd that had worked out where he was going and how long for.

*They think he is not coming back out,* Thole thought, walking a half pace off his left elbow with her hands loose and her eyes on the roofline and her jaw aching. *Four hundred people have come out of their houses to watch an old man walk down a hill and every one of them has done the arithmetic, and they are wrong about it, and I am not certain they are wrong about it, and that is the whole of the problem with this fortnight.*

The gate detail had lime-washed a line across the cobbles and had swept the stones on either side of it, and the wardens stood back from it, and the crowd stopped forty paces short without being asked.

Verinus stopped too.

He turned and looked back up the hill at all of them and, for a moment, he wanted it very badly.

He had been good at it. That was the plain truth and there was no modesty available in it. For twenty-six years he had stood up in front of assemblies and markets and harbour fronts and had said the thing that needed saying in language a fisherman could use, and the archive held forty-one of them transcribed and he had never once read one back. The Curia had ended it in his sixty-eighth year on the recommendation of the Court physicians, and Barrow had countersigned, and the wording of the instrument was a masterpiece of institutional kindness: *the Palatine's public address is suspended in consideration of his health and of the risk that a faltering delivery would diminish rather than furnish hope.*

Diminish rather than furnish hope. He had objected in writing. He had lost. Barrow had come to him afterward and had said, without any softening at all, that he was correct on the principle and wrong about his own voice, and that a man who could not finish a sentence standing up in the wind was not going to give four thousand people courage by trying, and that she would rather be hated for it than watch it.

She had been right. She was usually right. It had not stopped being one of the four or five worst things that had ever happened to him.

So he did not give a speech.

And a woman came out of the crowd.

She came out at the pace of somebody who had been working up to it for the length of a hill, fast at the start and then not fast at all, and she stopped ten paces off with her hands wrapped in her apron. Fifty, perhaps. Broad, red-forearmed, a fishwife's build and a fishwife's forearms and the specific permanent chapping across the knuckles that came from gutting in cold water. Her hair was iron grey and pinned badly. She had cleaned herself up to come out into the street and had done it in a hurry.

"Palatine." Her voice was steadier than her hands. "Sefa Bream. Third boat off the Steps. I have a thing to say and I would like it taken."

The whole street heard her.

Thole moved a quarter step and stopped, because she had seen what was going to happen and had understood at the same instant that there was nothing available to her.

Verinus looked at Sefa Bream for a long moment.

She meant it. He could feel that from where he stood without doing anything at all, the great dense downward mass of a thing that had been carried up a hill and had cost her the whole walk. She had come out in front of four hundred neighbours to say something that was going to take her apart, and she wanted the Palatine's hands, because that was what the Palatine's hands were for, and everybody in that street knew it, and half of them had a mother or a husband or a dead child who had been carried by them.

"No," Verinus said.

The word went out into the silence.

"Sire," Thole said, very quietly, and stopped, because she had no second half to that sentence.

*He is going to explain it,* she thought. *Here. On the cobbles. In front of everyone. He is going to stand in the street and explain to four hundred people why the Palatine will not take a woman's testimony, and it is going to cost him more than the Reading would have, and he knows that, and he is going to do it anyway, and this is the speech. He has found a way to give the speech.*

"Mistress Bream." Verinus took three steps forward and stopped inside her reach, which meant that anything he said now was said to her rather than at the street, and the street leaned in to hear it and he let them. "I am going to tell you the truth and I am going to tell it loudly enough that everybody behind you gets it as well, because if I say no quietly then by tonight the story will be that the Palatine has stopped taking from fishwives, and that story would be a lie, and it would be a lie that outlives me."

Sefa Bream's chin came up.

"On the ninth there is a thing I have to be standing up for," he said. "In four days. And a Reading takes what it takes and does not give it back, and I have taken four in my life above seven hundred kilopascals and I was carried out of the room on three of them, and I am seventy-one. If I take yours now, and it is as heavy as it feels standing here, then on the ninth I will be of no use to anybody, and the people who are going to need me on the ninth do not know yet that they are going to need me." He held her eyes. "That is the whole of it. It is arithmetic. It is not a judgment about you and it is not a judgment about what you have come to say, and I want you to hear me say that your weight is not the problem. Your weight is a good deal of the reason I am refusing. A light one I could have taken and lied to you about."

The wind came off the water and went up the hill and nobody in the street said anything.

"Then when," Sefa Bream said.

"The twenty-third. Fourteen days of seal and five more to get my legs back." Verinus did not look away from her. "The twenty-third, at the Court, first hour after the Curia rises, and you will not be made to wait in a corridor and you will not be asked what it concerns and nobody will write your name in a book on the way in. If I am not there, it will be because I am dead, and if I am dead you will go to Deacon Ilse Barrow and you will say to her that the Palatine gave you the twenty-third on the cobbles at the quarantine gate in front of four hundred witnesses, and she will take it herself, and she is better at it than I am and has been for six years and would rather die than have that said in her hearing."

*Four hundred witnesses,* Sefa Bream thought, standing in the middle of the road with her hands still wrapped in her apron and her heart going. *He has just given me the whole street as surety. He has done it out loud. He has done it because he cannot take it and he knows what a refusal does to a person on a doorstep, and my Halla always said this one was a fraud in a hat, and my Halla was wrong, and I am going to have to tell her so, and she has been dead eleven years.*

"Twenty-third," she said.

"Twenty-third."

She nodded once, hard, and did not move, and Verinus closed the last two paces and took both of her hands out of her apron and held them in his own without doing anything else at all, no working, no light, no weight moving anywhere, an old man on cobbles holding a fishwife's chapped hands for the length of about four breaths.

Then he let go, and turned, and stepped over the lime line, and the wardens brought the gate to behind him.

# The Unglamorous Half

The Zettari ships lay inside the boom at the Rimward with their yards struck down and their sides fended off one another with old cable, and the smell came up off them from forty paces before the tender touched: tar and wet hemp and bilge gone sour, and under it the particular compound stink of two hundred people living aboard a hull for eleven days, which was vomit and vinegar and unwashed wool and the sweetish rot of a wound dressing that had not been changed on schedule.

Verinus came up the side on a boarding ladder, which took him a long time, and he refused the chair twice.

Rigging knocked against the mast in the wind off the water. Somewhere below decks a woman was keening, low and unhurried and evidently long-established, the sound of grief that had settled into a rhythm because it had been going for days and had nowhere to be.

He stood on the deck for a moment when he got there and put a hand on the rail and looked out.

The sea was the colour it went in the first week of autumn, a flat pewter with the light coming off it in hard chips, and the sky above the boom had the high dry look of a day that was going to be cold and would not rain.

*Twenty-nine days,* Verinus thought, with his hand on the rail and the wind coming into his collar. *Twenty-nine days of looking at a thing that will not resolve, and I have travelled to it and knelt in it and counted it twice, and I can stand here at a rail on a borrowed deck and still not tell you what colour it is. My father would have had a word for that and the word would not have been complimentary.* To the east, past the point, the wound in the horizon sat where it had sat for twenty-nine days, occupying its two fingers of sky, resolving into nothing.

The gulls were all over the mastheads. Thirty of them, maybe more, screaming.

"They came back four days ago," Verinus said. "Nobody will discuss it."

"Nobody will discuss it because everybody has decided independently that discussing it will stop it." Maud Thole came up the ladder behind him, considerably faster, and did not look at the sea at all. "I am on a deck I did not fit."

"You are."

"I want the record to show that I am on a deck I did not fit, crewed by men I did not pick, inside a boom I did not close."

"The record shows it. The record has shown it since the tender."

"The record is going to keep showing it," Thole said, "at intervals, for the next four days."

He went down into the ship.

The long between-decks had been cleared and hammocks slung fore and aft in two tiers and the deckhead was low enough that Verinus had to walk with his chin down the entire length of it. Light came in through the gratings in bars and moved as the hull moved. There were perhaps ninety people in there, the ones who had not been carried ashore to the Rimward house, which meant the ones who were merely wretched rather than dying, and about a third of them were children.

They looked at him. Word had gone through the ship before the tender was made fast.

He started at the forward end.

The first was a girl of about eight with a fever, lying in her mother's hammock with her mother sitting on the deck underneath it, and Verinus got down onto the boards beside the mother with his knee doing what it did and stayed there.

"I am going to tell you what I am and what I am not," he said, "because everybody on this ship has been told the Palatine is coming and half of them have been told the wrong thing about what that means, and I would rather you had it from me than from the man in the next hammock."

The mother said nothing.

"I cannot heal her. There is nothing in me that closes a wound or breaks a fever and there never has been. What I hold is a working that moves a cost off one person and puts it onto another, whole, without reducing it by a grain, and it requires the person who owns the cost to accept the move out loud and mean it all the way down." He put his hand flat on the deck. "She is eight. She cannot mean anything all the way down yet and it would be a lie to pretend otherwise, and you cannot mean it on her behalf because it is not yours. So I cannot take this from her. I want you to hear me say that plainly rather than watch me look busy."

The mother's face did something complicated.

"Then what are you here for."

"Water, mostly." Verinus took the cup out of her hand, which she had been holding for some time without drinking from it, and reached back for the pail. "And I am going to ask you a question in a moment that has nothing to do with your daughter, and you are going to hate me for the timing of it, and I am going to ask it anyway."

He worked the length of the deck like that for two hours.

He carried water. He held bowls. He got a splint reset on a boy's forearm by a Zettari dresser who had been trying to do it single-handed for a quarter of an hour, and the whole of Verinus's contribution was that he held the child's shoulders and talked steadily about nothing while it was done. He took a fouled dressing off an old man's calf with his own fingers and did not flinch at the smell and did not comment on it, and put a clean one on badly, and was corrected by the dresser, and did it again correctly and thanked her.

And at every hammock, when the practical thing was finished, he asked the question.

"Were you in the valley."

*He has not stood up in two hours,* Thole thought, from the foot of the ladder where she had put herself because it was the only place on the deck she could see both ends from. *He is going to have to be lifted off this ship and he has known that since the tender, and he did the arithmetic and decided the two hours were worth what they will cost him on the ninth, and I am not going to be able to explain that to Barrow in a way that does not sound like an excuse.*

Some of them had not been in the valley. Most had come up afterward with the column. Perhaps a dozen had been in the valley in the first hour, and of those, four could still talk about it, and Verinus took names off all four and made them repeat the names and repeated them back and got the spellings from a dresser who could write, on the back of a ship's ration slate, in chalk, with his hands shaking by the end of it.

Nine names. Nine people who had been in Vaeloris and were not on his list of six hundred and six and were not anywhere.

Nine out of a hundred and thirty-five.

He looked at the slate for a while.

Captain Zamari Kesh had come down the ladder at some point in the second hour and had stood against the beam and had watched all of it without saying anything, with her arms folded and the dressing on her left forearm gone through again at the edge.

"Palatine."

"Captain."

"You have been on your knees on a wet deck for two hours."

"I have."

"There are forty people on this ship who could carry water."

"There are." Verinus straightened by degrees, using the hammock cleat, and did not entirely make it. "And every one of them has been carrying it for eleven days and none of them has been asked a question by anybody with the standing to do something about the answer. I did not come aboard to carry water. Carrying the water is how you get to ask the question. If I had come aboard with the slate and no pail I would have got four names and three lies."

*He is not wrong,* Kesh thought, looking at an old man in a fouled grey robe with chalk on his fingers and a ration slate under his arm. *That is the irritating part. He is not wrong, and he did it in the correct order, and he did it on his knees, and I am going to have to report all of that upward exactly as it happened and it is going to sound as though I have been charmed.*

"There is one more," Kesh said. "Aft. She has not spoken to a physic in nine days and she has thrown two of them off. She was in the valley in the first hour."

"Then take me aft."

Her name was Ifedi and she was thirty, a standard-bearer, and she had been under a wall.

She was long-boned and had been powerfully built before eleven days on a hammock had begun taking it back, and her hair had been braided close to the scalp in six rows and four of them had come out and nobody had been permitted to redo them. Her face was broad through the cheekbones with a heavy straight brow and her eyes were entirely dry and had the flat unblinking quality of a person who has stopped expecting anything from a doorway. Her left leg lay slightly wrong. There was a crush injury through the pelvis under the blanket that Kesh described in eleven words and Verinus did not ask her to expand on.

"I know what you are," Ifedi said. Her voice came out low and did not rise at the end of anything. "The dressers talk. You take it off and you put it on yourself and it does not get smaller."

"That is accurate and it is the first time anybody has said it to me correctly on the first attempt."

"Then no."

*He is going to argue,* Ifedi thought, watching him lower himself with the whole of his weight going onto one hand. *They all argue. They argue and then they look at the leg and then they stop looking at me, and I have nine days of practice at the next four minutes and I would like to get them over with.*

Verinus sat down on the deck beside her hammock without being invited, which took him some doing, and folded his hands into his sleeves.

"Very well."

"You are not going to argue."

"No."

Ifedi turned her head.

"Every one of them argues."

"Every one of them is a physic and their whole training is that a person in pain has stopped being able to decide things, and there is a great deal in that, and it is not my doctrine." Verinus looked at the deckhead. "The working requires you to accept it out loud and mean it entirely. A person who has been argued into it does not mean it entirely. So arguing with you would not be unkind, it would be incompetent, and I have been doing this for forty-five years and I am not incompetent."

Silence. The hull worked. The keening from the forward end had stopped at some point and neither of them had noticed when.

"Ask me your question," Ifedi said. "The one you have been asking all morning. I have been listening to it come down the deck since the ninth hammock."

"Were you in the valley."

"I was under a wall in the valley. I was under it for the whole of it and I could see out." Her jaw set. "Ask the real one."

"Names."

"Eleven," Ifedi said. "I watched eleven people go east up that street who are not in any account anybody has read out to me, and I have been saying so for nine days to men who write it down and put it in a satchel, and I have been told twice that I was concussed."

Verinus took the slate out from under his arm and set it on his knee and got the chalk out of his sleeve.

"Say them slowly," he said, "and correct my spelling, and if I get one wrong I will start the list again from the top."

It took a quarter of an hour. Twice she stopped and could not go on and he waited without filling it, and once she got a name out and then said a second thing about the person attached to it, a small domestic fact of no evidentiary value whatsoever, and he wrote that down too, in the margin, in full.

At the end of it he had twenty names.

"Twenty," he said. "Out of a hundred and thirty-five."

"Then there are a hundred and fifteen more."

"Yes."

Ifedi lay still for a while.

"Do it," she said.

Verinus did not move.

"Say it properly."

"I accept the move." Her voice came out of her flat and low and went a very long way down, and Verinus felt it arrive in the chest and the jaw and behind the eyes, and there was not one gram of hesitation anywhere in the whole of it. "Take it off me and put it on yourself and do not lessen it. I want to be able to hold the standard on the ninth and I have heard what the ninth is and I am not going to be in a hammock for it."

"*Non minuitur,*" Verinus said. "*Transfertur.*"

He put his hand around her wrist.

There was no light. Anyone watching from the ladder would have seen an old man holding a woman's forearm and would have seen his shoulders come down and forward as though somebody had set a sack on the back of his neck, and would have heard the breath go out of him in one long controlled stream through the teeth, and would have seen his free hand find the deck and stay there.

Pressure dropped across the whole of the between-decks. Ears went. Three people cried out at the forward end and did not know why.

Ifedi's face changed. The flatness came off it in one piece and what was underneath was a woman of thirty who had been under a wall, and she got one arm up over her eyes and kept it there.

Verinus stayed on the deck with his palm flat and his head down and did not make any sound at all for about eleven seconds, and then breathed in on the count the Court physicians had drilled into him, and then again, and the third one went most of the way.

"Do not stand up," Thole said from the ladder, without moving. "Sire. Do not stand up yet."

"I was not going to."

"You were absolutely going to."

He sat on the wet boards of a Zettari hull with the pelvis of a standard-bearer in his own body and let three more breaths go by, and then took the poppy heads out of his left sleeve, all three of them, and looked at them.

He had cut them for the ninth.

He put two of them into Ifedi's blanket, where her hand would find them, and closed her fingers around them without saying anything about it, and put the third back in his sleeve.

Then he let Kesh and Thole take him up under the arms, one on each side, and get him onto his feet, and stood there breathing for a while with his eyes shut in the low deck and his chin down because there was no headroom.

"Aft cabin," he said.

"Sire, you should go back over the side and sleep."

"I have four days and a hundred and fifteen names and I am not going to spend the afternoon of the fifth day being sensible." He got his hands back into his sleeves and straightened up as far as the deckhead permitted, which was not far. "Take me to the Sovereign's quarters."

They went aft.

The passage narrowed and the deckhead came down further and the light went brown, and at the end of it there was a door that was no better than any of the other doors on that ship and was closed, and a Zettari standing beside it who came off the bulkhead when he saw who was coming.

Verinus stopped three paces short.

He put a hand on the bulkhead and gave himself a moment, and got the breathing down to something that would pass, and got the chalk off his fingers on the inside of his sleeve.

Then he took the cap off his head and handed it to Maud Thole, and knocked.

# The Cabin, I: The Fable of the Paragons

The chair had a back and no arms, which meant sitting down in it was going to be a negotiation conducted entirely in public, and Verinus conducted it without pretending otherwise. He put his left hand flat on the table's edge, let the weight go down through the arm rather than through the hips, and lowered himself in one long controlled descent that ended somewhere short of comfortable. The pelvis that was not his own registered the transfer of load exactly as a crushed pelvis registers anything, which is to say completely and with no interest whatsoever in the circumstances. He breathed out through the teeth. He kept his face where it was.

*She held that for eleven days in a hammock,* he thought, *and she did not make a sound when the tender came alongside and shook the whole hull, and I have been carrying it for two hours and I have already decided that sitting down was heroic. There is a sermon in that and I am never going to be permitted to give it.*

He got his hands into his sleeves and looked across the table at the Sovereign of the Zettari and found, to his considerable pleasure, that he was being looked at the way a shipwright looks at a hull somebody else has patched.

That was worry. It had come out sideways and dry and had arrived dressed as an audit, and Verinus recognised the shape of it because it was the shape his own went in when he could not put it any other way. Sefa Bream had a husband once who used to check the moorings twice a night and never once said he was frightened of the weather.

"Do not let yourself be troubled on my account," Verinus said. "There is a queue. You are somewhere behind two physicians, a Deacon who has been writing to me about my heart since the year I turned sixty, and the woman standing against your bulkhead, who has been troubled about me professionally for eleven years and has never once been wrong to be."

Thole did not move and did not look at him.

"I have never asked anyone to be sentimental about work that was simply correct to do," he went on. "Carrying water is not virtue. It is carrying water. A man who requires applause for the ordinary discharge of his office has already begun charging for it, and the charge goes up. I want no glory out of this and I have never wanted any. What glory there is belongs to the Judger and I would not know where to put my share of it if it were handed to me in a bag."

He let that sit and felt what it weighed in the cabin, which was more than he had intended and rather less than it would have weighed from him at forty. There was some vanity in the difference and he noted it and set it aside.

"Kujo," he said.

He turned the name over. He had come aboard this ship with twenty names written in chalk and had not expected to leave with a twenty-first, and this one had arrived unasked in the middle of a sentence about counting.

"That is the first time anyone has given me a name for it. Four field reports, a casualty return, two Curia summaries and eleven days of testimony taken by hand, and not one of them contained a name. They contained *the incursion*. They contained *the event*. The Arbitration Division has a term of art for it that runs to nine words and commits to nothing." He kept his voice exactly where it had been. "A thing that has a name has an author, and an author has intentions, and intentions can be reasoned about. I have been trying to reason about weather for twenty-nine days. You have just handed me a person."

He did not ask the follow-up. There would be a follow-up and it would take an hour and this was not the hour, and a man who chases the first thread offered him usually pulls it out of the cloth.

"I came to say two things and I have already said the smaller one badly." Verinus shifted his weight fractionally and paid for it. "The first is that when the time is right I want you with me at Altherion. Not for the convocation. The convocation will happen without me and I have made my peace with that in the way one makes peace with a tooth. Afterward. When there is something to put in front of the Grand Church that they will have to look at, I want the man who was in that valley standing where they can see him, because I have spent forty-five years watching bodies of clever men discount a document and I have never once seen them discount a witness."

He drew a slow breath and did not get the whole of it.

"The second is a story, and I am going to tell it to you properly, because the version that circulates in the ports is wrong in the two places that matter."

The stern window ran silver. Beyond it the boom lay in a long dark line across the water and the gulls were still working the mastheads, and somewhere aft of them a dresser was calling down the passage for boiled linen for the third time in an hour.

"Before the kingdoms," Verinus said, "before the Accord, before there was anything with walls worth the name, the Archons walked with bodies. That is the founding claim and every branch of the faith holds it in some form. Those bodies are called the paragons. A paragon is not a servant of an Archon and is not a vessel in the sense a lamp is a vessel. A paragon is that Archon, wearing a life, subject to hunger and to weather and to being wrong about things."

He turned his right hand over in his sleeve, slowly, because the fingers had begun to go.

"The received account says they are born under prophecy. That the sign comes first and the child comes after, and that the office of the Church is to read the sign and go and find the child. There is a whole apparatus at Altherion built on that sentence. There are men who have given their entire working lives to the reading of it." A pause. "I do not believe it. I have not believed it since I was thirty-four and I have said so from the pulpit four times, which is three times more than was wise."

He looked up.

"Prophecy in the received sense requires the future to already have a shape and requires a person to be able to see it, and I have spent my life inside an instrument that measures what a thing costs the person carrying it, and I have never once measured a statement about tomorrow that had any depth in it at all. Men who prophesy weigh light. Every one of them. That is not proof and I would not enter it as proof, and it has been sufficient for me for thirty-seven years."

*And this is the part I have never got across,* he thought, *not from a pulpit and not in a pamphlet and not in nineteen years of the Curia, and I am going to try it once more on a wounded man who has no reason to give me the benefit and every reason to be asleep.*

"What I believe instead is tidal," he said. "A tide is not a prediction. Nobody prophesies the tide. The tide arrives because the conditions that produce it are already present and have been present the whole time, and the water is not deciding anything, and the moon is not sending a message. The paragons come when the world has begun calling for order and has begun calling loudly enough to be a condition rather than a complaint. They are not sent because it was written. They surface because the water has gone that way." He let one corner of his mouth move. "The Curia find this reading offensive and vulgar. Two of them have told me it removes the mercy from it. I have never been able to explain to them that a thing which comes whether or not you have been good is considerably better news than a thing which has to be earned."

He shifted again. The chair back was doing him no favours at all and he had been in it long enough for the transferred injury to begin arguing with the original architecture.

"They come with talents. That is attested and it is consistent across every branch that keeps records, including two that agree on almost nothing else. A paragon carries the working of its Archon in the body from the first, and it carries it the way a fish carries swimming. There is no study. There is no Formation to learn. It is not a Wellspring harmonized at nineteen after eleven years of failing at it. It is simply the case, from the beginning, and the child is usually a considerable nuisance to raise."

Verinus let the silence run for the length of two swells and did not fill it.

"You will want to know why any of this belongs in a cabin on a quarantined hull," he said, "four days out from the ninth, with a bowl of cold broth on the table."

He took his hands out of his sleeves and set them on the wood.

"Because the same arrangement holds on the other side of the ledger. If there is an Archon whose office is ending and hollowing and the removal of things, and the Grand Church holds that there is, and I am not going to say the name of it aloud on a ship that has ninety people in the hold and eleven days of fever in it, then there is a paragon that answers to it. The Void Paragon. And what has been happening on my coast for twenty-nine days is entirely consistent with somebody working very hard, and at enormous cost, to arrive at it."

He turned his head fractionally toward the window and the wound sitting in its two fingers of sky.

"Vaeloris was not destroyed. I knelt in the middle of it with my palm on the ground and I want you to hear the distinction because it took me three weeks to be certain of it. The frames were standing. The doors were on their hinges. There was a drying rack at the third house with the pegs still in it. What was done there was the entering of a zero. A claim, filed against the Continuum, that nothing had ever stood in that valley, and the Continuum accepted the claim and kept it on the books for three weeks until I went out there and contradicted it." His jaw tightened once and released. "Six hundred and six came back. A hundred and thirty-five did not, and they did not come back because they were not there to be given, and you have just told me there were sections where nothing capable of being counted remained. Both of those things can be true at once and I have been assuming they were the same fact for eleven days, and I was wrong, and I would like that noted."

He paused, and made himself slow down, because his hands had started to move and he did not like it when they did that.

"A titan does not need a village. A titan does not need a coast. If Kujo wanted that valley gone there are four ways to do it that leave no doctrinal residue at all and cost him nothing, and he chose the one that required three weeks of holding a false entry open against the accounting of the world, which is expensive and slow and visible from the sea." Verinus looked back across the table. "Nobody pays that for a village. He was not clearing ground. He was demonstrating standing. That is what a false entry is. It is a claim, made publicly, that he is entitled to make claims, and the hundred and thirty-five are what he took while everyone was busy being appalled at the six hundred and six."

The hull gave a long creak and the light through the stern window slid a hand's breadth up the bulkhead.

"So he is a means. He is a very large and very terrible means and I would not say that word within his hearing, but he is a means, and the end is access, and the access is to something that has to be born into a life before it can be used." Verinus's voice went quieter without losing any of its edges. "And that is the sentence I have not been able to put in a report, because the moment I write it down the Curia will spend four years arguing about whether the paragons are literal, and the men on the other side of this will not be arguing about anything at all."

He looked at the ration slate lying between them with twenty names on it in chalk.

"Which brings me to the discourteous part, and I would rather be discourteous to your face than tactful behind your back."

He did not soften it.

"If you intend to defeat Kujo, nothing you have learned as a Zettari will do it. I do not say that to diminish the Zettari. I have read what the Accord holds on your traditions and I have read the parts the Accord does not hold, and I would put your lineage against any three martial schools in the Inner World and take the shorter odds. It has nothing to do with quality." He tapped the table twice with one finger. "It has to do with what the void does to the question. Every discipline I know of, yours included, mine included, is built on the assumption that a thing is there and can be met. Struck, bound, turned, out-timed, out-measured. The void does not present a thing to be met. It presents an absence and files a claim, and a claim is not answered by force, and a man of exceptional skill can spend his whole life swinging correctly at the place where something is not."

He let that go down as far as it was going to go.

"I have exactly one working that has ever contradicted it," he said, "and it is the harmonization I have preached against for nineteen years, and it cost me two hours on my knees in the ash and I could not stand up afterward. So I am not offering you mastery. I am offering you the observation that the shape of the problem is legal rather than martial, and that I have spent forty-five years in a court and you have spent yours in the field, and that between us we might be looking at two halves of the same wall."

*He is going to ask,* Verinus thought. *He has been assembling it since I said the word talents. He is going to ask why a man who does not believe in prophecy has been carrying the fable of the paragons around in his chest for thirty-seven years and has chosen this cabin to unpack it in, and I have no intention of answering, and he is going to notice that I have no intention of answering, and that will be its own answer and I cannot help it.*

"They have a word for me at Altherion," he said. "The Curia use it when they think I am out of the room. I have never accepted it, I have never claimed it, and I have twice had it struck from a minute. What I will say is that when I told your Lord Knight Commander that I would go to the ninth whether or not you came, I said something else in the same breath that she has not stopped weighing since, which is that I would like to be used for the thing I was made for."

He reached into his left sleeve.

"You noticed the poppy. You were correct about all of it and you were correct in the order you took it, which was the injury before the sleeve, and I would like you to know that I have been enjoying myself since you opened your mouth."

He took the last seed head out, dry and pale and hard between his fingers, and turned it once so the seed rattled.

"There were three. Two are with Ifedi. I cut them for the ninth and I have four days and one left and you have just instructed me to stop spending pieces of myself before we get there."

He set it down on the table, on top of the ration slate, in the middle of the twenty names, and took his hand away.

# The Cabin, II: Two Halves of the Same Wall

Verinus laughed once, low, and it cost him across the pelvis and he paid it without complaint.

"You did that in eleven minutes," he said. "It took the Arbitration Division nine days to arrive at *event of undetermined aetiology* and they were rather pleased with themselves. You have gone from a name on a slate to conditions rather than prediction to the observation that Kujo is not searching for the Paragon at all, and I did not lead you to any of it, and I was watching for it, because leading a man to a conclusion is how you find out he does not hold it." He set both hands flat on the table. "You hold it. That is worth a great deal more to me than agreement."

The stern window had gone from silver to something closer to pewter as the light moved off the water, and the boom lay across it in a hard black line, and the gulls had thinned to a dozen and were working further out.

"You are correct about the conditions and I want to add one piece to it that you could not have, because it belongs to my side of the world." He turned his right hand over in his sleeve, slowly. The fingers had gone from tingling to something duller and he did not like the direction of it. "I do not think Kujo has been alone in doing this and I do not think all of them understood what they were doing. Cozbi has spent a great many years hollowing things and did it for reasons of his own that had nothing to do with an Archon wearing a life. Every soul he has taken and held, every rite that ended with the world agreeing to something it should have refused, every one of those is a small entry in the same ledger. He may have been building this without ever intending to. That is the part I find genuinely frightening. A conspiracy can be broken because it has members. A condition has contributors, and a contributor does not need to be told."

He let the hull creak underneath that.

"Now. You have asked me a great many hard things and given me nothing back that was easy, so let me be plain about where I stand and you may do what you like with it."

He gathered himself and got his shoulders square against the chair back, which the borrowed pelvis objected to at length.

"We should not be afraid in the manner the Curia is afraid, which is the manner of men who have decided the situation is too large to be acted upon and have therefore begun preparing statements. That kind of fear produces committees. It has never produced a single useful hour." He tilted his head. "But we should also stop behaving as though our enemy does not already know what we intend. He held a false entry open against the Continuum for three weeks in a valley he chose, and he arranged for there to be witnesses, and he wounded the one man on that field capable of describing it and left him alive to do so. That is not the conduct of a force that requires surprise. He is not hiding his hand. He is showing it, because a claim only works if somebody is watching it be accepted."

He looked at his own hands on the table for a moment.

"If I could go out there and meet him myself without the risk, I would go tonight. I would take a boat and I would not tell Maud and she would have my head for it and she would be entirely correct to." He did not look toward the bulkhead. He did not need to. "I have wanted that since the eleventh and I have wanted it in a way that is not clean, and I have examined the wanting and I do not entirely trust it. But I would go. And I do not believe I would win, and I do not believe any single one of us would, and I have stopped constructing arrangements in which one of us does."

"I am afraid," he said. "I want that said out loud in a room where it weighs something, because I have spent nineteen years being told that a Palatine who admits fear removes hope from four thousand people, and I have never once believed it, and they took the pulpit off me before I could test it properly."

He drew in as much of a breath as the ribs would give him.

"I am not afraid of dying. I have had forty-five years to become accustomed to that arithmetic and it is genuinely small. It is a thing I own and I can dispose of it. I am afraid for my people, and I am afraid for yours, and I am afraid because Fate has begun moving at a speed I have never measured in my life. That is not rhetoric. There is a fisherman on the Steps who could tell you the state of the Ebb Channel on any given day of the last forty years, and I sounded it on the ninth of every month for nineteen and it never moved a hand's breadth, and I sounded it three weeks ago and paid out nine fathoms and did not find bottom. Things that do not change have started changing. If we do not change at the same rate we will not be defeated. We will be edited, in exactly the manner you described, and the world will accept the edit because it has already accepted one."

He stopped, and let the room have the quiet, and did not rescue anyone from it.

"So. Your wounds."

He turned his palm up on the table and left it there, open and empty and doing nothing at all.

"I withdraw it. Not conditionally and not with the clause you gave me about the physicians exhausting everything first, though I accept that clause and will hold to it. I withdraw the pressure of it." He met the other man's eyes and kept his own there. "I have spent my life inside an instrument that reads what a thing costs the person saying it, and the great temptation of that instrument is to keep talking until the reading comes out where you want it. Every man who has ever held this office has been tempted by it and I do not know how many resisted. I will not take from you something you have not decided to give. If you decide, you will say it, and I will hear the depth of it and I will know, and until you say it there is nothing here for me to weigh."

A gull went over the stern low enough to throw a shadow across the table and neither of them looked up.

"I want to say one thing that has no business in a strategic conversation and I am going to say it anyway, because I am seventy-one and I have four days and I have stopped rationing this sort of thing."

He shifted. Something in the pelvis ground and he breathed through it and continued as though the sentence had not been interrupted.

"We should have been allies eleven years ago. The war with Cozbi took that from both of us and I have never been satisfied with the explanations I was given for why the Holy Sea stood where it stood. I read the dispatches out of the Zettari front the way other men read scripture. I read what you did at the crossing and I read what you did afterward with the people who had nowhere to go, and I read the part nobody quotes, which is that you went back for the ones who had already been written off, and I have modelled myself on a man I had never met, at my age, in an office four hundred years older than yours." His mouth moved slightly. "I would not admit that in front of the Curia. I am admitting it in front of your captain and my Lord Knight Commander, which is considerably worse, and I find I do not care."

He put one finger on the edge of the ration slate and moved it a fraction straighter.

"A king weighs his people with good intent. That is the whole of the office in seven words and every ecclesiastical apparatus I have ever served has been an elaborate scaffold around it. You have done it under conditions that would have broken the scaffold. I have done it inside a building."

He let it go, and moved on, because a man who lingers on a compliment turns it into an invoice.

"You are right that my people cling. They have been clinging since the year I turned sixty-eight and they have done it out of love and it has cost me the pulpit and it will cost me more before it is finished. I understand it and I have never once been able to accept it." He looked toward the window. "They are doing it about Darius now. Every man in Altherion has him buried and has begun arranging what happens to the seat, and I have sat in nine days of correspondence about a vacancy that I do not accept exists."

He said the next part carefully, because it was the one he had the least standing on.

"Darius was drawn into the Plane of Fate. He was not killed there, so far as any account establishes, and the Plane of Fate is not a grave, and a man who has been taken into the Works is not necessarily gone from the accounting. I have no proof. I have a reading and the reading is mine alone and it is exactly the sort of single unsupported finding I have spent my life refusing to enter, and I would be laughed out of a Curia session for saying it." His jaw set. "He is going to come back. I have believed that since the ninth day and I have not written it down anywhere, and I am telling you because if I am right then a great deal of what Altherion is currently arranging is going to be embarrassing, and if I am wrong then I would rather one person knew I hoped."

He came back to the harder ground with something close to relief.

"Which brings me to the thing your captain will care about more than any of this. I do not believe Kujo has come alone."

He turned the twenty names on the slate a quarter turn so they faced the other side of the table.

"A titan does not need generals to destroy. He needs them to hold. A claim against the Continuum has to be maintained, and it was maintained in that valley for three weeks while he was elsewhere, and something kept it open. That is a garrison. It may not be a garrison in a shape you would recognise from a field, but there is a structure out there doing the work of holding a lie in place, and the hundred and fifteen went somewhere that required moving them and keeping them." He tapped the slate once. "Everything I have seen in twenty-nine days is consistent with an army. Not a host on a road. An apparatus. And an apparatus is not answered by any one nation, and certainly not by mine, which has four hundred years of doctrine explaining why it does not project force past its own border and a standing quarrel with every polity that has ever needed it to."

He was quiet for a moment.

"That doctrine was mine to defend and I defended it and it is the reason a deacon's request sat in a drawer for eleven weeks. I am not going to pretend the two are unconnected."

*And that is the sentence I have not said aloud to anyone but Ilse,* he thought, *and I have just handed it across a table to a foreign sovereign in front of two witnesses, and if he chooses to use it he can end me with it inside a fortnight, and I have decided that a man who will not put his own hand on the record has no business asking anybody else to.*

"So it will be the nations together or it will be nothing. And I am aware of how that sentence sounds coming from the Holy Sea, which has spent four centuries requiring everybody else to come to it." He turned his hand over. "Which is why I am going to ask you for something considerably more tedious than your body."

He looked up.

"Teach me. Not the sword. I am seventy-one and I would embarrass us both. Your law. What the Zettari mean by Form and by Essence and by Existence and by local sequence, in the terms your own people use rather than the terms my Curia has translated them into, which I suspect are wrong in at least three places and confidently wrong in one. You said it yourself, and I have been sitting here since you said it doing nothing but turning it over. You have been speaking a legal language and using it as a sword. I have been speaking one and using it as a scale. If Kujo's standing is a question of what recognises his authority, then I need to know what your discipline holds is required for a thing to remain what it is, and you need to know what mine holds is required for a claim to be entered at all, and neither of us is going to acquire that in four days by being polite about it."

He glanced, once, at Kesh against the bulkhead.

"I will send whatever your Sovereign asks for. Archive access at Altherion, the Ebb Court's registers, the Alabastrine liturgical corpus, the parts of it we do not lend. If the Curia objects they may take it up with me in the fortieth day of a quarantine."

*And there is the other half of it,* he thought, *which he has already understood and has been courteous enough not to say. A kingdom that has been the richest and most comfortable arbiter in four kingdoms does not get to arrive at somebody's sickbed with a slate of names and call it partnership. There is a bill outstanding and it is four hundred years old.*

"I will say the last of it badly and then I will stop talking, because I have been in this chair considerably longer than my body agreed to."

He looked at the seed head sitting on the slate where it had been nudged back toward him and did not pick it up.

"I have spent forty-five years weighing people and I have reached exactly one conclusion that has held under every test I have put to it. Nearly every wound this world carries comes back to some arrangement in which one party had more standing than another and both of them knew it. Nations. Houses. A magistrate and a woman who could not corroborate. A slaver on a harbour step and the property he was collecting. It is the same shape at every scale and it is the shape Kujo is using, because a claim only gets accepted when the thing being claimed against has no standing to refuse. Vaeloris had no standing. That is why it worked there and not somewhere with a garrison and a treaty."

He set his palm flat.

"So if you ask me what the world's disease is, it is not the void. The void is opportunistic. The disease is that we have built four hundred years of institutions on the assumption that some testimony is heavier for having come out of the right mouth, and my kingdom is the only one that wrote down that this is false, and my kingdom has spent four centuries failing to act on its own document."

He stopped there and let the ship talk for a while.

Then he came back to the one question that had been sitting on the table since the man had put it there, and he came back to it the way he had come back to everything else in his life, which was slowly and without flinching.

"*Used*," he said. "You are right to dislike the word. I chose it deliberately and I did not enjoy choosing it."

He turned the poppy head once with one finger so the seed rattled, and left it where it lay.

"Altherion's answer is that a Paragon is called and consecrated and set in place by the Church, which is the answer of an institution that requires a role in the transaction. Mine is worse and I have never written it down." He looked up. "A Paragon is not a person with an Archon inside them. A Paragon is what an Archon looks like when it is subject to hunger and weather and being wrong. The life is not a container. It is the Archon in the only form that can act inside a world that has rules. Which means the working is not a tool the person picks up. It is what they are, the whole way down, and there is no floor under it and nothing left over that is separately a person."

He said the next part quietly and did not soften any of it.

"So to be *used* is not to be spent. It is to be resolved. A claim is entered against the world and something arrives that is constitutionally incapable of letting it stand, and when the contradiction is finished there is nothing to send home, because the contradicting was not a thing it did. And every account I have read in thirty-seven years, in every branch, including the two that agree on nothing else, ends the same way." He paused. "None of them come back. Not one. And the Church calls that consecration and I call it the arithmetic, and I have never once been able to make that distinction land in another man's head at speed."

He put both hands into his sleeves and looked across the table.

"That is my answer. Not Altherion's. Now ask me the question you actually want to ask, because you have been carrying it since I said *made for* and you have been too well brought up to put it down."

# The Cabin, III: The Lowest Ardency Ever Assayed

Verinus did not answer for a while, and he let the silence be exactly as long as it needed to be, because a man who rushes to the front of a question like that is running from it.

The light through the stern window had gone the colour of wet slate. Somewhere forward a bell was struck and two dressers argued briefly about linen and stopped. Under the table, the borrowed pelvis had begun to do a thing it had not done in the first hour, a deep grinding ache that arrived on a slow cycle rather than a constant one, and he had started counting the intervals without meaning to and had got them down to somewhere near eleven seconds.

"You asked it correctly," he said, "and you asked it in the order that costs you something, and I am going to answer it in the same order, and you are not going to like the first part."

He took his hands out of his sleeves and put them where they could be seen.

"No. I do not expect to survive a battle. I would not survive one at fifty-eight and I certainly will not at seventy-one, and I want that in the room plainly before we do any of the harder arithmetic." He turned the right hand over and looked at it. The fingers had gone from tingling to a dull absence and he flexed them once and got about two thirds of what he asked for. "You have been told, I imagine by somebody who thought it was a compliment, that the Palatine is a Stage above almost anyone in his own capital. That is true and it is the least useful true thing anybody has ever said about me. My Ardency is the lowest ever assayed in a practitioner of my Stage. The Measurewrights ran the assay three times because the first two of them assumed the instrument had failed, and there is a note in the margin of the third in a hand I do not recognise that reads *confirm subject is conscious.*"

He let the corner of his mouth move.

"I have expressed force once in my life. It was at forty-four, on the harbour mole, and it lasted about four seconds, and eleven people are dead of it and two of them were people I had gone there to get out. I reported myself to the chapter hold the same evening in writing before anybody thought to ask. The finding was lawful use of force in defence of persons. I have requested its review four times and been refused four times, and I will request it a fifth time if I am alive in the spring, and I will be refused again."

*And that is the honest reason I would go out to him alone,* he thought, *and I have never said it out loud to anybody including Ilse, and I am not going to say it out loud here either, because a man who has been carrying eleven names for twenty-seven years does not get to call his own arithmetic clean.*

"The Holy Sea does not usually elect men like me," he went on. "The Register will show you six centuries of Palatines with Ardency in the upper thresholds, because a kingdom whose entire theology is a scale prefers a hand strong enough to hold it steady, and there is nothing stupid in that. I was elected on economics." He said it without embarrassment. "On four papers concerning the pricing of testimony, the rate at which a court's procedure quietly converts poverty into guilt, and the interest question. Nineteen years ago that mattered more to the electors than what I could do to a wall. It has mattered rather less in the last twenty-nine days and I am aware of the joke."

The hull creaked. He let it.

"Now the part you actually asked."

He met the other man's eyes and kept his there and did not decorate any of it.

"I am not the Judger's Paragon."

He said it flatly, without weight behind it, and then said the rest before anyone could pick the first sentence up and carry it somewhere.

"And I want you to notice what that sentence did in this room, because you have been in my Domain for two hours and you have felt what a thing weighs when a man means it entirely, and you will have felt that one arrive at about the depth of a statement of preference. I know. I have measured it going out of my own mouth every time I have said it for thirty-seven years, and it has never once gone down the way a fact goes down."

He turned his palm up, open and empty.

"So I will give you the accurate version rather than the comfortable one. I do not know. I have no reading on myself that I would enter as a finding. My working contradicts a false entry, which is what the records say the thing does, and my working is the one harmonization I have preached against since I was thirty-four and hold at a depth that embarrasses my own Curia, which is either a very stupid arrangement or exactly the arrangement the records describe, and I have never been able to decide which and have stopped pretending I am close to deciding." His jaw set. "What I refuse is the title. That refusal is mine and it is real and I have had it struck from a minute twice. Altherion uses it when they think I am out of the room and my own parishes use it to my face, and there are four chapels on this coast with my likeness in glass, and I have had two of them taken out and been made to stop."

*There is the whole of it,* he thought, *the actual fear, and he has been circling it for an hour with more courtesy than I deserve. Not the resolving. The glass.*

"I do not want to be remembered as a god," Verinus said. "I want that said in front of your captain and my Lord Knight Commander, because they will both outlive me and I would like witnesses who cannot be edited. I have watched what a deified man does to the people who come after him. Every reform I have ever pushed through, somebody at Altherion has attributed to divine instruction inside a generation, and the moment a thing is divine instruction it stops being an argument, and the moment it stops being an argument nobody can improve it and nobody can be blamed for failing to enforce it. That is how a doctrine dies. Not by being repealed. By being canonised."

He put one finger on the edge of the ration slate.

"I want to be remembered as a man who cared about his people and was frequently wrong about how. That is not modesty. That is the only version of me that anybody can argue with in a hundred years, and a version that can be argued with is a version that survives."

He stopped and breathed, and did not get the whole of it, and went on anyway.

"You worry that I am practised at finding ways to make my death useful. You are correct. I have been doing it since I was twenty-six on a harbour step and I have got extremely good at it, and you are the second person to say it to my face and the first one has been saying it for twelve years." He did not look toward the bulkhead. "So I will give you the undertaking you are actually asking for and I will give it plainly, and you may hold me to it in front of both of them."

He set both hands flat.

"I am not going to the ninth to die. If death arrives there it will arrive because every other path was exhausted first, and I will be the one who has to demonstrate that they were, and I will demonstrate it to your Lord Knight Commander's satisfaction rather than my own, because my own is not to be trusted in this particular matter and I know it." A pause. "I am prepared for the version of the ninth that does not involve my death. I want that stated too. I have four days, one poppy, a hundred and fifteen names, an alliance that did not exist this morning, and a fortnight of quarantine to spend learning what your bloodline means by Form. That is a considerable amount of work for a man arranging his own ending, and I would rather do all of it than any of the other thing."

The gulls came back across the stern and their shadows crossed the table and went.

"As to whether the contradiction could resolve me four days from now. Yes. It could. I am not going to dress that." He said it steadily. "The working I would use is the one that emptied me at Vaeloris and I could not stand up afterward and I was on my knees in the ash for eleven more minutes doing it a second time for no evidentiary benefit whatsoever. If what is waiting on the ninth is of a scale with what was done in that valley, then contradicting it is not a technique I perform. It is a claim I enter against something that has already demonstrated it can make the world agree with it. And if I am what your books have prepared language for, then entering that claim is not something I do. It is what I am, all the way down, and there is nothing separately a person underneath to send home."

He let that sit and did not soften it.

"I am not certain. I am telling you what the exposure is. You have buried people who understood the cost, so I will not insult you by pretending the number is lower than it is."

*And he will not accept that,* he thought, *and he should not, and I have spent nineteen years surrounded by people who accept it and I have found that I do not like it as much as I expected to.*

"Now the thing you did not ask and I am going to answer anyway, because you have earned better than a speech about my own mortality."

He turned the poppy head once where it sat in front of him, and did not pick it up, and left it exactly where the other man had pushed it.

"My people cling to me and it is not because they love me. Some of them do. Most of them are frightened of a document." He tapped the table twice. "The Rite of Equality is mine. I drafted it at thirty-one, I got it through at forty, and there is not one word in it that is not a restatement of what the Wellspring already does, which is that a testimony's weight is its density and its depth and has nothing whatsoever to do with the vessel carrying it. I did not invent equality in the Holy Sea. I wrote down the physics and made the courts stop pretending they had not noticed."

He looked up.

"But they believe the instrument is me. They have watched it work for nineteen years inside a Domain that covers the capital and the harbour and four parishes and stops at fifty kilometres, and they have quietly concluded that when the Domain goes, so does the Rite. Every Beastkin family in the seaward parishes, every Dawi house on Cooper's Rise, every man who came off a boat with no parish and no name and got a hearing anyway. Four hundred people came out of their houses this morning to watch an old man walk down a hill, and every one of them was doing the arithmetic, and the arithmetic they were doing was not about me."

He drew as much breath as the ribs would give him.

"They are wrong. That is the important part and I have not been permitted a pulpit to say it from in three years." His voice did not rise. It did not need to. "The Sounding does not enforce the Rite. The Rite is enforced by four hundred years of Thaloré running under this coast whether or not there is a Palatine standing on it, and by a bench of magistrates who have been applying it since before I was elected, and by Ilse Barrow, who has been outweighing me in my own Domain on procedure for twelve years and who will still be doing it to whoever comes next. If the Rite depends on one seventy-one-year-old man then it was never law. It was a favour. And a favour extended by a powerful man to people with no standing is precisely the arrangement the Rite exists to abolish."

He let his hand rest flat on the wood.

"So if there is one thing I would like to arrange before the ninth, it is not my survival. It is a kingdom that does not require it."

He looked across the table then, and something in his face eased for the first time since he had come through the door.

"Which is the other reason I am glad you are here, and I am going to say it badly. I have spent nineteen years being the oldest man in every room I enter and being told by physicians and deacons and a Lord Knight Commander that I am the load-bearing member. I am proud to be sitting across from somebody younger than me and considerably more capable, who has already corrected me twice in an hour, and who has just pushed a poppy seed head back across a table at me because he has decided I am spending myself badly." He inclined his head very slightly. "You told me not to model myself on the reports. Understood. I will model myself on the man who went back because people were still there and who has spent this entire conversation being irritated by the fact that necessary and admirable are different words."

Then the ease went out of him, and what was underneath it was the thing he had been holding since he sat down.

"So. What happens on the ninth."

He drew the ration slate a little way toward himself, and set the poppy head down on the far corner of it, off the names, and put his hand flat beside it on the bare wood.

"The culling is at the Rimward marches. Inland, north of the moor road, on a stretch of the border the Accord surveyed and never garrisoned. It is not the Holy Sea's action. It was not proposed by me, I do not have a vote on it, and I could not stop it if it were mine, and I have spent six days failing to find a mechanism and I want you to hear that I failed rather than that I disagreed." His hand did not move. "It is a containment measure. There are people inside a line that somebody has drawn, and on the ninth that line is going to be closed, and the reasoning that was put in front of me is not stupid. That is what has kept me awake. It is not stupid. It is what I would have written at forty if somebody had shown me the same four reports and given me nine days."

He looked up, and his eyes were entirely steady, and his voice had gone very quiet.

"And I have counted six hundred and six people back into the accounting out of a valley where the world had agreed nobody had ever stood, and I have twenty names in chalk and a hundred and fifteen missing, and on the ninth I am going to walk onto that line and put myself between an instrument and the people it is about to be pointed at, and I am going to ask the men holding it, out loud, in front of witnesses, by what standing they enter the claim."

He turned his palm up.

"That is the whole of it. There is no battle in it unless somebody decides there is."

# The Cabin, IV: The Eleven Words

Verinus did not answer at once, and the pause was not evasion. He was doing the arithmetic on what he could give and what he did not yet own, and he had spent forty-five years refusing to hand a man a finding dressed as a fact.

The cabin had gone cold in the way a ship goes cold, from the deck up through the soles. The light off the water had thinned to a hard grey band across the bulkhead and the gulls had gone quiet somewhere in the last quarter hour, and he had noticed the quiet before he had noticed the cold and had liked neither.

"Those are all fair questions and I am going to give you the ones I have and be plain about the shape of the hole where the rest should be." He put his palm flat on the wood. "The line was drawn at the Grand Church's request and entered by the Accord's Enforcement Division under a standing provision for aetiological containment. Enforcement drew it. Altherion asked for it. Alabaster was consulted and Alabaster did not object, and the man who did not object on Alabaster's behalf is sitting in front of you, and he did not object because he was four days into a valley and a Deacon signed the acknowledgment in his absence with his standing authority, which she was entitled to do, and which I have not held against her and will not."

*And she has not slept properly since,* he thought. *She has not said one word about it to me and she is not going to and I have three witnesses to the fact that I never asked her to.*

"What is wrong with the people inside it. That is the honest hole." He turned his hand over. "The stated ground is a presentation. Enforcement's physicians have a description of it in eleven words and the eleven words are the only thing anybody outside that line has been given. Persistent discontinuity of recognition. Neighbours forget them. Not who they are. That they were there at all, in the last hour, in the room. A wife sets one place. A man's own dog will not come to him. It passes and comes back and passes again, and it has been reported in four settlements on that stretch of the moor road since the seventeenth."

He let that go into the table.

"You will hear the resemblance and you will be right to. It is the same operation as the false zero at Vaeloris, running slowly, in the small, in living people rather than in a valley. Whether that means those people are carrying something, or standing too near something, or have been marked by something that intends to come back for them, I do not know. Nobody knows. Enforcement does not know either and their filing says so in a footnote and their operational order does not."

He drew what breath the ribs would allow.

"So. Is the danger real. I believe something is genuinely happening on that stretch of moor and I would not tell you otherwise to win your support. Is it proved. No. There is no case series, no established interval, no mechanism, no test that distinguishes an affected person from a frightened one, and the entire evidentiary basis is testimony from neighbours whose recognition is the faculty allegedly under attack." His jaw set. "Which is the piece I have been unable to make anybody at Altherion look at for six days. The witnesses to the condition are the population being contained. Their testimony is the only evidence, and the operation treats that same testimony as unreliable, and it cannot be both."

"How many," he said, before it could be asked. "Enforcement's figure is between eleven and fourteen hundred. They will not commit to a number and I have asked for one four times. A number is a list and a list is names and names are precisely what nobody wants entered before the ninth, because a name is a person and a person has standing and a figure between eleven and fourteen hundred has none at all."

*Six hundred and six,* he thought. *I got that number kneeling in ash with my palm on the ground and it took me two hours and it cost me the use of my hands for a week, and there are fourteen hundred people on a moor whose names could be taken by four clerks with a cart in a single afternoon, and nobody has sent the cart.*

"The instrument." He said it evenly. "That is the part I will not soften. It is not soldiers. Enforcement has a Sealwright detachment and a warded perimeter, and the closure is a sealing operation. They intend to complete the boundary glyph around the marches and then withdraw the authorization glyph from everything standing inside it. In their filing it is described as isolation of an aetiological remainder."

He looked up.

"You will forgive me for using the language of my office once. It is a claim. It is a formal entry, filed by lawful practitioners under a standing provision, asserting that between eleven and fourteen hundred people are a remainder. And when the Continuum accepts the entry, what happens on that moor will be indistinguishable in kind from what was done at Vaeloris. Smaller. Slower. Correctly documented." He let his hand rest. "Kujo made the world agree with him and everybody has spent twenty-nine days calling it an atrocity. On the ninth my own institutions propose to do the same operation with a seal, a form of words, and a countersignature, and every man involved will go home and sleep."

The hull worked under them. He let it.

"That is why I am going. Not because they are monsters. Because they are serious men who have looked at a real danger and arrived at the one answer that requires nobody to solve the difficult version of the problem, and because I taught half of them how to construct a sound filing."

He shifted, and something in the borrowed pelvis caught and ground, and he waited it out.

"Your questions are better than mine and I am taking all of them. What evidence. What lesser containment was attempted. What threshold converts suspicion into execution. Who bears false inclusion. What removes a person from the line when the reason expires. And the last one." He tapped the table once. "Who decided the people inside no longer hold standing equal to the people outside. I will put that one first, because the answer is nobody. Nobody decided it. It was arrived at by drafting, and a thing arrived at by drafting can be unarrived at by drafting, and that is the only genuine advantage I have out there."

"On the Rite," he said, "you are right and I have been going about it backward for three years. Telling four thousand frightened people that I am unnecessary is a claim made by the man whose necessity is in question, and it weighs exactly nothing. Making them watch it work without me is a demonstration. I will send to Ilse tonight. She will have the Rite entered under Attestis by every independent office that can lawfully hold it, and she will outweigh me publicly on something real rather than something arranged, which she will enjoy more than any woman has ever enjoyed anything, and the magistrates will enter findings in my absence and the findings will be published in my absence." A pause. "She has been asking me to do that since the year I turned sixty-five. I have been refusing because I did not want to look as though I were arranging my own funeral. That was vanity and it has cost three years."

He looked toward the poppy head sitting on the corner of the slate and did not take it.

"Now the piece you did not ask for and will want, and it is the one thing I have that Altherion actually holds and does not publish."

He put both hands on the table.

"The received account is that Kujo acts against the Plane of Fate. Every summary you will be given says so. It is an assumption and I think it is wrong, and the Church's own oldest material does not support it." His voice went quieter without losing any edge. "A claim requires standing. A false entry that the Continuum accepts and keeps on the books for three weeks is not vandalism. It is filing. Nothing outside the Works has the standing to file, and the Works do not accept entries from strangers, and I have spent twenty-nine days trying to construct a version of Vaeloris in which he is an outsider forcing a door, and every version collapses on the same point. The door opened. It was not broken. It opened because whatever presented itself was entitled to be admitted."

He let that stand for a moment.

"So I do not think he is at war with Fate. I think he is inside it, and I think that is why nothing anybody has learned about resisting force has been of the slightest use, and I think that is what the fable of the paragons is actually a description of, badly transcribed by men who preferred the version with a prophecy in it." He raised one hand slightly. "I want to be precise about the status of that. It is a working theory. It is not a finding, it is not the Church's position, and there are two branches at Altherion that would put me on a bench for saying it. But it is the only reading under which everything I have seen on my own coast makes sense, and I have been unable to break it for six days, and I have genuinely tried."

He put his hands on the table's edge and looked at them.

"I do not have the rest. I am going to get it. Some of it is in the restricted corpus and I have already told you that you may have it, and I would rather you read the originals and told me I was wrong than have the Curia agree with me for the wrong reasons."

Then he began the business of standing, which took a while and which he did not apologise for.

The left hand went flat on the table. The weight went down through the arm rather than through the hips, and he pushed up in one long controlled movement with the knee registering its usual complaint and the borrowed pelvis registering something considerably worse, and he came upright by degrees and stayed still for three breaths with the deckhead a hand's width above his head. His fingers had not come back. The right hand closed to about two thirds and stopped there.

*Two hours in a chair and four days to the ninth,* he thought, *and I have to walk the deck, get down a boarding ladder, cross a tender, and climb a sea wall, and I am going to do all four of those badly in front of people who have already decided I am a monument. Ilse was right about the pulpit and she is going to be right about this and I am going to argue with her anyway.*

"We will speak again in the morning," he said, "and the morning after that, and every morning your physicians permit, because I have a fortnight and a bloodline's worth of jurisprudence to learn and I am seventy-one and slow. Rest. Sit tightly. Let them do what they came aboard to do, and do not remove a dressing because you dislike the smell of the antiseptic, since I understand that undertaking was given in this room and I intend to hold you to it in front of your captain."

He let a beat go by.

"We will both be moving very soon."

He turned toward the door and stopped, because there was one more piece and it was the piece he liked least.

"I will send to the Moto. I have been sending to the Moto since the fourteenth and I have had nothing back, and I have had nothing back from three other quarters that have never once failed to answer a Palatine's seal." He did not look round. "The Accord's relay through the Reach has been silent for eleven days. Nalūn acknowledged and has said nothing since. There is a chapter hold in the Old World that has missed two scheduled dispatches, and the last thing anybody heard out of it was a request for a Sealwright."

He got his hands into his sleeves.

"Everyone is treating those as separate failures. Bad weather, bad roads, a war. I have stopped assuming that. The Inner World is going quiet from the outside in, one relay at a time, and I would rather be wrong about it in front of you than right about it in a report nobody reads until the spring."

Maud Thole had come off the bulkhead without being told and was standing a half pace off his left elbow with her hands loose and her face doing very little and doing it badly.

"I am fine, Thole."

"You are not."

"I am upright and I have four days and those are different problems." He set his shoulders as far as they would go. "You may take my arm on the ladder and I will not fight you about it, and I will not fight you about the chair either, and you may enter both concessions in whatever record you keep of my failures. We should get back inside the wall before Ilse decides that fourteen days means something other than what she wrote and sends the royal guard out onto a tender in this light."

He turned back one last time and put two fingers on the ration slate, and pushed it a hand's breadth across the table until it sat squarely in front of the Sovereign of the Zettari, twenty chalked names and one dry seed head still resting on the corner where it had been left.

"Keep it," Verinus said. "Copy it three times as you ordered and keep the original aboard. I have carried enough today and you told me to stop spending pieces of myself, and I find that I would rather leave the twenty with the man who intends to go on calling the other hundred and fifteen missing."

He put his hand out for the door.

"Four days. I will come to you tomorrow with the Enforcement filing and the eleven words, and you will find at least three things wrong with them that I have not, and I would like you to say all three out loud in front of Maud so that she stops believing I am the only person in this quarantine who can read."

# What Answered

The wool store smelled of lanolin under the vinegar, a deep animal sweetness that four centuries of scrubbing had failed to get out of the beams and that came down warm whenever the sun found the roof slates. Lye on the floor. Chalk in everything, tracked in on boots from a foreshore that had been white since the shoal broke, so that every man who walked the length of the hall left a pale print and the prints stayed until somebody swept.

Kwon Mu-jin came up out of sleep the way he had been coming up out of it for nine days, which was all at once and from too far down, and sat forward on the trestle bed with his weight going onto his left hand before he had entirely worked out where he was.

The right side of the world was gone.

He put his fingers up and found linen over the eye, fresh, wound down and back and tucked at the temple by somebody with better hands than the dresser who had done it yesterday, and he understood two things in the same second. The dressing had been changed while he slept. He had not woken while it was being changed.

He sat with that.

Down the hall a kettle was going. Somebody had propped the doors at the seaward end and the light came in flat and low across forty trestles and made a long bright wedge on the boards, and in the wedge a Zettari dresser was picking through a basket of boiled linen with the deliberate slowness of a woman who had been awake since the fourth hour. Gulls outside. Fewer than there had been. Two men arguing about salt fish somewhere behind the partition, comfortably, at length, with neither of them wanting to win.

His waist had been redone as well. He could feel the pull of the new binding across the abdominal wall when he breathed in past halfway, and he stopped at halfway and did not go further, which he told himself was prudence.

Ara was sitting on the stool at the foot of the trestle with her sleeves pushed up and her forearms bare and a strip of used linen still hanging over one knee, and she had been there long enough that the tea beside her had gone cold and skinned over.

"What are you planning, brother?"

Mu-jin found the wooden cup on the crate beside the bed, which required turning his head considerably further than it used to, and drank, and put it down.

"I do not know what you mean."

"You do."

"I have been unconscious for most of nine days. My planning has been extremely limited."

"You were awake at the fourth hour and you sent Doyun to the harbour master's clerk to ask what time the escort forms tomorrow, and you told him not to mention it to me, and he told me before he had got his boots on." Ara did not raise her voice at all. "So we can begin from there, or we can spend the morning with me pretending I am not going to find out."

*She has cut the dressings and she has counted the days and she has already spoken to my student,* Mu-jin thought. *There are perhaps four people alive who would have done all three before breakfast and three of them are dead and the fourth is holding a piece of my own bandage.*

"Doyun is fifteen," he said. "You have taken advantage of a child."

"Doyun is loyal to you in a way that will get him killed and I intend to fix that before it does." She lifted the used linen off her knee and folded it once, precisely, and set it in the basin at her feet. "You fought. You had no business fighting. You had been out of the Draft for six days when it happened and your Shell was still ringing and every practitioner on that hull knew it, including you, and you went anyway."

"It may have been reckless."

"It was."

"It may have been reckless." He let a breath out and did not get the whole of it. "And the boy is alive. He is eleven years old, he is somewhere down that hall in a bed with his mother asleep on the floor beside it, and he is alive. I would like both halves of that sentence on the table before we go any further, because you keep putting one of them down."

Ara looked at him for a long moment.

"So now you are trying to be a hero," she said. "I thought you were a teacher."

"What kind of teacher would I be if I did not do the thing I have been telling them to do."

"An older one."

The kettle came off the fire down the hall and somebody swore mildly at it and the two men behind the partition stopped arguing about salt fish for exactly as long as that took.

Mu-jin turned his head so he could see her properly out of the eye he had, which he was going to have to keep doing all day and which he had already decided not to be irritated about in front of her.

"Say it in Sum-gol," he said.

Ara's jaw moved.

"Do not."

"You always argue better in it and you have been arguing in Concord Common since you sat down, which means you have prepared this and you are frightened of it going wrong." He kept his tone level. "So say it properly and stop managing me. I have been managed by four physicians and a Zettari captain and I would rather be shouted at by my sister."

She was quiet for a while. Down the hall the dresser found the linen she wanted and went away with it.

"You called something," Ara said.

Mu-jin did not answer.

"I had your hands. I had both of your hands from the moment they brought you in and I did not let go of them for four hours, because that is what I am for and because nobody else in this building could have held the Shell together while it did what it was doing." Her voice had gone very quiet and had not softened at all. "So do not sit there and tell me you spent Essence and took a wound. I know what spending Essence feels like coming back up somebody's arms. That was not that."

"Ara."

"Something answered you." She said it flatly. "And it answered fast, and it answered the whole way, and whatever went out from you to get it did not go out through the ordinary door."

*And there it is,* he thought, *and she has been sitting on it for nine days and has waited until I was upright to put it down in front of me, which is a courtesy I would not have extended to her.*

He looked at the beams.

"The wall was coming," he said. "Two courses of dressed stone and whatever the roof had been carrying, and the boy was under the line of it, and I had about a second and a half. I have no Draft that forms in a second and a half. I have never met anybody who does." He turned the cup a quarter turn on the crate without picking it up. "So I asked without the Draft."

"You asked without standing."

"I asked without standing."

The words came out of him plainly and he did not dress them, because there was no arrangement of them that would have made it better and she would have measured any attempt.

"Every teacher in our house has said the same thing to every student for six hundred years and I have said it myself in a room with nine of them sitting in front of me," he said. "You cannot compel. You are not the one holding the power in the exchange. You are recognised, or you are not, and a summoner who misrepresents himself gets exactly nothing, and the getting nothing is the mercy. That is the whole of the discipline. It is not a warning attached to the discipline. It is the discipline."

Ara said nothing.

"I asked as though I had standing I do not have," Mu-jin said. "And I would like to tell you that I did it in a moment of confusion, and I did not. I knew what I was doing while I was doing it. I had time for exactly one thought and the thought was that the boy would be dead in a second and a half and that I would rather answer for the other thing afterward."

"And?"

"And something answered."

The light came off the boards and moved a hand's breadth as the sun found a gap.

"That is the part I will not discuss with you," he said. "Not this morning and not for a while."

"Mu-jin."

"I am not being mysterious and I am not protecting you. I do not know what answered." He met her eye. "I have spent nine days trying to give it a shape and the shape will not come, and every time I go near it the eye does what it is doing now, which you can see and which I would rather you did not comment on. When I have something worth saying I will say it to you first. You have my word on that and my word on that particular thing has never once been bad."

Ara reached out and took his hand off the blanket and held it in both of hers, and her thumbs went automatically to the pulse point at the wrist, because she could not help it and had never been able to.

She counted. He let her.

"Eighty-eight," she said. "Sitting up, at rest, nine days out, in a cold room."

"That is high."

"That is high and you know it is high and you have known since the fourth hour, and you sent a fifteen-year-old to ask about escort times anyway." Her grip did not change. "I am afraid of losing you, brother. That is the whole of what I came to say and everything before it was scaffolding. Look at what you are. You cannot see out of one side. You cannot breathe past halfway. You have something in you that you cannot name and that you go grey about when you approach it, and the escort forms tomorrow at the second hour and you are going to be on it."

"Yes."

"Do you not think of yourself at all?"

He was a long time answering, and the honest part of him noticed that he was taking the time to arrange it and made himself stop arranging it.

"Not often," he said. "And I have decided that is a fault rather than a virtue, and I have not done anything about it, and I am aware of the difference between those two sentences."

*She was nine when Sum-gol went,* he thought. *She has been changing other people's dressings ever since and she has never once asked anybody to notice that she was doing it, and every argument she has ever won with me has been won by measuring my pulse in the middle of it.*

The doors at the seaward end banged.

"Teacher." Doyun came up the hall at the pace of somebody who had been told repeatedly not to run indoors and had negotiated it down to a very fast walk with a great deal of arm movement, fifteen years old, thin as a rope, with chalk to the knee and a slate under one arm and his hair doing something the sea wind had suggested. "Teacher, the escort forms at the second hour and it is the cut rather than the coast, which the quartermaster says is madness because the cut floods, and the quartermaster has been saying so at volume for an hour and nobody is listening to him, and I think that is because the Palatine chose it and nobody has told the quartermaster that yet."

"Doyun."

"Also there are two Alabastrine clerks at the outer gate with a cart asking for a list of everyone in this building by name and parish, and the Zettari captain has told them she will provide it, and the clerks looked as though nobody had ever said yes to them before in their lives."

"Doyun."

"Yes, Teacher."

"You told my sister where I sent you."

Doyun's arms came down.

"She asked me directly," he said, with the wounded precision of a boy who had thought about this on the way back. "You told me not to mention it. You did not tell me to lie about it. Those are different instructions and I have been taught by you personally that they are different instructions, and I would like the record to show that I have applied a lesson."

Ara made a sound into her own shoulder.

*He is going to be unbearable inside three years,* Doyun thought, standing at the foot of the trestle with his slate against his chest and his ears going, *and I am going to be the reason, and I have decided I do not care because he is upright and this morning is the first morning anybody has told me anything at all.*

Mu-jin looked at him for a moment.

"Correct," he said. "Go and eat something. Take the slate to the captain afterward and offer to write the list, because you have a better hand than her dressers and because a list with the names spelled properly is worth more than a list."

Doyun went. He got about six paces and turned round.

"Teacher. Is it true that the man they are all talking about is coming with us. The old one. The Alabastrine."

"It is."

"They say he knelt in a village and counted the dead out loud." Doyun's arms had come back up without his knowing. "For two hours. In his father's dialect. And then he did it again because one count is not a finding, which I did not understand until Captain Kesh explained it and now I cannot stop thinking about it."

"Then you have learned two things this morning and it is not yet the first hour," Mu-jin said. "Go and eat."

He went, properly this time.

The hall settled. Somebody had got the kettle going again.

"Nine of them," Ara said. "There were nine in your room at Sum-gol. There is one, and he is fifteen, and he has just quoted your own instruction back at you correctly in front of me, and you looked pleased about it for a full second before you remembered to look severe."

"I was pleased."

"I know."

She turned his hand over and pressed her thumb along the base of the palm and watched his face while she did it, and whatever she found there she filed and did not report.

"You are going tomorrow," she said. "I am not going to try to stop you. I stopped trying to stop you when I was fourteen and it has never once worked and it has cost me a great deal of sleep. So I am going to do the other thing."

"Ara."

"I am coming with you."

"You are needed here. There are forty people in this hall."

"There are forty people in this hall and eleven dressers and two surgeons and a Zettari captain who has run a field hospital in worse than this, and there is one of you." Her chin came up. "Do not use my patients against me. That is beneath you and you have never done it before and I will remember it if you do it now."

Mu-jin closed his eye.

"That was beneath me," he said. "I withdraw it."

"Accepted."

"I do not want you on that moor."

"No. And I do not want you on it, and one of us is going to be, and I have decided it will be both because that is the only version where somebody is holding your hands when it goes wrong." She let go of the wrist and put her palm flat on the back of his hand instead. "You promised me nothing would happen to you. Say it again."

He looked at her.

"Nothing will happen to me."

Ara did not answer, and Mu-jin heard the shape of it as it went out of his mouth, a thing with no depth in it at all, an old family sentence with nothing standing underneath.

He put his hand on the top of her head, the way he had at Sum-gol, when she was nine and there had been considerably more of the house to go back to.

"You are still my little sister," he said.

"You are still a liar."

"Yes."

She reached into the basin and took up the scissors, and slid two fingers under the linen at his temple to lift it clear of the skin, and began cutting the dressing away from his eye.

# The Witness Schedule

The tide had gone out over the chalk during the night and come back in dirty, and the whole of the Rimward shallows lay white to the depth of a man's arm, a flat milky suspension that took the morning light and gave none of it back. Alabaster in the water. The blow three days ago had torn the shoal and the sea had been carrying the wreckage of it up and down the coast ever since, and by the second flood every net in the parish had come up fouled with a fine chalk paste that stank of nothing and would not shake out.

Verinus stood at the Rimward wall with the wind coming into his collar and a leather satchel braced against his hip, and read.

The wall was old and low and had lime-washed stone under his forearms that came off on the wool. Below him the boom lay across the water in its long dark line and the Zettari hulls sat inside it with morning smoke going up off two of them and a tender working across from the third. Gulls on the mastheads. Fewer than yesterday. He had counted nineteen at dawn and had made himself stop counting them and had failed twice since.

Past all of it, east, the wound in the horizon.

It had moved. That was the thing he had come out at first light to be certain of and had now been certain of for an hour and had not yet decided what to do with. Three fingers wide at arm's length where it had been two, and lower against the water, and the point at which the sea began behaving incorrectly had come in from the eight-mile mark to somewhere near five. The Ebb Court had shifted its routing instruction a second time and the fishing fleet had obeyed it before it was issued.

*The tide is turning faster than the tide turns,* he thought, *and I have been saying that for a month in a poetic register and I would now like to stop, because the channel is nine fathoms and the shoal is in the water and the glass has not come up in thirty-two days, and a thing that was a metaphor in my mouth on the eleventh is a set of measurements in a clerk's hand this morning.*

Two days to the ninth.

He turned the page.

The file on the Mahuo ran to eleven pages and was the most useful thing anyone had put in his hands in a fortnight. Summoner and alchemist both, of a house whose ancestral seat was now a province of somebody else, holding a discipline that the Accord classified as Vocatia and that the Accord's classification did not begin to describe. Verinus had read the technical portion twice and the political portion once and had gone back to the technical portion a third time, because the whole of Vocatia rested on a proposition that his own courts had spent four centuries arriving at from the other direction.

You could not compel a thing to come. You could only be recognised by it. The calling had no force in it at all; it had standing, and the standing had to be genuine, and a summoner who lied about who he was got nothing and deserved nothing.

*They have been practising jurisprudence with their hands for six hundred years,* he thought, *and calling it an art, and my Curia has been practising it with paper and calling it a faith, and Kujo has been filing false entries against the Continuum while both of us congratulated ourselves on our separate vocabularies.*

The file on the one they called Haruki was four lines long and three of them were dates.

He had read it eleven times. He had sent back to the Court for whatever else existed and had been told there was nothing else, which was not a phrase the Alabastrine archive used often and had not used to him in nineteen years. Four lines. Three dates. A name that did not attach to a house the Accord recognised, in a hand that had recorded the other twenty-six passengers to a standard he would have called meticulous.

He put that page at the back, where he could feel it.

Behind him the gate wardens came off the wall.

He heard it in their boots before he understood it, the specific double scrape of men going from rest to attention, and he did not turn, because there were four people in the Holy Sea who produced that sound and three of them were on the wrong side of a seal.

"You are outside the line," Verinus said.

"So are you, by about a stride and a half, and I have already told the warden I did not see it."

Ilse Barrow came along the wall to him with her cloak over one arm and her sleeves shoved to the elbow in a wind that would have had a younger woman fastening everything she owned. Sixty. Square. Grey wool. No ornament. She had come out from the capital on the road rather than the water and it showed at the hem and she had not stopped to do anything about it.

She reached the parapet and stood beside him and did not touch him and looked out at the boom.

"Say it," he said.

"The quarantine is suspended as of the seventh hour this morning. Enforcement's instrument, countersigned at Altherion, entered under the same standing provision as the containment order at the marches." Her voice did not move at all. "It cites the operational requirement of the ninth and the necessity of the Zettari testimony being available to the field commander in person. It is four sentences long and it is procedurally immaculate and I have read it nine times looking for the seam."

Verinus closed the satchel.

"Over your signature."

"Under it. They did not need mine. That is the part I want you to hear before you start being gracious about it." Barrow put both hands on the lime-washed stone. "The provision permits Enforcement to suspend a health seal where the seal obstructs a containment operation already entered. It has existed since the founding and it has been invoked twice in four hundred years and both times it was cholera. I did not know it was there. Neither did you, and do not pretend otherwise, because I watched your face."

"I did not know it was there."

"Good."

*And she rode all night to say that to my face rather than send it,* he thought, *because she has worked out that I would have read a dispatch and thanked her for it and put it in a drawer, and she wanted to be standing here when I understood what it means about how long they have been looking for a way to move me.*

"So they have not merely opened the seal," Verinus said. "They have established that a containment order at the marches outranks a health instrument at the Rimward, and they have established it two days before the ninth, in writing, in a form I will have to argue against on the moor while standing on the wrong side of their own precedent."

"Yes."

"That is well done."

"It is extremely well done and I have been furious since the seventh hour." Barrow's jaw set. "Fourteen days was mine. It was the one number I have won off you in twelve years and it took me the convocation to get it, and a clerk at Altherion took it back with four sentences and did not have to consult anybody. I want that entered in whatever record you keep of my failures."

"I do not keep one."

"You should. It would be shorter than the other."

He let the wind have a moment.

"How many days do I have with him."

"None. That is the rest of it." She said it flatly and did not soften it. "The suspension does not release him to you. It releases him to Enforcement, at the marches, as a witness attached to the field command. The Zettari move inland tomorrow on the moor road under escort and they will be inside the operational perimeter before you are, and whatever you two were doing in that cabin every morning for six days is finished as of this afternoon."

Verinus stood with his hands on the wall for a while.

*Six mornings,* he thought. *I have Form and I have Existence and I have four pages on local sequence in my own hand that I do not fully understand, and I was going to have eight more days of it, and I am going to walk onto that line with a quarter of the vocabulary I needed and I am going to have to do the rest of it out loud in front of the men I am arguing with.*

"Then I have a great deal of reading to do on a moor road," he said. "Which brings me to the other half. Did you get him what he asked for."

"All of it." Barrow's mouth moved slightly, for the first time. "The Ebb Court registers, the whole run, back to the Long Reckoning. The liturgical corpus including the restricted third part, which required me to lie to a subdeacon by omission and I enjoyed it enormously. The Rite's drafting history with your marginalia intact, which you did not authorise and which I sent anyway because a man who wants to know what a law is for should be permitted to see the four versions that were wrong."

"Ilse."

"You told him he could correct our footnotes. I took you at your word, which is what you have spent nineteen years demanding people do, and I would remind you that you have never once enjoyed it." She turned her head. "There is a copy of everything with the Court and a second copy going overland to Altherion tonight under my seal, so that when the Curia discovers what has left the archive they will discover it as an accomplished fact and a filed one."

"You have been busy."

"I have been awake."

The tender crossed below them and the sound of the oars came up flat off the water and arrived late.

"You are thinking about how you will deal with the landscape," Barrow said.

"The landscape is worse every day I look at it and I have looked at it every day for thirty-two." He nodded east without lifting his hands off the stone. "Three fingers this morning. Two on the eleventh. The shoal is in the water and the fleet has stopped asking to be told. Whatever is out there is not sitting where it was put."

"That is not the landscape I meant."

"No. It is not."

He let a breath go and did not get all of it back.

"I have not answered his question. He asked me who decided that the people inside the line no longer hold standing equal to the people outside it, and I gave him the true answer, which is that nobody decided, and it was arrived at by drafting. And then he asked me the harder one and I have had six days and I have not answered it." Verinus turned to face her properly, which cost him across the pelvis and which he did not acknowledge. "If they are dangerous, prove the danger. If they are people, preserve what that requires. If both are true, solve for both. And I cannot solve for both, Ilse, because the only instrument I own that could distinguish an affected person from a frightened one does not reach that far and would kill me at the scale required."

"Explain it."

"The condition is a discontinuity of recognition. Neighbours forget that a person was in the room. So the testimony that matters is not from the affected. It is about them." He put one finger on the wall. "A statement about a person you can no longer properly hold in mind should read hollow. Not shallow. Not false. A hole where the person ought to be sitting in the middle of a statement the speaker entirely believes. I have never seen it and I have never had reason to look for it and I am reasonably certain it is there."

"And?"

"And the marches are past fifty kilometres, so the Sounding does not reach, and outside my Domain I am a deaf old man with a lead line. Which leaves the Reading, by hand, one at a time, at whatever that testimony weighs." His voice stayed level. "I have taken four above seven hundred kilopascals in my life and I was carried out of the room on three of them. There are between eleven and fourteen hundred people inside that boundary. I could take two. On a very good day, with somebody strong standing behind me, three."

Barrow was quiet for a moment.

"Two out of fourteen hundred proves nothing."

"Two out of fourteen hundred proves nothing, and I have spent six mornings looking for a way to make it four, and I have been reaching for the working every single time because the working is the thing I have." He looked at his own hands. "Which is the failure I have been diagnosing in other men since I was thirty-one. A man with a hammer keeps finding nails, and I have a scale, and I have been trying to weigh a problem that is not a weight."

The wind came off the chalk water and went up the wall between them.

"Give me the filing," Barrow said.

"Ilse."

"Give me the filing. The Enforcement one. Not the operational order, the substantive filing with the evidentiary annexes, which I assume you have because I assume you asked for it four times and got it on the fourth."

He got it out of the satchel and handed it across and she took it and opened it standing up in the wind with the pages cracking, and went through it the way she went through everything, which was fast and from the back.

"There," she said, inside a minute.

"Ilse."

"You are looking at the wrong document and you have been looking at it for six days, and I would not have seen it either if I had spent those six days learning Zettari metaphysics from a sovereign, so do not make that face." She turned the file round and held it flat against the parapet with the heel of her hand and the wind trying to take it. "Annexe four. Witness schedule. Enforcement's entire evidentiary basis for the presentation is neighbour testimony, and they have named their witnesses, because a filing that does not name its witnesses is not a filing and the Sealwright who drafted this is far too competent to make that mistake."

Verinus put his hand on the parapet.

"Say the rest of it."

"They have relied on those people. They have put their names in an annexe and entered their accounts as sound and built a containment order on top of them." Barrow's finger came down on the page. "And forty-one of them live inside the boundary."

The gull on the wall two paces down took off and neither of them looked at it.

"Enforcement cannot have it both ways and they cannot have it in their own document," she said. "Either those forty-one are reliable, in which case they are not remainders and the categorical claim fails at its own foundation, or they are unreliable, in which case the evidence for the presentation collapses and there is no lawful ground for the order at all. You do not have to test fourteen hundred people. You have to make them choose which half of annexe four they intend to keep."

Verinus read it. He read the whole schedule, all forty-one, and the parish attributions beside them, and he read it a second time because a single reading was not a finding and he was never again going to say that sentence lightly for as long as he lived.

"That is the ninth," he said.

"That is the ninth."

"And it required no working, no Domain, no Reading, and no part of my body." He let out something that was almost a laugh and had no humour anywhere in it. "Forty-five years. I have been on my knees in ash and on wet decks and on cobbles, and the thing that answers this was in an annexe, in a document I have been carrying in a satchel since the fourth day, filed by the people I am arguing with."

"You would not have found it. You were busy."

"I would not have found it because I was looking for something to weigh." He shook his head once. "Do not give me the comfortable version. I have three witnesses this month who have declined to give me one and I have found I prefer it."

"Then have the uncomfortable version." Barrow closed the file and put it back in his hands. "You have been the instrument for so long that you have stopped being the lawyer, and the office was never meant to be the instrument. The Rite is a document. The Sounding is a Wellspring that would run under this coast if you had never been born. You have spent nineteen years standing between the two of them being irreplaceable, and every one of those years has made the kingdom worse at doing this without you, and I have told you so since the year you turned sixty-five and you have called it my hobby."

"It is your hobby."

"It is my post." She held his eyes. "I have entered the Rite under Attestis in nine independent offices since you sent to me. It went through on the fourth day. Two magistrates have published findings in your absence and both were reported in the parishes and neither mentioned your name, and the Steps carried on exactly as they were, and nobody rioted, and nobody wept, and a fishwife named Sefa Bream has an appointment on the twenty-third which I intend to honour whether or not you are standing."

Verinus took a moment with that.

"Thank you."

"Do not."

"I am going to. You may record an objection."

Boots on the wall behind them, moving considerably faster than a man should move on wet lime.

"Sire." Elber Fend came up with a second satchel under one arm, red to the ears from the road and from having run the last of it. "Sire, Commander says the escort forms at the second hour and the moor road is four days if we take the coast and two and a half if we take the cut, and the cut floods, and she wants a decision from you before she wastes a morning arguing with a quartermaster about it. She also says, and I am quoting, that she is aware you will choose the cut."

"The cut."

"She said you would say that." Fend's shoulders came down about a finger's width. "Deacon."

"Sub-Serjeant."

*They are going to do it,* Fend thought, standing in the wind with a satchel he had not been told the contents of and both hands full of it. *Nobody has said a word about what happens if the men on that line simply decline to have the conversation, and I have been waiting six days for one of them to say it out loud, and neither of them is going to, and I am twenty and it is not my place, and I am going to go and ask the Commander instead.*

Verinus looked out one last time at the wound in the horizon, three fingers wide over a sea gone white with broken chalk, and at the Zettari hulls inside a boom that would be opened before the tide turned twice more.

Then he opened the Enforcement filing to annexe four and set it flat on the parapet under the heel of his hand against the wind, and ran his finger down the witness schedule, one name at a time, until it stopped.

# The Slope

The wind that came across the moor cut had been coming since the second hour and it had nothing in it that Verinus recognised. Cold, yes. Wet, coming off eleven miles of standing peat water and a cut that had flooded twice on the way up. But there was a pressure sitting under it that had no barometric explanation, and he had been walking through it for two days with the glass in the quartermaster's cart giving readings that no longer agreed with the sky, and somewhere in the last hour he had stopped trying to reconcile them.

He came up the slope from the picket line at his own pace, which was slow, and stopped when he could hear her.

The slope had cost him more than it should have. Forty paces of wet peat at a grade a boy would not have noticed, and he had taken it in three stages with a hand out each time for balance he did not find, and the borrowed pelvis had spent the whole of it grinding on a cycle he had stopped counting somewhere around the second stage. The last poppy head was still in his left sleeve where it had been since the garden. He had made himself a rule about it and he had been keeping the rule for six days and he had begun, in the last hour, to resent the rule.

Below and behind him the Enforcement pickets ran along the lip of the cut in a line of grey canvas and cold fires, and the boundary work went out from them east across the moor and lost itself in the light. Somebody down there had a hand drum going for the change of watch. Nobody was hurrying. That was the part he had not been able to get out of his head since they came up the road, that the men who intended to close a line around fourteen hundred people on the ninth had pitched a tidy camp and were changing the watch on time.

He did not announce himself. He stood where the ground gave him a footing and let the last of what she was saying go past him, because a man who interrupts somebody talking to the dead has ended the conversation for good, and because his own instrument had already told him a great deal more than he had any business knowing.

*This is not grief,* he thought. *Grief has a bottom to it and I have taken four hundred and sixty-one readings and every one of them stopped somewhere. This is the other thing. This is a person who has decided the failure was hers, and has entered the finding, and has begun sentencing herself under it, and has been doing so for long enough that the sentence has started sounding like humility.*

He had been thirty-four when his own parents went, both of them inside eleven days, in the second wave of a coastal fever he had spent two years drafting a containment against. The draft had been sound. He had checked it afterward, in the specific way that only a man in that condition checks a thing, and it had been sound, and the parishes had adopted it late, and eleven days later there had been nothing left in the house at Coldwater Steps but a boat and a set of salt scales. He had spent four years afterward constructing an arrangement in which it had been his fault, because a fault could be corrected and the alternative was that the flow of the thing simply ran that way and could not be mended by anybody.

He had never once said that aloud to another person. He had also never once met somebody doing the same arithmetic in front of him.

The blade in the ground was resonating. He could hear it under the wind, a thin sustained note off the steel that had nothing to do with the wind's pressure on the flat of it, and he was old enough and had read enough to leave that entirely alone.

He came the last few paces and stopped a little way off, so she would hear the gravel before she felt anybody near her.

"Dear child," Verinus said.

He put his hand on her shoulder. The left one, gently, the whole palm rather than the fingers, which was how his mother had done it and how he had done it for forty-five years and which had nothing whatsoever to do with any working.

"Do not lose heart. Your battle is only beginning, and I am aware that is a thing old men say and I would ask you to hear the second half before you file it."

The wind pushed once and went round them.

"I do not read minds. I want that established before you start wondering what I have taken from you without asking, because that is the first fear everyone has of this office and almost nobody says it out loud." His voice stayed low and did not carry past the two of them. "What I hold measures weight. When a person speaks, the thing they say has a density and a depth to it, and the depth is what saying it cost them, and I have no capacity to make anyone say anything and no permission to look where I have not been invited."

He let a beat go.

"But you have been talking out loud on an open slope in a wind, and I have been standing in it, and I heard what your account of yourself weighed."

*And it went down and did not stop,* he thought, *and I have felt that twice in my life and neither of those two people were the ones who deserved it.*

"So I will tell you what I actually observed, and you may check it against yourself and discard whatever fails."

He kept his hand where it was.

"Your want to fix this is not an ornament on top of your grief. It is the load-bearing thing. It went down further than the guilt did and it went down considerably further than the doubt, and the doubt is the loudest of the three, which is the ordinary arrangement and is why nearly everyone in your position gets the ranking wrong." He drew a careful breath. "You have decided you are unfit. That decision is the shallowest thing you have said this morning. I do not mean it is dishonest. You believe it entirely. It simply has nothing under it, and everything else you said today does."

The blade's note shifted a fraction and settled again.

"And there is something else, and I am going to put it in front of you plainly because I am seventy-one and I have stopped rationing this." He turned his head slightly toward the cut, where the water lay flat and wrong under a sky that had not been the correct colour in thirty-four days. "Will, held at that depth, bears on probability. That is not a comfort I am extending to you. It is a mechanism, and it is in the founding corpus in three places, and Altherion has spent four centuries burying it under commentary because a Church that admits the flow can be leaned on by an ordinary person has considerably less to sell."

He let his hand come off her shoulder and folded it back into his sleeve.

"You will hear men tell you that the flow of probability cannot be mended. I have believed that at various times and I have believed it hardest during the four years I was constructing a version of my parents' deaths in which the fault was mine." He said it without weight, in the tone he used for channel depths. "Coastal fever. Both of them, eleven days apart, in a draft containment I had written and the parishes had adopted late. I checked the draft afterward. It was sound. That was the worst part and it took me a long time to understand why."

The wind came round again and he waited it out.

"A fault can be corrected. A flow that simply ran that way cannot. So a person of any real conscience will take the fault, every time, because taking it is the only version in which there was ever anything to be done." He looked at her properly. "You have taken the fault for Cozbi. You are carrying a deception practised by a man who has spent his entire existence becoming very good at practising it, and you are carrying it as though you had been the one holding the pen. I would like you to notice how convenient that is for him."

He let that stand.

"Now, on Darius."

He said the name without any of the softening that people had been putting round it for a month, and did not lower his voice.

"He would be proud of you. I am aware of what that sentence is worth from a stranger, so let me put my standing on the table before I ask you to accept it. I have known that man since he was a boy. Before I was elected, before the cap and the Sounding and any of the rest of it, when I was a court Auditor with a bad knee and no prospects, and he was sent to me twice a week to be taught things about Fate that he did not understand and did not want to, and he argued with me about every one of them." The corner of his mouth moved. "He was the worst student I ever had and he was the only one who came back."

He looked out at the water.

"He got hold of one thing very early and never let go of it, which was that a verdict entered by a frightened man is worthless and a verdict entered by a certain one is dangerous. He spent his whole life refusing seats for that reason. Six years he refused the Will of Judger. Six. Every man in Altherion assumed it was humility and it was nothing of the sort. He was waiting to be certain he could hold it without wanting it, and he died still waiting."

He turned back.

"So I will tell you what he would not have said, and you may weigh that too. He would not have said you were ready. He never once told anybody they were ready, including me, and I outranked him for nineteen years." A pause. "He would have said that ready is a word people use to postpone. And then he would have said something extremely stupid, at length, to get you to stop looking at him, because he could not tolerate being thanked and dealt with it by being ridiculous. You have his cadence exactly, and I heard it from thirty paces, and it went through me rather badly and I would be obliged if you did not do it twice in one morning."

He let the wind have that.

"But your burden is not his and I will not let you carry it as his, because that is a mistake I have watched three generations of Altherion's best make and it kills them slower than the alternative."

*And she is going to hear the next part as flattery,* he thought, *and it is the least flattering thing I have ever said to anybody, and I am going to say it anyway, because somebody said the equivalent to me at thirty-four and I have never stopped being glad of it.*

"You hold sacrament. That is not a title and it is not a compliment. It is a description of a mechanism, and it means that when you enter a thing it is entered, and the Continuum has already agreed to that arrangement, and it does not require anybody's confirmation including yours." He kept his eyes on her. "Darius stood at the front of an order and his authority came down through it and the order could have withdrawn it. Yours does not come down through anything. It is upheld in you and it goes out from you and there is nobody above you in the chain who can take it back, and that is precisely why you have spent this entire morning trying to find somebody to hand it to."

He said the last of it quietly.

"The traditions of the Turgia hold because somebody upholds them. Not because they are written. Written things have been struck out by clerks with four sentences and I watched it done to a health seal nine days ago by men with impeccable procedure. A tradition survives inside a living person or it stops, and you are the person, and I am extremely sorry about it and I will not pretend to be sorrier than I am, because the alternative to you holding it is that it stops."

He looked down at the sword standing in the peat, and at the note still coming off it, and did not touch it and did not comment on it.

"You said you hope the Sanctum will do you better than he did. I would ask you to consider a different sentence, and I am not going to argue for it. I am simply going to leave it where you can pick it up or step over it." He drew what breath the wind allowed. "Institutions do not do people better. They do people cheaper. Every one of them, including mine, including the one I have run for nineteen years and would defend with my life. What Darius did for you, he did as a man, at his own cost, out of his own reserve, and no order in four kingdoms has ever been able to reproduce that at scale and none of them have stopped promising they can."

He put his hands into his sleeves.

"There is a thing at the marches in two days that I intend to stand in front of, and I intend to do it with one question and no sword, and there is a considerable chance it does not work. I would rather have you beside me than any three companies Enforcement can field. I am not asking you for that this morning and I am not going to ask you for it while you are standing over a blade in the ground talking to a man you have not finished burying."

He turned to go, and got two paces, and stopped.

"One more, and then I will leave you the slope."

He looked back over his shoulder, and the wind took the edge of the grey vestments and laid them flat against his side.

"He is not necessarily dead. I have no proof and I would be laughed out of a Curia session for saying it and I have said it aloud to exactly two people before you. He was drawn into the Works. The Works are not a grave. Everyone at Altherion has him buried because a vacancy is easier to administer than an absence, and I have declined to accept it for a month, and I am telling you because I would rather one more person knew that somebody was still hoping in the correct direction."

He faced the water again.

"Do not build anything on it. Hope is not proof. It is also not incompetence, provided a person labels it honestly, and I have labelled it."

Then he began the long slow business of getting himself back down the slope toward the picket line, one hand out for balance on ground that had been wet for two days, with the wind pressing at his back and the note off the blade still going behind him, and he did not turn round again.

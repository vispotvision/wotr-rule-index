# Aqua Fortis — Aetherion Academy

*Aetherion Academy. The broken cold room under the south range. Continuous.*

The Black Star pulled.

The bolt did not strike it so much as pour into it. The column of white plasma bent at its foot the way water bends over the lip of a drain, and the whole length of it, from the hole in the vault to the black over Malphas's palm, emptied downward into that single point and did not come out again. The light went in and stayed in. The Star swelled by a finger's breadth and then drew tight again, denser than before, and its hum dropped from half a tone flat to a full tone, low enough to feel in the teeth.

Then the thunder arrived.

It came down the hole all at once, the sound of air that had been heated past the surface of the sun and had nowhere to go but outward, and it hit the vault like a dropped cathedral bell. Grit leapt off every rib. The lamp glass on the far wall cracked in a star. Hail bounced on the flags in a white rattle and the green motes coming down through it scattered like sparks off a forge.

Mu-jin felt the noise a beat after he heard it, a pressure on the chest that arrived when the sound had already gone. The Arrears. His body was running behind him and would run behind him for hours yet.

The tick in his chest changed. It had been the long patient beat of a count still running. Now it skipped once and settled into something slower and different, and above the hole the dark of the storm drew in on itself and turned. Mu-jin did not need to see it to know what it was doing. The bear was facing its summoner again. The strike Raga had owed had been delivered. It had gone into the Star, and it was paid. Raga did not strike a target twice. Whatever happened in the cold room from here, the bear would never touch Malphas again.

Mu-jin read the Star with the lens still off, and it was terrible.

The bolt's structure was gone. The channel, the current, the name Raga had laid on it: all of it had come apart in the black the way sugar comes apart in a vat. What was left was the energy, and the energy was banked. Parked in the black organic mass as stored chemistry, a battery holding a lightning strike, its charge locked into the reduced compounds of the rot. The frost in the lattice of Malphas's left hand had melted. Water ran from the creases. A man that full did not need to be cold.

*Reserve 640,000,000 EU (est.). Down from 850,000,000 at the first word.* The figure arrived in him as the ledger of his own Crystal always did, plain and unasked. *The bear cost 180,000,000. The lens, 17,000,000. The chains and the pillars, the rest.* He had three-quarters of everything still. Malphas, by every assessment he had ever read, had a tenth of that to begin with.

It did not seem to matter to Malphas at all.

He came forward slowly. He stepped down off the swollen black of his own ground onto the flags, over the ashed grey where Mu-jin's pillars had burned the rot away, through the hail. He did not hurry. Mu-jin's Pressure lay across the vault like a hand on every throat in it. Two full Stages separated them, and two Stages should have put sweat on the man's lip and his stomach in his throat. The lamps told the truth of it instead. Every flame in the room still leaned toward Malphas, and they leaned harder the closer he came. He was not resisting the Pressure. He was eating it. It went into him at the edge of his reach and went over, and he walked on it the way a man walks on a floor.

Behind him Velder Crace had brought the revolver back up. He held it two-handed now.

"You are still being limited by the strings of this world." Malphas stopped at fifteen feet. His voice was mild, almost regretful, the voice of a senior reader giving notes on a junior's paper. "Every one of them. You are a very well-versed alchemist, Mu-jin, the best this world has made, and I mean that as precisely as I mean anything. But you cannot win operating within these limitations. Not against me."

He opened his mouth and breathed out.

It came out of him slowly, a long exhalation that did not end when a man's breath should have ended. A haze rolled off his lips and spread, faintly yellow where the grey light from the hole caught it, heavy and low. It did not rise. It poured. It slid down off his chest and spilled across the flags, filling the gaps between the stones and lapping at the cot legs, a tide coming in at the height of a man's shins and climbing. The smell reached Mu-jin a heartbeat later. Rotten eggs, struck sulphur, the vent of a volcano and the bottom of a stagnant pond together.

Mu-jin knew it before the smell had finished arriving.

Hydrogen sulphide. With the lens off he could see it the way he saw everything with the lens off, the small bent molecules tumbling through the air in their billions, a sulphur atom and two hydrogens each. He knew its numbers the way he knew his own name. A hundred parts in a million and the nose stops reporting it, so the stink vanishes at the moment it turns dangerous. Five hundred, and judgment goes. Seven hundred to a thousand, and a man drops in a breath or two. The Arrears made it worse. Whatever the gas did to his lungs, he would feel it late.

But that was not what frightened him.

Around him, every moment of every day since he was fourteen, the air had smelled faintly of struck matches. Iron had rusted where he looked at it. Paper had yellowed on his desk and bread had gone stale by noon. He had stopped noticing it years ago. Now, as the yellow haze reached the edge of his fifteen metres, he noticed it, because it stopped.

The match smell went out like a snuffed wick. The rust that had been blooming along the iron frame of the nearest cot, where his gaze had rested, halted mid-spread, an orange stain with a hard edge.

Sulphur. The oldest poison of a catalyst there was. It went to the active places on a catalyst's surface, the places where reactions caught and ran, and it sat on them and would not leave. Every chemist who had ever run a furnace over a nickel bed or a platinum gauze knew it. Lead, arsenic, sulphur. He had written the list himself, in the margin of his own Codex, as a warning to students.

Malphas watched it land on his face. He smiled, the small dry smile of a man who has set out a proof and watched the reader reach the last line.

"*Lapis*," Malphas said.

The word came out in Mu-jin's own Latin, with Mu-jin's own Sum-gol vowel in the middle of it, and Mu-jin understood two things at once. The first was that Malphas had stood near him before. Years of it. The reading floor of the Division, the long tables, the afternoons when two men who could not stand each other had sat six feet apart and read the same archive. The second was what that meant. Knowledge ran out of Mu-jin the way ink runs through still water. He could not stop it, and it had never once occurred to him to try. Every working he understood had been seeping into the man across the table, a little each afternoon, for years. Malphas had not guessed at Lapis. He had learned it from the source.

*The Book of Summons leaks,* Mu-jin thought, *and so do I.*

He was the stone.

The old alchemists had spent a thousand years looking for the philosophers' stone. They had wanted a thing that turned lead to gold and gave a man forever. What they had been looking for, without knowing it, was a catalyst: something that made a reaction run faster without ever being used up by it. It was the oldest secret in the trade, and it had been sitting in the furnace with them the whole time. Mu-jin was that. He had been that since the morning at fourteen when the glyph chain was wrong and he had seen it. Within fifteen metres of him, anything that would happen anyway could be made to happen now, a million times faster, a billion. He could not make wood from ash or bring back the dead. He could only hurry the world downhill.

And the man in front of him had just smothered the stone in sulphur.

So he cleaned it.

He had the vial out before he had finished the thought. Heavy glass, ground stopper, the liquor inside a deep fuming orange that smoked faintly even sealed, a thread of red-brown vapour curling under the stopper's lip. The old books called it aqua fortis, the strong water. The Guild's catalogue called it red fuming nitric acid, eighty-six parts in a hundred, and kept it in a locked case.

He threw it into the heart of the miasma.

The glass burst on the flags at Malphas's feet, and the yellow haze caught fire without a flame.

Strong nitric acid wants electrons the way a starving man wants bread, and hydrogen sulphide is full of them. The two met in the air and went at each other. The haze boiled. A cloud of choking red-brown smoke billowed up out of it, nitrogen dioxide, the colour of old blood under lamplight, and the heat of the reaction came off it in a wave that made the hail hiss where it lay. Through the brown, something began to fall. It was pale yellow and fine as pollen, and it drifted down onto the flags and the cots and the shoulders of Malphas's coat in a soft snow. The acid was tearing the sulphur out of the gas and dropping it as a solid, and every grain that fell was a grain not sitting on the stone.

Mu-jin reached for the reaction and hurried it.

The match smell came back like a door opening. Lapis woke in a rush at the centre of him, the active places clearing, and he poured it into the fight between the acid and the gas and sped it a thousandfold. *850,000 EU (est.).* A tenth of a percent of everything he held, gone in a breath, and the miasma within his fifteen metres scrubbed itself out of the air in less than a second, the yellow layer at shin height collapsing into brown smoke and falling sulphur and clean, stinging, breathable air.

The acid he had kept back, he shaped.

A third of the aqua fortis had not touched the gas. It lay on the flags in a fuming orange pool, and Coagulatio took it. The liquid rose. It drew itself up into a long tapering spear of acid, orange at its core and smoking red at its edges, and Fixatio sealed it into that shape the instant it formed. That was the price of being the stone, and he never forgot it. A catalyst speeds a reaction both ways at once, so anything he built near himself fell apart faster than anything built by anyone else, unless he pinned it with a seal. The lance held. It hung for one heartbeat at the level of his waist, and then he sent it.

He did not aim at the heart, or at the face. He aimed at the stomach.

He had seen where Malphas lived. With the lens off, from the first moment in the cold room, he had seen the Trait laid into the man's gut and liver and the vessels around them, the place where everything Malphas swallowed went to rot. That was where the bolt was banked. The whole store of it sat there as reduced chemistry, rich in electrons, a cellar full of fuel. And Mu-jin had in his hands the most ferocious electron-thief an alchemist could carry in a pocket. Ferment could digest a spell, a bolt, a slab of stone. It could not digest the thing that digestion feeds. Fuel does not eat the fire.

The lance crossed the fifteen feet in less time than it takes to blink.

Malphas bent.

He bent at the waist, back and to his left, the way any man bends when something comes at his belly, and he was slow. He had never fought, and his body knew it. He moved like a scholar, late and stiff. The lance missed his stomach by a hand's width and took him across the right flank instead, above the hip.

The acid opened his coat, his shirt and the skin beneath in a single smoking line. Where it touched, the flesh did not bleed; strong acid seizes the proteins of the body and cooks them stiff, so the wound sealed itself in a hard yellow-brown crust as it was made, the yellow that nitric acid leaves on everything it burns. The lance broke against him and the rest of the acid went into the wound and through it, down along the line of the flank toward the gut, and there it found the bank.

The wound erupted. A gout of thick brown-black smoke burst out of Malphas's side, rot and acid fumes together, and with it a heat that Mu-jin could see in the air, the shimmer of an oxidiser meeting fuel. For the first time since Mu-jin had come down the stair, Malphas's face changed. His mouth opened. He took a step backward, one hand going to his side, and the hand came away yellow and smoking, and he looked at it with an expression Mu-jin had seen before on the faces of students who had just been shown the error in a working they had been proud of.

Behind him Velder Crace shouted something wordless and swung the revolver past his lord's shoulder onto Mu-jin's chest.

Mu-jin looked at the gun.

He did not think about it. Lapis was awake and clean and the powder in those five chambers wanted to burn. It had wanted to burn since the day it was packed, and it was waiting only for the hammer. He let it stop waiting. *Every powder in the room goes off at once.* He had written that line in a teaching note once, as a caution.

All five chambers fired together.

The cylinder came apart. Only the round under the hammer had a barrel in front of it, and the other four had nothing but the frame, and the frame split along its top strap with a sound like a bell struck with a hammer. Brass and lead and blued steel went out sideways in a fan. Velder's scream came after the bang. He went down onto his knees with his right hand clutched to his chest, and the thumb and first two fingers of it were not where they should have been, and his blood ran down his wrist into his cuff. He folded forward, and his face went down into the low layer of gas where Malphas's breath still lay thick beyond the reach of Mu-jin's clean air. He coughed once and stopped coughing.

"Up," Mu-jin heard himself say, uselessly, to a man trying to kill him. "Get your face up."

The words went out across the vault, plain, carried to the back row as they always did. And every called thing in the room should have turned its head. The motes over the hole slowed in their fall, and far above the storm stirred, because a Mahuo was speaking and every summoned thing heard a Mahuo clearly. The two still on the cots did not turn. The girl in the village smock kept her eyes on her own hands, and the carter kept his on the ceiling. Ansel, held flat in the chains, his hands still steaming, kept pulling at the field without any sign of having heard.

They had no ear. They had been built and not called, seeded and not summoned, and there was nothing in them for a Mahuo's voice to reach. Mu-jin had known it already, from the read. It hit him anyway, late, the way everything was hitting him now.

Malphas straightened.

He did it slowly, one hand still pressed to the smoking crust along his side. The brown smoke from the wound had thinned to a thread. He breathed out, and the breath was yellow, and Mu-jin watched the new miasma spill from his lips onto the floor to rejoin the layer that still lay heavy beyond fifteen metres, around Velder and the cots and the steaming boy.

"Aqua fortis," Malphas said. He sounded, impossibly, pleased. "*Fortis.* Strong water for a strong reason. You found the hole in me in one exchange." He turned his yellow hand over and looked at the burn on his palm. "Oxidiser. Of course. A ferment cannot digest the fire that eats what it makes. I should have written that down."

He lifted his left hand, the lattice hand, and the Black Star came to it.

It settled into his palm like a ripe plum. And then, with the hiss of breath drawn in through teeth, it opened.

What came out of it was lightning, Raga's lightning, given back. But the rot had changed it. It came out black at its core, a crawling, branching black edged with a sick green-violet glow, and it did not strike. It crawled. It came out of the Star in slow forks that spread across the flags toward Mu-jin like the roots of a tree growing at the speed of a running man, and everywhere it touched the stone went soft and dark. It was not burning the stone. It was rotting it.

Mu-jin read it in the instant he had. Still electrical at its heart, a current looking for ground. The floor under it was wet. Hail lay on the flags in white drifts, melting where the brown smoke had warmed them, and the meltwater sat in every joint and hollow of the stone. But pure water is a poor road for a current. The current wanted salt.

The floor had salt. It had lime and gypsum and three hundred years of the dead's dust, ground into the stone of a room built to keep corpses, and every grain of it wanted to dissolve. It would dissolve on its own, slowly, over a season. Dissolution was downhill.

He made it now.

Lapis went into the meltwater around his feet and the minerals in the stone poured into it in an instant, as fast as sugar into hot tea, and the puddles turned to brine. The water clouded and went bitter and conducted. The black lightning reached the edge of the wet and found a better road than Mu-jin. It went down. It plunged into the brine in a hissing flash, ran through the wet seams of the floor in a web of black fire, and grounded into the bedrock beneath the vault. The flags it ran through went black and soft and slumped. The flag Mu-jin stood on slumped with them, and he stepped off it onto dry stone with the hem of his coat smoking.

The heat of the near miss reached his skin two seconds later. He flinched from a thing that had already gone.

He had used the man's own Wellspring against him, Dissolution, the law Malphas had harmonised before Mu-jin could read. It should have felt like something. It felt like the migraine arriving.

It came in from behind his eyes all at once, the weight Sight Through the Glass had set there before Raga was called, finally delivered by the Arrears. It had been on its way for minutes. It struck like a fist closing inside his skull, and his vision whited at the edges, and he kept his feet only because his feet had not been told yet.

*Reserve 621,000,000 EU (est.).* The ledger did not care about the headache.

Malphas had not moved while the black lightning ran. He stood with the emptied Star dull in his palm, watching Mu-jin with an attention that Mu-jin knew from the inside, because it was his own. It was the look of a man reading a working. His lips were moving.

"*Ratio... solvitur...*" Malphas murmured. He stopped. He looked down at his own mouth as though it belonged to someone else. "That is not mine." A breath. "That is yours. I have been hearing your Latin in my head since you came down the stair."

Fifteen feet. Close, and minutes long, and getting longer. Everything Mu-jin knew was seeping across the gap between them the way it had seeped across the reading-floor tables for years, and every second of this fight was another lesson Malphas had not paid for.

Malphas lowered his hand, and the girl and the carter rose from their cots.

They stood together, smoothly, in the same breath, and the cold threads from the lattice ran taut to their sternums. They walked to his sides and stopped. The frost came back into the lattice as they took his weight, the white returning to the creases, and the water on his fingertips froze in beads.

And the floor was wrong.

Mu-jin read it through the migraine. The yellow layer beyond his fifteen metres had not only been refilled. It had been fed, by Malphas's breath and by the Black Ground still swelling at the far end of the vault and breathing its own gas. It lay at knee height around the cots and the chained boy and the man face-down on the flags, heavier than the air above it, pooling in the old coffin niches. And hydrogen sulphide burns. Four parts in a hundred of it in air, and a spark will light the whole volume at once. In the niches and along the low walls it was past four. Mu-jin could see the fraction climbing.

Over the hole the storm had drawn in, dark and close, and the charge in it was building. The tick in his chest kept its new slow time. Raga waited to strike whatever Mu-jin named. Anything at all, except the one man in the room he most needed struck.

There was one other road. Mu-jin saw it in the same read, the way he saw the failure point in a glyph chain. Pervium. He could send a working through Malphas's Shell without breaking it, the way a particle sometimes passes a wall it has no right to climb. A Calcination tunnelled straight through the Voidic skin to the lattice on that left hand. Malphas's Ward was thick, two layers by any reading. *85,000,000 EU (est.), and arriving at half strength.* Half of an honest fire on the place where every thread met. It would cost him a migraine on top of this migraine, and hours of shaking afterwards. And it would mean letting Malphas stand within fifteen feet of him long enough to aim it.

Malphas saw him find it. Or he saw him find something.

"Your bear has paid what it owed me," Malphas said quietly, and the girl and the young man at his sides did not breathe. "It will not touch me again. You know its law better than I do." He tilted his head toward the storm over the hole, and then toward the yellow layer pooling round the cots, and the boy, and the man face-down in it. "So name someone else, Teacher. Name the carter, or the girl, or my student on the floor. Name the boy." His voice did not change. "And let us see what your lightning does in a room full of my breath."

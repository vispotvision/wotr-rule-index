# Two Collapses Leaning On Each Other

The ash came up over the welt of both boots and shut, and it shut the way wet sand shuts around a driven stake, from the bottom of the hole upward, so that the last thing Darius felt was the leather going tight across the instep. There was no pain in it. There was a smell, faint and immediate, of hot iron off a whetstone, and under his ribs a low pressure arrived that had no direction and did not go away, and above him and to the left the chisel stopped.

Kizami's head came around on a neck with too many joints in it.

"He has stood in it," the Notcher said.

"He has."

"Deliberately."

"Aye."

"I will need a second band." The chisel-jaw worked once on nothing, which is what it did when it was aggrieved. "Voluntary adhesion files under a different hook. The hook is at the top of the column and I have to go up. Nobody thinks about the going up."

*Sixty-one arrivals and eleven gates opened and nine walked out, and this one puts his own feet in the ground and expects me to have a hook ready for it.* Kizami climbed. *I do have a hook ready for it. That is not the point.*

Gimbzo did not answer it. He had turned his shoulders square to Darius, which he had not done at any point in the last hour, and standing at that mass with the shoulders square he stopped resembling a person who happened to be enormous and started resembling the thing the gate was hung on.

"Right," he said. "Then we'll start, and I'll say this part once because I've said it four times before and I've never yet got through it without a man arguing. You're going to be bad at this. Not for a week. For a while. You're sixty-odd years old and you've been the cleverest man in every room you've walked into since you were nineteen, and the first thing this takes off you is that." He pulled Tsuiji up out of the ash by the linen and swung the head down flat between them, and the impact went through the ground and into Darius's shins. "Lift it."

Darius looked at the axe.

The haft was as long as his arm and a half. The head was a bearded pattern, single-bitted, forge-welded steel at the edge with a wrought-iron body, and by eye it was perhaps four kilograms of head on maybe a kilogram of ash haft, which is heavy for an axe and unremarkable for a man who had carried mail and a longsword through two campaigns before his hair went white.

"With respect," he said, "I am not certain what this demonstrates."

"HOO. Neither was the last one."

Darius bent, took the haft in both hands at the correct spacing, set his feet, kept his back straight, and pulled.

Nothing happened.

He adjusted, went lower, got his hips under it and drove up through the legs the way a man lifts a thing he intends to keep his spine after lifting, and the linen creaked under his grip and the haft did not move a hair. He had the specific sensation of pulling against a wall that had been painted to resemble an axe. There was no give at all. Not the resistance of great mass, which yields fractionally and tells you it is yielding. It simply did not participate.

He let go and straightened and his lower back informed him, in the language it had been using since he was fifty, that it had opinions.

"It is anchored," he said.

"Aye."

"To what."

"To here." Gimbzo tapped the ash with one heel. "Same as me. Tsuiji has a coordinate and so does its brother and so do I, and the three of us have had the same one since the salt flat. I do nothing to the axe. The coordinate is what the axe is made of." He folded his arms. "Go on. Again."

Darius went again.

He went again eleven times, and he was aware of the count, and he was aware that being aware of the count was itself a failure of a kind he had not examined in forty years. The eleventh attempt he came off the haft with his hands shaking and his shirt stuck to the small of his back, and he stood breathing through his mouth in a country with no wind, and the humiliation of it was so total and so simple that it arrived almost as relief.

*Eleven.* Gimbzo watched without moving. *The last one gave up at six and the one before her went to thirty and neither of them was any use. Eleven is a working number.*

"Right," Gimbzo said. "Now tell me what you did wrong."

"I applied force against an anchored coordinate. The force had nowhere to go."

"That's what happened. That's not what you did wrong."

Darius wiped his palms on his coat. "Then instruct me."

"You tried to beat it." Gimbzo crouched, which took him a while and which put his eyes almost level with Darius's own, and there was something obscene about a shape that size folding down that far. "Everything you've ever done, you've done by being heavier than the problem. More reading, more procedure, more standing, more years. You have never in your life met a thing you couldn't outweigh, and now you have, and your whole instinct is to go and get more weight."

"There is no more weight to get."

"No. So stop pulling and start listening, because I'm going to tell you what Balance actually is and every one of your order has got it wrong for eleven hundred years and they've got it wrong in a way that's almost funny." He set one forearm across his knee. "What holds up an arch."

Darius stopped.

"An arch."

"A bridge, a vault, a gate, a cellar roof. Any of them. What holds it up."

"The keystone."

"That's what they teach. It's half a thing." Gimbzo held up two fingers and brought them together at the tips, tilted, propped against each other. "An arch is two collapses leaning on each other. Two halves both trying to fall, and neither can, because the other one's in the road. That's all it is. There's no strength anywhere in it. Every stone in a vault is a stone falling, and the whole reason a cathedral stands for nine hundred years is that the falling has been given somewhere to go." He moved his hands apart and down and out. "The load doesn't get resisted. It gets sent. Down the curve, out through the haunches, into the ground at the feet, and every stone in the run takes a share and passes the rest on. The keystone isn't the strong one. The keystone is the one that lets the two collapses argue through it without either of them winning."

The pressure under Darius's ribs shifted. He noticed it and did not know what to make of it.

"That's the labor," Gimbzo said. "That's Balance, and your lot named an order after the Balancer and then spent eleven hundred years thinking it meant *fairness*, which is a word for a feeling. It doesn't mean fairness. It doesn't mean mercy and it doesn't mean the middle. It means giving a load a path so a thing can be under strain and go on existing. Two forces that would tear each other apart, and a third thing in between that lets them both push through it and stands." He looked at Darius flatly. "You've been doing the other one."

"Which."

"Standing between them with your hands out and stopping both."

Darius did not move.

"That's not Balance, and I want you to hear how close it is, because the closeness is what ruins men." Gimbzo's voice went slower. "A man who steps between two things and holds them apart has taken the whole of both loads into himself and given them nowhere to go. He'll do it for years. He'll do it well. He'll get very strong at it and everyone around him will call it wisdom, and the entire time neither force is resolving and the whole of it is accruing in his body, and one day his arms come down." He straightened out of the crouch with a sound like a rope taking weight. "That's a man holding. It costs everything and it settles nothing, and the world does not get any lighter while he's doing it. Your house has one of those in it right now and if you don't know which one I'd be surprised."

"That is not the same as what I have done."

"No. Yours is worse, because you did it once, correctly, and it cost four hundred men, and you spent thirty-five years making certain nobody could ever route a load through a person again." Gimbzo picked the second axe up off the ash and held one in each hand, and did not appear to be holding either of them. "You built abutments and no arch. Eleven firewalls, four grades, and not one stone in the middle that takes both sides. That's a gate with nothing over it. That's two walls and a hole."

Darius put his hand on the haft again.

He did not attempt to lift it. He stood over it with his palm flat on the wound linen and his weight on his sunk boots, and he did the thing that thirty-five years of somatic drill had made faster in him than thought, which was to reach for the arrest.

He found the interval. He had held one open at the Bastion gate for nine minutes and one in his own Crystal for a length of time he had not been able to measure, and he went into it now the way a man puts his hand on a rail in a dark stairwell, and he took the axe's coordinate and he tried to hold it still while he lifted the object out from under it.

The wood went silent.

Not quieter. The tock stopped, and the ash stopped moving under his boots, and something enormous a long way down turned over and paid attention, and inside Darius's chest a fracture line that had been four for six years came up to the surface and rang like a struck rim.

He heard it. That is the wrong verb and it is the only one available. He heard the fifth one begin.

Gimbzo hit him.

It was not a strike. The flat of Tsuiei came across Darius's sternum with about as much force as a shove from a large man, which at that mass was a decision made with great precision, and it took him off his feet in the only direction the adhesion allowed, which was backward at the hips with his boots still in the ground. His spine bent. His grip tore off the haft. The interval collapsed and the entire held moment slammed shut, and the axe he had been trying to lift dropped one millimetre onto the ash and made a sound like a cathedral door.

Darius hung there from his own ankles with his ears roaring.

"Out." Gimbzo's voice had gone somewhere it had not been. "You bring that into my ground again and I'll put you in the root myself and save Kizami the climb."

"He would be in the root pile," Kizami said, from very high up, and its voice had lost every bit of its clerkly flatness. "There is no room in the root pile. I have said this four times."

"I know what you've said."

Darius got his hands under him and pushed himself upright off the ash and stayed bent at the waist, breathing, with both fists on his knees. There was blood in his mouth from where his teeth had come together. The fracture had gone back down. It had not closed, and he could feel it there under the fourth like a coin under a mattress, and he understood with perfect clarity that he had been one full second from ending.

"I was holding it," he said.

"You were."

"It was working."

"It was." Gimbzo let the axe head rest. "That's the part I need you to sit with, Weighing Man, because it's the reason I hit you and not the reason you think. It was working. It'd have gone on working. You'd have lifted the axe, and you'd have been proud of it, and you'd have learned a way to do this that costs a fracture every time and gets you a result every time, and you'd have got four more results and then you'd have been a hole in the ground with a column over it." He set the axe down flat. "Every man who's died in this wood died doing something that worked."

Darius spat into the ash. It steamed.

"Then teach me the other thing."

"I've taught it. You weren't listening, you were waiting for your turn to be clever." Gimbzo pointed at the haft with two fingers. "Don't take its coordinate. Don't stop it. Don't hold it and don't fight it. Put your hands on the wood and find where its weight is trying to go, and then give it somewhere better, which is through you and down your legs and into the ground you're standing in, which the ground already owns you so it costs you nothing to let it have the rest." He tapped his own chest with a thumb the width of a hammer face. "You're the stone in the middle. You don't win. You conduct."

Darius wiped his mouth on the back of his wrist and looked down at the axe.

The pressure under his ribs was still there. He understood, now, that it had been there since the ash closed over his boots, and that he had been treating it as a symptom, and that it had not been a symptom at any point. The ground had a load in it. The gate had a load in it. The trunk three hundred feet up had a load in it that went out along the branches until the branches stopped being branches, and every one of those loads was going somewhere, and the man who had been sitting in the gate for longer than chronology was the place two of them met and argued and did not win.

He bent. He put both hands on the linen. He did not pull.

He stopped trying to be heavier than it and let his knees come out over his toes and his hips drop and his shoulders come down over his hands, and he found, with the specific reluctant clarity of a man who has been correct about the wrong thing for thirty-five years, the direction the axe's weight wanted to travel, which was not down. It was sideways and slightly back, toward the ash where a dead man's coordinate had been sitting since the salt flat.

Darius put his own spine in the path of it and opened his legs to the ground and let it through.

The haft came up off the ash a finger's width and stopped, and his arms screamed, and his boots sank another knuckle, and he held it there while Gimbzo counted out loud to three in a language nobody living had a name for, and then set it down and knelt in the ash and put his forehead against the cold wound linen and stayed there.

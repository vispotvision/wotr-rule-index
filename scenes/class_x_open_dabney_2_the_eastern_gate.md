# Class X Open, Dabney 2: The Eastern Gate

*Aetherion Academy. The Crucible Field, the eastern tunnel, the opening match of the Class X Open Tournament.*

The eastern tunnel is longer than it looks from the benches. Dabney walks it with his boots laced and Aequitas across his back, and the walls on either side are dressed stone gone glassy where the wards run under them, so that his own shape keeps pace with him on the left and right, dim and stretched, a boy walking beside a boy walking. The air in here is cooler than the chamber. It smells of wet stone and old chalk and the hot-copper tang that conduit gives off when it has been drawing hard all morning. Halfway along, a gauge-housing is set into the left wall behind a brass grille, its needle trembling just short of the red, with a Guild seal of grey wax on the casing and a warden's chalk tally beside it: four strokes and a bar, then two more. Somebody has counted something here six times this morning. He does not know what, and he notes that he does not know, the way Edward taught him to note a gap in a column. Ahead, the mouth of the tunnel is a white square so bright it has no depth, and through it comes the noise of the stadium, flattened by the stone into one long sound like surf heard from a bedroom.

Then the noise has his name in it.

*Dabney Lambert.* The Scriptia carry it into every tier at the same weight, the far rows and the near rows equal, the way water finds its level. *The Paragon of Judgement.*

His stride does not break. He has trained seven years so that his stride will not break, and it holds now, heel and ball, heel and ball, while everything above his collar goes cold and very clear. He had planned to be nobody. He has spent seven days arranging himself into a talented thirteen-year-old with good instruction behind him, and somebody has just written the other conversation over the eastern gate in letters taller than a man.

He comes out of the tunnel into the light, and he looks up, because everyone looks up.

His name hangs above the gate in white fire. Under it, smaller, a pair of scales: two pans on a beam, perfectly level, drawn in the same light. He looks at the scales for longer than he means to. *Become the scale.* Somebody in the Academy's offices chose that mark for him, from a list of marks, on an ordinary afternoon, and has no idea what it means to the boy standing under it.

The Crucible Field opens around him. Up close it is bigger than it was from the benches, the floor a brilliant white under the lamps, so even that it looks poured, and the spectator tiers gone to a dark ring above it where faces show only as pale flecks, the way stones show in a riverbed at dusk. The silver-red boundary line runs around the whole floor at the height of his ankle, steady, faintly warm to look at. Under his boots the stone hums. It is the same push he felt through his bare heels in the chamber, a bell in the next street, only nearer now, and when he steps over the threshold the recognition line beneath him flashes once and goes quiet.

Once. He made sure of that before he left the tunnel. He found the place in himself where the plates are bolted on and he leaned on them, the way a man leans his back on a door he does not want opened, and the line lit for him exactly as long as it would light for anyone.

Holding it has a price, and he pays it where he always pays it. It sits in the hinge of his jaw first, a dull clench he has to keep letting go of, and then across the tops of his shoulders, as though somebody had laid a wet coat on him and told him not to shrug it off. Behind his eyes there is a faint, steady pressure that never quite becomes an ache. He meters his breath against it the way Darius taught him at the basin: in, only what he needs; out, only what he can spare; nothing given back all at once because holding it is uncomfortable. It is uncomfortable. It will be uncomfortable for as long as the match runs, and he has no way of knowing yet how long that will be.

*Fifty percent.*

He keeps walking toward the eastern mark, and the stadium noise comes back down over him in a different shape.

He has heard the stands talk all week. He hears them now. It comes in pieces, the near tiers mostly, the lower years who have been let in to watch Class X the way people are let in to watch a fire: *that's him*, and *that's the Judgement one*, and *he's tiny*, and *my cousin says*, and from somewhere low on the left, a girl's voice gone high with scorn, *that's not a Paragon, that's a first-year.* He hears the word the way they say it, too. In the mouths of the lower years Paragon is a word out of a chapel window, the kind of thing painted in gold leaf behind an altar, with a sword in one hand and a sun in the other. They do not say it the way they say a classmate's name. They say it the way the fishwives on the Palatinate quay said the Palatine's title, lowering their voices on it without noticing they did. Most of them had no idea who he was until a minute ago. Many of them had heard the title anyway. It has been going round the refectory since the second day of term, passed from table to table with the bread, and he has heard it at his own back more than once without anyone saying it to his face. Nobody says anything to his face. He has sat at the end of the long table at every meal since he came, and the bench beside him has stayed empty, and he has not known how to fill it. On the second morning he stood up with his tray, meaning to carry it down the table to where a knot of his classmates were arguing about something with their mouths full, and he had a sentence ready about the porridge, which was bad, and seemed a safe thing to agree on. He got four steps. Then somebody at the knot laughed at something that had nothing to do with him, and the sentence went out of his head like a lamp in a draught, and he turned the four steps into a walk to the water jug and sat back down where he had been.

The opponent's mark is twenty paces across the white. Taesyn Valorin stands at it.

Dabney has never spoken to him. He has not really spoken to any of Class X. He knows their faces and the way each of them sits, which is a different thing. He knows that the boy across the floor is the Grand Mage's son, because the voice in the air said so, and that is very nearly the whole of what he knows. He wants to talk to them. He likes talking. He likes people, in the abstract, the way you like a country you have read about. He asked Edward once whether there were children in Kharven, and he asked Darius whether the sea was the same sea everywhere or only being called different things, and he has been asking questions all his life of men three and four and five times his age, because those were the people in the room. The Academy is the first room he has ever been in where the other people are his size. He has not yet worked out what to say to them first.

*Maybe this,* he thinks. *Maybe this is where we start.*

It is a strange thought to have at the edge of a fighting floor. He turns it over and finds that he believes it.

Darius said a thing once, at supper, over a bowl of the thin Palatinate fish stew he pretended to like for the Order's sake. Dabney had asked how you were supposed to know what a person was really like, since everyone said things they did not mean and meant things they did not say, and Darius had set his spoon down very precisely on the rim of the bowl before he answered.

"You keep your heart behind your teeth all your life, the same as everyone. You have to. A corridor has no room for it." The old man had looked at him across the candle with those pale, patient eyes. "On a field there is no room to keep it anywhere else. A man's steel goes where his heart goes, and his feet follow it, and he cannot spare the attention to hide either one. That is where the heart of a true man lies, Dabney. On the field. If you want to know a man, stand across from him and see what he does with his hands."

He had been eight. He had written it down that night, in the back of his forms book, in letters too big for the line.

The floor smells of the lamps over it, hot glass and scorched dust, and under that the clean struck-flint smell of wards drawing hard. From the tiers come the smells of a crowd: wet wool, pipe smoke somebody is not supposed to be lighting, and the sugared almonds a vendor has been crying at the top of the east stair since the doors opened, a copper a twist, which Dabney knows because he paid it on the first day and has been sorry since that he did not buy two. He reaches the eastern mark and stops on it. The recognition glyph under his boots is warm through the soles. He sets his feet the way he sets them every morning, without looking at them, and he looks at the boy across the floor instead.

He does not weigh him.

It would be easy, and it has always been easy. He could open his Judgement the width of a hair and know before the referee's hand came down what Taesyn Valorin weighed, the whole heavy honest sum of him, the way he knows what a loaf weighs by looking at it. He keeps it shut. A boy who can only weigh will be cut in half by a man who can only swing, and besides that, and more than that, he does not want to meet his first classmate as a figure. He wants to meet him on the field, where Darius said the heart comes out. He wants to see what Taesyn does with his hands.

So he looks at him the ordinary way, the way anybody in the tiers is looking at him. A boy his own age, or near it. White hair. A stance.

Dabney lets his right hand come up and slide back along the grip of Aequitas where it rides over his shoulder, until his fingers find the pommel. He taps it once with two fingers. It is a habit from the yard, from before he was tall enough to wear the sword on his back without the point dragging, and it means nothing, and it settles him. The grain under his fingers lies flat. The whetstone's note, the one he could hear in his own blood in the chamber, has gone down a little. Not all the way.

He clears his throat, which is a mistake, because the Scriptia take that and give it to every tier at the same polite volume as the announcer's voice. Somewhere above him somebody laughs.

His ears go hot. He keeps his chin where it is.

"We haven't spoken," he says across the twenty paces, and the air carries that too, to every face in the dark ring, so that his first sentence to anyone in Class X is heard by the whole of Aetherion. He decides, in the space of a breath, that he does not mind. It is true, and the people in the tiers may as well hear something true about him today. "I'm Dabney." A small pause, while he finds the rest of it. "I hope we have a good fight."

He means it. He can feel himself meaning it, under the breastbone, where the plan to fight at half has been sitting all morning like a swallowed stone. The two things sit there side by side now and do not argue.

*Fifty percent,* he tells himself again, and the plates hold, and the line under his boots stays quiet. *Show him the forms. Show him the edge. Keep the rest behind your teeth.*

He has the list by heart, because he made it in the dormitory three nights running with Astro asleep across his shins. The forms Darius drilled into him in the yard, all of them, at the speed a good boy with good teachers would have them. The footwork. The edge, and the flat, and the cross, and the pommel, which a great many people forget is part of a sword. His body, which is his own and has been worked every morning for seven years. The Judgement stays shut, the strands stay where they hang, and nothing he does today will make a leaf stop on the water. If the match needs more than that, the match will be lost. He has turned that over too, and decided he can bear it. He is less sure, now that he has spoken to the boy across the floor, that he will want to.

He takes his hand off the pommel and lets it hang.

Above the eastern gate his name burns on in white over the level scales, and the dark ring of faces goes on talking about it. Many of the people in the stands still have no idea who Dabney Lambert is. All of them have heard the title. He would rather they found out the first thing than the second. He would like, very much, for the boy across the floor to be the one who finds it out first.

At the edge of the white floor the referee waits at the silver-red line in Academy grey, one hand at his side, the other not yet raised.

Dabney looks across the arena at Taesyn Valorin, sets his weight a finger's breadth forward onto the balls of his feet, and waits for the hand to come up.

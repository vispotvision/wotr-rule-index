# Class X Open, Dabney 6: The Flame of Judica

*Aetherion Academy. The Crucible Field, the opening match of the Class X Open Tournament. The fourth exchange.*

The three failures come up around him like weeds through a cracked floor.

He sees them on the graph before they are anything at all. With the gold still on, the field in front of him is a scatter of points finding their lines, and in the middle of the scatter three things are trying to happen that should not be able to. One is a line with no start: a road that has already arrived, and so has no need to travel. It is gathering out of the grey-gold Aether still hanging in the air around him, his own mist from the Letting, drawn up tight into a narrow knot level with his breastbone. One is a weight with nothing under it. A load borne by nothing presses down into the air beneath his rear heel, into the star of cracked stone he made a moment ago. One is a stillness with no subject, an ending that has already ended nothing. Beside his sword arm a handspan of air has begun to stop moving all at once, the way a held breath stops.

None of them has a line yet. That is what they look like on the graph: three places where the scatter refuses to settle, where every point disagrees with every other and the line of best fit runs off the edge of the slate. They are still finding out what they are. When they find it, they will ignite. He has, he judges, about as long as it takes a dropped coin to stop ringing.

His Assize opens on them without being asked, the way his hand would go to a burr on a blade, and for the first time in his life it has nothing useful to say. Every one of the three is nothing but flaw. A road with no destination, a foundation with no load, an end with no subject. He could put any of them on the scale and break it down item by item and find, at the bottom of the column, that the flaw is the whole of the working, and the working is the flaw, and there is nothing underneath to judge it against.

He understands, then, what the other boy has done, and he very nearly laughs.

"It's strange," Dabney says. The words come out of him short and shaken; the breath behind them stops at the hot bar under his ribs and he has to take the next one in small. The Scriptia carry it all the same, every catch in it, to every tier. His eyes are still gold. He can feel the ring turning in them, and he can feel it slow, the gold settling deeper rather than fading, the way silt settles in a jar of river water and leaves it clearer. "That you noticed I was fighting at half." A breath. "I thought I'd hidden it rather well."

The three failures tighten. He watches them do it.

"So you build spells that profit from their own weaknesses." He means it as praise, and he can hear that it sounds like praise, which surprises him. "The casuists in my master's Order have a name for that. They'd call it a double effect: one act with two results, the harm and the help in the same stroke, and the act takes its pleasure in both." He shifts his grip on Aequitas, carefully, because his right side does not like the shift. "It's a very rude thing to do to someone who spends his whole life looking for what's wrong with things. I can't find anything wrong with them. Everything's wrong with them, which is the point, isn't it."

He finds he is smiling.

"No hard feelings," he says. "My master told me never to show anyone my true power." The ring in his eyes turns once. "Not that I'm not itching to show you all of it."

---

That last part is true, and it frightens him a little how true it is.

His blood is going. It has been going since the box, since the gold, since the strings came down off him and left him standing on a cracked star with his side on fire and a stadium laughing at the word *ow*. It goes the way blood goes after a sprint, high and fast in the throat and the wrists, but there is something in it that is not his. Or that is his and has never been allowed out. It rises up through him from somewhere under the plates, under the Essence, under the place the Greater Hand reached up from, and it is glad. That is the only word. It is glad the way a dog is glad when the door opens. It loves this. It loves the ringing chain and the boy who walked his law and the sheet of light pressed flat against the steel and the bruise along his ribs. It loves the rush. It wants more.

He knows what it is, or knows its shape. He has read the Moto pages in Darius's books the way he read everything in that workroom, too young and too fast, and he knows the word they use for the thing that sits under every trained self like the floor under a room: the True Nature. He knows that when a Moto is pressed long enough and hard enough against the person their blood built, the floor can come up through the room. The pages call that Asami, the Revealing. He has felt the edge of it twice, alone: once on the night after the brick, sitting on the dark yard stones with his hands still smelling of brick dust, when something under him had risen so close to the boards that the gulls on the chapter house ridge woke and went up all at once and did not come down till morning; and once last winter, for no reason he could name. Darius has a private word for the edge, which Dabney has never heard him say aloud to anyone else: Semi-Asami. The floor seen through a crack in the boards, on its way up.

He is not Moto. Nobody has ever told him otherwise, and he has never had a reason to wonder. But the pages say the floor is not a Moto possession, that anyone who carries a Work long enough and deep enough has one, and he can feel his own pressing up under him now, glad and huge and hungry for the next exchange.

The pages also say what happens to a practitioner who opens that floor and mistakes what comes up for permission. A practitioner who lets it out instead of living in it, who looks inward at everything he has been denied instead of outward at what is actually there. They did not give that a proper name. They called it the Shallow-Seen Unburied, and they said they would not name it further because it was too dangerous to name, and Dabney at twelve had read that sentence four times and then closed the book and gone outside to sit in the sun.

He does not know how much trouble he would be in if he let it up now. Trouble with the plates, which would not hold it. Trouble with the old man in the stands, who would know in a heartbeat what he was looking at. Trouble with whatever the gladness is, and what it would want next, and whether he would be able to tell it no.

So he tells it no now, while he still can. He puts his hand flat on the floor of himself, the way you put a hand flat on a pot lid that is starting to rattle, and leans.

And there is the other reason, which is plainer. He is running low. He knows it the way you know a purse is light by the feel of it at your belt. The Letting took a tenth of what he carries and threw it out into the room as mist. The box took about as much again. The Greater Hand took what it took and is still collecting the debt in his calves and his back. And now the stuff he let out is being used against him. He can see on the graph that the first of the three failures, the road with no start, is building itself out of the grey-gold Aether still hanging round his own shoulders, the mist he made. He put a great deal of himself into the air, and the air does not belong to him any more, and a clever enough boy can pick it up off the floor and throw it.

Darius would say that serves him right. Darius would be correct.

He needs to stop spilling. He needs to stop being weak in the one place he is weak.

"Aether and Essence," Dabney says, and his voice steadies on it, because this is a thing he knows the way he knows his letters, "are two sides of the same ledger."

He has watched Edward keep the household books since before he could read them: two columns on every page. Every coin is entered twice, once where it came from and once where it went, so that the two columns always sum to the same figure and any error shows itself at the foot of the page. Every grain of Essence he lets out is a debit on his side and a credit on the world's; the world's Aether drawn in and made his is a credit on his side and a debit on the world's. The book never loses a coin. It only moves them.

"If you keep the book balanced," he says, "you can keep your viaforma whole across all of the planes at once. Body, Spirit, Attraction, Fate. You don't have to choose one to live in." He breathes in past the bar. "Unlike you, I don't do well on the physical plane. It's the only place I'm bad at. My only limitation." His mouth twists. "So I have to do certain things to stretch it."

---

Every soul leans. That is what viaforma means, in the plain teaching: the way a soul's Aether settles over a lifetime of Temperance into an allegiance to one of the four Paths, the way a tree leans after forty years of the same wind. Body men live in their muscle and bone. Spirit men live behind their eyes. Attraction women live in the threads that run between one person and the next, and feel every pull on them. Dabney lives in Fate. He has always lived there. The strands on the leaf, the possibilities on the graph, the threads at the root of his spine, the Works themselves: that is his home ground, the place his Essence runs easy and full.

The Material plane is the one he visits. He can fight in it. He has fought in it every morning for seven years, with a sword that is real steel and feet on real stone. But his Essence goes into it the way a strong current goes down a mismatched wire, and a great deal of it comes back. The Concord telegraph engineers know the problem better than anyone: run a signal down a line that does not match the instrument at the end, and most of it bounces back at the joint and is lost as heat and echo, and the operator at the far end gets a whisper. Match the line to the instrument and nearly all of it goes through. There is a trick they use for it, a short length of line laid at the joint, cut to exactly the right measure, so that the reflection off its near end and the reflection off its far end cancel one another and the signal crosses clean. Opticians do the same thing with a film on a lens, so thin it is a quarter of the light's own wave, and the glare goes out of the glass.

Darius taught him the soul's version at twelve, and called it, as he called everything, by an old-tongue word that meant something ordinary. **The Gilding.** *Inauratio.* A gilder lays a leaf of gold over iron or wood so thin that light shows through it, and the thing underneath takes on the gold's face without becoming gold. Dabney lays a leaf of his own Fate-allegiant Essence over his Material body: a matching layer, cut to the measure of the joint between the plane he lives in and the plane he is standing in, so that his Essence stops bouncing off the join and goes through clean.

He does it now.

It starts in his hands. Light comes up in the skin of his palms the way warmth comes up into a stone that has been lying in the sun, and then it is gold: a vibrant, living gold, the colour of a harvest field with the evening on it, rising up his wrists and his forearms under his sleeves and across his chest and up his throat to his face, until the whole of him stands on the white floor of the Crucible glowing like a figure cast in new bronze with a lamp inside it. It throws no light past arm's length; it sits on him. It sits on the steel too, running up Aequitas from the cross to the point in a thin even sheen, because the steel is his thread and the Gilding does not know where his hand ends and the blade begins.

Up in the dark ring the noise of the tiers changes. It drops, the way a crowd drops when a horse goes down in the street, a thousand people taking the same breath, and through it he hears one clear young voice say *oh* in exactly the tone he said it himself, at four, the first time he saw a ship come into the Palatinate harbour under full sail.

And it stops the spilling. He feels the leak close the way you feel a draught stop when somebody shuts a door in the next room. The Essence that was bleeding off him into the air as Aether has nowhere to bleed. The book balances.

---

High in the dark ring, on the third tier above the eastern gate, where the Academy seats the guests who have asked not to be announced, an old man leans forward on his stick.

He has white hair cropped short as a novice's and one shoulder carried lower than the other, and the lamp-light round him is a shade whiter than the light round anyone else on the bench, the way it has been round him for as long as anyone in the Order can remember. He has been sitting very still since the gold came into the boy's eyes. He has not been still since the gold came onto the boy's skin.

"He isn't using that form?" Darius says, to nobody, in a voice so quiet the Scriptia do not trouble to carry it.

*He felt it come up and put his hand on it. At thirteen.* The old man's grip on the stick eases a fraction. *Good. Good, and I will want to know what it cost him.*

---

Dabney breathes out, and his breath is gold.

It comes out of him in a slow plume in the cooling air of the Crucible, gold mist curling off his lips the way a man's breath curls in a Kharven winter, and it is nothing like the Letting. The Letting was Essence changed into Aether and given away, grey-gold and aimless, no longer his. This is Essence that is still Essence. The old way, the plain way, the way every practitioner's Essence shows itself when there is enough of it running hard enough near the skin: his own, shaped like him, carrying his weight, visible for a moment in the air before it draws back in on the next breath. The book stays balanced.

His body feels good, which he had not expected. His side still throbs on every breath and his calves are still knotted with the Hand's debt, but under the pain there is a feeling he has never had on a training floor in his life: the feeling of being in the right place. As if the Plane of Fate, which he has always had to reach up into, had come down instead, through the gold, and settled into the stone and the air and the lamplight and his own bones. The world is integrating. The two planes are lying one on top of the other, aligned, the way two panes of glass lie when you press them together and the line between them disappears. For the first time in the whole match he is not a visitor in the place he is fighting.

He does not reach for Balance. Balance is his pan of the scale and he has used it, and it fed the light. He does not reach for Ruin. Ruin is the other pan and he has used it, and the boy walked straight through it. The three failures are tightening round him, and on the graph they have nearly found what they are, and he can see the ignition coming down the line toward him like a lamplighter coming down a street.

This next thing will be Order.

---

The Balancer's own magic stands apart from the Works. The Works are older than Urion, the labour Bara set down before there was time to set it down in. Urion's magic is what a crowned principle does with that labour once there is a world for it to stand in, and the Order's books give it two names: Order Magic in the plain register, *Judicaris* in the formal one. The magic of measured force and lawful arbitration. It is the magic of the Hall of Equal Breath, where two lamps burn at either end of a windowless room, one gold, one blue, in a fixed relation that has not varied in nine hundred years. It is the magic the Ember Magistrates carry round the circuit of the Accord in their flame-lit courts, in towns that will never see the Grand Church. It is the magic the Inquisitors have etched into the steel of their gauntlets.

And in every culture it has reached, which is most of them, it has one thing people know it by, whatever language they say it in.

**The Flame of Judica.**

In the Palatinate they say it in the old tongue, *Flamma Iudicii*. In the Accord courts it is the Equal Flame. In the north, Dabney has heard, they have a word for it that means the fire you cannot lie to. It is the flame on the Ember Magistrates' table. It is the flame the Balancer's own root names. **[Hael]**, Flame, Urion's fourth glyph, whose office is set down in the Index in seven words: *marks verdicts; burns only falsehood and imbalance.*

He knows how it works. It is the one thing in the Order's curriculum taught first and explained last.

Put a pinch of salt in a clean flame and the flame goes yellow, a hard bright yellow that is always the same yellow. Put in copper and it goes green. Every substance in the world burns in its own colours and no other. Pass that light through a prism and each one breaks into its own fingerprint of bright lines, a few thin bars of colour on a dark field, as unique as a face. The Concord chemists worked it out sixty years ago with a burner and a prism and a box of salts, and proved something stranger besides: a thing glows in exactly the lines it would swallow if you shone light through it cold. What it gives out and what it takes in are the same account, written twice. A hot thing cannot hide what it is made of. Its light testifies.

Judicium is the Wellspring of that testimony. Its law is the law of the spectrum: everything that is authored, an oath, a working, a wall, a lie, gives off its own lines, and the lines cannot lie about what made them. The Flame of Judica is that law set alight. Whatever it touches, it heats until it gives off its true lines, and then it compares the lines against what the thing claims to be. Where they agree, it does nothing at all. An honest thing can stand in it and come out with its hair unsinged. Where they disagree, where a thing is giving off one set of lines and claiming another, where a working says *I am a road* and its light says *I have no destination*, the flame finds the disagreement and burns it. Falsehood and imbalance. Nothing else.

That is how the Order teaches it. He has seen it done, once, which is more than most boys in the Palatinate can say.

He was ten, riding pillion behind Darius on the coast road, and they stopped in a fishing village that had no name he ever learned because an Ember Magistrate's cart was drawn up in the square. The court was a trestle table and a brass bowl with a flame standing in it, steady in the sea wind the way no flame should be, and a crowd of women in salt-stiff aprons, and two men. One said the other had taken his nets in the night and sold them up the coast. The other said he had done no such thing. The Magistrate, a grey woman with burn-scars to the elbow and a voice like a door closing, had each man hold his hand over the bowl and say his piece. The first man's hand came away clean. The second man said *I never touched his nets*, and the flame leaned up into his palm, and his hand came away with a blister the size of a coin in the middle of it and not a mark anywhere else. He had not touched the nets. His brother had, and he had known, and he had been standing in front of the flame saying a sentence that was true on its face and false all the way down. The flame did not care about the face. It burned exactly the part that was a lie, and left the rest of the hand alone.

Dabney had been sick behind the cart afterward, from understanding it, and Darius had held his shoulders and said nothing until he was done.

Dabney's flame burns differently, and Darius is the only person alive who knows why.

The Order's flame takes its fuel from the will of the one who lights it. A Magistrate's flame burns as hot as the Magistrate's conviction, which is why the old Magistrates burn hottest and why the young ones are watched. It is the will of one thing, aimed. Dabney has never been able to light it that way. He tried at eleven, with Darius watching, and could not get a spark, and cried, and Darius sat with him on the workroom step until it was dark and then said something he did not understand for a year: *You are trying to want it with one voice. You have never had one voice.*

He has a graph instead.

He looks at it now, with the gold settled deep in his eyes. Every point of possibility in front of him, every one of the ten thousand small futures scattered across the field, each one leaning, the way every possible path leans toward the one they all agree on. And he sees, as he saw at twelve when he finally understood, that every one of them that ends with him still standing wants it. The possibilities themselves want it. Each one is a little will, a little hunger to be the thing that happens, and the ones that end in his winning are all pointing the same way, and there are a great many of them.

He lights the flame with all of their wills, and lets his own stand in line with the rest.

**Omnivolence**, he calls it to himself, a word he made up at twelve out of two Latin roots and has never said aloud: the will of every possibility at once. The Order's flame burns on the will of one thing. His burns on the singular ambition of everything that might happen, gathered and summed and aimed: every road on the graph that leads to him winning, adding its small want to the next and the next, the way every possible path of a ray of light adds its small wave to the others, and at the place where they all agree, the light is brightest.

That place is in his hands.

It catches on the blade first. Along the fuller of Aequitas, where the grain lies flat, a thin line of fire comes up out of the gold sheen of the Gilding, starting at the cross and running toward the point the way a flame runs along a trail of lamp oil, and it is gold too, but a deeper gold than the skin, the gold of the lamp at the far end of the Hall of Equal Breath. It burns quiet and steady and very bright, without roar or heat, a flame standing up off the steel the height of a finger, perfectly still in the moving air, the way no ordinary flame has ever stood. The air around it smells of hot brass and struck flint and, faintly, of salt.

Then it catches on him.

It runs up from his hands along his forearms, over the Gilding, a second skin of slow gold fire on top of the first, and it does not burn him, because there is nothing in him for it to burn. He is exactly what he says he is. He has always been exactly what he says he is; it is the only thing about him that has never once been in doubt. The flame tastes him and finds nothing false and settles on him like a cloak.

On the graph, the ten thousand small wills lean toward him. He can feel them lean. It is the most frightening thing he has ever felt and the most wonderful, and he has the strong, plain sense that if he opened the floor of himself now, the gladness under it would take this flame and run with it into places he does not have names for.

He keeps his hand flat on the lid.

The three failures find what they are.

He sees all three lines form at once, three bright impossible lines snapping out of the scatter around him: the road from his own mist, aimed across his middle; the weight with nothing under it, coming down into the stone beneath his rear heel; the stillness beside his sword arm, about to stop. He does not know what the flame will make of them. Each is a thing made true twice. He has never put a thing made true twice into the Flame of Judica, and nobody in the Order's books ever has, and he does not know whether the flame will call that a lie or a verdict.

He finds that he very much wants to find out.

The gold fire stands up off Aequitas the height of a finger, and his gold breath curls out over the white floor and draws back in. The ignitions come down their three lines toward him, and Dabney Lambert, lit from the inside and the outside both, lifts the burning sword to meet them.

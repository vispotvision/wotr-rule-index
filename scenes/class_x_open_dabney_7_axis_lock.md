# Class X Open, Dabney 7: Axis Lock

*Aetherion Academy. The Crucible Field, the opening match of the Class X Open Tournament. The fifth exchange.*

The flame on Aequitas will not decide.

It burns. It has not stopped burning since the three failures came down their lines into it, finger-high and gold and perfectly still in the moving air, and it is reading. He can feel it reading, the way he can feel his own eyes moving in the dark. It has the three failures in it and it knows exactly what each of them is, down to the last thread of how they were built, and it has found the falsehood in each, and it has found that the falsehood is true. So it reads again. And again. It hums against the steel like a hive with no way out, every line of its testimony perfect, and no verdict at the bottom of the page.

Then the rest of him goes wrong, one piece at a time.

The Gilding stays on his skin and stops working. He feels it before he understands it: the gold still lying over his hands and up his arms and along the blade, unbroken, every edge of the leaf still matched to the joint between the planes, and nothing crossing it. It is like holding a speaking-tube to his lips and hearing his own voice come back off a stopper at the far end. The bridge is whole. The bridge carries nothing. His Essence presses against the inside of a perfect door that will not open.

The graph fills with snow. Every possibility that was settling toward its line stands up again, and every one of them is right. The best line is still the best line; he can see it, bright as ever; and beside it, at exactly the same brightness, stand all the lines it beat. His eye has never had to choose before. It has only ever had to see, and the seeing did the choosing. Now it sees everything, perfectly. Nothing tells him which of the perfect things to obey.

The ten thousand small wills that leaned toward him stop leaning together. They still lean. Every future where he wins still wants him to win. But every future where he loses has been handed the same right to say which way is forward, and the great convergence that lit his flame comes apart into a crowd of arrows pointing every way at once.

Equipoise folds in on itself. A blow comes, an equal answer comes, and the answer will not leave. It closes back into the blow that made it, the way a coin spun on a table falls back into its own shadow.

And the Greater Hand, the strings at the root of his spine, the part of him that has never once in his life had to ask: the Hand still chooses, faster than thought, the right place for every grain of him to be. But behind every right place it drags, like a man dragging a net, every wrong place it refused, and every one of them is as real as the place he is standing.

He stands in the middle of the Crucible with all of his magic intact and none of it his.

---

Then, one by one, the wrongnesses lift.

He feels them go the way you feel stitches come out: a small tug and then a release, and then the next. The graph clears first. The snow settles and the lines that lost go dim again and the one bright line stands alone where it belongs. Then the stopper comes out of the speaking-tube, and his Essence goes through the gold and into his hands with a rush that makes his fingers tingle. Then Equipoise lets go of itself, and the answer he owes the world can leave the question again.

The boy across the floor is paying for it. Dabney can see that much from twenty paces, with the gold clearing in his eyes: whatever was holding all of that in place was being held up by a person, and the person has started putting it down to stay standing.

But not all of it.

Something is still in him.

He finds it because it is in his house, and anything in his house can be put on a scale. The Assize opens on it without being asked, the way it opened on the light that had weight and nothing to weigh, and this time it has a great deal to say. The thing is small, and it is quiet, and it is clever past anything he has met. It has no shape of its own. It waits. The moment any working of his speaks, the moment any of his magic says *this is what must be true for me to work*, it listens, and it builds, out of what it heard, the exact opposite truth, and it hands that truth equal weight. Nothing breaks. Everything he does is made to argue with itself. It is a lodger who learns the house by listening to the house describe itself, and then stands in every doorway saying *also the other way*.

And it has a thread.

That is the first thing the scale finds. Everything that is made has a maker, and everything that stays alive after it is made stays alive along a line back to whoever is keeping it alive. This thing is not finished. It cannot finish; it has to keep building, answer after answer, every time his magic speaks. So someone is still paying for it. And running out of the lodger, out of his own architecture, across twenty paces of white stone, there is a thread. Fine, taut, and singing under strain like a fiddle string tuned past its note, it runs back to the boy who wrote it.

The thread is the bridge. Every contradiction the thing builds has to stand on that boy's soul and his nerves and his breath for as long as it stands. The thread is where all the weight goes. It is the one place in the whole beautiful machine that is carrying more than it was ever built to carry.

The boy's voice comes across the floor.

*I'd rather disappoint mine and stay alive.*

"You're right," Dabney says. His voice is rough, but it carries. "He'd hate that you're right." Something that is almost a laugh catches on the bar under his ribs. "He'd hate it so much."

---

He thinks about it for the length of a breath, because it deserves a breath.

*What is a paradox, other than an imbalance in reality itself? And what is judgement, but an assessment of all things?*

They are both questions, equally, and he lets them stand as questions. The boy has spent this whole match making the world carry things at equal weight that do not weigh the same, a hand on the beam holding two unlike loads level, and paying with his own body for the privilege. Dabney has spent his whole life being the beam: every possibility, every claim, every road and load and ending, put on the scale and let fall where it falls. The same beam, read from opposite ends. And his flame is stuck because it has been asked to weigh a held imbalance and find that it is level, and it cannot, and it will not lie.

The lodger gives every contrary thing equal local authority. That is its whole art. It does not reach the whole world; it never claimed to. It works here, in this field, inside his own magic, a handspan at a time. Everything the boy has built today has been local. Local roads, local floors, local endings, local truths held level in one place by one boy's hand.

If he wants this battle to be local, then by all means. Dabney will make it a battle of local authority.

Local power.

There is one thing in the world that has always had more say over a place than anything else in it, and the old natural philosophers wrote it down before they wrote down anything else. Weight. Put a heavy enough thing in a room and the room has a down, and everything in the room learns which way down is, and no clever hand on any beam can hold two things level on a slope.

He has been leaning his back against the plates since the tunnel. Now he takes his weight off them.

---

There are six. There have always been six, as long as he has been able to feel them, though he has never seen them and never will: six plates bolted one over another on top of whatever he is, each one put there by a Stage he crossed before there was a person in him to remember the crossing. Every Stage is a measure of restraint. Darius told him so at six and has told him so every year since. *They count the cage, Dabney, and call it the animal.* Most practitioners climb their Stages. He was born at the bottom of a well and the Stages were the lid going on.

He does not take them all off. He is not mad.

He takes off two.

**Remissio**, Darius calls it, the word the Order's physicians use for the slackening of a fever, a tightened string let out a turn, a debt forgiven by part. He has never done it in front of anyone. He did it once in the workroom with the old man's hand flat on his back, and the bench split along its grain, and Darius said *enough* in a voice he had never heard before or since.

The top plate first. The newest, Glory's, the one that went on before he could remember. He finds its edge the way you find a door in the dark with your fingertips, and he lets it lift.

Then the one under it.

The room answers first, as the room always does.

Every lamp hanging over the Crucible swings toward him on its chain at once, a hand's breadth, then two, and stays there, leaning, the flames inside their glass bending sideways like grass in a gale. This time the shadows on the white floor pour toward him across the stone like water toward a drain. The chalk lines the wardens drew lift off the floor in a fine white haze and stream in. The draw-hum in the stone drops past a half-tone, past a whole tone, down and down until it is not a hum any more but a groan, the long groan a ship's timbers make in a heavy sea.

Then the floor.

The white stone of the Crucible is the most fortified ground in Aetherion, built to take whatever Class X can throw at it, and it bends. A long slow ripple goes out from under his feet in a ring, the stone heaving up a finger's breadth and settling, heaving and settling, like the skin of a drum after the stick. The ripple reaches the silver-red boundary and runs along it and comes back. The whole arena gives a long low shudder, every tier at once, and the benches creak, and somewhere up in the dark ring a great many people grab the rail in front of them in the same instant.

And above it all, the sky parts.

The Crucible is open at its crown, a great ring of evening over the arena where the Scriptia hang, and there has been a ceiling of grey cloud over that ring all afternoon. Now the cloud moves. It draws back from the centre of the ring in every direction at once, slowly, the way the skin on boiled milk draws back when you blow on it, until there is a clean round hole in the overcast directly above him, and through the hole the first evening star, very bright and very small.

It contorts. All of it. The tiers lean in toward the floor at the edges of his sight, and the lines of the arena, its rails and stairs and arches, bow inward in a way that makes the eye water, the way the edge of a room bows when you look at it through the bottom of a glass. Nothing tears. Nothing falls. The field is simply being told, by a weight that has not been in it before, where its middle is, and the whole architecture of the place is listening.

The front rows feel it in their bodies. He sees, at the edge of the gold, a boy in the first tier sit down hard on the bench he was standing on, and a girl beside him put both hands flat on the stone as if the stone might buck. Somebody further up is sick. The referee at the silver-red line has gone to one knee without seeming to decide to, his hand raised, his face grey, holding on, the match still running.

In Dabney's own body, the plates coming up is the most frightening relief he has ever felt. It is like taking a breath after holding one for seven years. His chest opens. His shoulders come down. The ache in his calves and back goes far away. The bruise along his ribs is still there, and his jaw still clamps, but there is so much more of him now than there was a moment ago that the pain is a small thing in a large room.

And something under the floor of him, under the plates, the gladness, the thing that loves this, stands up all the way and looks around, and he has to put his whole hand on it, both hands, his whole weight, and say *no*. Not that. Not yet. He has lifted two plates. Two.

---

The lodger in his house is screaming.

He can feel it. Every truth it holds level is a truth held level in a field that now has a down, and a beam held level on a slope takes more and more hand to keep it there. The thread back to its author is singing higher. The local authority it gave every contrary thing is still authority, but now it is authority in his place, under his weight, on his slope.

It is not enough to strip it. He wants more than that. He wants to strip possibility itself, here, for a moment: to take the Plane of Fate in this field and take away the one thing that makes it what it is, the thing he has loved and feared since he could first see it. The branching. The many. The ten thousand small futures each finding their own way. For one breath, in one place, he wants Fate to have only one road.

The Order has a working for that. It is in the Parun Index the Order keeps in the Grand Church, the catalogue of Applied forms that echo a single root, numbered in the order the Measurewrights proved them, and it is the thirtieth. Darius taught it to him at twelve and made him swear on the Hall of Equal Breath never to use it before he had crossed into Refraction, because the Index lists it at Refraction and above, and nobody below that line had ever held it and kept their eyes.

He is a Stage below the line. The line counts the cage. He has just taken the lid off two plates.

He grips Aequitas in both hands and holds it straight out in front of him, point up, cross level with his chin, the flat toward the boy, the way a witness holds the book to swear on. The gold fire on the blade stands up straight. His knuckles go white on the grip.

He would end this.

"Paru," Dabney says, and the Scriptia carry it into every tier of a stadium that has gone dead silent, "number thirty."

The breath.

"Axis Lock."

---

**Axis Lock** is a Materia-family working, and every Materia-family working stands on the science of what matter is made of: the lattice of a crystal, the state a substance is in, the bonds that hold its molecules to each other. The root it echoes is **[Ur]**, Balance. Its Wellspring is **Fixatio**, the Binding Flame, the anchoring law, the first thing Darius was ever good at, the law that held a single strand of a boy's thread still on a workbench long enough for it to become a sword. Its second Wellspring is **Judicium**, because it is a working that exists for exactly one purpose: to hold the world still while a verdict is cast.

Here is how it works, and the Order teaches it with a jar of water.

Cool clean water slowly enough, in a smooth enough jar, with nothing in it, and it will go below the point where it ought to freeze and stay liquid. It does not know which way to freeze. Every way to arrange its ice is equally good, and nothing in the jar is telling it which to choose, so it chooses none, and hangs there, poised, colder than it should be and still a liquid. Then drop in one grain of ice, one speck of dust, one seed, and the whole jar freezes from the seed outward in a breath, every molecule falling into line with the first. The cold did the freezing; the seed only told the cold which way.

Iron does the same. Heat a bar of iron past the point the Concord physicists call by its discoverer's name. Above it, every tiny region inside the bar points its magnetism its own way, and the bar as a whole points nowhere at all, a crowd with no leader. Let it cool through that point with nothing nearby, and the regions settle each its own way, at random. The bar is still a muddle. Let it cool with a strong enough magnet held against one end, and every region settles the same way, all at once. The bar comes out of the cooling a single magnet pointing a single direction, and it stays that way after the magnet is taken off.

Anything that can go two ways with equal right sits on a knife edge between two hollows. It has to fall into one. But which one it falls into is decided by the smallest push at the moment it falls. That is the whole of the art. Fixatio is the cold. It is the law that makes a thing settle, anchor, fix, resolve into one state and stay there. **[Ur]** is the seed and the magnet. It supplies the one condition Fixatio needs to resolve the whole field toward a single state instead of letting it settle at random into a thousand. Together they take a field where many outcomes stand at equal weight and quench it, freeze it, into one.

Axis Lock freezes one outcome branch. One. The others survive unchosen, the way the other ways the water could have frozen survive; they are only no longer possible here, now, for as long as the lock holds.

And it holds for exactly as long as it takes to cast the verdict, and not a heartbeat longer without cost. That is written in the Index too, in the column for backlash, in four words: *backlash if verdict delayed*. A frozen field wants to thaw. Hold it frozen without spending it on a judgment and the cold comes back into the one who holds it.

The counter is written there as well, in the last column, for anyone to read: **[Wy]**, Motion, Wyther's root, whose office is to add momentum and turn stasis into movement. A field can only be quenched into one state if nothing in it is moving fast enough to shake the seed loose. Stir the jar while it freezes, heat the iron while it cools, put a second condition into the field before the first one finishes resolving, and the field never reaches the single state it was narrowing toward. It boils off. Or it freezes toward the counter's state instead.

He knows all of this. He has known it since he was twelve. He has never once felt it.

He feels it now.

---

The lock goes through the field like frost through a window.

He sees it go on the graph. Every point of possibility in the Crucible, every one of the ten thousand small futures that had come back after the snow, stands for one instant poised on its own knife edge. Then the seed drops. Out from the blade in his hands, out from the cross and the gold flame and the weight of two lifted plates, the field quenches. This time the points go still instead of dimming. They go cold and clear and fixed, like a spill of mercury on a table seized in a single instant into a sheet of mirror. Every line on the graph but one locks into place, unchosen, unbroken, simply no longer in play. One line stays bright.

It is the strangest thing he has ever seen and he understands, as it happens, why the Index says *Refraction and above*. The cold of it comes back up the blade into his hands. His eyes ache. A hot thread runs down from his left nostril over his lip, and he tastes iron on top of the ash and tin and copper, and he knows that he has started paying the backlash already, because he has not yet cast anything to spend the lock on.

The Flame of Judica feels the field freeze and finishes its sentence.

He feels it happen in the steel. All this while it has been reading three failures and finding them each made true twice, and it could not choose, because the field let both truths stand at equal weight. Now the field has one way down. One of the two truths is on the line that stayed bright and the other is on a line that has been frozen out of play, and the flame, which has never lied and cannot, looks at what is left and begins, for the first time, to lean toward a verdict.

It goes from gold to white along the whole length of the blade.

And the lodger in his house meets the lock.

He feels that too, and does not know how it will go, because the lodger answers every truth his magic declares with its equal opposite. The lock has declared the oldest truth there is: *one*. The lodger is answering. He can feel it building *also the other*, as fast as it has built anything, and the thread back to its author sings so high it is nearly out of hearing, and the frozen field and the thing that will not let anything be only one thing are leaning against each other in the middle of his own magic like two men shoulder to shoulder in a doorway. He does not know which of them will give. He does not have time to find out. The backlash is coming up his arms like cold water up a wick.

Cast the verdict.

---

He does not aim at the boy.

He has thought about that, in the one breath he gave the questions. He could. The bright line on the graph runs straight across the floor to where the boy stands, and Aequitas could follow it. But the boy is thirteen and bleeding and has written the best magic Dabney has ever seen, and this is a tournament, and the old man in the stands taught him that the scale weighs the act and leaves the man to himself.

He aims at the thread.

He can see it plainly now, with the field frozen: the fine taut line running out of his own magic and across the white stone back to its author, singing with the weight of every contradiction it is carrying. It is the weakest place in the whole design, because it is the place where all of it rests on one person. Cut the bridge and nothing that stands on it can stand. Nobody needs to be hurt for that. A bridge can come down without the river being harmed.

The Greater Hand takes up the strings. With the field frozen there is one correct place for every grain of him and nothing to drag behind, and the Hand moves him along the single line like a bead along a wire, three long steps across the rippling white floor that his feet do not remember taking. The Gilding conducts. All of the Essence the two lifted plates set loose pours down through his arms into the steel without losing a drop at the joint. Equipoise loads. Every shove the contorting arena has put into his feet, every heave of the drum-skin floor, every lean of every lamp, has been met by its equal coming up from him, and he does not send it into the ground this time. He holds it in the blade.

And the blade turns in his hands, so that the flat comes round to lead.

It is the plainest strike he knows. It is the first one Darius ever taught him, before the leaf, before the brick: the level stroke, the blade carried flat and true across the line of the target the way a mason's rule is laid across a course of stone to see where it is high. The old man called it **Level Ground**, after the sword's name. A blade laid flat across a thing measures it. And anything standing proud of the line, anything carrying more than it was built to carry, is struck level.

He has done it a thousand times in the yard. He has never done it with the Flame of Judica gone white along the steel and two plates lifted off his soul and a whole field of Fate frozen into a single line in front of him.

The white flame leans forward off the flat, toward the thread.

The flat of Aequitas comes down out of the gold, level and true and burning, onto the singing line between his magic and its author. The sky over the Crucible stands open on its one bright star. The frozen field holds its breath around him. The backlash bites cold at his wrists.

And the verdict falls.

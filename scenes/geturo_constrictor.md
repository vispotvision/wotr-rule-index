# Geturo: Constrictor

The shoulder made a small wet sound inside him, and every finger on his right hand jumped at once.

It was not out. He knew what out felt like; he had put Hiromi's back in at twelve, in the orchard, with his heel in his brother's armpit and both of them crying. This was the ball of the joint grinding up against the lip of its socket and the cuff of muscle round it stretched to the last fibre it had, and a hot bright line of pain going from the top of the shoulder to the back of the hand. His cheek was pressed into the sand under a thigh. His right arm was bent up behind him, the wrist held, the elbow owned. Every breath he took moved the joint a hair and the joint told him about it.

A word came down to him through the thigh and the sand. He heard it as a shape more than a sound.

"Not yet," Geturo said into the grit.

The truth was he had wanted to be exactly here.

Not the shoulder. Nobody wants the shoulder. But he had come up out of the stand-up with his hands at his temples knowing that every hold worth having on a longer, lighter body ends with the holder's legs wrapped close round it, and that when they were, those legs would be a hand's width from his left hand. The ribs had been left open as bait. The elbows had been left high as bait. He had been fighting the whole afternoon to keep other people out of reach of his coat. For the last three breaths he had been fighting to bring one person all the way in.

His left hand was free. It was the only free thing he had.

It went not to the bandolier, which was crushed under him with vial three lying crooked and cold against his ribs, but to the outside pocket of his coat, the deep one on the left hip his mother had lined with oilskin. His fingers found the one vial he kept there on its own.

It was a hand long, heavier than the others, stoppered in grey wax with the Mahuo mark pressed in it. The liquid inside was blue. Not the blue of dye or of copper salts. It was a thick, slow, luminous blue like deep water with the sun behind it, and it did not slosh when he moved it. It leaned. It had taken him eleven months to fill, a drop at a time, sitting at his father's bench every night with his hands flat on the glass and his Essence going down into the solution and staying there, until the liquor held more of him than any liquid should have been able to hold. It was a supersaturated Draft. It was the one thing in his coat that was not alchemy at all.

He did not throw it. There was no room to throw. He flicked it, a short hard snap of the wrist from the hip with the fingers rolling off the glass, and it spun, and on a spin that tight over that short a distance it curled in a hand's breadth and broke against the leg across his face.

"Infinite Summoning Arts," Geturo said.

His voice came out low and level, with sand in it. It was the first time in his life he had said those three words where anyone outside the family could hear.

The blue did not splash. It hung where the glass had been, a clot of light in the shape of the broken vial, and waited to be told what it was.

"Essence Draft."

That was the medium. His father's summons came up out of the room, out of whatever the air was carrying, and when the room was poor they came up poor. A Draft summons carried its own air. Everything it would be made of was already in the bottle, eleven months of him dissolved and held, and the second word told it so. The clot of blue began to thicken. The light in it went dim at the edges, and the edges went solid.

He drew a breath through the pain in his shoulder.

"...Constrictor."

The third word was the law.

It came out of the blue the way a rope comes off a drum. First a blunt head no bigger than his fist, flat and wedge-shaped, with no eyes at all, only a row of deep pits along the upper lip that turned toward warmth. Then a body. It was the thickness of a man's forearm and it kept coming, poured out of the hanging clot a coil at a time, and as it came it went from translucent to cloudy to solid, the colour of wet slate with the blue still moving underneath the scales. The scales were not smooth. They were keeled, each one ridged down the middle like a roof tile. It was cold where it brushed his cheek, and it smelled of nothing at all, and it was heavy the way muscle is heavy, every hand's length of it thick and packed and working.

It found the leg and it wrapped.

Once round the thigh above the knee. Twice. Three times, the third coil laid down over the first two, and then the head went down under the back of the knee and anchored there and pulled.

Geturo felt it take up slack. He did not see it. He felt it through his own face, pressed into the sand under that thigh: a slow, enormous, even tightening, like a wet rope drying on a post.

The name carried three laws and the Draft had taken all three.

The first was the wrap itself. A rope turned round a post three times will hold a ship against a tide in the hands of one boy on the dock, and the rope does not have to be strong. Every turn multiplies the grip of the turn before it. Seamen had known it forever, and so had every carter who ever snubbed a runaway wagon on a tree. Three turns of keeled scale on cloth and skin did not have to squeeze hard. It only had to hold, and anything that pulled against it, a leg straightening, a hip turning away, would only drive the coils down harder on themselves.

The second was the heartbeat. A real constrictor does not crush. It feels the pulse of what it holds through its own belly, and it tightens on the moment between beats, when the blood has gone through and the vessel is soft, and it does not give back what it takes. The Constrictor did the same. Under Geturo's cheek he felt it pulse once, and cinch, and pulse, and cinch. It was a ratchet. It only turned one way. Enough of that on a limb and the blood stops going in, then stops coming out, and the leg past the coils belongs to the snake.

The third was his own, and it was the reason he had worked a year to make it and never shown it to anyone.

The Constrictor fed on spending. His Tide had taught him that much: a field that takes from whatever burns brightest inside the thing it crosses. He had built the Tide to lie on the ground. He had built this to lie on a body. Every thread of live Essence the held leg carried, every working its owner reached for through it, the coils would take in through the scales, and every scrap they took would go into their own muscle as mass. A Tide with a spine. Fight it with the arts and it grew.

The cost came in after them, as it always did.

The Tide under them was still running, thin and broken, and it taxed live work. The Draft had been paid for at the bench and cost him nothing, but the naming had not, and the naming was the most he had ever spent in one breath. Blood came from both nostrils now, not one. The edges of his sight went grey and closed in to a tunnel with the sand at the end of it. Under his ribs, crushed into the bandolier, vial three went from cold to colder, so cold it burned, and he had no idea why.

On the boards, his father made a sound.

It was not a word. It was the sound a man makes when he sets down a cup and finds the table is not where he left it. Kwon Mu-jin's right hand had come up again. It did not rise to stop anything. It was only open, the fingers spread a little, as if feeling the air for something that had not been in it a moment ago. His left palm still held the seam of the dome. His uncovered eye was on the snake.

He had not known. Geturo could not see his face and knew it anyway. His father had not known.

At the tunnel mouth, the tall Class S boy took his hand off the stone.

"Mahuo," he said to the girl. He said it the way people say the name of a disease they have read about and never seen. "Put that at the top."

The girl's pencil stopped. Then it went on, faster.

The warden looked from the snake to her gauge and back, and the gauge needle was not moving at all, and that seemed to worry her more than if it had been.

Behind her, the service door opened again. A thin, bent man came through it in the long black coat of a Guild draw-inspector, with a brass-bound case in one hand and a folding stool under the other arm. His hair was white and fine as thistledown and stood out from his head in a pale halo. His face was narrow and fallen in at the cheeks, with a nose like a pruning hook and a wide, lipless mouth, and his eyes behind half-moon spectacles were a surprising clear blue. The coat hung off his shoulders as off a peg and was shiny at the elbows and green at the seams, and a ring of keys the size of a dinner plate hung from his belt and chimed when he walked. His fingertips were stained dark brown to the second knuckle.

He set the stool down beside the warden, sat on it, opened the case on his knees, and looked at the sand.

"You said a ward," he said to her. His voice was dry and thin and very precise. "You did not say a Mahuo."

"I didn't know," said the warden.

"No." He took out a slate and a stub of chalk and began to write. "Nobody ever does."

The Dawi orderly had set his stretcher down across two chairs. He had not taken his eyes off the coils.

"Snake," he said. And after a while: "Good snake."

Tam Rudd put his chalk behind his ear and did not write anything at all.

On the sand, under a thigh, with his right shoulder screaming and his left hand open and empty in the grit, Geturo felt the Constrictor take the next beat of a heart that was not his own, and cinch.

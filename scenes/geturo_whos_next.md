# Geturo: Who's Next

The pencil was in Geturo's hand before he had properly decided to take it out.

It lived in the left breast pocket of his Academy black, behind a second lining his mother had sewn in so the glass would not wear through the wool. Brass cap, brass ferrule, and between them a barrel of thick clear glass as long as his middle finger. Inside the glass a core of grey lead stood in a sleeve of milk-white paste. He had made the paste himself, three evenings running at his father's bench in the dormitory workroom, and his fingertips had smelled of it for a week afterward: chalk and hot stone and the faint vinegar of the carrier.

Gypsum, first. Crushed, sieved, then calcined in the shallow iron pan over a low flame until it had given up three quarters of its water and gone soft and thirsty. His father had made him hold the pan and count, and had taken it off him twice when the heat climbed too far and the powder went dead. Burn it too hot and it would never take water back. Burn it right and the moment it touched water again it would grab it, crystal locking into crystal, the whole mass going from slurry to stone in minutes and warm to the touch while it did. Masons had known that for three thousand years. They called it plaster. The paste in his pencil was that plaster held in a carrier that would not let it drink until it was told to.

The telling was the lead. Graphite cut with silver, soft enough to mark skin. He was Welling, two Stages from anything that could work without a hand or ink behind it, so the hand and the ink were how he worked. Drawn once around a forearm, the Coagulatio line closed on itself, the carrier let go, and the plaster bled out along the mark and pulled water out of the air and the sweat on the skin and set. A cast, from a pencil, in the time it took to walk across a courtyard. He had drawn it on his own wrist in the orchard twice, and on Hiromi's once, and it had held.

He had it uncapped. Then he watched the stylet go into the masked boy's radius from the inside, and he watched the white follow the crack along the bone and set in it, and he put the cap back on.

A cast held a broken arm still from the outside and did nothing at all to the break. Dallae had just fixed the break.

Whelp.

He sat back down. The gallery bench was oiled pine, gone tacky where the sun through the dome glass had been lying on it all afternoon, and a splinter near his knee caught the wool of his trousers when he shifted. The draw-hum in the conduit under the tiers was running half a note flat since the ward cracked. It made his back teeth itch. Down on the sand the smell of cooling iron was still coming up in waves, and somewhere under it the copper of his brother's blood.

He slid the pencil back into its lining and rested his big square hands on his knees where they could not do anything else.

Oldest of the bunch. He turned that over. Fifteen, and nobody else on this bench had him beaten by more than a season, and in most rooms that was the sort of thing that got a boy made into a leader whether he liked it or not. People would start looking at him when something went wrong. They would wait for him to say what happened next.

Nah.

Leaders got looked at. He had spent a long time arranging not to be. If Class X needed somebody at the front, Taesyn could have it, or Zaire. He would take either of them. The two of them were practically steaming with it, the kind of potential you could see from across a room without trying, and he found he did not mind that at all. Somebody had to walk the outside. He was good at the outside.

His father was talking to Hiromi and Xanelor now, low and level, and every word of it was true. Geturo listened to all of it without looking away.

He was part of the reason, though. Nobody down there was going to say so, so he said it to himself. He had been the one out in the orchard at Sum-gol every morning of the summer with a practice staff, making Hiromi hold a binding circle while Geturo knocked at it, making him hold it again when it broke, telling him he was the best there was until the boy's nose bled onto the grass and he laughed and wiped it and went again. You pushed a prodigy. Everybody knew that. You pushed him as far as he would go and then a little further, because a prodigy that nobody pushed went soft or went strange. Geturo had pushed. Somewhere in the pushing Hiromi had stopped expecting anyone to be able to push back.

And Hiromi was a prodigy. That was never in question. At six he had done something inside a binding circle that none of the adults standing around it could name, and at seven he had done it on purpose. The Paragon of Dominion. The world knew what he was before he opened his mouth.

Geturo had not been born with anything of the kind. He had good marks. He had a slate full of his father's corrections and a pan he had learned to calcine gypsum in without killing it. He worked. He was a normal enough boy who worked hard, and he had never corrected anybody who stopped there.

That arrow had been moving awfully slow.

He had watched it the whole way. The fletching burning off in two separate licks of flame. The shaft bowing once just off the string and then straightening. The skin of air in front of the point going white and folded before the vial met it. He had watched it cross the ring at about the pace of a cart on a flat road and thought, idly, that Hiromi ought to have rolled left.

Then everybody on the bench had flinched. All at once, a full second behind him, when it was already over.

Really fast, then. Compared to everyone else.

He had never asked himself about that and he did not start now.

There had been a girl in the tunnel mouth past the judges' box, silver cord, pencil going on a card. He had clocked her before she had finished settling her shoulder against the stone, before his father had seen her. Class S. She had been writing about the masked boy, the one who had just thrown an arrow through the sound barrier and wrecked his own arms doing it, and she would be back tomorrow with a friend to write some more.

*Let her watch the one with no bow,* he thought.

Ah, what the hell.

He stood up. The bench creaked. He put two fingers on the gallery rail and leaned over it far enough to be heard without shouting.

"Hey, Father." He heard it come out and did not bother to walk it back all the way. "Teacher. Who's up next, since we're all out here anyway?"

Kwon Mu-jin looked up at the bench. The binding over his right eye had gone grey at the knot with arena dust, and the uncovered eye moved along the row of them from one end to the other, taking about as long on each face as it took to count a pulse.

"Mm." He brushed sand off the heel of his hand against his coat. "Whoever wants it."

Nobody moved. His father waited out the nobody moving without seeming to mind it.

"I'd like every one of you down here before the week's out," he said. "You've got a tournament in seven days, and I'd rather find out what you can actually do on sand I can stop than on sand I can't. So. Show me." He tipped his head toward the ring, the trench, the long white crack in the dome above all of it. "Mind the hole."

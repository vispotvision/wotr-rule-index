# Glass and Irons

---

## I · The Integers

*The Kessen road below the iron hills, the first afternoon out of Sum-gol; the paddies brimming on both sides and the sun standing high*

Naiser had noticed the phenomenon of time slowing before anyone else in the carriage did.

It came in through the open window first. The carriage had been rolling down the long bend under the iron hills for an hour, the four gray horses at an easy trot and the iron tires singing on the packed clay, and on both sides of the road the paddies of the lower valley lay brimming to their dikes with spring water, every one of them a long flat mirror with the new rice standing in it in green needles, and the sky lying in the water upside down, blue as a kingfisher's back. The rice-mist that had come off the terraces at dawn still hung over the low ground in white rags. The frogs had been going all afternoon, a wet ratchet from every dike at once. A dragonfly had kept pace with the window for a quarter mile, its body the hot blue of a struck spark and its wings four flickers of glass, and Hiromi had been watching it with his chin on the sill.

Then the rice-mist stopped drifting.

Naiser felt it before he saw it. The spiritual reverberations surrounding the tangible world, the long low hum every living and made thing gives off into the Aether the way a struck bowl gives off its note, were smoothing down. He felt them go flat and even, as if somebody had laid a palm across the whole valley's bowl to still it, and the moments running under them, which ordinarily ran like water over stones, began to come apart into separate integers of time: one, and then the next, and then the next, each sitting a little longer in its place before it let the next one in. Outside the window the dragonfly's wingbeat thickened. He could see the strokes. Then he could count them. Then the dragonfly hung there beside the carriage like a blue pin stuck in the air, rowing, going nowhere.

He slowly pulled the cigarette from his mouth.

It was Lambert leaf, cut fine and cured over cedar, and the smoke coming off its ember should have climbed to the carriage roof and spread there in a gray sheet. It climbed a finger's breadth off the coal and stopped. It sat above the ember in a little gray knot, folded on itself, as still as a fly set in jeweler's resin.

*The clocks are keeping their count.* Anamnesis told him so the moment he asked it. The Wellspring of Remembrance reads the history every thing keeps in itself, and the valley's history was running on at its proper pace: the sun had not slowed, the rice had not stopped growing, the heart in his chest beat its ordinary sixty. What had changed was the air. It had gone thick. He could feel it on the backs of his hands like a heavy cloth laid over them, and in his lungs when he breathed, a weight on the inhale, the air coming in the way honey comes off a spoon. Something ahead of them was taking the valley's air toward glass.

Glass is a liquid that has given up moving. The Stannvaard philosophers kept a funnel of pitch in a glass case in their hall to prove it, and the pitch let fall one drop every ten winters, and students sat up through the nights of the tenth winter to watch it go. Pitch is a liquid; so is window glass, in the long view; and between them and water there is only the matter of how long a thing takes to move once it has been asked. Whoever was ahead on the road was asking the air very slowly.

"It seems like someone is in our path."

He looked across the carriage at Senna, and then at Hiromi.

His daughter sat straight on the gray bench with her hands folded in her lap and her violet hair tied back at the nape with its gray cord, the Yukari composure on her like a coat cut too big for her that she meant to grow into. She was trying very hard not to yawn. He watched the yawn arrive in her jaw, and watched her refuse it, and watched it go down her throat unswallowed like a stone. Hiromi had no such scruple. The boy turned from the window with his mouth open wide enough to show his back teeth and yawned like a cat on a step, enormous, *haaaaam*, with fox fur still caught in the olive wool of his coat, and on the bench beside him the fox-woman Dari yawned with him, her two tall white ears folding back and her nine vermilion-tipped tails sagging off the cushions in a soft white heap.

"How bothersome..."

He felt the malformation then, under the flatness: the spiritual dread of it, a soul out on the road ahead that had gone wrong in a particular and recognizable way, the way a bone that knits crooked is still a bone and is still, forever, wrong. Yes. This was a Sin of sorts. The Church of the Holy Sea had its grades for what such a thing was, and the Yukari had an older word in the record that meant *a soul that has made a law of its worst hour*. He let Anamnesis lean out through the window toward it, delicately, the way a smith tilts a blade to read its temper colors in the light, and the record came back to him: a long, long life of things begun. A job begun. A promise begun. A love begun, and an apology begun, and a fight begun a hundred times. He looked for the end of any of them and there was none. Every act in it lay open like a door off its latch.

It was Sloth, in all probability.

In all probability this would be a great showing of Hiromi's and Senna's potential. A Sin who would not finish a thing was the gentlest enemy a pair of children could draw, and also one of the worst, and they would learn more from him in a quarter hour than from a season in the yard. However, this was a very capable threat. The air was not lying about that. The air was turning to glass for a hundred paces in every direction, and the creature doing it had not yet troubled himself to try.

Naiser tapped his chin.

He did the sum in his mother's blood, the Lambert count, while the two notes of his aura murmured under his breastbone a hair apart. Even fifty percent of his true power would be enough to cripple the Sin. He would not need the Twin Mantle. He would not need the Corridor. A line of dimmed air across the road and a single sentence set in smoke would end the matter before the dragonfly finished its stroke. That was exactly why he would do neither.

"I know you're in there, YUKARI!"

It came down the road from ahead, loud and lazy at once, a big voice from a small chest.

"GET YOUR ASS OUT HERE, I DON'T GOT ALL DAY!"

Naiser leaned to the window.

A hundred paces down the road, where it ran out onto a causeway between two of the widest paddies, a goblin was floating in the air.

He lay on nothing, a man's height above the water, as if on a long chair in a garden: one leg crossed over the other at the knee, one arm behind his head and the other trailing, and his weight dropped on one hip in a slouch that looked as if it had been practiced in front of a glass. He was small even for a goblin, half a grown man's height, mossy green skin mottled brown and black with dark streaks running down from his eyes like old tear-tracks, ears long and pointed and sticking out sideways through a deep olive hood, and every inch of the ears he could reach was pierced: gold rings, silver hoops, bone studs and thin iron spikes driven through the cartilage. A long dark-olive cloak with a high stiff collar. A pale green silk tie knotted loose. Black platform boots heavy with buckles. He lazily flicked his head back to toss a spiky crest of oiled black hair off his brow, and touched it with one lacquered claw to make sure it was still there, and at his shoulder, floating beside his ear like a pet, a riveted glass sphere the size of an apple turned slowly in its brass bands. There was a curl of steam frozen inside it, white, and a few droplets hanging, the way a paperweight holds a flower.

"I've come to get payback for all the goblins you killed!" He drew the words out like taffy. "FOR ALL THE GOBLINS!"

Naiser put the cigarette back between his lips, drew on it, and breathed the smoke out of the window toward the road.

The smoke came out of him slowly in the thickened air and lay there outside the carriage in a long gray skein that did not drift, because Fixatio was in it. Fixatio is the Binding Flame, and it cures what it touches the way a varnish of pine-resin and lime cures on a lacquered box: once the long molecules have joined arm to arm, the other shape is no longer anywhere it can go back to. The smoke set. It hung in the air as a fine gray script. And when the goblin's vow came down the road and passed through it, the smoke did what Naiser's smoke always did with a sentence spoken inside it: it waited to see whether the sentence would bend it, the way a lie bends it, or straighten and set it, the way a truth does.

It did neither.

The words went through the skein and the skein did not move at all. They slid off it the way rain slides off oiled cloth, leaving nothing, finding nothing to grip. A vow that had never been meant to be finished. Naiser watched the smoke not move. Out of the side of his eye he watched his daughter watching it too, with her gray eyes narrowed and her lip caught in her teeth, and he said nothing about it.

Senna looked at her father, who had stopped tapping his chin.

"Seems like we have to handle this."

Hiromi looked excited. The yawn had gone out of him all at once and his eyes were enormous.

"I get to fight this early on? Sounds *fun!*"

"*Ouuuu,*" said Dari, ears up again, both of them, pointing out of the window like a pair of hands. "Can I squish him? He looks *so* squishable. Look at his little *boots!*"

"You'll stay on the step, Dari," said Naiser.

"Aww."

"On the step." He did not raise his voice. He never did. The fox-woman's tails went flat on the cushion one after another like nine white flags being lowered, and she pouted at him enormously with her whole face, and stayed.

Naiser opened the door and walked out.

The pressure met him on the step. Vaashuuk Snorr-Khal stood at the tenth of the Temperance Stages, Realization, and a soul at that clarity sitting a hundred paces off fills the space round it the way a stove fills a small room: the footman on the box above went limp in his seat with his chin on his chest, asleep before he knew he was tired, and the four gray horses dropped their heads in their traces with their lips hanging loose and their eyes rolling shut. The heavy air under the Sin's nature leaked off him in every direction, and Naiser felt it settle on his own lids like a hand. It was nothing to him. Two Stages above the goblin, he stood in it as a tall man stands in a stream that comes to a child's chest. But the children came down the step behind him, and the stream came to their chests.

He breathed the smoke out round them.

He did it without turning his head. The two notes of his aura drifted together until they were one, long and low with a hall's echo under them, *ooonnnn*, and the air round him dimmed the way a corridor dims when somebody goes along it turning the lamps down, and the gray of him went over to black-violet at the edges like black silk drawn over a blade. The charge came up from the Sovereign Crystal under his breastbone through a Shell so fine that very nearly all of it went where he sent it; the small part that did not came off his fingertips as a faint warmth on the paper of the cigarette, as if he had held it near a lamp. It was a sip of what he carried, and he would not have felt it go. The smoke left him in a gray stream and curled once round Senna and once round Hiromi and once round the fox-woman on the step, and set, cured into three thin shells of skein a hand's breadth off their skins, so fine that the sun came through them gray-gold, and Naiser spoke through it, low.

"Nothing inside this smoke sleeps."

Kemuri no Han, the Smoke Verdict. An aerosol is a tracer: blow smoke into a draft and you can see where the air is going. The Binding Flame cures that tracer into a standing record, and the record holds the sentence spoken into it, and the sentence binds whatever stands inside until the smoke clears. The drowse came on against the shells and found a law in its way. It ran round them like water round a stone. The Stage-gap weight of the Sin came on too and stopped at the skin of smoke, because the smoke had already been told what its air was for. Hiromi shook himself all over like a dog coming out of a river. Senna's shoulders came down a finger's breadth. On the step Dari sneezed. The cost was the sentence itself: it bound them, and it would hold until the last wisp of the skein came apart, and none of the three of them would sleep easily tonight. Naiser had decided that was a fair price before he paid it.

The goblin was scratching the back of his head with one claw. The bottle-green eyes, half shut under heavy brows, slid over the carriage, over the crest on its door, over the man in gray standing on the clay of the road with the smoke round his children, and Naiser watched them decide, slowly, that he was a disappointment.

"You're not who I was lookin' for."

He leaned on the empty air on one elbow and squinted. A pierced ear twitched.

"You don't look like Senri, but you share his essence." The eyes opened a third of the way, which on that face was a great deal. "Though you must be *HIS SON!*" He flung out an arm, and the gesture went slowly through his own glass like a man's arm through a heavy curtain, and he did not mind. "No matter! The SINS of the father shall fall onto the SON!"

Naiser looked at him with a peculiar look and shrugged.

Senri Yukari had gone into a seam in the Soul Plane the winter Naiser turned nineteen, and the road he had walked down into the dark in Ashura's wound was not one anybody came back up. If his father had killed goblins, he had killed them before Naiser was old enough to be told. It was very like him to have left the debt lying in a ditch somewhere for a lazy creditor to trip on. It was very like the creditor to have taken thirty years to bend down and pick it up.

"You won't be fighting me anyway." He drew on the leaf and let the smoke out sideways, where it stood in the air beside his face and did not move. "I'll let my daughter and her training partner Hiromi handle you."

Vaashuuk looked at him.

He felt DISRESPECTED. Naiser could see him feel it. The heavy lids lifted all the way for the first time, and the long ears went straight back against the hood with a little shiver of all their rings, *chirin*, and one lacquered claw went to the crest and touched it twice, quickly, in a way that had nothing lazy in it. The lavender shimmer round the goblin thickened. The droplets hanging in it, a slow halo of them, all turned at once toward the carriage like a crowd turning to stare.

"WHO THE HELL DO YOU THINK YOU ARE, RELEGATING ME TO FIGHTING CHILDREN!?" His voice cracked at the top. "I WILL SQUASH THEM!"

*Will*, thought Naiser. He had not said *squashed*. He never would.

Hiromi looked at the ugly goblin.

He had come down off the carriage step with his hands in fists and the smoke shell round him gray-gold in the sun, and now he stood on the clay of the road with his feet apart and his chin out and his eyes alight, and Naiser saw on his face exactly what Cozbi Mahuo must have seen there every day for three years: a seed that had decided it would be a flower and did not care how long the winter took.

"WHO ARE YOU GONNA SQUASH, *GREENIE!?*"

Hiromi rushed at him.

He went off the mark like a hare out of a furrow, three steps on the hard clay of the road and then up onto the grass dike between the two paddies, and as he ran he flung his right arm out ahead of him with the palm open and down, the way his uncle had in the yard at Sum-gol, and summoned.

Naiser watched it the way a lord watches another house's work: closely, and with a professional's pleasure. A Mahuo Founding is a building more than a calling. The air in front of the running boy took a breath, and the space between him and the Sin went strange: a boundary unfolding out of nothing above the dike, a little Realm the size of a barn door, the light at its edge bending toward it and away at once. The taste of struck flint came down the road. Then the core, a bead of the boy's own Crystal pressed into the middle of the Realm the way a potter presses a thumb into clay to start the hollow, and the sound of it was a heartbeat from the wrong side of a door, *kung... kung...*, and under the heartbeat the fine crystal ticking of Eidolyn laying down the shape, *ttak-ttak-ttak-ttak*, the Shaping Mind growing a living thing the way frost grows ferns across a window: water freezes most readily onto ice already there, so every crystal becomes the pattern for the next one laid against it, and Eidolyn is that law with an intent for the pattern. Whatever the boy held clear in his head would be laid down as real bone and feather. Whatever he held badly would come out flawed in exactly the shape of his forgetting.

Hiromi held it very clearly. It was a kingfisher.

It came up out of the Realm the size of a pony and filled the space between the two of them, blue, the blue of the paddy sky, a back of shot-silk cobalt and a breast of hot rust-orange and a long black dagger of a beak a full arm's length from the face to the point. Its eye was black and bright. One of its wings came out a hand longer than the other, where the boy's memory of the bird had been surer of one side than the other, and the long wing's primaries were clumsy and too many. The Founding drew from him hard. Naiser saw it go: his aura, which ran a clear wet green like new rice with the black-violet seam of Eidolyn opening under it, flared bright and then thinned, and the boy's lips went white at the corners and his nose began to bleed, a thin line, before he had even reached the Sin. It was a big bite of what he had. He was a child, and he did not care.

The bird folded its wings flat to its sides and lanced out forward in a streamline path, the beak and the head and the whole long body making one single sharp line from the point back to the tail, the shape of a thing that was made by the world to go from thin air into thick water without a splash.

Senna simply shook her head in disappointment.

Then she was rushing in at the opponent too, down the clay and onto the next dike over, and pulling out the kunai that Naevra had made her.

## II · The Glass

*The same causeway, the same breath*

Senna came off the road onto the dike at a run and drew both kunai on the second stride.

Aunt Naevra had made them for her on her ninth name-day, in the forge shed behind the shrine at the Enclave, and they were the plainest things the Yukari owned: two leaf-shaped blades of folded gray steel each the length of her hand, ground hollow on both faces so the light ran down the middle of them in a bright line like water down a gutter, with black cedar handles wrapped in the same gray cord as her hair-tie and a ring at each pommel for her fingers. On the flat of each, near the handle, the Yukari crow, small, struck in with a punch. *They don't do anything*, Naevra had said, giving them to her. *Except be exactly where you put them. That's more than most things.*

She put her forefingers through the rings and the blades lay back along her wrists and she ran.

The third stride was harder than the second. The fourth was harder still. It was like running into the sea: on the first step the water is nothing, at the knee it drags, at the waist it holds you, and the harder you throw yourself at it the harder it throws you back. The air did that. It thickened round her shins and her knees and then her whole body, the faster she pushed, until she was flinging herself against it and moving at a walk, and her breath came in through her nose slow and heavy as warm milk through a cloth, and the smoke shell her father had set round her moved with her a hand off her skin, untroubled. Her braid swung out behind her and stayed swung. She stopped.

She stopped on the dike at the edge of the paddy with her feet set in the packed grass, both kunai low, twenty paces from the Sin, and she watched.

She would watch how the first part of this fight would unfold. Her father always said the first exchange was the expensive one, and that you could buy it for nothing by letting somebody else pay.

Hiromi was paying.

The kingfisher went in at the Sin like a thrown spear. Hiromi had thrown it with his whole body: his right foot planted on the dike and his hip turned through and his shoulder and arm flung forward after it as if he were skimming a stone, and the bird went down that line with its wings folded and its beak at the end of its neck like the head of a lance, straight at the goblin's face where he lay on his nothing above the water.

Vaashuuk opened one eye, and that was all of him that moved.

The bird slowed.

Senna watched it slow, and her skin went cold. The beak found nothing to strike, only the air nearer the goblin, and that air was thicker than the air she stood in, thicker every pace, and the faster the bird came at it the harder it pushed back, so that the beak's point, which had been crossing the paddy faster than she could follow, was crossing it at a gallop, and then at a trot, and then at a walk, the black dagger slowing and slowing until it came to a stop a hand's breadth from the end of the goblin's nose and hung there, quivering, and the bird hung behind it with its wings pinned to its sides and its black eye bright and furious.

The force had to go somewhere. Senna had spent her whole childhood in the Yukari yard being told so: *the blow you do not land lands somewhere else.* It went into the bird. The glass in front of the beak had met it the way a wall meets a hammer, and everything that had been thrown down the line of that long neck went back up it, and the kingfisher's neck folded on itself in a short sharp S with a sound like a green branch stepped on in snow, *pakiri*, and the keel of its breastbone, the deep blade a bird's flight muscles hang from, cracked down the middle under the push of its own body coming on behind. A puff of rust-orange breast feathers came loose and hung in the air round it like sparks that had stopped. It made no cry. Hiromi did. He yelled, out on his dike, a raw shout of surprise with his hand clapped to his own chest, and she understood, because the bird was him, a little: what struck the form reached its founder as a change in himself.

Vaashuuk lifted one lacquered claw and flicked the beak.

*Tink.*

The beak turned aside, slowly, a quarter turn, like a weathervane in a soft wind, and the bird hung there sideways in the glass, broken, alive, with its wings pinned and its eye on him.

"Ouuuuu," said Vaashuuk. He stretched, from his ankles to his ears, like a cat on a warm wall. "Lemme tell ya somethin', handsome, 'cause watchin' you two figure it out's the closest thing I got to a hobby. Everythin's a liquid if you give it long enough. I just make it real, *real* thick." He rolled onto his side on his nothing and propped his head on his hand. "An' it's a funny kind of thick. Ya ever stir cornflour inta water? Push it slow an' it's soup. Punch it an' it's a brick." He wagged the claw at the hanging bird. "Faster ya come, thicker I get. That's the whole trick. Exhaustin', explainin' it. I'm gonna need a nap after."

Tar Time. Senna had read the name on a page in her father's study and not believed it. She believed it now. The Sin had yawned before the carriage ever came round the bend; that was the yawn that had come in through the windows and into all their jaws. When a liquid cools toward glass it does not freeze the way water freezes, all at once into crystal; it simply goes on thickening until it stops flowing for any purpose that matters, and the goblin was doing that to the air for fifty paces round himself, and the air he had done it to was the kind of thick that stiffens under a blow. Cornflour in water. The Stannvaard pitch. She looked at the stillness of the rice-mist hanging over the paddy in white gauze, every thread of it fixed. She looked at the horses' breath, which had come out of their nostrils in two gray plumes and stayed there in front of their noses like wool. The steam had stopped rising off the warm paddy water. Nothing in the whole valley was moving faster than a walk.

Except the Sin. He moved in it at his ordinary amble. He rolled and stretched and flicked as if the air were air. Whatever he had done to it, he had made himself one with it, the way a fish is one with the water and feels no current in a river that carries it.

*Faster you come, thicker I get.*

She thought of the stone.

Hiromi came again before she could finish thinking it.

He came along the dike at a sprint with his head down and blood on his lip from his nose, faster than before, furious, every stride of it fought, his boots punching into the grass of the dike and throwing up clods that rose into the air and hung behind him like thrown loaves. He came in on the goblin's left, the side away from the bird, low, aiming to get under the floating chair and come up through it, and Senna saw what he meant to do and saw, at the same moment, the claw.

Vaashuuk had seen him coming the whole way. Of course he had. Inside his glass, a boy at a sprint was a boy in slow motion, and the Sin had time to watch every stride arrive, yawn twice, and think about something else. He lifted his left hand off his knee, lazily, palm out, as if he were waving off a waiter, and the air between the sprinting boy and the floating chair went from thick to stopped. It went glossy. A pane of it, a yard across, set in the space in front of Hiromi's face like a window with no frame, clear as water, and the afternoon light caught on its edges and ran round them in a thin bright line.

Hiromi ran into it face first.

He ran into it with everything he had brought: his left foot driving off the clay of the dike and his weight coming over it and his head down and forward like a goat's, and the pane met his brow and the bridge of his nose at exactly the speed he brought it, because a thing that thickens under a blow is hardest to the hardest blow, and that was the hardest blow he had. His forehead stopped. The rest of him went on for a moment, and folded up behind it. The little bones of his nose, the two thin plates that make the bridge, broke inward against the glass, and the gristle of the septum behind them buckled and slid to the left, and the skin of his left brow split over the bony ridge above the eye in a short deep curve down to the white of the bone, a cut like a mouth. The sound of it was a wet crunch, *gushatt*, and then a clean ringing note from the pane, struck, like a finger flicking a wine glass.

The blood came out of him slowly.

That was the thing she would remember. It came out of his nose and his split brow in the ordinary way, the bright red of a child's blood in the sun, and then the thick air took it, and it went out from his face in a string of beads, red, round, each one hanging separate from the last, a necklace of them drifting out from his face toward the water and hanging there in the afternoon light, glittering, as slow as the mist. He sat down. He sat down hard on the edge of the dike with his boots in the paddy and both hands over his face and the beads of his blood going out between his fingers, and he said something into his palms in Sum-gol Korean that Senna's tutor had never taught her and that she was entirely sure was a curse.

The pane rang on behind him, dying, *hmmmmmmm*, slow as a gong in a cellar. That was the Sin's sound. It had been under everything since the carriage rounded the bend: a long jaw-cracking yawn, and then a low hum like a pane of glass ringing very, very slowly.

"Sorry, sorry." Vaashuuk did not sound sorry. He rolled onto his back again and crossed his ankles. "I don't hit people. Hittin' people's work. I just leave stuff lyin' around an' let people run inta it." He yawned. "Squashin' happens on its own, if ya wait."

*He does not hit people.* Senna put it away the way her father put away a sentence: carefully, where she could find it again. *He makes the world hard, and lets you hit it.*

She looked at the glass sphere at his shoulder, turning slowly in its brass bands with its curl of frozen steam. She looked at her father's smoke, still hanging in its gray skein over the road where the goblin's vow had gone through it, and still not moved. *He says things he does not mean to finish*, she thought, *and the smoke cannot find the end of them.* She put that away too, unpriced.

She knew what the stone was worth.

Her father had taught it to her in the yard at the Enclave on the cold mornings, the two of them standing on the flat river-stones by the stream in their bare feet, not moving, for an hour. *Everything that goes fast is in a hurry*, he had said, *and everything in a hurry is making a mistake somewhere. Be the stone. The stone is never in a hurry, and the river goes round it.* She had thought it was a lesson about patience. She understood, standing on the dike with the blood of the boy beside her hanging in the air in beads, that it had been a lesson about this.

She let her breath go down.

She let it go down into the Yukari count her father kept in himself, the long one, in for six and hold and out for six and hold, and her heart came down after it the way a ball comes down a stair. She could not do what her father did with his two bloods. She had only the one count. But she could slow herself to it, and she did, and her aura came up round her inside the smoke shell, which she had not meant it to, a soft wisteria violet like her hair with a gray stone-color under it, and it lay along her skin as still as paint, and the sound it made was a low hush like a river going round a rock, *sharararara*, very faint. The Stone Count, Naevra called it, the name the children used. It cost her little. It cost her speed, all of it, which was the point.

Then she walked into the glass.

She walked very slowly. She walked the way the old women walked to the shrine at the Enclave at the year's turn carrying the lamps, one foot set down and the weight brought onto it and the next foot lifted, and the glass let her in. It was the cornflour. Push it slow and it's soup. Any thick stuff of that kind has a pace under which it lets you through and over which it stiffens, and she found that pace by feel the way you find the floor of a cold river with your toes, and kept her body under it, and the air flowed round her like heavy water round a wading heron and did not hold her. She stepped down off the dike into the paddy. The water came to her shins, thick as cooling honey, and the new rice parted round her shins with a sound like silk drawn across silk. She walked. Ten paces off. Nine.

She felt the cost come on as she went. It came on in her chest first. Her heart was slow and the Sin's air was slowing the rest of her too, the small quick salts that carry a message down a nerve from the brain to the hand, so that she would tell her foot to lift and the foot would hear about it a moment late, and her breath came heavy and slow through her nose, and at the edges of her eyes a soft gray began to come in like dusk. Her lips went cold. She counted under her breath. *In, two, three. Hold. Out.*

Eight paces.

Vaashuuk opened both eyes and looked at her.

"Ouuuuu." He sat up on his nothing. The lavender shimmer round him moved, and the halo of hanging droplets turned toward her all at once. "Smart one. Smart *an'* pretty, look at that. Ya get that from yer daddy?" He smiled. One fang was capped with gold, and an iron spike went through his lip below it. "Thing is, princess, slow's slow. I been slow my whole life. I'm *real* good at it." He leaned on his elbow, and the floating chair under him drifted backward over the water exactly as fast as she was coming forward, at his ordinary amble, without his lifting a finger. Eight paces. Eight paces. Eight paces. "Ya can walk at me all day. I'll walk away all day. I got all day. Ya heard me say so."

He looked past her, at the dike.

"Or," he said.

He lifted his left hand and pointed one lacquered claw at Hiromi, who was trying to stand up on the dike with his face full of blood, and closed the claw slowly into his palm, like a man pinching out a candle.

Hiromi stopped breathing.

Senna saw it from eight paces, through the gray coming in at the edges of her eyes. The boy had been dragging air in through his mouth, past the broken nose, in big ragged heaves, and the air in front of his mouth went glossy the way the pane had, not all the way to a wall, only thick, thicker, thick as tar, and the next heave of his chest pulled and pulled at it and brought in nothing at all. Air goes into a chest down a long branching tube, and the harder it is to push a thick thing down a pipe the more of a man's strength it costs to breathe it; the goblin had made the air in front of the boy's mouth so thick that his whole chest, heaving with every muscle it had, could not draw it down. Hiromi's hands went to his throat. His lips, which had been white, went gray. His heels drummed on the dike, slowly, in the slow air.

"*Hiromi-sama!*"

That was Dari. Senna saw her at the edge of her eye, off the carriage step and halfway to the paddy in one long white rush with all nine tails flared out behind her like a fan of white knives, and saw her father's hand come down on the fox-woman's shoulder from behind, lightly, and stop her as dead as if she had run into a cedar. *Not yet*, he said. Senna could only see it, in the set of his mouth.

"There ya go," said Vaashuuk. He sounded pleased with himself, and tired. "Ya can walk slow, princess, or ya can save yer little friend. Can't do both." He yawned again, and the yawn went through her jaw too, through the smoke and all. "Go on. Hurry."

He wanted her to hurry. She saw it as clearly as if he had written it in the air. The instant she hurried, the glass would take her the way it had taken the bird and the boy, and she would be one more thing hanging in his paddy with her own blood in beads round her face.

She did not hurry. She did not go back either. She stood in the paddy water in the Stone Count with her kunai low and her lips gone cold and the gray coming in, and she could not think what to do, and she hated it.

Hiromi solved it himself.

Out on the dike, gray-lipped and drumming his heels, the boy turned his head and looked at his bird.

It was still hanging in the glass where the goblin had flicked it, sideways, broken-necked, its keel cracked and its long wing pinned. It was still alive because he was. Senna saw his right hand come off his throat and close into a fist, and saw the clear wet green of his aura flare once round him, bright, almost white, with the black-violet seam of Eidolyn ripping open under it like a tear in a sleeve, and the founded heartbeat came again under everything, *kung... kung...*, harder, and in the glass the kingfisher began to beat its wings.

It could not fly. It did not try to fly. It hung where it was in the thickened air and beat its wings in place, both of them, the true one and the clumsy one, as hard and as fast as they would go, and the glass fought every stroke, stiffening under the speed, and the bird fought it harder, and the sound of the fight was a low thrumming roar like a mill-wheel in a flood, *burrrrrrrrrr*.

The air round the bird began to shimmer.

Senna had stirred a pot of starch-glue at the Enclave in the winter, for the paper screens, and her arm had ached, and the glue had grown warm in the pot under the paddle before the fire ever reached it. Stir a thick thing hard enough and the stirring itself becomes heat; the thicker the stuff, the more heat every stroke leaves in it. A blacksmith knows it, who hammers a cold bar and feels it warm in the tongs. And glass softens as it warms. The goblin had said so without meaning to: a liquid going toward glass thins out fast the moment it is heated, the way cold honey thins in a warm spoon. So every stroke of the bird's wings heated the glass round it, and the heated glass thinned, and the thinned glass let the wings beat faster, and the faster wings heated it more, and the heat ran away from the bird in a line along the path of least resistance, the way a crack runs, the way a fire runs along a fuse. A thread of shimmer bored out from the bird across the paddy. It went to Hiromi's mouth first, because he wanted it to; it crossed the three paces to his face in a wavering tunnel of hot light like the air over a summer road, and the boy's chest heaved, and the hot thin air went down into him in one enormous, whooping gulp, *hhhuaaaaagh*, and his lips went from gray to blue to red.

It cost him. Senna saw what it cost. The bird's breast feathers were smoking where they beat; the founded flesh under them was cooking in its own effort, and every feather that blackened on the bird blackened a little in the boy, so that his own face flushed scarlet and a sweat broke out across his split brow, and the blood came out of his broken nose faster in the thinned air, running now, no longer hanging. His right arm shook from the shoulder. He did not let go.

Then the thread of shimmer turned, and went toward the Sin.

"Huh," said Vaashuuk.

He watched it come. He was slow to watch it; the Sin was slow to everything, the slowest she had ever seen a thing be that was not asleep, and the thread was crossing the paddy at its own pace, and he watched it the way a cat watches a beetle it has decided not to bother with, and then the thread reached the edge of the floating chair under him and the chair sagged.

It sagged like toffee left in the sun. The slab of glassed air he lay on had been set by the same trick as all the rest of it, and it could hold his weight and very little else, and any real heat made it go soft. The near end of it drooped. A long clear drip of it, thick as syrup, came down off the edge toward the paddy and hung there. The goblin slid a hand's breadth down his own bed toward the water, his elbow skidding.

"Hey," he said. "*Hey.* That's my *chair.*"

"SENNA! THE HOT BIT!"

She saw it the instant he said it.

The thread was thin glass. Inside it, the air was only air. Inside it, fast was fast.

She stepped out of the Stone Count and into the shimmer, and let her heart go.

It went up like a bird flushed out of rice. Her breath came up after it and the gray went out of her eyes all at once and the color came flooding back into the world, the blue of the paddy sky and the green of the rice and the rust of the bird's smoking breast, and the warm thin air in the thread took her like a gust takes a kite. She ran. She ran up the tunnel of hot air on the surface of the paddy with her bare speed, her left foot driving off the soft mud bottom under the honey-thick water and her right coming down on the far dike's edge three strides on and driving her up off it into the air, both kunai up and back along her wrists, straight up the line of shimmer at the sagging chair a man's height over the water and the goblin on it with his bottle-green eyes going wide.

Vaashuuk was slow to wake. She had known he would be. It was in every line of him, the slouch and the yawns and the way he had watched a beetle cross a paddy. He saw her coming the way a man wakes in the night to a sound and lies there a moment not knowing what it was. Then he knew, and his left hand came up off his knee, palm out, the same lazy waiter's wave that had stopped Hiromi on the dike, to set a pane in front of her face.

He set it in the hot air of the thread, and it was soft, and she went through it like a hand through cold porridge.

Her right kunai met the back of his raised hand.

She cut it from the outside in, a short flat drawing stroke from her right shoulder across her own body, the blade led by the cutting edge and her wrist rolling over at the end of it the way Naevra had made her practice on hanging paper for a whole winter, and the hollow-ground steel went across the back of the goblin's hand over the knuckles where the green skin went to polished black, and the black skin parted like the skin on boiled milk. Under it the cords that open the hand ran in their white sheaths over the knuckle-bones, the long tendons that pull the fingers straight, and the edge parted two of them, the ones to the first and second fingers, cleanly, *put, put*, and the two fingers fell closed into his palm on their own as if they had been cut on strings. The blood came out of him dark, nearly black-red, like plum wine, and slow, sluggish, with so little push behind it that it welled in the cut and hung in a fat dark bead, the way blood comes from a creature whose heart beats like a sleeping dormouse's.

Her left kunai came round after the right on the backswing.

She did not plan it. Her hips turned through after the first cut and brought the left hand round at the height of his head, and he jerked his head back from it, slowly, a lazy man's flinch, and the point did not reach his eye; it went past his cheek and into the long pierced ear, through the first gold ring at the top of it, and caught there, and her hand went on round, and the ring came with it. It came out through the gristle of the ear's rim. The cartilage at the top of the ear tore in a ragged notch the size of her thumbnail, *bichi*, and the gold ring went spinning off the end of her kunai into the air with a scrap of green skin still in it and hung there, slowly turning, catching the sun.

She landed on the drooping end of his chair on her right knee and her left foot, both blades out to the sides, a hand from his face.

Vaashuuk put his good hand to his ear.

He touched the torn place with one lacquered claw, very gently, and took the claw away and looked at the dark blood on it. Then he looked at his other hand, at the two fingers lying closed in his palm that would not open when he told them to. Then he looked at her.

Something happened in the air.

She felt it go through the whole paddy like the shiver that goes through a horse's skin when a fly lands. The lavender shimmer round the goblin, which had been as still as a bath, rippled out from him in a slow ring, and every hanging thing in the glass moved with it: the rice-mist shivered in its rags, the beads of Hiromi's blood swung together like a chime in a draft, the stopped plumes of the horses' breath bent, and far off along the dikes the frogs that had been stopped mid-croak let out one single, simultaneous, strangled note, *gekk*, and stopped again. It went down into the water. The paddy round them dimpled in a thousand little rings at once, as if a shower of rain had begun, and then lay still.

"That's my *good* ear," said Vaashuuk.

He said it quietly. The drawl had gone thin. The long vowels had shortened, and something in the bottle-green eyes had come up from very deep down, the look of a creature that had been sleeping on top of something for ninety years and had just been poked through the mattress.

"Nobody..." He touched the ear again. "Ya know how long it's been since somebody cut me when I was *awake?* Never. That's how long. Never's how long." He laughed, a high short sound with no breath behind it. "Ouuuuu, princess. Ouuuu, ya little..."

The sphere at his shoulder dimmed.

Senna saw it, and remembered it, and knew in the same instant that she had seen it before the fight ever started and not thought about it: the riveted glass apple in its brass bands, turning by his ear with its curl of steam frozen inside. It went dark, the way a lamp goes dark when the oil runs out, and dropped.

It fell past his shoulder toward the paddy. It did not reach the water.

A fresh field opened out of it with no yawn and no warning. It was full and cold and whole, a night's sleep's worth of glass packed into an apple of brass and let go all at once, and it came out from the falling sphere like ice over a pond in a single breath, and the hot thread that Hiromi's bird had bored through the old glass went out like a candle in a bucket. The thin air round Senna was gone. She was at full speed. She had come up the thread at her bare speed and landed on the chair and turned her hips into a third cut at the goblin's throat, already, before she saw the sphere dim; her right arm was already going forward with the blade, all of her weight behind it, and the new glass met her at exactly the speed she brought.

It took her at the right shoulder.

The arm stopped. The rest of her did not. Her body went on forward over the stopped arm a finger's length, and the round head of the bone of her upper arm, which sits in the shallow cup of the shoulder blade the way a ball sits in a saucer held by a ring of gristle and the tight cords round it, levered out of its saucer forward and down, tearing the gristle lip at the front of the cup as it went. She heard it inside her own body more than outside: a deep, wet pop like a cork drawn out of a full jar, *gopon*. The pain came after it, white. The arm hung from her shoulder at a wrong long angle, and the kunai was still on her finger, its point a finger's width from the goblin's throat, and it stayed there, because everything stayed there.

She was held. The glass had her. She hung in it on her knee on the end of the drooping chair with her dislocated arm thrown out in front of her and her other hand back and her braid out behind her in the air, like a fly in amber, like the dragonfly, like the bird.

She did not scream. Her father's daughter did not scream. Her breath went in through her teeth in one long hiss like water on a hot stone, *shhhhhhhhh*, and stayed there, because her chest could not move fast enough to let it out.

"There," said Vaashuuk. He breathed out. He looked down at the point of her kunai, a finger from his throat, and moved his head a little to the side away from it, slowly, at his amble, and nothing in the glass prevented him. "That's the one charge. Ya see? Ya made me use the one charge." He sounded hurt about it. "I gotta sleep a whole night ta fill that. A *whole* night."

He sat up on the sagging chair. His torn ear dripped dark beads that hung beside his face. He looked at her a long time from very close, with his crest of oiled black hair fallen across his brow and his gold fang showing.

"Now," he said. "I'm gonna squash..."

He laid his good hand flat in the air in front of her chest, palm toward her, an inch off her collarbones, and the glass under it began to set. She felt it begin. She felt the air in front of her breastbone go from thick to thicker, from honey to pitch, from pitch to stone, a slab of it forming against her chest that would press, and press, and stop her ribs from rising at all.

It stopped.

It stopped halfway. The slab stood half-set in front of her, thick, not stone, and stayed that way, and Vaashuuk frowned at it. He pushed his hand forward again an inch and the setting began again, and went a little further, and stopped again, and he frowned harder, the way a man frowns at a word on the tip of his tongue that will not come. He looked at his own hand as if it had stopped belonging to him.

*He says things he does not mean to finish.* Senna hung in the glass with her shoulder screaming and the gray coming back at the edges of her eyes, and the thought came up through all of it clear and bright, like a fish rising. *And the smoke could not find the end of them because there is no end. He does not finish. He cannot.* The goblin had told her so himself without knowing: *squashing happens on its own, if you wait.* On its own. He left things lying for other people to run into, so that nobody could ever say he had done it.

She could not move her mouth fast enough to speak. She looked at him instead, and let him see on her face that she knew.

Vaashuuk saw. Something went across his face like a cloud across a paddy. He opened his mouth.

## III · The Hierarchy

*The causeway, the same afternoon; the glass still standing over the paddies*

Naiser had his hand on the fox-woman's shoulder and his eyes on his daughter's lips.

They were going blue. He could see it from the road, a hundred paces off, through the lavender haze of the fresh glass: Senna hung in it on one knee on the drooping end of the goblin's chair with her right arm thrown out in front of her at the wrong long angle, the kunai still on her forefinger a finger's width from the Sin's throat, her braid out behind her in the air like a pennant in a gale that had stopped, and in front of her breastbone the half-set slab of air the Sin had begun and could not finish stood thick and glossy, pressing. Her chest was not rising. It could not rise fast enough. Under his palm Dari's shoulder was shaking like a bowstring, all nine tails gone rigid behind her, and he could feel her charter pulling at her: *you'll put yourself between him and anything that means him harm.* She was looking at the boy. Naiser was looking at the girl.

He did the count. The Lambert half of him had already done it, and gave it to him the way it always gave him things, as a figure set down in a clean hand: twelve more beats of her heart at the pace the glass held it, and then the gray would come in from the edges of her eyes and stay. He set his right foot. The two notes under his breastbone drifted a hair closer together. A line of dimmed air would go across the causeway in front of the Sin's chair, and the Corridor would stand up out of the paddy on its pillars, and the lesson would be over.

Then the threads moved.

He saw it with the Yukari half, Itomi, the sight that holds: every current of Aether in a place laid out for him as a line, the way a weaver sees the warp through the weft. To that sight a space with power in it is strung. The threads run through it from every source to every working, crossing and knotting, and they are the reason any invocation can happen at all; you call, and the threads carry the call, and whatever answers comes down them. Over the paddy every thread ran to the Sin. They had run to him since the carriage came round the bend, the whole valley's weave drawn in toward the small slouching figure on his chair the way the threads of a spider's web all run to the hub where the spider sits.

Now, one by one, they were turning.

They turned toward the dike. Toward the boy sitting in the grass with his boots in the paddy and his face a mask of his own blood. They turned the way iron filings on a sheet of paper turn when a second magnet is drawn underneath, slowly at first and then in whole patches at once, every little region of them snapping round together to point the new way, and the boundaries between the patches that had turned and the patches that had not crawling across the paddy like the edge of a shadow. Naiser had seen that once in a Stannvaard natural philosopher's lecture room: a bar of soft iron is full of small regions each already pointing some way of its own, and a strong enough field does not make new ones, it only makes the existing ones choose, and the regions that agree with it swallow their neighbors. It was the authority of the space. The hierarchy of it. Who the place answered to was being restructured, thread by thread, and a child was doing it.

He took his foot back.

Hiromi had his eyes shut. His right fist was closed on his knee and the clear wet green of his aura had gone in round him, close to the skin, the way a candle flame goes small and blue just before a draft takes it, and through the olive wool of his coat, through his shirt and the skin and the cage of his small ribs, Naiser could see the Crystal under his breastbone. It was a little thing. A Mahuo boy's first Crystal, raw as a creek pebble. And round it a hot flame of spiritual fire had wrapped itself, white at the root and gold above, close against the stone the way the flame of a lamp clings round its wick.

It began to bend outward.

It went slowly. It came out from the Crystal through the boy's chest in a line of white-gold fire and then turned, sharp, and ran back across itself, and turned again, sharp, and ran on: a zig-zag, a slow one, three strokes, top and slant and foot, shaped like the letter Z laid on its side in the air in front of him. It crackled. The sound of it was green bamboo in a bonfire, *papapapapa*, every node bursting in its turn. The smell that came off it was scorched rice-straw, sweet and sharp. And the boy was paying for it out of a purse that had no business being open: his Shell was a child's, coarse as a sieve, and most of what he drew through it came off him as plain heat before it ever reached the fire, so that the grass of the dike under his boots went brown in a ring and then black, the paddy water round his ankles began to steam and then to tick with small bubbles, and the hair on his head lifted off his scalp in the updraft and began, at the ends, to smoke.

Hiromi focused his intent forward.

He leaned into it with his whole small body, the way a boy leans into a door that is stuck, and the Z went out from him across the paddy toward the Sin and ripped through the slow, concentrated places in the glass.

Naiser watched the air come back to life along it.

Heat is only the speed of the smallest pieces of a thing. Every grain of air is always moving, knocking about against its neighbors like dice in a cup shaken hard, and the faster they knock the hotter the air, and the hotter the air the less any one of them can be held still. That was why the Sin's glass feared fire. He had slowed the passage of everything through the valley and left the shaking of the grains alone where they stood, and the shaking was what melts glass. The Z went into the thickest pockets of his air like a hot wire into wax and the molecules there were forced back to a faster pace, all at once, kicked back up into their old quick knocking. The glass along the Z came apart. The rice-mist caught in the path whirled up in a spinning white rag and blew away as if a door had been flung open on it. The dragonfly, still pinned in the air beside the carriage for all this while, was in the edge of the Z's heat, and its wings caught and went, *bzzt*, and it shot away down the road like a flung blue nail. And the Z ran on. It ran the way lightning runs down out of a cloud, in short sharp strokes that each feel for the weakest place ahead before they jump, which is why lightning forks and kinks and is never once straight. Wherever the glass was most crowded, the Z found it, and burned it back to air.

Naiser felt his own mouth go dry.

He asked himself what this was. This power. This control of the hierarchies. The Lambert count gave him nothing; it had no prior for it. The Yukari record gave him the oldest name it had. It was like the power of Irath. Irath, the Archon of Will, seventh of the Fourteen, the crowned idea of command and sovereignty and imposed order, the power that builds thrones, whose fire the old Imperaris schools said burned in any soul strong enough that a space would rearrange itself to obey it. It was like the power of his great-great-grandfather Malvin Yukari, who was said in the record to have been the Paragon of Dominion: who had walked into the hall of a hostile house one winter of the Long Quarrel with no sword and no escort and sat down in its lord's chair, and the hall had simply begun to answer him instead, the lamps and the guards and the very floor, until the lord himself had stood up from the bench and asked his leave to speak. Naiser had read that page at twelve and thought it a family boast.

*How interesting...*

Cozbi had hidden this from him. Every term of the eleven, entered in the Yukari record in the Mahuo's own olive ink, and not one of them had said *the boy can take a room away from whoever holds it.* Naiser turned that over and found he was not angry. Cozbi Mahuo did nothing by accident. He had likely wanted Naiser to see the boy's potential on his own, and not on any man's say-so, and he had paid a carriage for the privilege of knowing exactly how Naiser's face would look when he did.

On the dike Hiromi opened his eyes.

"No..."

The Z had burned through the smoke shell Naiser had cured round him. Naiser saw it go: the fine gray skein charred black where the white-gold fire crossed it and fell off the boy in flakes like burned paper, and the instant it was gone the Sin's pressure came down onto him unbuffered. Two Stages and more between them, and the weight of a soul at Realization's clarity sitting fifty paces off: Naiser watched it hit the boy like the first wave of a flood hitting a man standing in a dry riverbed. It hit him in the chest. It collided with his whole small body and drove his shoulders back and his chin up, and his knees bent under it, and the blood came out of his nose in a fresh rush and ran down over his lip, and the grass around him lay down flat in a circle as if a great hand had been pressed onto it.

"You won't just slow things down..." Hiromi got one foot under him. "I won't allow it..."

He was of a lower Stage. Naiser could see exactly how much lower; the threads told him, and the threads did not lie about Stage. By every law Naiser knew of pressure between practitioners, the boy should have been on his face in the paddy, and somehow he was capable of pushing back against it. The pressure came down on him and he pushed back, and the line between the two of them stood in the air over the paddy like the line where a river meets the sea, churning, and did not move toward him.

Hiromi put his hands together.

He put them palm to palm in front of his chest, fingers up, the way the Sum-gol monks put their hands together at the shrine, and then unfolded them in sequence: the little fingers parting first and then the ring fingers and then the middle, one pair after the next, like a fan being opened a rib at a time, until his two hands were open side by side in front of him with the palms up and empty.

"Summoning..."

Blood lolled from his mouth. It came over his lower lip in a slow dark rope and hung off his chin in the slow air and swung there.

"Magic..."

In the glass, the kingfisher moved.

It was still hanging sideways where the Sin had flicked it, broken-necked, the keel of its breastbone cracked down the middle and its long clumsy wing pinned, and Naiser saw the black-violet seam of Eidolyn open along its back like a tear running down a silk sleeve. A hand came out of the tear.

It was a bird's hand, if a bird had hands: long and fine and blue-black, four slender fingers and a thumb, grown out of the kingfisher's back the way a fern's first frond uncurls out of its stem. And out of its wrist, grown on exactly the same pattern, smaller and turned a little further round, came the second. And out of the second the third. A spiral of hands shifted out of the bird as it became the basis of a recursive draft, each hand drafted on the last and each a little rotated from the one before, so that they came out of the kingfisher's back in a turning coil like the seeds in the head of a sunflower, or the chambers of a nautilus shell laid out along a stair, and at the end of every one of them, where the long middle finger should have ended in a nail, the finger ended in a pike: a narrow blue-black spike as long as a man's forearm, glinting.

Naiser knew the craft. Every structured working has four parts: an authorization that names who is calling and by what right, a boundary, a direction, a seal. A Mahuo Draft writes those four into a circle on the ground or the air before the thing it calls can come through. The boy had written his first one into the body of the bird, and then, instead of drawing a second circle, he had let the first Draft write the next out of itself, the way frost uses the ice already there as the pattern for the next crystal laid against it. One Draft. Twelve answers to it. It cost a little more for every hand, and it would go on costing, because every hand was a mouth on the same small purse.

"Twelve Hands of Parun..."

The Hands of Parun. Naiser almost smiled. Parun is the root script of law, older than any kingdom, and the scholars of the Accord call each of the old ways of writing it a Hand, because a script is how a hand moves; there are fourteen Hands in the grammars. The boy had made twelve, and every one of them was a hand that wrote.

The first hand lanced outward toward the man's shoulder.

It went out from the top of the spiral the way a snake strikes from a coil, the long arm straightening out of its S in one stroke, the pike leading, aimed at the top of the Sin's left shoulder where the stiff collar of the olive cloak met the hood. Vaashuuk saw it come. He saw everything come, inside his glass. He lifted his good hand off his knee, palm out, the waiter's wave, and set a pane in front of the pike.

And the hand wrote.

At the very point of the pike, a finger's breadth from the pane, a glyph bloomed: a hooked, broken mark in burning white like a crack in a mirror, *Uur*, the Glyph of Disruption, the sign of Uurgath, who unmakes. It did not touch the pane. It did not need to. It disrupted the field around him. The pane shattered from the inside, the way ice on a pond shatters from the place a stone strikes it, white crazing running out through it from the glyph to every edge in an instant, *pishiiii*, and then it fell apart into glittering grains that hung in the air and went dull; and the crazing did not stop at the pane. It ran on out into the Sin's glass in every direction, the thickened air round his chair going milky with a thousand fine cracks like old crackle-glaze on a pot.

The pike went through after it.

It went into the top of the goblin's left shoulder above the collarbone, through the olive wool of the cloak and the black shirt under it and the skin and into the round cap of muscle that sits over the shoulder joint and lifts the arm, the deltoid, and through the meat of it a hand's depth, until the point struck the flat shelf of bone at the top of the shoulder blade, the acromion, that roofs the joint like an eave, and chipped it, *gachin*, and stopped. Vaashuuk's whole small body jerked in his chair. The blood came out round the pike dark and slow, plum-dark, sluggish, welling round the blue-black shaft and hanging off it in a fat bead.

"*Ouuuuuuuuch*," said Vaashuuk. It came out of him on a long high note that was nearly a yawn, and was not. "Ow. Ow, ow, *ow*. That's my *shoulder*. That's my shoulder an' my ear an' my hand an' my *chair*. Somebody... look, somebody *stop* this kid..."

Nobody stopped him. From the ashes of the disruption the next hands were already building.

The second hand and the third came down out of the spiral to either side of the first and wrote in the milky, cracked glass round the Sin's chair a second mark, and a third: *Th*, the first hands of Foundation, a plain square-shouldered glyph like the footing-stone of a house, in steady gold. Foundation does not break. It gathers. Where the Disruption had cracked the Sin's glass into a thousand loose pieces, the Foundation glyphs took the pieces and pulled them in, the slow aether round the chair drawn toward the gold marks in drifts the way snow drifts against a fence, and set them down in a new order; and over the Foundation the fourth hand wrote the last mark of the three, a crowned glyph in deep amber like a coal under glass, *Dom*. Dominion. Irath's own sign. The slow aether settled under it into a field of dominion that was the boy's.

Naiser watched the threads.

Every thread over the paddy that had run to the Sin, and turned, and been laid down again under the gold, now ran to the dike. The glass was still glass. It was still thick, still slow, still the stuff a dragonfly hangs in. But it answered the boy now. And the boy, sitting in his black ring of burned grass with his two hands open in front of him and the blood rope swinging off his chin, set about doing to it what a farmer does to a field he has taken: he hollowed it. Naiser saw him reach for each slowing tide in turn, every pocket where the glass was thickest and the Sin's hold had been strongest, and empty it, the way a man hollows a gourd, scooping the slow out of the middle and leaving a thin shell round nothing. Around Senna first. The glass round his daughter went from thick to hollow, and her chest rose. It rose all at once in one huge heave, *haaaaah*, and the color came back into her lips.

Senna focused her spiritual energy.

Naiser saw her do it and knew exactly what she was going to do before she did it, because he had taught her, and because he had watched her try it in the yard at the Enclave every evening for a year and fail. The Stone Count went out of her. Something else came up in its place. Her right arm hung out of its socket and the kunai slid off her dead finger and hung in the hollowed glass, turning. She did not look at it. She let her left hand come round from where it had been held back, and her hips turned on the end of the sagging chair, and her left foot set down on the soft drooping glass and pushed, and she threw.

She threw sidearm, wide, a flat whip of the left arm from the shoulder with the wrist snapping over at the end, the forefinger through the pommel ring sending the blade spinning round the ring as it left her, and the kunai went out across the paddy in a whirling gray disc, *hyuhyuhyuhyu*, far out to the right of the goblin, past his shoulder, past his ear, out over the water and the rice, wide.

Vaashuuk laughed. It was a high, cracked laugh with no breath in it, and blood in it, from the shoulder.

"*Missed*, princess."

He turned his face back toward the dike, toward the boy and the twelve hands and the gold marks standing in the cracked air round him, because the boy was the one taking his valley away and the girl was a girl with one arm who had missed. Naiser watched him decide it. He watched the bottle-green eyes slide off his daughter and onto the dike, and he watched his daughter watch them go.

The kunai came back like a boomerang.

It had gone out on its spin, and a spinning blade is never quite a thrown stone. The edge of the whirl that turns into the oncoming air meets it harder than the edge that turns away, and that difference pushes the spinning thing sideways, always to the same side, the way a spun ball curves in the air at the players' game in the Stannvaard courts, so that a thing thrown on a hard spin bends its path round, and round, in a long arc, and if it is thrown right, comes home. It curved through the air. It went out wide over the paddy, and bent, and swung round in a great flat loop, and came in from behind the goblin's right side; and it came trailed by a black-light.

Naiser had never seen it on her before.

It was a light that was the opposite of light. It ran out behind the spinning kunai along the whole curve of its path in a thread of black that was blacker than shadow, as if the air there had been cut open onto nothing, and where it passed the thickened glass did not crack or melt or soften. It simply was not glass anymore. Every wave has its mirror, crest for trough, and when a wave meets its own mirror the two lie down together into stillness; the boatmen on the Marrow know it, where two wakes meet and the water goes flat between them. Senna's null-light was that. Her manifested null-light ran along the Sin's working in antiphase, his own law answered exactly backward, and canceled it. The Yukari had a word in the record for the thing it came from, and Naiser's mother had had it, and he did not, and his daughter did. It cost her. He saw it in her: the fingers of her left hand, still hooked from the throw, had gone white to the second knuckle as if from frost, and her gray eyes had gone dark at the centers, the pupils blown wide, because a light that cancels light takes a little from every light near the one who makes it.

The kunai ripped through the air along its black thread at its full speed, through air that was only air now, and smashed through the waist of Vaashuuk.

It came in from behind and below on his right side, spinning edge-first, and took him across the flank above the hip bone, the soft place between the bottom rib and the crest of the pelvis where nothing guards a body but muscle. The hollow-ground edge went through the olive cloak and the black shirt and the green skin, and through the three thin sheets of muscle that wrap a belly's side one over another with their grains crossed, the way the plies of good plywood are crossed so the board will not split: the outermost running down and forward, the middle running up and forward, the innermost running round like a belt. It parted all three. It cut through to the thin shining lining that holds the gut in its place, and opened that too, a hand's length, so that for an instant behind the cut there was the pale wet gleam of coiled bowel, and then the kunai was out of him on the far side of its spin and still going, still curving, the black thread drawn behind it, home. The sound was a short tearing rip like a sail splitting in a squall, *zubaa*. The blood came out of the goblin's flank slow and dark and heavy, and the torn edges of the muscle bulged out of the cut under it.

Senna caught the kunai by its ring on her left forefinger as it came round, without looking, and it spun twice on her finger, *kurukuru*, and stopped.

Vaashuuk folded. He folded round the cut like a man round a kick, his good hand clapped to his flank, his knees drawing up on the chair, and the chair under him, cut through by the boy's Foundation and hollowed by the boy's Dominion and robbed of its glass, dropped two feet toward the water and caught itself and hung, swaying.

"WHO..." He got a breath. It came in wet. "Just WHO THE HELL ARE THESE KIDS!?"

His voice had gone up into a shriek. The drawl was gone out of it entirely; the vowels had shortened to nothing, the jokes had run out, and what came out of him was raw.

"WHY ARE THEY FIGHTIN' THIS GOOD!? I'm *bleedin'*! I'm bleedin' outta *four places*! I don't *bleed*, I'm not *built* for it, I..." He looked down at his flank and at the dark blood welling between his claws and his whole face changed. "It's really startin' to PISS ME OFF!"

He did not yawn.

That was the first thing Naiser noticed, and the thing that made him set his foot again. A goblin who yawned before everything did not yawn. He opened his mouth, and instead of the yawn a hum came out of him, the slow pane-ringing hum that had been under the whole valley since the carriage came round the bend, *hmmmmmmmmm*, only now it was not slow. It was quick. It pulsed. *Hmm-hmm-hmm-hmm*, a steady beat, like a man working a bellows handle, and the lavender shimmer round him began to pulse with it, swelling and shrinking, swelling and shrinking, and for the first time in a life of ninety-one years a bead of sweat came out on the mossy green skin of his brow under the crest, and ran.

He was working. Vaashuuk Snorr-Khal, who had never once in his life tried too hard, was trying.

He used Tar Time. Naiser saw the shape of what he did and named it as he saw it: he took the glass that was still his, the glass beyond the boy's gold marks and all round the paddy's edges where the threads had not yet turned, and he drove it thicker, past honey, past pitch, past anything a dragonfly could hang in, into a thickness where nothing in it could move with any speed at all, and then he rocked it. He rippled it. He pushed the whole field one way in time with the hum and then back the other way, one way and back, one way and back, the way a man rocks a cradle. And in the interval, between one stroke of the hum and the next, everything inside the glass stopped, and then swirled outward, and then came back.

Naiser had seen this one too, once, in the same lecture room. The philosopher had poured clear corn syrup between two glass cylinders, one inside the other, and let fall three drops of colored dye into it, and then turned the inner cylinder slowly round, four times, so that the drops were drawn out into long thin streaks and smeared all round the cylinder until they were gone, mixed, lost. Then he had turned it back four times. And the drops had come back. Out of the smear, one streak drawing in to another, the three round drops of dye had gathered themselves up exactly where they had been, and sat there, and the students had shouted. In a thick enough stuff, moved slowly enough, there is no momentum to carry anything past where it is pushed. Every motion is only the push. Undo the push and the motion undoes itself, exactly, all the way back to the start.

The Sin had made his whole field into that, and then set it rocking.

Everything in it began to loop. The rice-mist at the paddy's far edge went out in a swirl and drew back into its rag and went out and drew back, over and over, like the breath of something asleep. A frog on the far dike croaked, and the croak went out across the water and came back to the frog and went out again, *gek, gek, gek, gek*, the same single note, the same instant of it, again. The hands felt it first. The fifth of the boy's twelve, which had been reaching down out of the spiral toward the Sin's other shoulder with its pike, went out a pace, and stopped, and came back a pace to where it had started, and went out, and came back, and went out, striking and unstriking the same empty air like a needle stuck in a groove. Senna, who had begun to rise on the end of the chair with her one good arm, rose and sank and rose and sank. Even the Z, the boy's white-gold fire, ran out across the paddy one stroke and was drawn back into itself, and ran out, and was drawn back. Infinite loops of the same instances. Every moment of the fight went out, and came home, and went out, and nothing in it got anywhere.

Except the Sin. He sat in the middle of it bleeding into his lap and hummed.

"There," he panted, between hums. The sweat ran into his eyes. "*There.* Now nobody's... *hm*... goin' nowhere. Ya swing, it comes back. Ya stab, it comes back. Ya breathe out, it comes *back*." He laughed, high and wet. "Nothin' ever gets *done* in here. Ya like that, handsome? Now ya know how it feels. *Hm. Hm. Hm.*"

It was costing him. Naiser could see the cost the way he could see the threads: Tar Time ran on a sip a second, and this was not a sip. Every stroke of the hum pushed the whole weight of the glass one way and back, and the goblin's reserve was going out of him in long pulls now, a share in every few heartbeats where before it had been a share in every few minutes, and his torn flank bled faster for every stroke, the dark blood coming out of it now in small pulses in time with the hum. His face had gone the gray-green of pond water. His Shell wasted almost nothing; the little it lost came off him as a faint, steady warmth that made the air round his chair shimmer like a summer road. It was a Master's working, and a lazy one's: it did not need to hurt anyone. It only needed nobody else to finish anything before he did.

Naiser's hand had closed hard on the fox-woman's shoulder. He made it open.

Because the boy had seen it too.

Out on the dike in his ring of black grass, with the twelve hands going out and coming back over his head like a crown of swaying reeds, Hiromi was watching his own hands. He watched the fifth one strike and unstrike. He watched it for three strokes of the hum, four, his bloody face perfectly still, and Naiser saw on it the look of a boy at a fishpond who has worked out that every fish he can see is swimming round in the same circle.

Then he looked at the spiral.

The spiral had not stopped. Naiser saw it at the same moment the boy did, and could have laughed aloud. Of all the twelve hands, only the ones that struck went out and came back. The spiral itself, the coil of them growing one from the last out of the kingfisher's back, each a little turned from the one before, was turning. It had been turning all along, slowly, the way the head of a sunflower turns to follow the sun, round and round in one direction only. And a thing that turns always one way has nothing to undo. There is no backstroke in a turn. Push a thing out and back in that syrup and it goes nowhere; a scallop that opens and shuts its shell in honey will open and shut forever on the same spot. But turn a corkscrew in it, always the same way, and the corkscrew goes forward through the cork. The smallest swimming things in a pond drop, too small for water to be anything to them but a syrup, do not paddle; they turn a little twisted tail like a corkscrew, always the same way, and so they go.

"Senna! *Spin it!*"

His daughter was already spinning it.

She had the kunai on her left forefinger by its ring, and she had been watching her own blade too; the moment she caught it home it had spun twice on her finger and stopped, and she had felt, in the loop, the spin was the one thing that did not come back. She set it spinning now round her finger, round and round, *kurukurukurukuru*, a gray disc on the end of her hand, and the null-light came out of it as it spun in a turning thread of black, and she held it out in front of her the way a woman holds a lamp in a dark hall.

And the boy turned his spiral into a screw.

He did it with his hands. He brought his two open palms together again in front of his chest and twisted them against each other, the right palm over the left, the way you twist the two halves of a jar's lid, and the twelve hands of the spiral answered: they drew in toward the kingfisher's back and laid themselves along one another, pike over pike, every hand a little turned from the last, until they made a single long helix of blue-black arms and pikes and fingers coiling round a line drawn from the dike to the Sin's chair, like the thread of an auger, like the narwhal's tusk, and they turned. They turned the one way only. And the auger of hands went forward through the rocking glass the way a corkscrew goes through a cork, never stroking, never coming back, a hand's breadth with every turn and then a pace and then three, and the boy's Z went into the helix and ran round inside it like fire up the inside of a chimney, white-gold, so that the whole long screw of hands was burning as it bored.

Heat breaks the trick as well. The corn-syrup drops come back only if the philosopher turns his cylinder back at once; leave it an hour before turning, and the dye has crept out into the syrup on its own, grain by grain, and nothing will ever gather it up again. The shaking of the small pieces is the one motion no push can reverse. The burning auger came through the Sin's loops carrying its own shaking with it, and behind it the loops came apart. The frog's croak went out across the water and did not come back. The mist rag swirled away and stayed gone.

Vaashuuk saw it coming, and for once in his life he tried to hurry.

He could not. He had never been able to. He threw his good hand up and set a pane, and Senna's spinning null-light went into the pane from the side, a turning black disc, and unwrote it. He set another; the burning screw went through it. He opened his mouth on the hum and the hum cracked into a yawn halfway and the yawn cracked into a shriek, and the point of the auger, twelve pikes turning together in a single coil, took him in the left thigh.

It took him as he tried to roll off the chair away from it, at the front of the thigh a hand above the knee, through the charcoal trousers and the skin and into the big four-headed muscle on the front of the thigh that straightens the knee, the quadriceps, and bored into it. It went in turning, the way a screw goes in. The turning pikes drew the muscle round with them as they went in, wringing it, the fibers tearing round in a spiral like a rope unlaid, and the white-gold fire inside the auger seared the wound as it went so that it smoked, and the point went on through the meat to the long thighbone and turned on the bone, *gyarigyari*, grinding, and stopped. Vaashuuk screamed. The scream went out across the paddy and did not come back.

The loops went out of the valley like a held breath.

Everything that had been going out and coming home went out and stayed out, all at once. Senna came up off the chair onto her feet, swaying. The fifth hand's pike, freed of its groove, went out and on into the empty air and dissolved there in blue sparks. The Sin's chair, robbed of its glass and its law, came apart under him, and he fell off it into the paddy water on his back with the burning auger still screwed into his thigh, *bashan*, and the auger let go of him and came apart too, twelve hands unwinding off one another in a long blue-black ribbon and fading into the air in a rain of sparks, and the kingfisher at the root of them, the basis of the whole Draft, dissolved last of all, a slow bloom of blue light and rust-colored feathers spreading on the water.

On the dike Hiromi fell forward on his face.

He fell the way a boy falls asleep at the supper table, all at once and straight down, into the burned grass, and lay there. The white-gold flame round his Crystal guttered and went small and blue and out, and the threads over the paddy, all of them, let go of him at the same moment and hung loose, belonging to nobody, swaying like the strings of a harp nobody is playing. Dari was out from under Naiser's hand before he could close it, a white streak across the dike, and had the boy turned over and in her lap and wrapped in all nine tails before Naiser had taken his second step.

He could see the boy breathing. He could see the blood coming out of his mouth with every breath, not much, a thread. He could see the tips of the boy's fingers, which had gone black at the nails as if they had been held in a fire, and the scorch-marks on the front of his shirt where the Z had come out through it, in the shape of a Z.

Naiser stood on the causeway with his cigarette gone out between his fingers and looked at the threads hanging loose over the paddy, and thought: *Twelve hands. At his Stage. Ashura's breath, Cozbi. What have you sent me.*

In the paddy, the Sin of Sloth sat up.

He sat up slowly, at his amble, in water to his waist, with his hood gone and his crest of oiled black hair plastered down across his face and the water round him going dark with his blood from four places. He looked at his thigh. He looked at his flank. He looked at the dike, where the fox-woman was rocking the boy, and at the girl swaying on her feet on the causeway with her right arm hanging out of its socket and her kunai spinning slowly round her left forefinger, and the black thread of the null-light still coming out of it, very faint, like smoke from a snuffed wick.

He opened his mouth. Naiser thought he would yawn.

His head turned north.

It turned the way an old dog's head turns at a sound in another room, all at once and against his will, and the bottle-green eyes went wide and stayed wide, looking past the girl and past the carriage, up the valley, past the iron hills, to where the Kessen road ran on toward the cedar wood. Naiser turned to look with him. The sky above the cedar wood was the wrong color. It was a kiln's mouth: a great dome of orange air standing over the black cedars like a bell of fire set down on the forest, so huge and so far that it looked small. As they watched, the dome trembled, and folded in on itself, and fell, and a column of black-violet went up out of it into the blue spring sky and did not come down.

Two Sins in one valley, thought Naiser, and the other one is pulling somebody in.

"Aw, *no*," said Vaashuuk. It came out of him small. "No, no, no. He's *pullin'* somebody *in.*" He put his good hand over his face. "The Captain said *alive*. I'm supposed ta be *deliverin'* him."

He took his hand away and looked at the girl, and then past her at Naiser on the road, and for a long moment the two of them looked at each other across the paddy, the lord in gray with the dead cigarette and the goblin to his waist in bloody water, and Naiser saw him think about it. Saw him think about finishing. About the girl with one arm and the boy unconscious in the fox's lap and how near they were, and how tired he was, and how far.

"This ain't over, handsome," said Vaashuuk. "Nothin' ever is."

He let go of everything at once.

The last of his glass went out of the valley the way breath goes out of a sleeper. The rice-mist came loose and rushed forward across the paddies in a white wave. The four gray horses threw their heads up in their traces all together and blew and stamped, and on the box the footman woke with a shout. The frogs went off along every dike at once in a deafening ratchet, *gerogerogerogero*. Every bead of the boy's blood that had hung over the dike all this while fell at once, a hundred small red drops striking the water together, *tatatatatatata*, like the first hard rain of the summer on a roof.

The goblin was already going. He went up out of the paddy onto a new slab of air the size of a door, slowly, lying on his side on it with one hand pressed to his flank and his ruined leg out straight, and drifted north at an amble over the rice toward the iron hills and the cedar wood and the black-violet column standing in the sky, his cloak trailing water, dark drops falling off the edge of his bed into the paddies behind him one by one. He did not look back. He was not in a hurry. He never had been.

Naiser went down the dike to his daughter.

He went at his walk. Senna was standing on the causeway where the chair had dropped her, swaying a little, with her right arm hanging and the kunai still on her left finger, and the black thread had gone out of it now. Her fingers were white to the second knuckle. Her pupils were still wide. She looked up at him, and her face did what it had not done since she was small: it came apart, for one instant, all of it, the Yukari composure falling off her like a coat dropped on a floor, and what was under it was a girl who had just done a thing she had failed at every evening for a year, and knew it.

Then she put the coat back on.

"My shoulder," she said. "It's out. Forward."

"Yes." He crouched in front of her on his heels on the clay. "I'll put it back. It will hurt more than it did coming out."

"I know."

"You threw it wide," he said, "so he would look away."

"He looks at whatever is taking something from him," said Senna. "Hiromi was taking the field. So I let him think I wasn't taking anything." She swallowed. "He couldn't finish it. The squashing. He started it and he couldn't finish it. And your smoke couldn't find the end of what he said, because he never means to get there."

Naiser looked at her for a long time.

"Yes," he said. "That's his sin." He put the dead cigarette back in his mouth and did not light it. "What would you do with it, next time?"

She thought about it, standing on the causeway with the water running off her braid. Then she looked down at the paddy beside them, where the gold ring she had torn out of the goblin's ear lay on the mud between two rice shoots, a small bright circle with a scrap of green skin still hooked in it, winking up at the two of them in the sun under a hand's depth of clear brown water, the only finished thing on the whole road.

## IV · The Kept Morning

*The Hall of Verdicts, inside Enma's Register; the morning that was never let end*

The breath went in through Muken's nose until his broken ribs screamed, and the Sin of Wrath leaned down to meet it.

"SHOW ME THE POWER OF THE KŌKAN..."

Muken was underneath him, bloody, flat on his back on the black floor with the long burning face a hand above his own, and he saw the stroke coming in the Eye before it came. The weight went down Shōki's left arm into the hand on the white sword's grip. The wrist cocked. The gold lines of the long wire body lit along the path the point would take, down, into the gold seam of his left eye, and the Eye gave it to him the way it gave him everything, worst first, because it could not help itself. He had one move. He had kept it. He snapped his head to the right on the black polish, hard, as far as his neck would turn, and the breath he had drawn went out of him in the snap.

The point missed the eye. It came down where the eye had been and struck the bony rim of the socket above it, the brow ridge, and skidded on the bone, *gichi*, a sound like a nail drawn across slate. And the Sin of Wrath ripped his blade through Muken's face.

He did not lift it to strike again. He did not need to. He drew it, the way a headsman draws a stroke through and not at, with his whole long arm, from the shoulder: the white edge came off the brow ridge and down across the face on the diagonal, from the outer end of the left eyebrow through the bridge of the nose to the corner of the right jaw. It opened the left brow to the bone. It went through the bridge of the nose where the bone meets the gristle and cut both, so that the end of the nose came half away and hung. It crossed the right cheek and laid it open through the skin and the fat and the flat muscle of the cheek that holds the mouth to the teeth, the one a trumpeter blows with, into the mouth itself, so that the air went in and out through the side of his face. It nicked the facial artery where it climbs over the jaw-bone, and the blood came up out of the cheek in a bright jet, red as the harness, and the white sword was hot enough that the jet hissed where it touched the blade and stank of scorched meat. The heat seared the edges of the wound as it went. It would scar the way a field scars where a plowshare has gone through it, a thick ridged furrow corner to corner across the whole of his face, and a part of Muken that was very far away from the pain noticed that, and filed it, the way he filed a crack in a salt face before he set the pick.

He screamed through his cheek. It came out in two places at once, from his mouth and from the hole in his face, a wet whistling shriek.

The note broke.

He felt it go. He had held it in the Kharven breath since the road, one crack, the dead one ringing sour at the back of the Sin's neck, and the pain came through his face like a door blowing open and blew the note out of him, and in his right hand Tsugigane stopped ringing. The silver thread in its marrow went quiet. *Break the note*, the Sin had read off him somewhere in all that reading, *and the Work has nothing to stand on*. The Eye was still open. It was still showing him everything. But nothing in him was holding anything now.

Shōki stood up off his chest.

He stood the way he had knelt, unhurried, the knee coming off Muken's breastbone and the burned harness peeling away from the blistered skin over his heart with a sound like a bandage pulled, and then he bent and took hold of the front of the harness in his right fist, and lifted.

Muken came off the floor like a sack. The Sin straightened his arm over his head, lifting the whole length of him up off the black polish to arm's length, and Muken hung there with his broken left arm swinging and the bone through its skin and the blood running out of his face onto the Sin's wrist, where it hissed, and the long hollow face looked up at him from below, scarred, pale as a man long underground, with the one living eye turning its red rings and the cooked one dead and white.

The Eye showed Muken the Sin's body under him. Ten thousand gold cracks in it, all loaded, all humming, the crazed glaze of a pot run through the kiln one firing too many. The open one at the nape, ringing sour and bleeding like a man's. And at the wrist of the hand that held him, the iron.

He had not looked at the irons before. He had not had reason to. They were cuffs: two bands of old dark iron as broad as a man's palm, one round each wrist, and from the eye of each hung a short stub of chain, three links, the last link broken open at the end where something had once been struck off it. They were the condemned's irons from that morning. The Eye read them as it read the hall: every line in them rang sour, the same dead note, a thing that should have come off and been thrown in the river three hundred years ago and had been worn instead. And through the eye of the right-hand cuff, where the chain was hung, there ran a seam. It was fine and dark and old. Two fronts of hot iron had met there once in a smith's forge without truly joining, the way two streams of wax poured into a mold from either side can touch and set against each other without ever mixing, and the place they had met was a crack that had been born with the iron and had never once been anything else. It carried the whole cuff's weight. It was the worst flaw in the right hand of the Sin of Wrath, and the Eye ranked it, and Muken had no breath to do anything about it, and filed it.

Shōki threw him up.

He threw him straight up, with a short hard heave from the hips and the shoulder, the way a man throws a sack up onto a high wagon bed, and Muken went up off the Sin's fist toward the roof-beams of the Hall of Verdicts with his arms flung out. Tsugigane went out of his right hand. His fingers were broken and skinned and the throw tore the grip out of them, and the sword went spinning away through the kiln-light shafts, end over end, flashing, and fell, and struck the black floor somewhere behind him with a long bright clang, and skidded, and lay still. He went up a man's height. Two. He saw the beams over him, black, three hundred years of soot on them and the seven chairs' carved crests along them, and the slanting shafts of light that did not move, and for one long weightless moment at the top of the throw he hung there among them with nothing under him at all.

Then he fell, and the Sin of Wrath was waiting under him with both arms.

Shōki set his feet. He set them wide on the black polish, the left heel down and the right, and sank his hips, and as Muken came down out of the air he drove both forearms upward together into Muken's belly, from below, the elbows bent and the fists clenched and the whole drawn-wire length of him rising behind them from the heels, the way two men drive a ram up into a gate. Muken saw it in the Eye and could not do anything. A falling man cannot move.

The two forearms took him under the ribs in the soft hollow at the top of the belly, the pit of the stomach, where the great sheet of muscle that draws the breath down sits on top of the stomach and the liver, with his whole falling weight driving down onto them. The air went out of him through his mouth and the hole in his cheek in one huge, flat *HOKK*. The blow went in through the muscle of the belly wall, which tore at the middle where the two long straps of it meet, and through the sheet of the breath-muscle, which went into spasm and locked, and into the stomach under it, which it crushed against the spine so that its lining split and bled. It struck the left end of the liver beside it. The liver is a soft dark heavy thing wrapped in a skin like the skin on a sausage, and the blow burst the skin over the end of it, so that the blood came out of the liver into the space under the skin and began to swell there, slow, a bag filling. He felt that one as a deep sick heaviness under the right ribs, a pain that had no edges. The heat came with the blow: every place the Sin's forearms touched him, through the harness and the mail and the padding, the skin went cherry-red and blistered under it.

He folded over the Sin's arms like a coat over a rail.

The greave and the knee-cop on his left leg closed on the shattered kneecap and drew it round toward where a kneecap belongs, grinding, and held it there like a splint. The cords at his left forearm cinched above and below the break. It hurt more than anything yet. He bore it because it was holding him up.

He stood.

He stood on the broken leg because the harness would not let it fold. He stood on the black floor of the Hall of Verdicts at the foot of the steps with Tsugigane in his right hand and his left arm bound to his chest and the Sin of Wrath half-sitting against the block four paces off, and he looked over Shōki, and his eyes thrummed.

Both of them. The bright gold of the Kōkan came into both his eyes, the left with the seam that had been torn wide on the road and the right that had never had a seam at all, and the gold spilled out past the irises and thrummed. It beat in them like a pulse, gold and darker gold and gold, and the bell held on one note in the back of his teeth began to beat with it, and the note split.

It split into seven.

He heard them come apart: seven notes where there had been one, each a little higher than the last, the way a struck bell has under its one great note a whole stair of smaller ones that only a bell-founder ever hears. Ruins, low and sour. Purity, steady. Spirit, faint as breath. Temporality, ticking. Knowledge, clear. Balance, two notes leaning on each other. And over them all a seventh he had never heard, that he had asked the Bound Flame about once in the place made of threads and been told the name of, and been told he would not be taught. It came anyway. He had not called it. He did not know how. It came up out of the crack under his breastbone through all the others, and gathered them, and the seven notes that had come apart came back together into one chord, and Muken pulled ZENTAI-SEI NO HATARAKI around him.

The Work of Totality. The crown of the Works, the Kōkan's own, the labor by which all the other labors are made one law; Faisal had withheld it, and the Bound Flame had said it would wait perhaps his whole life, perhaps someone else's after him. It did not wait. He did not have the reserve for it. He had been near empty since the iron broke, the cold coming up from the soles of his feet, his teeth chattering; there was nothing left in his Crystal to give it. So it took from him instead. He felt it go past the bottom of his reserve the way a pick goes through the floor of a gallery into the gallery under it, through the place where the Essence ends and into the place the Essence comes from, his own substance, the stuff of him, and draw on that. That was a debt the Crystal does not refill. He knew it as it was paid. He paid it.

It fractured the reality in front of him into pieces.

He saw it happen with the Eye, and the Eye had never seen anything like it. The air between him and the Sin crazed. The kiln-light shafts crazed. The black floor, the Hell Fire's standing sheet behind the block, the block itself: everything in front of him went to glaze, fine gold crackle laid over all of it the way crazing comes up on a pot, and the cracks were not new. They were all already there. Every one of them was already in the thing, every place it carried more than it should, every load locked into it, and Totality only showed him the whole of them at once, as one total thing, and let him hold the whole. And in the Sin of Wrath, sitting against the block with the X glowing on his chest, the cracks were brightest of all. The Sin's whole body lit up in front of him in colored bands, gold and rose and violet and green, running through the long wire frame in fringes the way the colors run in a sheet of stressed glass held up against the sky, every band a place where the load was locked in, and the bands were thickest in the right arm. The bare one. The arm whose iron he had broken.

He understood what he was looking at. He had broken a hundred glass drops as a boy, on the gallery floor at Halcrest, where the glassmen's waste came down the chute: the little tadpoles of glass the glassmen let fall hot into a bucket of water to show children a trick. The bulb of a drop like that will take a hammer and laugh at it, because when it fell into the water its skin froze hard at once while its middle was still molten, and the middle, cooling after, pulled in on the skin and squeezed it, so the drop is a skin held in a grip by its own heart, every inch of it loaded. Hit the bulb and the load holds. Snap the thin tail and the whole drop goes to powder in an instant, every crack in it running at once, because one break lets go the whole stored load. Exuroth was in the Sin, the Trial Flame, quench and temper in his Crystal; he had been heated and quenched and heated and quenched for three hundred years. He was a drop. Every inch of him was skin held by its own heart. And the right arm had been held together by an iron for three centuries, and the iron was gone.

The Sin of Wrath came off the block.

He came because he had to. The law the Sin had made of himself at the block would not let him sit under an X on his chest; it was entered, and it had to be answered, and the cool turn was over. He came off the base of the block in one long lunge, low, his right foot driving off the floor and his whole wire body stretching out after the bare right arm, the long fingers spread, reaching for Muken's neck again. He reached the way he had reached on the road and in the hall, for the throat, to lift and slam and read and close the book, and Muken watched the colored bands in the reaching arm all brighten together as it straightened, every locked load in it lighting at once.

"Split..."

He sliced down.

He did not raise Tsugigane high. There was no room and no need. He turned his right shoulder over and brought the blade down short and straight in front of him in a single falling cut, the hips turning behind it as far as the harness would let them, onto the reaching arm a hand's breadth below the shoulder, where the arm comes out of the joint, where the colored bands in it ran thickest. The Eye ranked it worst first. The gray blade went down onto that place with its silver thread blazing, and touched.

"...and Resolve..."

Shōki's right arm was cut off.

It came off at the shoulder where the edge met it, clean through the skin and the round cap of muscle over the joint and the bone under it, at a stroke. And then the rest of it went. The whole arm, from the cut to the fingertips, flashed once with every colored band in it at once, and shattered like glass. It went the way the glass drops went on the gallery floor when the tail snapped: all at once, from the cut outward, the upper arm and the elbow and the long forearm with its band of old white scar and the hand with the long spread fingers all bursting into a spray of glittering pieces no bigger than grains of rice, *SHARIIIIIN*, a sound like a whole cupboard of crystal falling down a stair. The pieces flew out into the kiln-light in a glittering fan, gold and rose and violet, and hung there one moment, catching the light; and then they disappeared. They went out the way sparks go out over a forge, each one winking and gone, until there was nothing in the air where the arm had been but a faint gold dust drifting down toward the floor and not reaching it.

That was the Resolve. Ruins split it, every crack at once. Purity resolved it, every piece to its proper end. The two Works his sister had taught him without knowing she was teaching anything, the one that breaks and the one that holds, done in the same stroke by a third thing that held them both.

Shōki screamed.

It came out of him the way the Hell Fire had come out of the floor, standing, all at once, from the root up, a scream that had nothing of the judge in it and nothing of the furnace, only a man. The blood gushed from his cut arm. It came out of the shoulder in a great hot jet from the artery that runs down under the arm, the big one that feeds the whole limb, pumping, and it did not hiss. ENSHŌ did not take it. Nothing seared it shut. It ran down his side and onto the black floor the color of blood, red, ordinary, a man's, the second wound in three hundred years that his nature could not close, and it pooled round his knees and kept coming. He went down on his knees in it at Muken's feet. His left hand came up and clutched at the place the arm had been and found nothing, and clutched again, and his living eye had stopped spinning altogether. It was only an eye. It was looking at the stump.

The Hell Fire behind the block guttered. The sheet of it sagged and tore along its top edge where the black-violet hem had been and fell in on itself in tatters of white flame that went down into the floor and out. The furnace roar dropped to a cough. In the dark at the end of the shortened hall, the black-violet edge of the Register flickered like a lamp in a draft.

Muken's body was a husk.

He felt it as soon as the cut was done: the stuff of him gone out of him into the Work, and what was left standing in the harness light and dry and hollow as a wasp's nest, as a locust's split shell on a fence. The cold had gone. That was the worst of it. All his life the cold had been how he knew he was empty, teeth chattering, jaw shaking; and now there was not even the cold. There was nothing. His skin had gone the gray of ash on a hearth. His lips had no feeling in them. The Eye was not flickering blind anymore because it was not doing anything except seeing, gold, steady, everything, and he understood dimly that this was worse. He looked down at the Sin of Wrath on his knees in his own blood.

The black floor under them was polished. It was wet-looking, and so black that it held the kiln-light like water holds the sky, and in it, under the Sin's bowed head, Muken saw himself.

For an instant he did not know the face. It was too old. It was a long face, hollowed under the cheekbones, with a gold light in both eyes and a furrow cut across it corner to corner, and the hair round it was white. His hair was black. He knew it was black; it had been black all his life, black as his father's, and the Hell Fire had burned it short at the back. But in the polish, for one instant, it flashed white, all of it, white as frost, white as the beard of a man who has been old since before there was time, and the face in the floor looked up at him out of the black with the patience of something that had written the first laws of the world and was waiting to see if anyone would read them right.

Shōki saw it too. His head had come up. He was looking at Muken's face above him, and the living eye had gone wide again, and the burned lips moved.

"...Bara," said the Sin of Wrath.

Then it was gone. The hair in the polish was black. It was only Muken, eighteen and gray-faced and cut to pieces, standing in a harness that was holding him up.

He walked.

He walked forward on the bound leg, one step, with the knee grinding in its splint of lacquer and cord, and kicked Shōki's body forward. He kicked him with the right foot, the good one, standing on the broken left because the harness made the broken left stand: the right knee drawn up and the foot driven out flat from the hip into the Sin's chest, below the X, the heel of the boot taking him on the breastbone. The Sin's body was in shock. It did not answer. The law of the Unburied said that he must answer every blow before he did anything else, and the body on its knees in its own blood could not, and the kick drove it back off its knees and over onto its side on the floor with a thick slap, and the heat of the chest seared the sole of Muken's boot so that it smoked. He walked forward again. He kicked again, into the ribs on the left side, the side with an arm still on it, and felt them give under the boot, two, three, *bokibokiboki*, and the Sin's body slid a pace across the polish through its own blood, a long red smear behind it, toward the foot of the block. Again: the boot into the belly, folding him round it, and a gout of dark froth out of the torn lung onto the floor. Again: into the back of the shoulder above the stump, driving him the last pace across the polish until his face was against the base of the block in the wide dark stain that had never dried, and he lay there, one arm and a stump, with the blood running out of the stump in pulses.

Muken stood over him.

He stood with Tsugigane hanging from his right hand, the point down, the silver thread in it lit, and his left arm bound across his chest, and the gold beating in both his eyes, and the harness alight on him red-gold in every cord, and he said it. He said it the way the Seat had heard him speak at the woodpile, low, slow, every word set down on the last like stones on a cairn, so that if there had been lamps in that hall they would have swung on their chains all together; and he said it loud, too, the last of it, as loud as he had ever said anything, so that it went down the shortened hall and struck the black-violet edge where the seven chairs had been and came back.

"All those who raise their hands up against the ones I LOVE WILL DIE!"

The hall took it.

He felt it go in. It went into the black floor and the beams and the stain on the block the way a word goes into a man's hall when it is sworn there before witnesses: entered. The sworn word was a layer of him, and he had never sworn one so big. It set in him under his breastbone where the hate had been standing, and the hate sat down; it had nowhere left to go. And the seven notes in his teeth, the chord that had come together, shifted. One of them leaned. Ruins, the low sour one, came up a little louder than the others, the way one voice in a choir comes up over the rest when the singer means it more, and the gold in Muken's eyes went for one beat the color of old blood before it came back to gold. He did not notice. He was looking at the block.

Under the Sin's cheek, on the scooped top of the block where a neck goes, the hairline of gold still ran. It was carrying its weight the way a heart does, badly, giving and taking, fainter than before. The thread from the Sin's chest to the block. The one living note in the dead hall. And at the back of the Sin's neck, a hand's breadth below it, the dead crack at the nape rang sour, open, bleeding, under the point of Tsugigane.

Behind him, at the edge of the hall, something gave.

He heard it first: a long splitting creak like river ice in the first thaw, *mishimishimishi*, from the place where the black-violet edge of the Register stood in the dark where the seven chairs had been. He turned his head. The Eye showed him a crack in the edge. A real one. A seam of daylight standing in the black, thin as a hair, and through it, very far off, the cedar wood: black trunks, the gray light of the robbed sky, frost on the road, and a cold that came through the seam into the furnace of the hall and touched his gray face like a hand.

And the light in the seam was the wrong light. It was lavender. It was thick. It came through the crack in the Register slowly, the way honey comes off a spoon, glossy, and the dust in the kiln-light shafts near it stopped falling and hung. And through the seam from the far side, faint and enormous and unhurried, came a yawn.

It went into Muken's jaw. His mouth opened on it before he could stop it, and the gray face in the black polish yawned with him.

He stood over the Sin of Wrath with the blade above the dead crack and the living thread under the Sin's cheek and the glass coming through the crack behind him, and his eyes were heavy, and Tsugigane was in his hand.

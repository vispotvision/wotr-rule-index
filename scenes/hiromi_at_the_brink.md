# Hiromi at the Brink

*Aetherion Academy · the Crucible floor, Hiromi Mahuo's bout, the trench beneath the barrier*

---

He came to rest in a trench of his own making, hard against the plinth of the barrier, with the shattered ashlar of three broken slabs heaped about him like the scree beneath a cliff.

For a moment he felt nothing at all, only a dumb concussive blankness, and that was the worst of it. The palm had gathered every motion inside him into one outward impetus and hurled him across the oval, and he had struck the floor once and spalled a slab, struck it twice and sundered another, and ploughed the last of the distance through sand and stone on his shoulder with the sky careening overhead. Now he lay still. The ward-lines beneath him gave one fulgurant flash and guttered out to a wan glow. Friable dust sifted down over his face. Then the hurt arrived all at once, the way a wave that has drawn far back down a beach arrives, and it was everywhere, inexorable.

The blood came up out of him in a precipitate rush before he could stop it. It burst from his mouth in a bright incarnadine gout that sprayed across the sand and the base of the barrier and his own white hair, and he rolled onto his side and let it come, and it kept coming, more than he had thought a body so small could hold. His ribs on the right had gone past cracking into something with torsion in it. Something there had shifted under the fist and stayed shifted; every breath ground the broken ends against each other with a faint wet crepitus he could feel in his teeth. Under them the liver had become a livid ache with a sick tide riding on it. His sternum throbbed, contused, where the palm had smitten it. His shoulder, the one that had taken the floor three times, had gone numb as gelid clay from the collarbone down.

He shrugged it off. He pushed himself up onto one elbow and grinned through the blood with all his old effrontery, because that was what one did, and for perhaps three heartbeats it worked.

Then he felt it after, as fighters do, and the last of his sangfroid went with it. All of it, all at once.

---

"The house can be yours. But ownership has to keep reaching the things inside it. And reaching is motion."

The words came to him across the whole length of the Crucible as clearly as if the boy had said them into his ear, and Hiromi lay in the trench and turned them over with the obdurate patience he gave to every enigma that had ever beaten him.

The boy was right. Hiromi made himself say so to himself first, plainly, because a summoner who indulges in sophistry about how a working failed will call the same failure again tomorrow. Kain had not tried to change his rank. He had taken hold of the instant in which rank attached to him, the infinitesimal crossing between *Kain not yet judged* and *Kain judged*, and pushed it forward with exquisite temerity, always forward, so that each verdict the Dominion handed down landed on a Kain who had already become the next Kain. The recognition had lagged, and Hiromi had felt it lag like a hysteresis in his own nerves. He had felt the amber lattice of his own conviction stamping its seal again and again onto an evanescent sequence of outdated selves, a palimpsest of judgements each written over the last and none of them on the living page.

And the nacreous haze had stopped coming to him. The boy had turned his own leaking Essence back into himself before it could finish becoming loose, heat into tremor, tremor into sinew, sinew into heat again, a wheel that never let the coin fall into the drawer. The court of dust that had circled Hiromi had been given a thousand moving centres and had spiralled outward into the gloaming beyond the barrier. Up in the Soul's reflection the black dragon had learned the same trick and was flowing round the haetae in vertiginous coils so that every verdict the guardian gave fell on where the dragon had been. And last of all the boy had scrambled the order in which Hiromi's own workings answered, so that GRASP came before the Weight was ready and Hyeonmu tried to keep a blow that had not yet arrived. The whole intricate engine had gone juddering against itself. It had become a clock with every gear true and every gear out of turn.

It was the most brilliant thing anybody had ever done to him, and he could have sung it an encomium.

He lay with his cheek on the sand and his blood soaking into it and thought, with a febrile rapture, that he would have given a great deal to have done it himself.

---

But he saw the other half of it too, and the other half was why neither of them was winning, and why the floor between them had become a kind of interregnum.

There was an old riddle in his father's books, set down by some mordant philosopher of the lineage halls a thousand years dead. A swift runner chases a tortoise that has been given a head start. By the time the runner reaches where the tortoise was, the tortoise has crept a little further; by the time he reaches *that* place, it has crept a little further still; and so, says the riddle, the runner can never catch it, though he run until the world's last eventide. Hiromi had puzzled at it for a whole winter as a small boy, until he had worked out on his slate why it was casuistry. The pieces of the chase grow smaller and smaller, and an endless sum of shrinking pieces can add up to a finite whole; the runner catches the tortoise at a moment you can name. But only if the pieces really do shrink. Only if the runner is faster than the tortoise. If the two are matched, step for step, the pieces never shrink, the sum never closes, and the chase truly does go on forever.

That was what was happening on this floor. Hiromi's recognition was the runner. Kain's continuity was the tortoise. And they were matched.

His Dominion was not too weak to judge the boy; every verdict it gave was adamantine, and would have held, if it had landed in time. The boy's change was not too strong to be judged; it only kept a hair's breadth of lead, and the lead never grew. Every time Hiromi's will reached, Kain moved the reaching. Every time Kain moved, Hiromi's will reached again. Irath ranked the motion. Wyther moved the rank. The ranking of the motion was itself a motion, and the moving of the rank was itself a thing that could be ranked, and each law swallowed the other whole and was swallowed in its turn, like two serpents each eating the other's tail.

He knew what that was, too. He had read it in a paper about the brazen governors that regulate the great engines of the southern line: a correction that always arrives a little late does not settle a machine. It sets it swinging. Push a pendulum back toward the centre, but always a fraction too late, and it will never come to rest; it will oscillate forever, round and round the same ring, neither falling nor stopping, a perfect sempiternal cycle that feeds on whatever energy both hands keep putting into it. Engineers had a dry, saturnine name for that ring. Hiromi had another. He called it what it was, without equivocation: a loop with no exit.

Two mirrors facing each other in a tenebrous room make a corridor that runs to infinity. Every reflection holds the one before it. But every reflection is a little dimmer than the last, because no glass gives back all the light it is given, some always lost to the silvering and the refraction, and if you look far enough down the corridor it goes dark. The corridor is infinite. The light in it is not.

Neither law would ever trounce the other. Neither Archon had ever beaten the other, in all the immemorial ages since the first star was lit; the old books spoke of them as rivals, never as victor and vanquished. The loop between them was perfect and perdurable. What would end was the light. It would end in whichever body went dark first.

And Hiromi knew, lying in the trench with the hot saline taste of his own blood in his mouth, which body that was going to be.

---

His body was failing. He had always known it would, the way a man who builds his house on a riverbank knows about the freshet. It had only been a question of when.

He could feel it going now, in an order he understood from the infirmary books Lily had left on his pillow, with pointed solicitude, after his second collapse. He had lost a great deal of blood, from his mouth and from deep inside where nobody could see it, from the tumid, torn liver bleeding quietly into his belly, and a body that loses blood fights to hold what is left by narrowing every vessel in its outer reaches, the hands, the feet, the skin, to keep the heart and the brain supplied. His hands had gone cold and sallow. His fingertips had a faint plumbeous tinge to them when he lifted them before his eyes. His heart, robbed of the volume it needed to fill itself, had begun to race to make up the difference, a thin quick rataplan against his cracked ribs, faster and faster and doing less with every beat. When it could no longer keep the pressure up, the first thing to fail would be the brain, because the brain is the first thing to starve, and the first to slip into syncope.

He had not eaten. He never ate. Under the bleeding there was the old hunger he always denied, and it was no longer a hunger now but an absence: the sugar in his blood spent long ago, the stores in his liver spent after it, his field and his summons and his conviction all running on whatever his body could rend out of its own muscle. His Essence reserve was a well with the water gone down to the brackish mud. He could feel the bottom of it, desiccated and cracked.

And his influence faltered.

It showed in the room first, as it always had. All round the oval the lamp flames that had stood tall and bent toward him began to tremble, lambent and uncertain, and one by one they straightened, and stood upright, and stopped leaning toward anything at all. The needles of the draw-gauges in the walls sagged back off their stops and drifted in a slow torpor toward the middle of their dials. The ward-lines' deep hum faltered into a ragged susurrus. In the Soul's reflection the amber hands that had breathed with his breath grew faint, and fainter, until they were only a russet glow, like the last light on the hills after the sun has gone. GRASP still came off him, because GRASP always came off him; but it came now in pulses, one with each racing heartbeat, a field that swelled and failed and swelled and failed, a stertorous breathing in the Aether.

This fight was spiralling down into a charnel mess of blood, and he could see the whole of it from where he lay.

There was blood everywhere. His was on the sand in long bright arcs where he had bounced and skidded; on the barrier plinth behind him; on the black shell of Hyeonmu, still standing where it had turned too late. The boy's was there too. It was on Hiromi's own coat, dark stains on the black collar where Kain's hands had seized him wet from his own bleeding nose, and in a fine spindrift across the stone where Kain had blown the held breath out of his chest, and on the floor beneath the far mark where the doubled paw had first bitten. They had painted the whole Crucible with each other. If the loop ran on, they would paint it again, and again, round and round, each pass a little darker than the last, each body a little emptier, until the two of them lay at the bottom of the spiral, exsanguinated, covered in each other's blood with nothing left in either to lift a hand. That was where an infinite loop went when the things running it were made of flesh and gristle.

So this was the brink. The very escarpment of his strength, the place where it simply stopped, and he had found it here, on this floor, fighting this boy to a standstill. Kain was hurt, breathing hard, bruised to the bone across the ribs, his arm still aching where Hyeonmu had bitten it, blood crusted under his nose. Hiromi had done that, and he felt no compunction for it. Hiromi had held the Paragon of Motion to a perfect, endless, ruinous tie, and the tie was going to cost him everything he had left.

He tried to sit up the rest of the way. The trench tilted and wheeled in a slow caracole. The sky went white, then a dull glaucous grey.

---

He went away.

There was no sound, and then there was sound again, a great muffled clangour like the sea heard through a wall, and he was on his back without remembering lying down. The barrier above him was very tall and very far off and patterned with a tracery of argent light he could not read. Someone in the tiers was screaming a name, a high ululation that broke and came again. It might have been his.

He went away again.

When he came back the sky had gone a strange crepuscular colour, though it was the middle of the afternoon, and that was his own eyes; he knew that. The world had narrowed to a bright coin at the end of a stygian tube, and inside the coin, very small and very far off, a barefoot boy with long black hair stood on the broken floor and did not move.

His thoughts had lost all their tensile strength. He would take hold of one and it would come apart in his fingers like wet paper. *Wyther. The step. The gate. The tortoise.* Words with nothing behind them. He reached for the next move, the next answer, the next thing he had been going to do, and found only a blank white page, a mind fading into obscurity like a lamp turned down by degrees until the wick is a single candent thread and then not even that.

He went away.

---

In the dark there was a binding circle, and he was six.

The practice hall was cyclopean and cold and full of adults, and every one of them had gone quiet. He did not know what he had done. He had only walked into the circle because it was there and he wanted to see what it did, and something in the world had turned to look at him, and now the masters of summoning who had made the art into a system stood round the chalk in a sepulchral silence, so complete he could hear the lamps hiss. He was frightened. He did not know of what.

His father's hand came down on his back, between the shoulder-blades, warm through his small shirt.

It was the only warm thing in the room. It stayed there. It did not push him and it did not pull him away; it simply rested, a wordless benison, the way a hand rests on a door it knows will open, and in all that terrible silence his father had said nothing at all, and had not needed to.

Hiromi had spent seven years since then trying to earn that hand. Not the title. Not the Paragon, not Dominion, not any of what the adults in the hall had named him afterward. The hand. He had stood last at the introductions and told the whole class he would be the greatest summoner who ever lived, and laughed, and said *of course*, and every word of that hauteur had been for one man in one doorway. He had gone to the circles in the rime-cold dark before dawn so that, one day, when it counted, he could do something so good that his father would look at it and put his hand on his back again in front of everyone.

All he had ever wanted was to make his father proud.

He came back.

---

The coin of light was smaller now. The boy in it had not moved. The blood in Hiromi's mouth had gone thick and cold and acrid with iron and dust. His heart was a frantic little bird against the broken ribs.

He was going down. He knew it with the plain clear certainty of a thing that has already happened. His mind was going, and his blood with it, and the Dominion after both, and in a moment or two he would be gone, and the loop would break the only way it could break, in the body that went dark first.

But the Dominion did not go all at once.

That surprised him, faintly, from very far away. He had thought it would go out like a lamp. It did not. As his thoughts came apart, one by one, the thing underneath them did not come apart with them, because it had never been a thought. Conviction is not something a mind does. It is what remains in a mind when the doing stops: the sediment of seven years laid down like stone in a riverbed, what a soul has been sure of for so long that it no longer needs to be thought at all. His own sheet said it plainly, the one he could read in his Crystal, in a rubric he had always found faintly embarrassing: when he was shaken, his field surged with his mood and spilled workings he had never meant to start. He was more than shaken now. He was going under. And as the last of his conscious will let go of the threads, what was left of him, the part that had never needed to think and owed fealty to nothing but itself, did the only thing it had ever known how to do.

It called the guardian home.

In the Soul's reflection the haetae stopped judging. It stopped trying to find the step, stopped hurling verdicts at where the dragon had been, stopped doing anything that a chase could make late. It simply turned, out of the dragon's spiral, and came down. Out of the reflection and onto the floor of the Crucible, faint and glassy and almost without light, a lion made of the last russet glow of a boy's will, and it walked to the trench under the barrier and stood over him.

A guardian at a gate does not chase. It does not pass judgement on whoever has already gone by. It stands, and the house is behind it, and anything that wants the house must come up the step. The house was a white-haired boy lying in his own blood at the foot of the barrier's revetment. The step was the broken floor in front of him. The haetae planted its forefeet on either side of his body and lifted its great opalescent head and looked across the Crucible at the Paragon of Motion, and its eyes, which were only two faint points of amber, did not move.

It could not judge in time. The boy had proven that. It was not trying to. It was only there, as the stone haetae at Sum-gol had been there every morning of Hiromi's childhood, between the lintel and the road.

He saw it, from the bottom of the dark tube, through the closing coin of light. The lion's legs stood on either side of him like the pillars of a gate. Its belly was above his face, and through it, through the faint gold glass of it, he could see the high scud of cloud.

*Teacher,* he tried to say, and the word was wrong, and he did not have the strength to find out why.

"Father..." It came out as a breath with blood in it. Nobody could have heard it. "Did you... see..."

The coin of light closed to a point.

Above him, the guardian of the house stood its ground, coruscating faintly, and waited for whoever would come up the step.

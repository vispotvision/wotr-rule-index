# Hiromi: Something Special

There were petals on the back of his hand.

He noticed them when he looked down to take the handkerchief. Three of them, small and white and faintly pink at the edges, stuck to his skin where his fingers had rested on hers. They had not been there before. He turned his hand over and one slid off his knuckle into his palm, and it was still cool, the way a flower is cool first thing in the morning.

He did not ask. He closed his hand on the petal very carefully, so it would not crush, and put the hand in his pocket as if that were where it had always been going.

"My mother won't be too hard on you," Hiromi said. "Probably. She's hard on everybody for about a minute." He thought about it. "But she likes you. She asked about you twice this week, and she never asks about anyone twice. So I don't think she'll mind."

He took the handkerchief. It was folded neatly round the paste and smelled of oak and flowers and the sharp green of the leaves. Then she held out the sapling too, and he took that as well, cupped in both hands because it seemed like the kind of thing that ought to be carried in both hands. It was so light. Its roots were a small pale tangle, no bigger than a thumb, and the leaves shivered when he breathed near them.

Not mint. A tree. From the south reefs, where she wasn't supposed to go.

He did not say anything about that either. A secret was a secret. He had a brother who had kept a snake in a bottle for eleven months. He knew how to leave a thing alone.

"That's really nice of you," he said. "Thank you."

Then, because it was true and he had never been any good at keeping true things in his mouth:

"You're strange, you know that?"

He heard it land and his ears went hot all over again.

"No, I mean..." He shifted on the bench, and his ribs told him not to, and he did it anyway. "Good strange. Other girls don't act like this. They just want things. Or they want me to train them, because of Father, or because of the summoning thing." He looked at the sapling in his hands instead of at her. "You just gave me a tree."

That was the whole of it, really. He did not have better words for it, and he was thirteen, and the words he had would have to do.

"I'll make you something," he said. "Back. Something special." He frowned at the leaves. "I'm not very good at alchemy. That's Geturo's. Last time I tried to distil anything I set my sleeve on fire and Father made me write it up." He brightened. "But I'll ask him. He'll grumble, and then he'll help, and I'll do the part that matters myself. So it's from me."

Down on the sand a bell rang, two short strokes and a long one.

A tall girl in an upper-year sash had come out to the rail with a slate on a cord round her wrist. She was all elbows and freckles, her copper hair scraped back in a knot so tight it looked painful, a fat stub of chalk behind one ear and three more in her breast pocket. Her voice carried the way a gull's does.

"Class X, drill line in five!" she called. "Anybody not on a stretcher is on the sand!" She squinted down the row, found Hiromi, and pointed the chalk at him. "That includes you, Mahuo. I watched you walk up here. You can walk down."

"I'm going," Hiromi said.

He stood up slowly, one hand pressed to his side, the sapling held against his chest in the other so it would not bump anything. Then he stopped and looked back down at her.

"Keep an eye out for me on the drill line," he said. "If I fall over, you're allowed to laugh."

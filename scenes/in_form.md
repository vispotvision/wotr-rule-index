# In Form

*Coldwater common, continued.*
*POV: Ruvaen Aloreth. RP reply. Dova'Kan, Gorgi, Xmere and Avo'Kan are not written; their beats stay open.*

---

## I. THE CORRECTION

Ruvaen Aloreth took the correction standing still and did not defend the Archive.

*High Vaeloris. The Valorins speak it, and the language is not their family name.*

*Nine hundred years. Four entries, three of them in verse, and the fourth got the name of the thing wrong in the first line, and every clerk since has copied the first line because copying is what a clerk does.*

There is a particular shame available to an Eressean and it is not the shame of being ignorant. Ignorance is ordinary and survivable and he had said so out loud four minutes ago. The shame is in having called a thing by a name it does not hold, in a culture where a name is an office, where the yard convenes and the count is called and the witnesses are paid precisely so that nobody will ever have to guess at what a thing is called.

Eresse had been guessing for nine centuries, in writing, in its own Archive, and had been charging the Court for the paper.

"It will be corrected," he said. "Not amended. Struck and re-entered, with the error named as an error in the margin, so that the next man who opens that page is told plainly what his predecessors did. If Velthaeir wants to argue about it they may argue with me in person and I have four days a week free."

*And the second thing goes in the same entry. He did not bring you. You did not bring him. You came together.*

*I looked at a difference in pressure between two people and built a hierarchy out of it inside a second and a half, and I have been doing exactly that for eleven years and calling it reading.*

"The other correction as well," he said. "I made an assumption from the weight of the two of you and it was a bad piece of work. I will not repeat it."

He did not apologise for it. An Eressean does not apologise for an error. He enters it.

---

## II. THE INSTRUCTION

Then he had the man repeat nothing, because he had it, and gave it back instead.

"Do not pour Essence into it. Do not probe it again because the first probe did not answer. Do not put living tissue into the margin to test whether it is still consuming. Inert boundary first, then observe." He said it flat, in the order it had been given. "Chalk, iron-stone, dark glass, damp earth, packed against surviving scale on the living side of the line and not into the hollow."

He turned his head toward the rise.

"Sergeant."

Hesh Orovain was four hundred yards off and eighty feet up and heard him, because Ruvaen Aloreth had learned to put a voice across a field from a wall at Ollavaeth and had never needed a horn for it.

"My lord."

"Write this and read it back to me before you move."

*He is going to make me shout it across a burnt village in front of a dragon. Good. Then it exists.*

He gave it to the old man in pieces, four hundred yards at a time, and Hesh Orovain wrote it in a Common hand on a tally board and shouted it back, and got the order of the last two clauses wrong the first time, and was made to do it again.

"Now the distribution," Ruvaen said. "Copies to every company on the Landward Wall and to the Cliff Watch and to the quarry gangs and to every reeve of every holding inside a hundred miles, and it goes out in Common and not in form, because a man who has to work out what a Reckoner means is a man who has already put his hand in it. Corvienne Sorreth will want it entered properly and she may enter it properly afterward. It goes out tonight in the version a quarryman can read."

"And the source, my lord."

He looked at the two of them, and at the grey mineral packing on the juvenile's shoulder where the weeping had stopped.

"Attribute it," he said. "By Name. The whole of it and none of it as mine."

*And write, at the foot of the sheet, that the Prince of Eresse put his read into that wound twice before anyone told him not to. First at forty feet, second at nine. Both times for less than a second, and both times because the first one did not answer me.*

*I do not know what I have done to myself. I felt nothing either time and I have not felt anything since and I have no idea whether that is the good outcome.*

"Add that I did the second thing," he said. "Twice. On this animal. Before I knew better. Put my name on that too, because the next officer who reads that sheet is going to want to know whether the warning is theoretical."

---

## III. IN FORM

Dova'Kan had given him a Name with nothing hung on it.

No title. No house. No claim on what he was owed for it and no requirement that Ruvaen recognise whatever had crossed the sea ahead of him. It had been set down the way you set down a thing you are not worried about anyone mishandling.

There are perhaps nine people alive who could do that in front of an Eressean and not be corrected.

He put his right hand flat on his own chest and held it there a moment, which is the form for a name received, and then took it away, which is the form for a name about to be returned.

"I am Ruvaen Aloreth," he said.

He let it stand alone first, because that is how it is done, and then gave the rest of it in the order the yard requires, flat, without any of the lift the Sunroot heralds put on it.

"I hold the domain of the Grand Principality of Eresse. I am styled True Heir of the High Empire, which is a claim and not a fact, and I will tell you exactly what sort of claim it is because you will hear a different version from anybody else you ask.

"I was not born to it. There is no blood in this that puts me at the front of any line. I was a soldier. I was trained for war from the age of thirty-one and I have never been trained for anything else, and at sixty-eight I was commanding on the Landward Wall and expecting to die on it in the ordinary way. The princedom was granted. Assembly convened, count called, witnesses paid, and the reason entered in the minute was that I was the best commander they had left and that everyone with a better claim was dead.

"That is the whole of my standing. It is a name they handed me because the people who should have had it were in the ground."

He did not move his hand back to the hilt. It stayed where it was, six inches clear.

"The war is with a woman who calls herself the true Empress of the Empire of Eresse, and her forces killed my sister."

He stopped.

It was not a pause for effect and it was not grief arriving. It was simply the place where the sentence ran out of words he was willing to use, the way it had run out in a chapel and in eleven sealed entries and in a council room in front of six people who had all pretended not to notice.

*Say it. He has given you his and you are standing here with hers in your mouth like a stone.*

*Eleven years. Say it.*

"They killed my sister," he said again, which was all of it he had, "and I am the last of my bloodline alive."

---

## IV. WHY HE HAD HOPED

"I came down here twice not knowing what you were," he said, "and I want to be plain about what I was hoping and what I was afraid of, because you have been straight with me and I am not going to be clever at you.

"I was afraid you were hers.

"There is a reason the fear was reasonable and the reason is that her people kill wyverns. Nine of them off the high road in the last fortnight. Six dead, three hamstrung at the wing root with hooks that were commissioned before that army had ever laid eyes on one. And four days ago they took a holding called Harrowbeck on my march and put two hundred and six people into the mud of it, and nine of those were one household. The Hales. Grandmother to a boy of eleven who carried messages between the ridge farms because he was fast and it made him feel useful."

He looked at the four scorch stars walking across the common toward the barn.

"The Hales kept the only pear stock that ever took on that ridge. Grafted off a tree that came out of the Vessel Coast eighty years ago and died in the second Thin Weeks, and every graft since has come off the Harrowbeck trees, and the Harrowbeck trees went up with the rest of it. There will be no more of that pear. It is a small thing to put in front of you while there is a hurt animal on this common. I am telling you because it is the only part of Harrowbeck I can still describe to somebody and have them understand what was taken.

"So when a draconic presence arrived on my frontier out of nothing, in the middle of a wyvern migration, four days after that, I came down the stair expecting to find out that she had acquired something new."

He let that sit.

"Instead I found a boundary packed into a shoulder with somebody's thumbs, and the weeping stopped. Her people do not do that. Nothing her people have done in eleven years has ever once been that."

---

## V. WHAT THE PLATE SAID

"I have one thing to put against yours," he said, "and I have been sitting on it for two days because I did not understand it and I still do not.

"Velthaeir sent a Measurewright to Vaelmarr eleven days ago to take a reading on me, which is a political act and not a scientific one and we all knew it. Her name is Neyra Corvenn and she is very good. She levelled her plate on my drill ground, which sits on four hundred feet of chalk with a south-westerly running across it. Chalk holds nothing. The sea shears whatever settles. That ground has read thin for the whole eleven years I have trained on it and I chose it for that reason, because a man who trains where the ocean is thin does not find himself surprised by a thin ocean.

"Her plate read it as nominal. Same as the lower yard, forty feet down and out of the wind. She would not enter an estimate and she would not enter the word exceeded and she made her clerk write it as it stood."

*Which means the drain has not reached my headland yet. Whatever it is, it is eating Drakyssia and it is eating the southern water and it has not got as far as my chalk, and the floor over there is simply what a floor looks like when nothing has been at it.*

*That is the good reading and it is the one I have been carrying for two days and it is the reason I have slept at all.*

"You said your continent is going poor," he said. "Mine has a ground that will not go thin when every reason in the world says it should. I have not worked out whether those are the same fact from two ends or two unrelated pieces of luck. I am telling you because you have more of this than I do, and because I would rather be corrected in front of a dragon than be right in private."

*And there is the other question and I am going to leave it on the ground between us, because I do not have a way to ask it that does not sound like an offer.*

"Your open question," he said. "How separate broods knew where to come.

"I sat on that quarry lip from the eleventh bell until dawn and I read three hundred of them on the river eleven miles south-south-east. I could not tell you how, and I will not pretend I can, and two jurists at Velthaeir wrote me down as a mystic over it and I have never disputed the entry. What I can tell you is what I had. Every one of them is reaching. Grief narrows the reach and makes it clean, and three hundred clean reaches interfere instead of blurring, and where they interfere the pattern has a bearing on it.

"Last night the bearing pointed east and north, at the thing that killed nine of them on the high road. That is loss, and loss points at whoever did it, and that told me nothing about where they were going.

"But they are still arriving. Four came over that ridge in the dark and found the water. If they are coming toward something instead of away from it, then somewhere under all that grief there is a second reach, and it will be pointed somewhere that is not the river and not the Korvaeth camp, and I would need a night and a high place to separate it out."

He turned his head and looked north, along the chalk, up the line of the quarry stair he had come down.

"I have a high place," he said, "and there are about nine hours of daylight left in it."

Then he crouched, on the burnt ground at the edge of the common, and put his fingers into the spoil where the quarry gangs had been tipping their off-cut for eleven years.

Chalk, mostly. Flint nodules. And in among it the dark glassy stuff, brittle and sharp-edged and black to the eye and green when it went thin, which came out of the seam at about the fourth course and which Brannoch Skell had spent his working life cursing because it blunted a drill in half a shift.

He turned a piece of it over twice in his fingers.

There were eleven tons of it on that spoil heap, and another eleven behind the mill, and however many hundreds of tons were still in the face, and until about a quarter of an hour ago every one of them had been an inconvenience to a man with a drill.

He put the piece in the pouch at his belt, stood, and looked up the line of the face to where the seam ran out of the chalk in a black band four hundred feet long.

# Star Crusher

*Above Sum-gol. Mid-morning.*

---

The walk took eleven paces and covered four thousand leagues, and Kaalabad counted them because counting was the only thing to do in there.

The tunnel had no walls. It had a floor, or something that answered when his boot came down, and it went downward at a grade steep enough that a man would have braced, and he did not brace. On either side there was a thickness that his eyes reported as black and his skin reported as pressure and his ears reported as a sound at the very bottom of hearing, and none of the three reports agreed with the others, and he had stopped requiring them to agree about nine hundred years ago.

The fabric gave ahead of him as hide gives to a knife held at the correct angle. It never tore. It parted, and the parting ran ahead of his stride and closed behind his heel, and the closing was the part that would have killed anything else that tried to follow him in.

On the seventh pace the seal around his throat began to notice.

That was always the seventh. Never the sixth, never the eighth. Something in the working took hold of the scar tissue that ran the full circumference of his neck and drew it inward by a fraction, a slow deliberate tightening under the gorget, and the air going down became narrower and had a shape to it. Nine hundred years. He had never once told anybody about the seventh pace and never would, because a price known is a price that can be waited out.

Eleven.

He came out into open sky.

---

The village lay below him in a shallow valley opening south toward the gulf, and it was smaller than the reports.

Sixty roofs. Slate and split shingle, weathered grey, laid in the untidy organic sprawl of a settlement that had grown along a road and had never been planned around a square. A teaching house at the high end with a terraced yard cut into the slope and forty small figures standing on it in ranks. Pine below the treeline, thick and old. A stream coming down out of the eastern shoulder in three drops. Smoke from perhaps twenty chimneys, going up straight, because there was no wind at all.

The sky cracked.

It went from directly above him, black lines opening across the blue in the pattern that glass takes when it is struck in the centre, and the lines widened, and behind them there was no colour at all. Within four seconds the whole vault over the valley had gone from a bright morning to something that hurt to look up into, and Kaalabad hung at the middle of it with the halberd across his back and the well he had come out of still turning behind his shoulders.

Someone on the road dropped a bucket. He heard it. From that height, with everything else going on, he heard a wooden bucket hit a road, and that told him more about what he had just done to the local air than any instrument would have.

The faces came up.

All of them together, in the manner of a field of sheep, and Kaalabad looked down at four hundred upturned faces and found no pleasure anywhere in himself. Men assumed otherwise about him. They were wrong, and he had stopped correcting it. What he felt was the specific flatness of a workman who has arrived at a job, has seen that the job is smaller than the tools he brought, and now has to do it anyway because the day has been spent getting here.

*Sixty roofs. I have opened cities. And I am going to do this properly, because the whole point is that it be done properly and be seen to have been done properly, and a thing done carelessly teaches the wrong lesson to everyone who hears about it afterward.*

He raised his left hand.

---

The ground answered before anything visible happened to it.

The three drops of the stream stopped being drops. The water came off the eastern shoulder and did not fall, and hung, and then went up in a thin curtain that broke into beads and kept rising. Below that the pines began to lean, all of them, in the same direction, which was toward a point in the air about forty feet under his boots.

Then the first tree came out.

It came out root-ball and all, a hundred-year pine with eight feet of frozen earth still gripped around the base of it, and it went up through the treeline turning slowly on its own long axis, and eleven more came out behind it in a ring, and then the ring became a spiral, and then the whole eastern slope of the valley was in the air.

The typhoon started at the well behind him and worked down.

Kaalabad did not watch that part. The mechanism of it bored him and always had, and he had learned to keep his eyes on the ground and let the sky do what it was told. What he watched was the road. There were people on it and they were doing the four things that people always did in the first thirty seconds, in the same order, in every country he had ever visited: they looked up, they looked at each other, they looked for their children, and then about a third of them made the fatal error of running toward a building.

Buildings were where things fell.

Two houses at the low end of the village came apart while he was watching. Lifted, the way a lid comes off a pot, roofs going first and then the top course of the walls and then the rest, and everything that had been inside them going up in a slow expanding cloud of thatch, table legs, bedding, a copper pot, and at least four people who had run inside eleven seconds earlier because inside is where you go.

He had time to think that they had been badly advised and then he stopped thinking about them, because he had work.

"Draw."

---

The word came out of the ruin of his throat and went nowhere, because there was no air to carry it, and it did not need to be heard by anybody. It was a thing he said to himself. He had been saying it for nine centuries and had long ago stopped being able to work without it, which was a weakness and one he had made peace with.

The energy came back along the gauntlet.

Purple, and wrong for purple, so dense that it read against the black sky as a colour and against his own armour as a hole. It ran up the plates of the vambrace against every rule of running, gathering at the elbow and then at the shoulder and then pulling itself in through the seams of the cuirass to a place under his sternum that had not been a place before he was sealed.

The seal at his throat closed another fraction.

He had four seconds of gathering and he spent all four, and by the third one his breath had gone down to a thread and the edges of his sight had begun to go grainy, and this was the part nobody had ever seen and nobody was going to. A man drawing that much through a body that had been shut has to hold the shut part open with his own will while it happens, and the shut part fights him, and it fights him harder every decade, and Kaalabad had privately worked out somewhere around the fourth century that there was a number of times he could do this and that the number was finite.

Not today's problem.

The mass came together above his gauntlet and it was black. Dark is the wrong word and he had never had a right one. It gave back nothing at all, and the black lines across the sky bent toward it, and the beads of stream-water rising past it went out of their line and fell into it and were gone.

"Star Crusher."

---

It went down.

There was no arc. It left his hand and arrived, and the four hundred faces on the road had time to change and not to move, and the ground under the village compressed before the thing reached it, dirt and slate and standing water all going flat and grey under a pressure that arrived before the object did.

And it stopped.

Kaalabad felt that in his forearm, up through the gauntlet and into the shoulder, a hard reversed shock like driving a spike into a joint you thought was open.

Something had come up over Sum-gol.

Calling it a wall was wrong. He had broken walls and knew what a wall does, which is stand between you and a place and behave like an object. This behaved like a decision. It came up from the valley floor in a single unbroken curve, and where the black mass met it the curve did not resist and did not yield, it simply declined, and about a third of what he had spent went sideways along the surface and out over the eastern ridge and dumped into the Veil in a long spilling smear of light that made the air over four square miles smell of scorched iron.

The rest came back at him.

Some of it, slowly, and enough that he had to take it on the gauntlet a second time, and it drove him up nine feet and he arrested and hung there with his arm numb from the wrist to the neck and the seal at his throat clamped down so hard that for six full seconds he could not draw any air at all.

Below him, the pines that had been in the sky came down.

They came down outside the curve, all of them, a hundred trees and eight thousand tons of frozen earth landing on the eastern slope and the upper road and the terraced yard's outer wall, and the sound of that arrived a full second after the sight of it, and when it cleared the village was still there and the road was not and the yard's wall was gone and there were people under it.

---

Kaalabad got a breath.

He hung in a black sky over a valley he had failed to flatten, with his left arm dead to the shoulder and his throat closed to a straw, and he read the working the way a man reads a hand of cards that has just taken a trick he thought was his.

It was two.

That was the first thing and it took him under a second. There was a braid in it. Two separate structures laid over each other and holding, and they were holding on strength alone, with no fit between them at all, and in three places along the curve he could see where they had been forced to reconcile and had reconciled badly. Whoever had raised it had raised it in a hurry, with somebody they had never done this beside.

The second thing took him longer and pleased him a great deal more.

One of them was Valorin. Concealment worked at a scale that had stopped pretending to be concealment, the whole grammar of it built by people who had spent a thousand years making it expensive to be found and had finally been cornered into using the same principle to make it expensive to be reached.

And the other was Mahuo.

He held on that one. He hung in the air over the ruined road with his arm hanging and he held on it for four seconds, which was longer than he had spent on any single thing since he came up out of the quiet, because it meant that the third item on the list he had recited in that black chamber was standing in the bottom of that valley, on this morning, on a piece of ground he had chosen because the reports said sixty roofs and a school.

Nobody down there was a civilian.

*And they will not have rehearsed. The braid is wrong in three places and neither of them has corrected it, which means neither of them can see the other one's half from inside their own.*

*Whoever is standing under the Mahuo side of that curve has been holding it for eleven seconds and has never once had to hold anything against me.*

The seal at his throat eased by a fraction and let him have half a lungful.

He would not be able to draw again today. That was simply a fact and he took it without any particular feeling, the way he took weather. What he had left was the halberd and his body and the whole rest of the morning, and there was a valley below him with two houses standing in it that had both been on his list, and the coalition Kujo had described was not going to exist for two winters yet, and here were two of its threads in one small place with a bad seam between them.

He looked at the yard.

Forty small figures had been standing on those terraces when he came out of the well, in ranks, being taught something. The outer wall was under a hundred tons of pine and frozen earth. He could see perhaps twenty of them still up and moving on the inner terrace, and a great deal of shouting was happening at the collapsed end, and somebody down there had organised a line and was passing stone hand to hand along it inside forty seconds of the trees landing.

Forty seconds. That was fast. That was drilled.

He filed it, because it was the kind of detail the Generals wanted and the kind he usually failed to bring back.

The road was gone. Two houses at the low end were gone with the people who had run into them, and the eastern slope was a debris field, and the stream had been thrown off its bed and was already carving a new one through what had been somebody's winter cabbage. All of that had happened in under a minute, and none of it had been the working, and none of it had been aimed. It was the overspill from a thing that had failed.

That was the part he would put in front of Kujo if Kujo ever asked, and Kujo would never ask.

*They will count their dead tonight and they will get a number, and the number will be small enough that they will call it a victory, and by the spring somebody in that valley will be saying that the barrier held.*

*The barrier held. And I have not touched the ground yet.*

Kaalabad reached back over his shoulder and took the haft in his right hand and drew the halberd off his back.

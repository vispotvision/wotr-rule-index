# Lion's Bane

*Aetherion Academy · the Crucible floor, Hiromi Mahuo's bout*

---

The gate opened on noise, and Hiromi walked into it with his collar buckled to the throat.

The light hit him first, white off the sand and white off the tiers, and then the roar, a wall of it, thousands of voices coming down off the stands with the weight of surf. The Crucible floor stretched out in front of him in a great raked oval, pale gold, its fortified stone showing through in long grey seams where the sand was thinnest. Ward-lines ran under it. He could feel them through the soles of his boots the way he could feel every working in every room he had ever walked into: the deep steady hum of the arena's protections, inscribed into the bedrock by people who had spent their lives making sure a fight like this one stayed inside the oval and did not climb into the stands. Somewhere above him a voice like a struck bell was calling his name.

"...from the house at Sum-gol, **Hiromi Mahuo**, the Paragon of Dominion!"

A year ago he would have raised both arms. A month ago he would have bowed to the north stand and then the south and then grinned at the east until somebody up there laughed. He did neither. He walked out to his mark with his hands at his sides and his coat swinging at his calves, and the crowd, which had got its breath ready for the show it knew Hiromi Mahuo gave, found it had nothing to spend it on, and the cheer came down off the tiers a little ragged and uncertain and broke up into talk.

His eyes went to the west rail first. Habit. There was a seat there at the end of the Class X row where his father always sat to watch a bout, upright, hands folded over the head of his cane, spectacles in his breast pocket until the moment he needed them.

It was empty.

He looked at it for the space of one breath. Then he looked across the sand.

The boy on the far mark was standing still. Barefoot, brown-skinned, long black hair loose on his shoulders, arms hanging loose at his sides. Hiromi read the posture the way he read every posture before he read anything else in a room, and set the reading aside. Whatever the boy over there was, he would find out the way the crowd would. The name the announcer had given meant nothing to him yet. He knew a stance that wasted nothing.

The bell went.

---

Hiromi did not move.

He had spent his whole life moving. Shouting, laughing, crossing rooms, taking the centre of every corridor and every argument, making so much noise that nobody ever looked at the quiet work underneath. All of that was gone now. He stood on his mark with his weight even on both feet, his hands open at his sides, and he let the world come to him.

It came the way water comes.

That was how he had always felt it, under the noise. Other people saw a room: walls, floor, faces, lamps. He felt a body of water with all of those things floating in it. The Aether was the water. It filled every space, pressed on every surface, ran through the stone and the air and the living bodies in the tiers, and it moved when anything in it moved, in slow currents and eddies and standing waves, the way a pond moves when a fish turns. Every working in the arena was a current in it. Every Crystal in the stands was a stone dropped in it, sending out rings. And Hiromi had felt, since before he could tie his own boots, that the water leaned toward him. His card called it GRASP. The world lined up toward him the way iron filings line up toward a magnet, every loose thread of Aether within ten paces of his body drawn into line with him whether he asked for it or not.

Usually it spilled. That was the trouble with it. It came off him the way heat comes off a stove, in every direction at once, leaning lamp flames and bending gauge-needles and popping the ears of everyone who sat too near him, and on top of it sat the other thing, WEIGHT, his presence pressing down on the room like a lid on a pot, so that small magic could not get started anywhere near him. He had never once in his life been quiet, in his voice or in his Dominion.

Today he folded it in.

He did it slowly. He had practised it alone in the binding circles before dawn for a year, and told no one: not his father, not Geturo, not Lily. He took the field that always came off him like heat and turned it back on itself, the way you turn a cloak inside out. Every line of it ran inward now. He took the Weight that always pressed down on the room around him and drew that inward too, and pressed it down on nothing but himself. Then he held it.

The lamps along the arena wall, which had begun to lean toward him the moment he walked out of the gate, stood up straight. One after another. Their flames came upright on their wicks and burned tall and still and ordinary, as if he were nobody at all. The draw-gauges set into the barrier at every quarter of the oval, which had begun to tick toward his side of the dial, ticked back to centre and stayed there. Up in the front row of the north stand, a man who had his hand to his ear because it had just popped lowered his hand and frowned at it. Nobody else noticed anything.

All of it was still there. All of it was pressing inward, into him, instead of out.

He had a name for that, though no one had ever heard him say it. **The Still Sea.** The whole water of his Dominion drawn down into one body, so that nothing at the surface moved, and everything underneath did.

---

And from the inside, the world looked very different.

When his Dominion stopped spilling, he could see what it had been spilling into. He could see the arena the way the old texts said the world truly was, under its skin of things: an assortment. Aether and Essence, and nothing else. The stone of the floor was Aether settled into the shape of stone and told to stay that way by the ward-lines cut in it. The sand on top was the same, settled looser. The air was Aether so thin it barely held a shape at all. The crowd was ten thousand small hard knots of Essence, each one a Crystal ringing at its own pitch, sitting in the water of the world like stones. Even the light that came down off the sky into the oval was only Aether moving fast in a particular way. Nothing in the whole Crucible was anything except those two things, arranged.

And if everything was only an arrangement, then everything could be arranged again.

That was the thought that had come to him at seven, sitting in his father's practice hall after everyone else had gone home, staring at a binding circle he had just broken by understanding it too well. He had never told anyone. It sat under everything he did. Every grown summoner in that hall had been taught that summoning was a door. You opened it, and something came through. They argued about what came through, and from where, and what it cost, but they all agreed it was a door.

Hiromi had looked at the circle and wondered whether it was a door at all.

*What does it mean,* he asked himself now, standing on the sand, the same question he had been asking since he was seven, *to summon?*

The old answer, the one in every textbook on his father's shelves, had two halves, and the Academy taught them as two different arts. One half was the fishing line. You cast your Dominion outward into the world, a long thin line of your own recognition, and you hooked something that already existed out there, a spirit, an echo, a principle, and you hauled it in and negotiated with it and called it your servant if you were lucky and your master if you were not. The Academy called that Vocatia, the art of recognition, and it was the old family art of the Mahuo, the reason they had been a court line for three hundred years. It was dangerous and it was slow and you could not touch it safely below the tenth Stage.

The other half was the mirror. You did not hook anything. You took a piece of yourself, broke it off your own Crystal, and shaped it into a thing that could stand up and walk around: an eidolon, a construct, a living picture of your own will. The Academy called that Animatria, and every summoner's first construct was one. The textbooks had a line about it he had learned by heart at five. *A summoner's spirits are confessions.* Whatever you broke off yourself to make them, they told the world what you were made of. It cost more Essence than a boy of his Stage had in him, and you could not touch it before the sixth.

He was at the third.

His father had a third answer, and it was the best one Hiromi had heard from any grown summoner. *A Draft-circle is not a door,* Kwon Mu-jin said to every first-year who came to him, and resented that he still had to say it. *It is a request form.* You did not hook a spirit and you did not break off your soul. You asked the Continuum, the great slow body of the world, for the nearest lawful shape it could make for the thing you needed, and if your request was clean the world answered it.

Hiromi had loved that answer. He had built himself on it.

And at seven he had seen what was underneath it.

If the world answered a request, then the world was the one doing the work. And if the world was only an arrangement of Aether and Essence, and his Dominion already pulled the Aether of every room toward him like iron filings toward a magnet, then he did not need to break off any of his own Crystal to make a summons, and he did not need to cast a line into the dark and hope. He needed only to know, exactly, what shape he wanted. Then he could pull the room's own Aether into that shape with the field he already had, and let the world recognise what it was looking at.

The room paid for the body of the summons. All he spent was the pattern.

And the pattern was made of the only two things he had ever truly owned, the two things no textbook had put a price on: memory, and identity. What he had seen. What he was.

*Memory and identity,* he had written in the margin of his first binding text, at seven, in a hand his father later said he could not read for a week afterward. *That is the fuel. Everybody else spends their soul. I spend what I remember.*

He had called it, privately, the **Infinite Summoning Arts**, because a boy of seven does not know yet that you should not call anything infinite. He had never stopped calling it that.

---

His body began to heat.

He felt it start in his chest and go outward, a dry fierce warmth that ran down his arms into his hands and up his throat into his face, as if he had swallowed a coal and it had decided to stay. Sweat broke out along his hairline and on his upper lip. It did not steam. Nothing steamed near him; the Weight he had folded inward still sat on every drop of water within his reach and held it down. The sweat only beaded and ran and stood shining on his skin in the hard light.

Not one thread of Aether left him.

That was the strangeness of it, and he could feel the people who could read such things leaning forward up in the tiers trying to understand it. A practitioner who ran hot always leaked. Heat was what a working gave off when it pushed Aether out through a Shell, the waste of the push, the way a mill wheel warms its axle. A Crystal working this hard should have been throwing off Aether in shimmering sheets, the air bending around him like air above a forge. The air around Hiromi did not bend, and nothing came out of him at all. He was a sealed jar on a fire. Everything that went into him stayed in him, every scrap of the field he had folded inward, and with nowhere to go it did what anything does when it is held and worked and not let out. It became heat. His father's own first law, the one he taught before he taught anything else: you can change a thing into another thing, but you cannot make the sum of it go anywhere. The work had to go somewhere. It went into Hiromi.

He was going to pay for that later, and Lily knew it as well as he did. Holding his field cost his body more than he ever ate. He had eaten one barley cake today, and half of that because his sister made him.

It would do.

And the arena began to shake.

It started under his feet, a faint trembling in the stone like the trembling in a bridge when a heavy cart goes over, and then it was everywhere. The whole floor of the Crucible was shaking. He could hear the fortified stone groaning in its seams, a deep grinding note too low to be a sound and too loud to be anything else. The water in the troughs at the barrier walls jumped in its stone basins. Up in the stands people grabbed the rails, and the timber under them hummed, and somebody dropped a cup and it rolled down three tiers and nobody went after it.

And there was nothing to see.

No pressure wave rolling out from him. No shimmer. No shining lines of force. The crowd up there had watched Pressure in this arena all day, the heavy visible push of a practitioner's presence bending lamp flames and knocking the weak to their knees. This was nothing like that. The arena was shaking itself apart and there was nothing at all to see doing it, only a boy in a long black coat standing very still on his mark with sweat running down his face.

Hiromi knew what it was. He had felt it once before, on a much smaller scale, sitting as a child against the iron casing of a draw-main substation in the old quarter at Sum-gol and feeling the whole great iron body of the thing hum against his back. His father had explained it to him on the walk home. When you pull hard enough on the Aether in a worked thing, iron or stone or anything with ward-lines cut through it, the worked thing changes shape a hair's breadth to follow the pull, and changes back, and changes again, a hundred times a breath. It shivers. It sings. The big iron mains do it all the time and the Guild engineers have a name for it they argue about. He had never forgotten it.

The Crucible floor was the most heavily worked stone in the Academy. Every inch of it was cut with ward-lines. And Hiromi's whole Dominion was drawn down tight into one body standing on top of it, pulling inward on every line of Aether in reach, and the stone was shivering under that pull the way an iron main shivers.

He looked down and saw the sand moving.

It was moving the way flour moves on a struck drumhead. His father had shown him once with a brass plate and a violin bow. The loose grains on the surface jumped away from the places that shook hardest and gathered where the stone shook least, and the places that shook least were lines. The lines drew a figure. All across the Crucible floor the raked sand slid into thin pale ridges and curves and knots, a vast shifting web drawn over the whole oval, the stone's own shivering made visible, its nodes thickening into bright little heaps where several lines crossed.

Points of light, joined by lines.

He could have laughed. It looked like the sky.

---

Then he closed his eyes and looked at the sky he carried.

It was there, where it always was, behind his eyes, in the place no textbook had a name for. He called it the **Firmament**, when he called it anything. Thousands of points of light hung in the dark. Each one was a single shape he had seen and understood all the way through, stored exactly, ready.

There was the bullet train he had watched pull out of the station at nine, every line of its body and the dead-straight rail it could run and nothing else. There was the pale warden from the summer he was eight, broad and faceless behind its great shield, which a masked boy with no magic had put a palm through six days ago. There was Juggernaut's Fist. There were a thousand smaller things, every bird he had watched long enough, every animal in every book of plates in his father's study, every machine and every bridge and every statue in the courtyard at Sum-gol, all of it held, all of it clean, each one a star in the dark that he could reach for and pull down into the world.

He did not reach for the train. It ran one road and the road had been bent. He did not reach for the warden. Its shield was a memory of being beaten.

He reached past all of them, to the one that had been waiting the longest.

It was the stone lion at the gate of the house at Sum-gol. Nobody in Hon-guk had ever seen a true lion. This was a haetae, the old lion-dog of the Korean gates, carved out of grey granite three hundred years ago. Its mane curled like a breaking wave. A horn stood on its brow, and its mouth was open on a roar no one had ever heard. The old stories said a haetae could tell the guilty from the innocent at a glance and that fire would not cross a gate it guarded. He had climbed on it at three and fallen off it at four and sat in its shadow at six, the evening after the binding circle, while his father stood beside him not saying anything, one warm hand on his back.

He had looked at that lion every day of his childhood. He had looked at it until he knew it better than his own face. And in the years since he had added to it every lion he ever found in a book of plates, the true beasts of the southern grasslands his father's books described: the weight of them, the reach of a foreleg, how a cat that heavy gathers itself to spring, four hundredweight of cat coming off the ground at once, the claws that slid out of their sheaths as long as a finger joint. He had laid those over the stone lion of his gate the way a carver lays gold leaf over wood, until the star he carried was both: the guardian that judged, and the hunter that killed.

It was the brightest star in his sky.

He opened his eyes.

And the stars were in the arena.

They came out over the Crucible floor the way stars come out over a field at dusk. One, then three, then all of them at once, where none had been. Points of pale gold light hanging in the air above the shaking sand, scattered in a great loose spiral that reached from the middle of the oval almost to the far wall. Each one was a knot of Aether pulled tight, the room's own water drawn down by his folded field into a single point and held there, burning with the pressure of it. They shimmered. They glittered exactly the way stars do on a cold night when the air between you and them is moving. Lines of fainter light ran between them, joining them, the same lines the sand was drawing on the floor below, and slowly, one line at a time, the scatter of points began to resolve into a figure.

And still nothing came out of him. That was the thing the readers in the stands would argue about for a week. The stars shimmered like the night and not one drop of Hiromi's Essence went into them. There was no bright braid of a summoner's tone running out from his body to his construct, the way every summoner's tone ran out to what he called. They belonged to the arena. He had only told the arena where to stand.

He spoke three syllables under his breath, very quietly, into the shaking. They were glyphs, and he had chained them so many times in the circles before dawn that they came out of him now as easily as his own name.

**[Ma]**, Recall. A root of Maelor's, the Archon of Memory, the glyph that keeps the soul-true record of what was. It was the name of the star he had chosen: *this one, exactly, as I have it.*

**[Dom]**, Dominion. A root of Irath's, whose work is command and hierarchy, the glyph that stabilises a chain of command and stretches its reach. It was the field he had folded into himself, unfolded now and laid over the arena: *here, all of this, is mine to arrange.*

**[Ath]**, Command. Irath's again, the oldest glyph in the summoner's grammar, the one that compels obedience in a bound thing: *be what I remember, and do what I say.*

The Codex writes the last two into Judicium and the family of the lightning, Fulguria, whose law is the law of fields and charges and the lines of force between them. Every Mahuo summoner who ever lived has carried that resonance. His father carries it in every construct he has ever called. Hiromi had only ever understood it one way: his Dominion was a field, and a field was a thing you could aim.

He drew breath.

"Infinite Summoning Arts," Hiromi said.

His voice did not rise. It went out across the sand quiet and level, and in the shaking hush of the arena it carried to every rail.

"Lion's Bane."

---

The roar came out of the stars.

It came out of all of them at once, every point of light in the spiral giving it up together, so that it seemed to come from the whole sky over the arena and from nowhere in particular. It arrived whole, a single vast sound like a mountain splitting. It was low enough at its bottom to be felt in the gut more than heard, and harsh enough at its top to scrape the inside of the skull. It was every lion in every book of plates and the stone lion of Sum-gol's gate, which had been opening its mouth on that roar for three hundred years without making a sound, finally making it.

The arena broke under it.

The fortified stone of the Crucible floor had been shivering under his pull for half a minute, singing in its seams, the sand dancing on it in its shapes, and the roar came down on top of that singing like a hammer on a bell that is already ringing. The floor cracked. The cracks ran out from under the spiral of stars in a great starburst across the oval, fast, branching, with a sound like river ice going in the spring, and then the whole top skin of the Crucible floor came apart into plates, slabs, shards, fragments the size of doors and the size of fists, lifting and tilting on the shaking bedrock under them. Sand poured off them into the gaps. The ward-lines underneath flared through every crack in a hard white-blue glare as the protections woke all at once and took the shock and rang, and the ringing of the wards went up into the stands, and every glass and cup and lens in the Crucible shivered on the same note.

Up on the tiers a thousand people flinched at once. In the front row of the north stand a dog on a leash flattened itself to the boards, and the man who had been frowning at his ear put both hands over both ears.

And out of the stars, the Lion came down.

It came slowly, the way a figure comes out of a sketch, line by line, as the stars that made it pulled tight and joined and filled. First the back. A long curve of gold light ran from a point high over the oval down to a point near the sand, the spine of something enormous. Then the shoulders, heavy, knots of muscle gathering out of the light under a sculptor's thumb. Then the forelegs, each as thick as a column in the Academy's great hall, ending in paws as wide as cartwheels. Then the mane, which was a wave, a crest of pale gold light curling back off the neck and breaking and curling again, the granite wave of the gate-lion of Sum-gol made of fire. Then the head. The broad flat skull of the great southern cat, and on the brow, between eyes that were two of the brightest stars of the spiral, the short blunt horn of the haetae.

It filled in as it formed. The light thickened from lines into planes and from planes into a body, gold and white and shot through with the faint blue of the ward-glare from below, solid now, real, heavy, the Aether of the arena packed into a living shape so densely that the air around it bent. It stood at the heart of the shattered oval with its forepaws on two tilted slabs of the broken floor, and the slabs sank under it with a grinding crunch. It stood half again the height of the barrier wall at the shoulder. It was very slowly opening its mouth.

It was the first time today he had called it. It would never be weaker than this. His card had a name for that too, ECHO, the family's old rule that every time a Mahuo calls the same thing in one day it comes back louder, until it comes back too loud and tears itself apart. The first call costs what a first call costs.

A first call was enough.

The cost landed. He felt the warmth in his chest go from a coal to a forge. Something gave behind his nose and the blood came, the way it always came, warm over his upper lip and into the corner of his mouth, iron and salt. His knees wanted to fold. He did not let them. His hands, which had been open at his sides, had closed into fists without his deciding it, and his nails were cutting into his palms. He breathed through it the way his mother had taught him to breathe through a push-up past counting.

*This one's for you, Father,* he thought. Not Teacher. Here, in his own head, there was nobody to call him Teacher for.

The Lion turned its great head toward the far mark.

---

"Cleave..."

The word went out of him as hardly more than a breath. It trailed. It was the voice he used for himself when something was too large for the noise, one word with no end on it.

And everything around the far mark began to close.

He felt it as a pull in his own body, the field he had folded inward letting go of him and reaching out across the broken floor in one direction only, toward the place where the barefoot boy stood. All the Aether of the arena was suddenly running one way. It ran toward that place and it ran inward on itself as it went, drawing tight around it the way a drawstring draws the mouth of a bag.

There was a thing his father had shown him in the alchemy hall, with a glass tube of thin gas and a jar of lightning. When you drive a great enough current down a column, the current makes its own field around itself, and that field squeezes inward on the column from every side at once, harder the stronger the current, until the whole column is crushed down toward a single bright line. A pinch, the Guild engineers called it. You could see it in the tube. The glow drew in on itself until it was as thin as a hair, and then thinner, and burned.

That was what the arena was doing around the far mark now.

The air there went strange first. It thickened and wavered, the light passing through it bending in toward a centre, so that the far barrier wall behind that place seemed to lean in and stretch, the way a face stretches in the back of a spoon. The loose sand around the mark lifted in thin sheets and slid sideways through the air toward the same line. The small broken fragments of the shattered floor nearest the mark tilted on their edges and began to scrape across the stone toward it. Behind it a lamp's flame lay down flat. The draw-gauge set in the wall there slammed its needle to the end of its dial and stuck. Every line of Aether in the place, every thread of the world's water, every working and every ward and every loose glimmer of somebody's spent power, all of it was being pulled in and pulled tight toward the one line that ran from the Lion's raised paw to the ground where the boy stood.

It was everything that place was made of: the Aether that held its stone together, the field of any working that stood there, the laws that let a thing there be what it was, the Dominion of it, every function of it, drawn together into a single narrowing line, the way the glow in his father's tube was drawn into a single burning thread.

The Lion had made a projectile out of the place, and its claw was the point.

It gathered itself.

Hiromi watched it do it the way he had watched it in the books of plates a thousand times: the great hindquarters sinking, the weight of it all going back onto the haunches, the forelegs bending, the spine curving down into a bow. The slabs under its hind feet cracked and drove down into the bedrock. The whole body of light compressed itself like a spring. The mane-wave curled back off its neck and broke.

And it jumped.

It went up off the shattered floor in a single uncoiling of gold, straight up, higher than the barrier, higher than the first tier of the stands, the wind of its going lifting Hiromi's white hair straight off his head and throwing grit into his face, and at the top of the leap it hung for one heartbeat against the sky with its forepaws spread and its claws out, every one of them as long as a man's arm and drawn out of their sheaths of light. The crowd's breath went out of the tiers all at once.

Then it came down.

It came down on the line. On the narrowed, drawn, pinched line of everything that place was made of, with all of its weight and all of the arena's Aether drawn into the point of a single paw, and the paw came down in a long slashing arc through the air toward the far mark, where the line ended.

And where the claw passed, it left a hole.

It ripped through the air like a knife through a drum's skin, and the air did not close behind it. Behind each claw, for a long moment, there hung a trailing wound in the world: a long dark tear in the air, a tube of nothing where air and Aether had been pulled into the claw's point and flung forward and not yet had time to rush back in. It was warm. Hiromi felt the warmth of it from across the broken floor, a breath of heat against his face, because everything that had been squeezed into that line had been squeezed hard, and anything squeezed that hard burns. The air at the torn edges of the wound shimmered, glowing faintly gold. Grit hung in the tears, turning, lit from within.

Up in the tiers the roar had stopped and not yet started again.

Five long warm wounds trailed in the air behind the falling paw, raking down toward the far mark.

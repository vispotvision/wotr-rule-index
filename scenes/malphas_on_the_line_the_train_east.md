# Malphas on the Line — The Train East

The compartment smells of coal smoke and old leather and the particular staleness of a space that has been sealed too long against weather. The train has been climbing since dawn, the grade steepening as the line pushes toward the edge of the inner territories where the draw-grid thins and the standpipes grow sparse and the gauge-housings along the track stand dark and unstaffed. The vibration of the rails has settled into the rhythm of wheels working against track that has not been maintained in the way track closer to the capital is maintained. Every third joint is louder than it should be.

Malphas sits with his back to the direction of travel. He is a tall man, narrow through the chest and wider across the shoulders than the narrowness suggests, the proportions of someone who spent the first half of his life carrying things and the second half choosing not to. His face is long. The jaw clean. The skin the colour of steeped tea left to cool, and the veins at his temples are visible in the lamp-light, blue-grey, close to the surface. His hair is grey at the temples and black everywhere else, cut short enough that it does not move when the train sways. His hands are the most notable thing about him. Scholar's hands, long-fingered and precise, but the knuckles are thick and the nails trimmed to the quick in the way of a man who works with substances that punish inattention. He wears a dark coat of good cloth, unembroidered, buttoned to the throat. No Guild seal. No insignia of any kind. The absence is its own statement.

Across from him, Velder Crace finishes his cigar and grinds it out against the brass lip of the ashtray bolted to the wall. He is younger than Malphas by twenty years and heavier by three stone, a broad man with a broad face and the particular quality of patience that comes from having been someone's student long enough to know when the lesson is still arriving. His coat is cheaper and less clean. A burn scar runs from his left ear to the hinge of his jaw, the skin pulled tight and shining, the kind of mark left by something that was not supposed to be near a face at the moment it was. He learned from it. He did not learn enough to stop smoking.

"Do you believe it will hold, Lord Malphas?" Velder's voice carries the careful weight of a man who has been holding a question for three hundred miles. "The process. At the scale we are approaching."

A porter knocks once and enters without waiting. He is short, efficient, grey-haired, wearing the dark uniform of a railway line that does not advertise which railway line it is. He carries a tray: black coffee in a steel pot, two cups without saucers, a plate of dried meat and hard bread and a small covered dish of something that smells of vinegar and pepper. He sets the tray on the folding table between them with the precision of a man who has been told not to linger. He does not linger. The door closes. The lock does not engage.

Malphas pours his own coffee. The liquid is dark enough that it does not reflect the lamp. He drinks without tasting it, the automatic gesture of a man who stopped treating food as anything other than maintenance years ago.

"The question assumes I am building something that needs to hold." He sets the cup down. His voice is unhurried, the cadence of a lecturer addressing a student who has asked the right question in the wrong frame. "We are not constructing a bridge, Velder. What my team has accomplished is a challenge to the principles this world treats as permanent." He looks at the window. The landscape outside is grey and sparse, the grass short, the trees stunted, the sky the colour of unworked tin. A single standpipe stands in a field, its gauge-housing dark. "Alchemy accepts the boundary between the living and the inert as fixed. The Wellspring taxonomy treats Mortalis as a terminal function. Death is where the mechanism stops." His eyes return to Velder. "I am asking whether the mechanism is required to stop there, and the results suggest that it is not."

The train sways. The coffee in the pot tilts and does not spill.

"The reanimation architecture is stable in four of the seven subjects who survived the full process." He tears a piece of the dried meat and eats it without ceremony. "Biological function persists. Crystal architecture persists. The original Essence does not." Something moves in his face. Not regret. Recognition. The expression of a man who has looked at a cost long enough to stop flinching at it and has not decided whether that makes him honest or dangerous. "The original Essence is the price. I have made peace with the price because the alternative is a world where every village outside a government draw-grid watches its children die in cores that no Guild arrives in time to contain."

Velder shifts his weight. The bench creaks.

"The Accord's guilds have been contracted in the eastern district." He says it flat, information delivered with the understanding that the man across from him will decide what it means. "Three independent operations. Containment. The emergence rate has been noticed."

"The emergence rate was always going to be noticed." Malphas picks up the cup. "A Crystal that has been restructured bleeds residual Saturation into the local Aether, and the Saturation breeds. That is the exchange." He drinks. "You cannot rewrite the architecture of a living soul without the room registering what has been rewritten."

*And the room is registering. Sooner than I had accounted for.*

Outside the window, a settlement passes. Three buildings and a standpipe, the pipe's gauge-housing dark, the brass dull, no seal visible on the access panel. A village outside the grid. The kind of place where a core can open and close and kill everyone inside it before anyone with the authority to contain it learns that it existed.

"The benefactors are satisfied with the current rate of progress." Malphas sets the cup down. The statement carries neither pride nor reassurance. It is a fact filed beside the emergence rate and the survival ratio and the number of subjects who did not survive. "Their patience is not permanent, but it does not need to be. It needs to outlast the gap between where the process is and where the process becomes scalable."

He looks at Velder directly.

"You are asking me whether we will be found before the process is ready." Malphas does not frame it as accusation. He frames it as a diagnosis, one professional reading another. "That is the question underneath the question, and it is fair."

Velder does not deny it. His hand goes to the burn scar on his jaw, the gesture of a man who knows what being caught by your own work feels like and has the face to prove it.

"The Night Register has been active in three of our operational districts." His voice drops, not for secrecy but for the precision that thin walls require. "An Arbiter. Accord-dispatched. Travelling with a woman my sources describe as young, credentialed through the Office of Attribution, and carrying instruments that are not standard Guild issue."

Malphas says nothing for a long moment. The train rocks over a junction and the coffee pot rattles against the tray.

"Attribution." He repeats the word. Not alarm. Assessment. "That is Ashmore's division."

"It is."

In the corridor outside, a conductor passes. His shadow moves across the frosted glass of the compartment door, the shape of a man in a peaked cap carrying a brass lantern and a clip of tickets, and the shadow does not pause. The footsteps recede toward the front of the train where the engine noise swallows everything smaller than itself.

"And the Inquisition?" Malphas lifts the covered dish and looks at what is underneath. He sets the lid back without eating.

"Moving. They have been seen in the same districts." Velder reaches for the bread and breaks it in half. "Their stated purpose is elimination. Practitioners below a threshold they define and do not publish." He eats the bread. The crumbs fall on his coat and he does not brush them away. "Their presence complicates the operational landscape. The Accord will have difficulty separating our footprint from theirs."

"Or they will separate both without difficulty and find everyone." Malphas folds his hands on the table, fingers interlaced, thumbs parallel. The posture of a man who has sat across from people who wanted to kill him and found the conversation instructive. "The Inquisition is not our concern, Velder. The Inquisition is a symptom of the same disease we are attempting to cure. Practitioners are dying in villages the government refuses to protect, and a holy order has concluded that the solution is to ensure there are no practitioners left to die." His mouth thins. "We are attempting the other solution. The one where the dead do not stay dead and the cores do not stay open."

The train noise fills the silence the way water fills a bowl.

"Lord Malphas." Velder sets the bread down without finishing it. "The girl. From the village."

"What about her."

"She is alive."

"She is functional." Malphas's voice does not change. "Whether she is alive in the way you mean the word is a question I do not yet have the instrumentation to answer with the precision your conscience requires." He unfolds his hands and places them flat on the table, palms down, fingers spread. "The Shell is intact. The biological systems continue. The working sustains what the original Essence sustained. Whether the girl who lived inside that body before the working began is still present behind the Shell is something I think about." He looks at his student. "I think about it more than you imagine. And I continue because the alternative is a world where she dies in a core at thirteen without anyone coming, and the next child after her, and the one after that, until the cores have eaten every village that cannot afford a Guild contract and the Accord shrugs and calls it natural attrition."

Velder picks up the rest of his bread. He eats it. He does not ask another question.

A woman in the next compartment coughs, the sound muffled by the wall. Through the partition, the low murmur of conversation and the clink of a spoon against porcelain. Ordinary passengers travelling the same line toward the same edge, unaware that the man in the next compartment is the reason three guild contracts in the eastern district are not holding and an Arbiter from the Night Register is asking questions about origins in an academy arena.

Malphas pours a second cup of coffee and holds it between both hands. The steam curls against the window glass and vanishes. Outside, a gauge-housing stands alone in a field of short grass, its brass dark, its needle still.

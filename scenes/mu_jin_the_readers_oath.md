# Mu-jin: The Reader's Oath

Mu-jin hears the last three words go into the stone and stay there. *Failures leave records.* He has written that sentence himself, in a margin, at twenty, and been proud of it for a week.

He takes the lens out of his coat, turns it once between finger and thumb, and puts it back without using it. Nothing on the gallery needs magnifying. It is the habit of a man deciding how much of a sentence he is permitted to finish.

"I'll tell you what I'm allowed to," he says. "Allowed is the word, Grand Mage, and I'd ask you to hear it as exact."

The staff comes off the floor a finger's breadth and settles again, and the draw-hum under the gallery dips around it and comes back.

"The Zettari keepers take two oaths. The first is sworn at the door by every reader they admit, and I swore it at twenty-three with my hand flat on a ledger that had four hundred years of other hands on it before mine. The second is sworn in a room past the stacks, by the keepers to each other. I was never asked into that room." He says it plainly, the way he would read out the name of a reagent he does not stock. "So I know what a reader is shown, and I am sworn never to reproduce the working methods. Those clauses exist so the mechanism can't be carried out of the building in a clever boy's head and used on a living person. I agreed with them then. I still do. What sits behind the reader's shelves I can't tell you, because I never saw it. They keep themselves very close, and three generations of Board writs have learned the same."

He lifts his left hand. The two pale scars on the inside of the forearm catch the high window light, and he uses the hand to count on.

"The oath covers the method. It says nothing about the paper. The paper I can give you all of." One finger. "The reader's copy is a fair copy. One clerk's hand throughout, iron-gall ink gone brown the way it does after a century, a committee hand with the long descender they taught in the old Concord schools. Clean. Nothing struck through. A clean copy of a record of failures means somebody made the failures tidy before they let readers near them."

Two fingers.

"The margins carry a second hand. Smaller, lamp-black ink, which doesn't brown, so it reads as newer than it is. That hand corrects figures. It never argues with the text. It changes a number and moves on, the way a senior corrects an apprentice's arithmetic without comment. I took it for a later keeper when I was twenty-three." He pauses on that. "I'm less sure of it at thirty-eight."

Three fingers.

"In the third volume a gathering has been cut out of the binding. Eight leaves, by the stubs, taken close to the spine with a sharp knife and a steady hand. The stubs are still there. On four of them you can read the first letter or two of each line where the knife went just shy of the gutter." His eye goes somewhere past the railing and does not see the arena. "I copied those fragments into a notebook the week I found them, because I was young enough to think I'd work out what the leaves had said. I never did. I still have the notebook."

The hand comes down to the staff.

"And one thing more, since you asked for anything that knew more than the page admitted." He looks back at Xhem. "Failures leave records, you said. Correct." The teacher's word is out before he can stop it, and something close to embarrassment crosses his mouth and goes. "Readers leave them too. Every reader signs the roll at the door, the date and the shelf-mark of every volume they call up. My name is on that roll under the third volume. So is the name of whoever read it after me, if anyone did, and whoever read it before. The keepers will not open that roll to anybody outside the second oath. But it exists, and it is kept in ink, and the Zettari have never in their history thrown a page away."

He lets it sit. Below, the anchor-point hums alone in the emptied section.

"You'll have the notebook, the shelf-marks and everything I can remember of the hands in writing by the morning bell. I'll draw the stubs at their true size."

The next part costs him. It shows in the time he takes before it, and in the way his thumb finds the knot of the binding over his right eye and presses it, the old habit from Rimward, from the first year when the socket ached in the cold.

"As for the children." His voice drops out of the lecturing register and into the one his family hears. "You're right. And I'd rather be told it on a gallery than learn it at a bedside. I asked for them because I'd sooner have them in a room where I can see the door." A breath. "That's the fear. You named its coat correctly. I've worn it since the eldest could walk, and I stopped noticing I had it on." The corner of his mouth moves. "Hold me to that. Hold me to it first, before them."

Then his attention goes past everybody, and the stillness that comes over him is the one his students know means he has seen the wrong link in a chain.

The light off the anchor crystal lies across the arena stone in five broken pieces. The clouds are moving outside the high windows. Four of the pieces are moving with them, drifting slowly over the flags the way sunlight drifts across a floor in the afternoon.

The fifth one does not move at all.

Mu-jin says nothing about it. He only watches it for the length of a pulse, and then another, the way he watched Xanelor's last arrow in the air. Then he looks back at the Grand Mage.

"Five pieces of light down there. Count how many are keeping up with the clouds." He inclines his head, short and correct, the bow of a man taking leave of the one who holds the room. "My wife is in the infirmary with my eldest. One of us ought to be the calm one, and it's never going to be her."

---

The service stair behind the gallery drops in a tight spiral of worn stone, and it has always carried sound. Some mason four hundred years dead cut the well so that a proctor at the bottom could hear the gallery at the top. Every student in the Academy learns it by the second week of the first term.

Hiromi is sitting on the ninth step down, knees up.

He has the colour of a boy who spent everything he owned in a ring an hour ago. His white hair has gone flat with sweat at the temples, and the skin under the red eyes is bruised-looking and thin, the grey of weak tea. The three old cuts across the bridge of his nose have been cleaned by somebody. The fresh one has been left. His hands are laced round his knees and they are shaking a little, fine and steady, in the way a pan lid chatters on a low boil, and he has laced them tighter to hide it.

He does not pretend he has not been listening. He grins, and the grin still reaches every corner of his face, although it takes him visibly longer to get it there.

"So," Hiromi says. "Hmm." He says the hmm out loud, as a word, rolling it. "I get to learn how to investigate worked phenomena. From an investigator. Ex-investigator." He waves a hand and the shake shows, and he puts the hand back on his knee. "Retired. Whatever you call a man whose seal the Board still salutes when he signs his name."

Mu-jin stops two steps above him.

"That's pretty damn cool, Teacher." It comes out loud because loud is the only size Hiromi's voice comes in, and it bounces down the well and back up. "That's genuinely, truly, damn cool. I wouldn't mind lending the man my totally awesome talents at all. He's lucky, actually. I'm expensive." The grin widens. "I'll do him a rate."

"Nobody's lending anybody." Mu-jin comes down the last two steps and sits on the eighth, which puts his knee level with his son's shoulder. His voice has stayed in the family register. "You heard all of it, so you heard this part too. No one in this Academy is being handed round like a set of tongs. If you want it, you ask him yourself. If he wants you, he'll tell you after he's spoken with you, and not with me. And the first time you decide a procedure is only an efficiency problem nobody's solved yet, he sends you back to class." He tilts his head. "That rule isn't mine. I'd have made it harsher."

"I would *never*."

"You would before lunch."

Hiromi laughs, the whole-body laugh, and it catches halfway and turns into a cough, and he bends round the cough with one hand flat on his chest. Mu-jin waits. Then he reaches into his coat, takes out a folded twist of waxed paper, and puts it on the step between them. Barley cake, pressed with honey and sesame, the kind the cook in the east kitchens makes for the lower forms and sells to everyone else at twice the price.

"Eat that before you stand up."

"I'm not hungry."

"I didn't ask you. You've gone the colour of the step." Mu-jin taps the waxed paper once with one finger. "An empty Crystal pays full price for the first call and every call after. You know that, because I taught you that, and then I watched you do it anyway in front of two hundred people."

Hiromi eyes the barley cake like a draw-gauge he doesn't trust. Then he unwraps it and bites into it, and his eyes shut for a moment against the sweetness in spite of himself.

"There are three Class S on the infirmary steps," he says round the mouthful, quieter now, because the real things always come out quieter from him. "Pretending to wait for someone. The tall one's been there since Geturo's match. His weight's on his back foot the whole time, like he's ready to leave the second somebody asks him what he's doing." He swallows. "Nobody's asked him."

Mu-jin looks down the well, as if he could see through four hundred years of stone to the east range.

"Finish the cake," he says. "Then walk with me. You can look at him on the way in. Don't let him see you do it."

---

The infirmary smells of carbolic, boiled calico and the iron stove in the corner, where a kettle has been kept at a simmer since the tournament bracket was pinned up. Under all that is the sweet scorched smell of a burn dressing, and the faint mineral bite of foreign Essence, which Mu-jin's nose files and does not like. Along the long wall six cots stand under six high windows. Two are occupied. In the far one a lower-form girl with her forearm strapped to a board is asleep with her mouth open, and a brass draw-gauge ticks on the sill above her, measuring nothing, because nobody has remembered to switch it off.

The three Class S boys on the outer steps shift their feet as Mu-jin and Hiromi pass them. The tall one looks at Hiromi. Hiromi looks at the tall one's boots, and then at his hands, and then past him, and does not stop walking.

Frithia can be heard from the door.

"Twice. *Twice.* You put it out, and then you rolled on it and put it back in again, and you're lying there as if that were a thing a person decides to do on purpose."

She is standing over the second cot with a roll of calico in one hand and her son's arm in the other. Mu-jin's wife stands a full hand taller than he does, long in the bone and angular, bent over the cot in an infirmary apron with a long rust-brown smear across the bib. The ringed-seal hat she stitched herself is pushed back off her forehead. Her brown hair is pinned up tight under it and coming down anyway at the nape in two strands, and the four blue lines on her chin stand out dark. Her face is Lily's face before Lily sharpened it: the high flat cheekbones, the jaw that comes to a point, and the wide mouth that Geturo also got, which is set now in a line so hard it has gone pale at the edges.

Her hands are doing something else entirely.

While her voice goes on, her fingers bring the calico round under Geturo's elbow and up across his chest with a gentleness that takes no notice of anything she is saying. She lifts the elbow the width of a thumb to pass the roll under it, and stops at the first catch of his breath, and waits, and goes on only when his breath goes on.

"Eleven months," she says. "*Eleven months*, and not one word to your father. Not one to me."

"You'd have told him," Geturo says.

He says it to the ceiling. He is lying propped on two bolsters with his Academy blacks cut off down the right side and his shoulder bare, swollen and mottled already, going plum-coloured over the round of the joint. His right hand lies open on the blanket in a wet dressing, the palm burned in a long clean stripe where the vial went cold against it. His face has gone the grey of old candle wax. He has the stillness Mu-jin has seen on him since he was four, the stillness of a boy lying under a table while the adults above it argue. *Refuses unnecessary attention*, someone wrote once in a school report, and Mu-jin remembers laughing, and then remembers not laughing.

"I would have *told* him," Frithia agrees, "because that's what you do when your son is building a thing in a drawer that could eat his arm off."

"It didn't."

"It nearly did!"

Geturo sighs. It is a perfected sigh, thirteen years in the practising, and it is the sigh of the only person in the family who ever has to walk the outside of a corridor. Then he sees his father in the doorway and goes very still, and the sigh stops halfway.

The infirmarian is at the washstand by the stove, scrubbing his forearms to the elbow in water that steams. He is a broad bald man in shirtsleeves and a leather apron, sixty or near it, with forearms like a cooper's and a Stannvaard burr worn down by thirty years south. He does not look round.

"Kwon," he says to the basin. "Capsule's torn, like I said. It'll hold if he lets it. Sling a month, nothing above the shoulder till the leaves turn. The hand's clean, and it'll scar." He shakes his hands dry, one sharp flick each. "That hum in his chest's nothing. Foreign stuff always sits loud a day or two. It'll settle by the morning, and you'll never know it was there."

Mu-jin looks at his son's chest, where the doubled note sits under the breastbone like a second pulse that has not agreed on its time with the first. He says nothing to the infirmarian.

At the foot of the bed, on a three-legged stool that she has somehow made look like a lecturer's chair, sits Madam Ha Seol-ok.

She is exactly as she was at the service door: very small, very straight, in the old green-brown gown she has gathered into her lap. Her white hair is pulled back so hard it lifts her brows. The little bottle of blue Draft hangs on its chain against her breastbone. Her knotted hands rest one over the other on the head of a cane Mu-jin has never once seen her lean on. She has been watching Geturo the way she watched the Constrictor on the sand, and the Dawi orderly, a square red-bearded man with a split ear and a clay pipe cold in his teeth, has placed himself against the wall behind her, as far from Frithia as the room allows.

*She's been here since they carried him in*, Mu-jin thinks. *Frithia's let her stay. That's worse than if she'd thrown her out.*

Frithia turns. She sees Hiromi first, at his father's hip with sesame on his lip and the grey still in his face, and something in her shoulders comes down a fraction. She sees Mu-jin second.

"You," she says. "You let him."

"I didn't know," Mu-jin says, and it is the same sentence he gave Madam Ha in the arena, and it has not improved since.

"You didn't *look.*" She pulls the sling's knot tight at Geturo's good shoulder with one short hard tug, which is the first thing her hands have done all afternoon that agrees with her voice. "Thirty years reading everybody's workings in every room you walk into, and you never once opened your own son's drawer."

"Now, now." Mu-jin comes to the bedside and stands where the light from the window falls across the sling. "He hid it well. That's to his credit and not to mine."

"*Credit*," Frithia says.

Geturo is looking at his father. He has Frithia's mouth, wide and set, and he holds it very straight.

"Grand Mage says I can help," he says. "After the Head Physician. With the investigation. The anchor." It is not a question and he does not lift the end of it. "Hiromi's face said so. He was on the stairs."

"I was *resting* on the stairs."

"You were listening on the stairs."

"I was resting *audibly* on the stairs."

"When you're cleared," Mu-jin says, over both of them. "And not a day before, and not because of what you did out there. He was very clear on that, and I agree with him." He looks down at the burned palm, the neat, clean stripe of it. "He wants you for what you saw that the rest of us have stopped seeing. So do I. That means you have to still be able to see in a month."

Frithia makes a sound in her throat that is not agreement. She sits down on the edge of the cot, at last, all at once, like someone cutting a rope. Her hand goes to Geturo's hair and pushes it off his forehead, once, and comes away.

Madam Ha has not moved in all of this. Now she lifts one finger off the head of her cane.

"Kwon," she says. Her voice is dry and small. It carries to the far window anyway. "The Grand Mage may have your son for his investigation when the physician says so. I don't argue with the Grand Mage." The finger turns, and points at Geturo, and holds there. "But the boy built a summons with a vein in it, alone, in a drawer, from a working you did not teach him. That makes him mine as well. I'll have him in my room the first morning after the tournament, sling or no sling, and you'll not be in the room when I do."

## Author notes

**What changed**
- **The beat's oath line** was hard to follow: he was sworn to secrecy, yet never inducted into the order. It now works as two oaths. Mu-jin swore the readers' oath at the door but was never asked into the keepers' own oath. That keeps the meaning: he knows the documents and not the order's inner methods, and the order stays closed.
- **Answering Xhem:** Mu-jin gives Xhem everything he can about the physical records: the handwriting, a second hand's marginal notes, and the eight leaves cut from the third volume. He also gives him a new lead: every reader signs the reading-room roll, so the roll holds the names of whoever else read that volume. He accepts Xhem's warning about fear and the children, then hands him an unexplained clue: one of the five patches of light thrown by the anchor crystal isn't moving with the clouds.
- **Hiromi:** the beat's lines are kept ("pretty damn cool", "totally awesome talents"), now loud, cocky, and paid for, since he is still drained from his match.
- **NPCs:** three return from the arena scenes: the infirmarian, the Dawi orderly and Madam Ha Seol-ok. The Class S scouts on the infirmary steps are the Class S threat to Class X, the Front, moving along.
- **The session's wrong fact:** the infirmarian says the foreign-Essence hum will settle by morning. It won't, and nobody corrects him.

**Made up here, not yet ruled:** the Zettari keepers' two oaths, the reader's roll, the second hand in lamp-black ink, the cut leaves and Mu-jin's notebook, the stair that carries sound, the unmoving patch of light. Madam Ha claiming Geturo ends the post on her line. What Mu-jin says back is the player's.
- **Frithia's body** follows the Frithia ruling (frithia-timeline-2026-09-28 §5): tall, taller than Mu-jin, angular, brown hair, chin tattoos, the ringed-seal hat. The draft's shorter Frithia and her twelve years teaching spear were cut before archiving.

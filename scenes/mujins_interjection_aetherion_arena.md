# Mujin's Interjection — Aetherion Arena

Mujin has been listening from the second tier of the stone seats, one leg crossed over the other, his hands resting on the pommel of a staff he carries the way some men carry an opinion: always present, seldom raised, never forgotten. He rises now. The motion is unhurried. His knees do not protest the way thirty years on a bench stool might lead a man to expect, and the reason for that lives in the architecture of his Crystal rather than the condition of his joints.

He is thirty-eight and wears more years than that. Tall, but the height compressed into something denser, the posture of a man whose spine has been rearranged by years of bending over worktables and straightened again by something more deliberate than exercise. His face is weathered in the way of leather that has been oiled and stored and used again, lines already set deep at the mouth and across the forehead, the skin the colour of aged wood. His hair is white-blond, the colour it was in court. It has grown out past any barber's notion of order, pulled back from his face and bound at the nape with a strip of dark cloth that has been retied so many times the fabric has thinned to translucence. His eyes are dark and steady and carry the quality of a man who has seen something that reorganised his understanding of what the world permits, and who has spent the years since deciding what to do with that knowledge.

The scars are visible when he descends the steps. Two of them, running parallel from his left wrist to the inside of his elbow, the tissue raised and pale against the darker skin, the kind of marks that do not come from a blade or a burn but from something that reached inside and rearranged what it found there. He does not cover them. He has not covered them in years. They are his credentials for this conversation.

"Grand Mage." His voice is lower than the staff suggests, the register of a man who learned to speak in rooms where the wrong word at the wrong volume could destabilise a working in progress. He addresses Xhem first because Xhem is the authority in this room, and Mujin is old enough to know that offering expertise to a man who has not requested it requires the correct approach. "I would ask that you consider my son Geturo for this work. And that you allow me to contribute what I know."

He reaches the group and plants the staff. The base strikes the stone with a sound that is quieter than it should be, the contact dampened by something woven into the wood itself. The draw-hum beneath the arena floor registers the staff's arrival the way a pond registers a stone: a brief distortion in the frequency, then stillness.

"I have spent thirty years in the discipline they are now calling spiritual alchemy in the reports that cross your desk." He says it the way a physician states the number of years he has practised: as a fact that establishes what he has earned the right to say next. "The Mortalis boundary is not new ground to me. Nor is the architecture Miss Zettari describes." His gaze moves to Shiori, and what crosses his face is recognition. Her father's blood, the shape it takes behind someone else's eyes, the particular quality of sight that runs in that line. He has encountered it before, in another context, under circumstances he does not intend to share in an open arena. "The overwriting mechanism. The secondary Signature forced into a Crystal that cannot sustain it. I have read the theory behind what you found."

He turns the staff once in his hands. A slow rotation, the wood moving through his fingers with the ease of something that has been held ten thousand times.

"In the Zettari archives." He watches Shiori when he says the name and does not look away. "Marginal notes from the period before the Accord existed to enforce alchemical law. When the original statutes were being drafted, the committee preserved records of what the statutes were written to prevent. Attempts at Essence transfer across the Mortalis boundary. Forced Crystal restructuring. Techniques for sustaining biological function after the Essence architecture has been overwritten or removed." He lets each phrase settle against the stone floor with the same weight his staff carried. "The experiments failed, and the failures were documented in enough detail that anyone with access to the archive and enough patience could reconstruct the methodology and begin correcting for the original errors."

On the arena floor below, the younger wardsmith has finished her third count. She writes a number on her slate, compares it to the number on the first wardsmith's slate, and the two readings do not agree. The younger woman looks at the crystal set into the floor. The first wardsmith looks at the crystal. They stand three feet apart, holding their slates, and between them the anchor-point hums at a frequency that should be producing the same number on both instruments.

*Cozbi understood this. The Mortalis boundary, the architecture behind the Shell, the mechanism by which a living Crystal can be separated from the body it inhabits. He understood it the way a locksmith understands a door. And Cozbi is thirty feet below where I am standing, and he has not stopped being patient.*

Mujin's left hand moves to his right wrist. His fingers press once against the raised tissue of the nearer scar, a single compression, the gesture of a man confirming that something he locked away is still where he left it. The thought passes across his face as a tightening around his eyes and nothing more. He has carried the memory of Cozbi Mahuo for longer than some of the people in this room have been alive, and he has learned that the memory does not lighten with years. It clarifies.

"What I have not seen before is this level of sophistication applied outside a controlled archive." His voice steadies. Whatever the thought cost him does not reach his words. "What Miss Zettari has described is the work of someone who has studied the same source material I have studied, arrived at the same theoretical conclusions, and then done what the original committee spent six months drafting laws to ensure no one would do." He looks from Shiori to Wystan to Xhem, the triangle that holds the investigation. "That is a different order of problem than a lone practitioner experimenting in a cellar. That is someone with access, resources, and a methodology that is actively improving."

Shiori's gaze has settled on the scars at Mujin's wrist. Her eyes carry the look they carry when they are doing what her father's blood built them to do: reading the origin of something. The origin-point of those scars. Whatever she sees there, she does not name it. The stillness that crosses her face is the expression Wystan would recognise as the one his apprentice makes when a piece of evidence confirms something she hoped was not connected.

"I would also ask that my son Hiromi be assigned to this effort." Mujin's tone shifts. The scholar recedes. The father arrives, and the father carries a different weight, the expression of a man who knows his son's capabilities and his son's limitations and is about to name both in a room where the naming costs him something. "Hiromi's grasp of alchemical process design is sharper than mine. He was raised in it. Both my sons were, from the age when most children are learning their letters." He pauses. "What Hiromi lacks is the patience to work within a framework he did not construct. He will argue. He will question every procedural constraint that he believes slows the work, and he will be correct often enough that the arguing becomes difficult to dismiss."

His eyes find Rovhen.

"I would pair him with you." The request is direct. It is the directness of a man who has chosen his candidate with the same care he applies to choosing a reagent: the purpose is specific, the selection deliberate, the margin for error smaller than the room assumes. "You understand institutions, Rovhen. You understand the kind of mind that sees every rule as a challenge to be optimised rather than a boundary to be observed. Hiromi will be invaluable in any room where alchemical architecture is being discussed." Something that is almost a smile crosses Mujin's face, there and gone, the movement of a man who finds his son simultaneously impressive and exhausting. "He will also be the most difficult person in any room where someone tells him his methodology is wrong. He inherited that quality from his mother, and his mother was never wrong often enough to learn the habit of concession."

Rovhen meets Mujin's gaze. The offer is plain. Beneath it, the request: take my son, keep him useful, and keep him from burning the structure down around himself because he decided the structure was slower than his own process. Rovhen has taught students who believed they were the sharpest instrument in every room they entered. Some of them were right. The lesson was never about whether they were sharp. It was about whether sharpness alone was sufficient for the work, and the answer arrived when the work became something that cut back.

He inclines his head. The acceptance carries no conditions and no speech, because the conditions will be set when Hiromi arrives and the work begins, and Rovhen has learned from Lambert that the terms of a mentorship are established by the work itself rather than by declarations made in advance. He has also learned that a father who chooses a handler for his son with this degree of precision is a father who has tried handling the boy himself and discovered that proximity and authority are not the same tool.

*He is not wrong about the arguing. Lambert would have appreciated the problem.*

A porter crosses the upper walkway behind them, younger than the grey-haired man who passed during Shiori's briefing, carrying a crate of containment rods in both arms. The rods are brass-capped and slate-bodied and they rattle against each other with each step, the sound of instruments being moved to where they are needed rather than where they are stored. He glances at the group. He does not slow.

Below, the two wardsmiths have abandoned their third count. The younger woman stands and wipes her palms on her leather apron. The first wardsmith collects both slates and tucks them under his arm, the gesture of a man who has decided that the disagreement between his instruments is a problem for a different conversation. They walk together toward the far end of the arena floor, and the anchor-point they leave behind hums alone, its crystal catching the light from the high windows and throwing it against the stone in fragments that shift with the slow movement of clouds outside.

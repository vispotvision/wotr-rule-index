# Niran's Arrival — Aetherion Academy

*Aetherion Academy. The Grand Mage's tower. Late morning.*

The Academy made a sound before it showed a shape. Niran heard it from the carriage road a quarter mile out, the whole black pile of it ringing low under the wind: four storeys of dressed stone on three sides of the long quad, sealed conduit humming under the eaves in brass gone green at the joints, a drill floor slapping somewhere behind the east range. Under all of it ran a thinner note he did not like. It sat a half-tone flat, the way a bell rings once a crack has started in the lip and nobody has found it yet.

He had heard that same flatness at home four nights ago, under the cedar line on the western terrace, where the enclave kept its dead.

The under-porter met him at the lodge with her keys already in her fist. She was short and heavy and freckled to the ears, and today a strip of black ribbon was knotted through the ring at her belt, tied off among the coloured wool tags of forty-odd keys.

"You'll be the Yukari." The gap where a front tooth had been put a whistle in it. "Agnes Tull. Under-porter. Your wire came down at the fourth bell, and the Board's not countersigned it, so by the office you're not here yet." She held up the slip between two fingers.

CROW SEAT YUKARI ARRIVES NOON RE STUDENTS RE DEAD.

"Spelt you with a y on the end," she said. "And it says dead twice, if you count the students, which the kitchens do."

"I sent it short," Niran said. "Wires cost."

"Everything costs. Stannvaard's put the draw up another copper on the thousand." She was already walking, fast for her legs, and talking faster than she walked. "You'll want the Grand Mage. Everyone wants the Grand Mage this week. He's got a man up there already, came up the quad an hour since in plain robes, no banner, no nothing, and the stairs got out of his way." She sniffed. "Stairs never get out of mine."

They passed under an arch where centuries of the same shoes had worn the step into a shallow trough. Her voice dropped the way a porter's does when she is about to give away something she was never told.

"Chapel bell's rung itself three nights running, the hour after midnight. Sexton swears blind he tied the rope off, and he's a drinker, but he's not a liar, so." The keys jingled once. "There's a second-year girl in the infirmary who walks and eats and says please and thank you, and won't turn her head to her own name. Matron's put a card on the door. No visitors." She glanced up at him sidelong. "Kitchens say it's Moto work. The old eye-reading come back. Kitchens say a lot."

"Who sits with the girl at night?"

Agnes chewed on that for three steps. "Matron, till she drops. Then nobody." She sucked air through the gap. "That's the trouble with nobody. He's never there when you want him."

Niran listened past her while she talked. The quad gave him its usual traffic, students crossing in grey with their slates under their arms, a standpipe ticking as the noon draw came on, a class on the second storey reciting a table in unison and getting the same line wrong together. The Academy's own living were loud and ordinary. Beneath them, very faint, the chapel's bell metal still held the shape of a stroke nobody had made. It hung in the bronze like a held breath.

At the foot of the tower Agnes stopped short at a line on the flags that only porters could see.

"I'm not to go up," she said. "Nobody tells a porter anything. So I find it out myself, mind, which is harder on everyone."

"Your third key from the left," Niran said. "The brass one with the green tag. It's cracked at the bow. It'll snap in the lock before the winter."

She lifted the ring and turned the key to the light. The hairline ran across the bow where her thumb had pressed it a thousand times. She looked at it for a long moment, then at him, and did not ask how. She tucked it behind the others with a care she had not shown it before.

The Wandering Stairways did not get out of his way. They considered him. The first flight slid a hand's width to the left with a grind of stone on stone and stopped, and the second rose a step and settled again, and Niran stood on the landing with his weight forward and his head slightly tilted and let them read him the way he would have read a gate post. Old work, layered. Somebody had built these to tell a guest from an intruder by the way he stood still. He kept still. After a while the stair turned under him like a sleeper rolling over and gave him the way up.

At the top, an under-secretary sat behind a narrow table piled with wires. He was perhaps twenty, with ink to the second knuckle of his right hand and a collar starched harder than the rest of him, and he rose so fast his chair knocked the wall.

"Hollis Grebe. Under-secretary to the Grand Mage. Master Yukari, the Grand Mage has someone with him, and your wire's not been sealed, so formally I can't..." He looked at the crimson in Niran's eyes, the thread-patterns turning slowly in it, and lost his place in the sentence. *He's going in anyway, and I'm going to be the one who let him.* "Formally."

"Then I'll knock," Niran said.

He did not knock at once.

The cold dumplings were still in his pack, wrapped in a cloth that smelled of her. Mira had stood at Senri's Gate in the grey before dawn with ink on her fingers and her mouth set, and she had argued with him for the length of a broth and then stopped arguing, which was worse. The enclave needed one of them who could hear the wards, he had told her, and the dead under the cedar line needed someone who would sit with them. Both of those things were true. Neither was the reason. The reason was that something was pulling at the threads of the dead from a long way off, and he did not want the person he loved most standing between him and whatever it was when it pulled back. She had known that. She had put the dumplings in his pack and said, "You haven't eaten," and gone back inside before he could answer her.

The Ash-Signal Pouch sat warm against his sternum, unburned.

He knocked twice, the knuckle-strokes evenly spaced, and let himself in.

The office smelled of tea gone cold and medicinal smoke, old paper and the laboratory sharpness of some compound that had been spilled and wiped and spilled again. A desk that had given up on order. A folder lying on the leather. Two men, and two heartbeats, and Niran heard both before his eyes had finished with the room. He did not listen past their breathing. It was not his house to read.

"Xhem." There was a pause where a younger man's smile would have gone. On Niran it sat in the eyes, briefly, and then the crimson settled. "It has been too long. You look like you've been sleeping in that chair."

He turned to the other and inclined his head, the shallow nod a Yukari gives to someone he means to be honest with.

"Cousin. We've never met. I was born under your sky and left it at thirteen, with salt in my pack and a ward-carver's tools on my hip." His voice was quiet, and the room seemed to arrange itself around it. "I'm glad to fix that. I'm sorry it's today."

He took a sealed letter from inside his coat and laid it on the corner of the desk without pushing it forward. Black wax, the crow pressed into it, the Council of Threads' cord through the fold.

"I sent word down your wire this morning. Your man at the top of the stair tells me it isn't sealed yet, so I'll say it plainly and let the seal catch up." He kept his hands clasped loosely in front of him. "I'm here for the Council. The Crow's Seat has the last word in a deliberation and no power to make anyone do anything. I built it that way. So I have no right to ask you for something. I'm asking anyway."

He let one breath go by. Below the window a bell counted off the Guild hour, and a burst of laughter came up from the courtyard and died against the stone.

"Four nights ago the dead at home turned over. We lay our dead facing east, the way Senri died. The bodies are where we laid them. Their threads face west now. Every one of them, toward here." He said it the way he would have reported a cracked cistern. "I went onto the Soul Plane to look. The road back to the ancestors has knots in it, the kind you get when someone ties a rope, cuts it and ties it again badly. The dead who walked it this week came out the other side the wrong shape. Somebody on this side is pulling architecture out of living Crystals, and the other side is carrying the weight of it like a net with a stone in the bottom."

Mira was not at his shoulder. He was aware of the space where she would have stood the way he was aware of a missing tooth.

"I've come to inquire about the investigation your people have just begun. Your students." His gaze went once to the folder and came back. "I've spent my life listening to the spirit side, and the last seven years of it I spent there without a body. Whatever is being done here is being felt there. I think what I know of it can help you." The thread-marks along his forearms gave off a faint light in the shadow of his sleeves. "Your porter told me about a girl in the infirmary who says please and doesn't know her own name. A Shell left standing with nothing behind it, and the body still eating. I know what that is from the inside. I rushed a body once, coming back through the Veil. For a while there was nothing behind mine either."

He let that sit. He did not soften it.

"And I can tell you whose hand this is."

The words came out level. Behind them, in a stone room below daylight a long way from here, a thread he had been holding for years drew tight.

"I won't say the name yet. Where I come from a name said aloud pulls on its thread, and this one has pulled enough. Dhaerin died putting it away." A muscle moved once in his jaw, under the scar Sōma had left there. "Before I say it in this room, I want my palm on one of your victims and an hour to read the work. If I'm right, you'll want me in the room when you move. If I'm wrong, you'll want me to have been careful."

His head tilted the last degree, weight forward, the stillness of a man who has finished laying out his tools and is waiting to see which one the work wants.

"And Rengai is still not home. Your people were looking. I'd like to know what they found, and what the Zettari archive your students are reading has to do with him." He looked from one of them to the other, and the patterns in the crimson slowed. "The girl in your infirmary. Let me read her today, before the dead at home turn any further."

# Paru 86

*The blackened plain under the cracked sky, the fourteenth day; and Midreach, the Hall of Equilibria, above*

Faisal listened to all of it.

He stood at the edge of the black circle with the straight gold light coming down round him out of the crack in heaven and the hundreds of open hands hanging in the air at his back, and he let his brother talk, the way he had always let him talk, across every age and every field. He heard the laughter. He heard *cleaning up my mess*, and *the universe's most obedient little servant*, and *I should have made the pillar bigger*. He heard *how many things can you catch before those lovely hands finally break*. Under the falling Vow-Light every word his brother said was true, which was the worst of it; Malakar had never once needed to lie to be cruel. He meant all of it. He had always meant all of it. And under the meaning, under the mockery and the delight and the terrible patience, Faisal heard the other thing, the thing his brother had said last and quietest, with his hand on his chest.

*I did miss you.*

That was true as well.

Faisal supposed this was the sad part of their story. Every story has one, and theirs had been waiting a long time for its turn. He had taken the responsibility for his brother at the very beginning, before there were Choirs or Bells or mortals to watch them, the way an elder takes responsibility for a younger, and he had never once put it down, not when it cost him the Host, not when it put him on his knees at the edge of creation for seven hundred years. He would not put it down now, even if carrying it this last distance meant breaking his body to do it. The body did not matter. In his spirit he would remain what he had always been. That was the one thing nobody, not even his brother, had ever found a way to take from him.

And he did not want his brother to feel bad. That was the strange, foolish, unkillable thing at the bottom of him. It had never been personal between them. Not once, not in all the ages. In the grand scheme of things they were two people with roles, set at the two ends of the same scale by a hand older than either of them, and each had played his part with his whole heart. His brother broke. Faisal caught. Neither of them had chosen it and neither of them had ever, for one moment, wished to be the other.

He wondered, standing there, whether their mother would have wanted it to end like this. He remembered her hum. He remembered the white courtyard and the practice blades, *klak, klak, klak-klak*, and two young things laughing every time they landed a blow, and the hum going on above them as if it would never stop. He did not know what she would have wanted. He knew she had made them both.

He looked across the plain at his brother.

[slow, quiet, finished] "You are right," Faisal said. "I spent too much on the light. I always spend too much. You have told me so since before there were mortals to hear you say it." The gold hands at his back shifted, very slightly, as if they were listening too. "And you are right that I cannot catch everything. I learned that the morning you cracked the Bell. I learned it again this morning, a hundred leagues from Kushara, with my hands held out to the east and nothing in them." He paused. "I am not ashamed of that, brother. A thing that knows it cannot catch everything and reaches anyway is not a fool. It is the only kind of thing worth being."

The wind came up off the black ground and lifted his white hair.

"I did miss you too," he said. "That is also true. You know I cannot say anything that is not."

He was quiet for the length of a breath. When he spoke again his voice had not risen, because it never rose; but something under it had come loose, the way a joint comes loose in a wall that has carried a load too long.

"I want one last battle." He looked down at his hands. "I do not think I can last another thousand years. This body is breaking down. I can feel it going, a little more each hour, since I stood up." He looked up again. "I hope I can give you something worthwhile. You came all this way."

He tried not to sound as if it mattered. It mattered. Deep down, in the place in him that had grown on its own across eleven thousand years and that the Lawbell had never intended, this was not how he had wanted it to end. He had wanted, he understood now, standing on the black ground, to go on. To keep the Tally. To kneel. To hear the next king swear on a woodpile and the next surgeon's last oath in a cold room and the next child's first promise to a mother at a bedside, and to hold all of it, and go on holding it, for as long as there were people to make promises. The honor of it. The hopes. The dreams. The quiet admiration of children and grown people of every age, who had never seen him and had sworn their small true oaths on honor all the same, and every one of them had come to him and lived in him, and they were in him now, all of them, packed into the core of him like seed in a pod.

This would be the final state of his celestial evolution. He knew it. Once he did what he was about to do there would be no going back from it, for him or for anything he carried.

He put his right hand flat on the breastplate over his heart.

The plate opened for him. It did not tear; it had been sworn to him, and it parted along its gold line from the throat down the way a door opens for the master of a house, *shhk*, and under it, under the skin and the long bones of his chest, the core of him came up to meet his hand, burning.

His Soul Crystal.

It came out into his palm the size of a man's heart and many times as bright: a long double-pointed prism of gold so deep it was nearly amber, faceted like a cut stone, and inside it, turning slowly, the whole architecture of him. The Core, bright as a coal. The Layer, threaded in fine gold lines in every direction, every bond he had ever made. The Shell, clear and hard and cracked along one side in a long hairline where seven hundred years of the Bell's weight had pressed it. And running out of the base of it, fine as a hair and humming, the old thread every Celestial is born with: the anchor, the line that tied him back into the Host, into the Throne-network, into the borrowed architecture of law that had Sung him in the first place. Every Celestial's power is lent along that thread. His Domain was the only thing in him that had ever grown on its own.

He looked at the Crystal in his hand for a long moment, the way a man looks at a letter he has kept for years before he puts it in the fire.

Then he closed both hands round it and began to crack it.

*Kiiiiiiiii...*

The sound was high and thin and enormous, the sound of a wet finger on the rim of a glass the size of a mountain, and it went out across the plain and up into the sky and through it. His long fingers bore down on the Shell. The hairline along its side ran. *Ki-ki-kiik.* It forked. It forked again.

*PAKIIIN.*

The Crystal broke in his hands.

The realm shook. The whole Material World shook, the way a house shakes when its foundation shifts; the black plain heaved under his feet, *GOGOGOGOGOGO*, and the low clouds above it boiled, and a hundred leagues away the river beside the Crownlands stood up in a long standing wave and fell back, and in the Hall of Equilibria in Midreach the ten thousand gold scales all swung at once, *ting-ting-ting-ting-ting* like a field of wind chimes, and did not stop. The anchor-thread at the Crystal's base snapped with a sound like a harp string, *TWANG*, and went slack, and fell away into nothing. For the first time since the Lawbell Sang him, Faisal was not tied to anything at all.

This is what the Choirs know and do not speak of, and what Malakar, who had seen the Host break, would have understood at once.

A Celestial's Crystal is an anchor. It holds the being to the Host and the Host's law, and it holds what the being carries at a distance, in the structure, where it can be borne. When a Grand Angel breaks his own Crystal with his own hands, the anchor goes, and the distance goes with it. Everything he has been carrying stops being held in the architecture and comes into the body all at once. For Faisal that was everything: the whole of his reserve, and under it the weight of the cracked Bell, every oath sworn on honor under it for eleven thousand years and every oath sworn on honor in the Material for seven hundred more, the whole of the load he had knelt beneath at the edge of creation, poured into one body in one instant, with nothing left between it and him. A body cannot hold that for long. It holds it long enough. It burns at its purest, brightest, truest state, the being with nothing borrowed left in it, entirely itself, and then it fades, the way a star at the very end of its life burns hotter and whiter than it ever has before it goes out. And the spirit, freed of the anchor that kept it in the Host, goes where every other spirit goes. Into the cycle. Into birth, and life, and death, and birth again, like every mortal that has ever been.

The light in him went white.

His wings changed first.

They came up from his back as they had before, the whole gold one and the ash-gray ruin of the left, and then they went on coming, and as they rose they began to be made over. The broken feathers fell away in a slow shower of gray ash, *fffff*, and in their place came something new, feather by feather, and every feather was a different color. Rust and russet. Indigo. A Stark's frost-gray. The green of a Yukari valley. Copper, crimson, the near-white of a Byakuya's glass beads, the brown of a salt-cutter's hands. Every color of every aura of every mortal he had ever heard swear on honor, coming into the wings one by one as if every one of them were laying something there: a struggle, an adaptation, a small hard-won order made in a short life. The shape of the wings changed with it. They lost the long smooth sweep of the Host and came into a shape that was rougher and stronger and asymmetric, the left wing heavier than the right, the primaries notched and mended and notched again like the edge of a working tool. They were wings that had been used. Mortal wings, if mortals had wings: shaped by the path mortals walk, by being born and having to learn everything and losing things and getting up, by the order that mortal lives follow because they have no other, the cycle of nature, the seed and the fall and the seed again. Everyone who exists in this world lives and dies. For the first time, so would he.

And they poured into him.

He had heard their oaths for seven hundred years, filtered through the Crystal the way light is filtered through glass, oaths and only oaths. With the Crystal gone there was no filter, and oaths were only the beginning of what came. Everything came. Every mortal heart on the plane that had looked up at the crack in the sky that morning and felt something. A mother in a salt gallery with her hand on a sleeping child, sick with worry. A boy in Kushara under a broken roof, so frightened of the red sky he could not breathe, praying for somebody strong. An old man on a ridgewall with his hand on a cold stone. Ten thousand children who had heard the THRUM in their sleep and woken screaming, and every one of them reaching, the way people do in the dark, for someone, anyone, who would stand between them and the thing that was coming. Their fear came into him. Their worries came into him. And under all of it, more than all of it, their hope came: the plain stubborn mortal hope for someone strong enough to stand up, and it went into the hole in his chest where the Crystal had been, and it burned there.

Faisal's chest was open. Where the Crystal had sat there was a wound the size of a fist, and out of it came white light so strong it was nearly a sound, *hmmmmmmMMMMMMM*, and blood came out of his mouth, a thin bright line of it from the corner of his lips down his chin, and dropped onto the dull silver of his plate. *Tap. Tap.* He did not wipe it away.

[very softly] "Ah," he said. "So this is what it is like."

---

Far above, in Midreach, Marium was watching.

She had followed the crack in the sky to its root, and the root was in the floor of the Hall of Equilibria, a long hairline of white-gold running across the gold paving from the great west window to the foot of the high mantle at the end of the nave, and through it, as through a window in the floor, she could see down into the Material: the black plain, the red clouds, the straight gold light, and in the middle of it, very small and very bright, him.

She could not stop crying. She did not try. She stood at the edge of the crack in the floor with her pink wings fallen round her like a dropped cloak and her hands pressed over her mouth, and the tears ran down between her fingers.

Behind her, on the mantle, something had appeared.

It had not been there a moment before. It had come at the instant the Crystal broke, at the *PAKIIIN*, the way a reflection comes into a mirror when someone walks in front of it: an egg. Gold, and no bigger than her two hands, smooth as a river stone and warm to look at, sitting in the very center of the high mantle where once, before the Bell cracked, the Choir's Tally-book had lain. Fine lines of white light moved under its shell, very slowly, turning, the way the threads move in the Plane of Fate. It made a sound she could only just hear, under the ringing of the scales. A hum.

The egg of the Grand Angel sat on its mantle and waited to be hatched.

Marium did not look at it. She could not yet. She looked down through the floor at the small bright figure on the black plain, and wept, and watched.

---

Faisal walked forward.

He went across the black ground at an ordinary walk, a man's walk, one foot and then the other, and the ground under each step remembered what it was, and greened, and the green ran out from his feet in wide rings, *fsshh, fsshh*, so that behind him, as he walked, there was a trail of grass across the dead plain like a road. Blood ran from his mouth. The light came out of his chest. The new wings stood up behind him, wide as the plain, every feather a different color. He did not hurry. He had never once hurried in his life.

"You asked how many things I can catch before my hands break," he said, walking. "Here is your answer, brother. I am done catching."

The hundreds of open gold hands hanging in the air behind him stopped.

Then, all together, for the first time in eleven thousand years, they closed.

*GYU.*

It was a small sound for so many. A sound like a great deal of leather drawn tight. Every gold palm at once folded its fingers down over its thumb and became a fist, hundreds of them, hanging in the air above the black plain with the straight light falling on their knuckles. Faisal had never once closed a fist to strike first, and these did not strike first either; his brother had struck first a hundred leagues away at sunrise, and again when he put a current of chaos into an open hand to see what it would do. He was closing them because mortals close their fists. That was the struggle. That was the thing he had come down to show. A child who has never been hit learns the fist when something hits him, and Faisal, who had held out his hands for eleven thousand years, had finally been hit hard enough.

The fists began to burn.

Lines of script came up across them, out of the gold, the way frost comes up on a window: fine, dense, deliberate, every line of it Parunic, the oldest written law, the script the Archons' lattice is read in. Every glyph is a path traced across a lattice that is read from fourteen positions, one for each Archon, and a glyph written into a working tells the world what that working is *for*. Faisal wrote the fists as he walked, the script running across their knuckles and up their wrists in white fire.

[Ho], Order, that locks causal chains. [Va], Law, that binds an act to its lawful consequence. [Eq], Equivalence, that enforces equal exchange and turns an excess into its debt until the two are even. [Ur], Balance, that equalises opposing forces. [Vo], Voice, the spoken tag that binds an oath to its listener. [Vorn], End, that forces the termination state and collapses instability. [Th], Foundation, that imposes geometry. [Grv], Gravity. [RenB], Boundary. [Sel], Measure, that reveals the true weight of intent.

And the fists began to move into their places.

They went out from him across the plain and up into the sky, every direction at once, above, below, behind, on every side, and as they went they arranged themselves with purpose. The positions they took in the air were the positions of a lattice: fourteen stations round a center, and lines between them, and every fist hanging at a node, so that the whole field of them, seen from above by a weeping Celestial through a crack in a gold floor, was itself a glyph, a single Parunic sentence written in three dimensions with fists for its letters, eighty-six strokes long, every stroke a law, and all of them pointing in.

At the center of the sentence, where every line converged, stood his brother.

Faisal stopped walking. He wiped the blood off his chin with the back of his hand at last, and looked at it, red on dark skin, and smiled, a very small, very human smile.

"You said evil never needed permission to exist," he said. "You are right. It never did." The light from his chest went on singing. "Neither did good, brother. Neither did the mother in the salt gallery, or the boy under the roof in Kushara, or anyone who ever got up in the morning frightened and did the right thing anyway. Nobody gave them permission. They did it. They are doing it now. I can hear every one of them." He lifted his chin. "You are the embodiment of evil itself. You have always been, and you have always been glad of it, and I have never once asked you to be anything else. But I am not the universe's most obedient servant. I was never obedient. I was faithful. I kept my word." His eyes burned white-gold. "And this morning I am the embodiment of what they keep. Every one of them. All at once."

He raised his right hand, and closed it, slowly, into a fist of his own, the first in his life. His knuckles cracked. *Kki.*

[clear, carrying, every word a law] "Divine Fist."

The whole lattice of fists flared white.

"Paru Eighty-Six."

The script on every one of them caught at once, *VWAAAAM*, eighty-six lines of Parunic burning white along eighty-six arms, and the sentence they wrote in the air became legible, all of it, to anything on the plane that could read law at all.

"Combination."

The first fist went.

*DOOOOOOOON.*

It came straight down the center line out of the sky, the biggest of them, a fist the size of a house, and the black-red Chaos Force in its path could neither slow it nor turn it nor eat it, and it tore through. The script on its knuckles, [Vorn] and [Ho] and [Va], went into the chaos ahead of it like a plow-blade into wet earth, and the contradictions in front of it snapped into agreement as it passed, *ka-ka-ka-ka-KAK*, a long tearing rattle like a whole string of locks turning at once, and the chaos fell away on both sides of its path in two curling black-red sheets, and it went on, straight as a plumb line, at the place where his brother's chest would be.

*BAKOOOM.*

The second came from behind. It rose up out of the hillside at the plain's edge with the whole slope breaking open around it, stone and black soil and the roots of dead trees flung up in a great fan, *GRAAAAKKK*, an open hand again, for the one stroke in the sentence that needed one, the hand that catches, turned for the first time to take hold; [Grv] and [Th] burning on its palm, and it closed on the place where his brother stood and hauled toward the first, back through the hillside, through the earth, *ZZZZRRRRRRRRK*, a furrow opening in the land behind it a hundred yards long.

And the other eighty-four came.

From every angle. Above, below, from the sides, from the sky, out of the ground, out of the light falling from the crack in heaven, eighty-four burning gold fists closing on one point from every direction at once, each along its own line of the lattice, each with its own law written on its knuckles, and as they came the field of the sentence tightened round its center like a hand tightening round a stone. *GOGOGOGOGOGOGO.* The black-red Chaos Force that had poured out across the plain since sunrise, every grain of disorder his brother had spent on the Material that morning, was swept in ahead of them. The fists pushed it. They herded it in from every side the way the tide herds water into the mouth of a bay, [Eq] and [Ur] burning on their knuckles, equal exchange and balance, every excess driven back toward its debt, and the whole of the chaos on the plain, all of it, came pouring back across the black ground and in through the air in long red ropes and black sheets, backwards, into the one place it had come from.

Into the center point. Into his brother.

The plain went white.

*Shiiiiiiin.*

For one instant there was no sound at all. Every fist in the sentence hung a hand's breadth from the center, all eighty-six, the script on them burning so bright it had stopped looking like light and started looking like law, and the chaos packed into the middle of them like a storm forced back into a bottle, and between the fists and the point they had closed on, nothing, a gap thinner than a blade, holding.

Faisal stood at the edge of it with his fist raised and blood on his mouth and the hope of a whole world singing out of the hole in his chest, and his new wings, every color of every mortal who had ever kept a promise, spread wide behind him across the black plain like a dawn.

His heart was beating. *Thrum.* His brother's answered it, somewhere inside the closing sentence. *THRUM.* The same as always. Equal. Feeding each other.

He was bright. He was so bright. He could feel the brightness eating him from the inside, the way he had known it would, the flame drawn up the wick to the very end and burning white there.

"Here is my order, brother," said Faisal. "It is theirs."

And he brought his fist down.

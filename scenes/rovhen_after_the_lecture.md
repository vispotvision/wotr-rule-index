# Rovhen — After the Lecture

*Aetherion Academy, the old dispensary. The last hour before last bell.*

---

Rovhen looked at Imogen Pell for as long as it took the rain to gust against the window and fall away.

"Everyone says a great many things, Miss Pell." He set the chalk in the tray and squared it with one finger. "In this room we start with what the room says, and the room I'm teaching you to read tonight is this one. The arena will keep." He turned to take in the rest of them. "That will be all for tonight's lecture. Read the pamphlet before you sleep, and again when you wake, and bring me one thing you noticed on your way here tomorrow that you would have walked past a week ago. If any of you have questions, stay and ask them. I'll be here the next hour or so, until the Proctor throws me out."

Imogen held his eyes a moment longer. Then she turned her slate face up, wiped it with the heel of her hand in one long stroke, and stood.

The benches scraped. The porters rose together, the way they had come in, and the elder one lifted his cap off the bench and turned it round twice in his big hands before he put it on.

Behind Rovhen, on the dais among the lamps, his notebook lay open against the shelving where nobody on the benches could see its face. The pen lay across the page where it had stopped. It was an ordinary steel nib in a black horn holder. For the last hour it had been writing without a hand on it.

He had held it the whole lecture the way Edmund had taught him to hold a lamp in a cellar, which was without thinking about it. A thread of his own Essence ran out through the Shell to the steel and closed the nib on the paper, lifted it, closed it again. It asked very little. It asked it continuously. Now that he had let it go, the cost came in all at once, a dull pressure behind his left eye and a cramp in the web of his left hand as though he had written every word himself. He flexed the fingers once under the edge of the bench where nobody would see.

The page was dense with his hand, smaller and more slanted than the hand he used on the slate.

> *Aldery, J. First bench, by the bucket. Clerk, widowed within the year (band still stiff, black not yet rusted by washing). Reads everything twice. Wrote at "the room first." Pressed hardest at the stair.*
>
> *Left cuff: three cuts through the black, parallel, a finger's breadth apart, running toward the elbow. Mended in black thread, the thread still glossy: a week, perhaps less. She is right-handed. A right-handed woman puts up her left arm to take a blade she did not see coming.*
>
> *Or she caught it on a nail three times. Ask nothing yet.*
>
> *Pryor, H. and Pryor, D., porters and brothers, Hob the elder. Father lost at Cullen Pit. Dunstan watches Hob for permission to speak and doesn't get it. Hob asks the right question and apologises for asking. Good hands. Both of them flinch at "shut."*
>
> *Pell, I. Class S. Enrolled day four, narrow pen, not the registrar's. Writes without looking. Watched the back corner more than the slate until the Wild column, then the slate. Knew about the chain. Knew about noon. Somebody told her, and not a porter.*
>
> *Velthrae, N. Back bench. Arrived without the door: wet hem, dry flags, no tracks. Wrote at the four depths and stopped when Pell said Visitation. Held the locket.*

The last line on the page was a single word, underlined twice: *cuff.*

He closed the notebook on the pen and slid both into his coat.

The corridor outside had filled with the sound of feet and the smell of wet wool going cold. Proctor Crale stood just outside the doorway with a hooded lantern in one hand and the ring of night keys in the other, her long shadow laid across the flags into the room. Beside her waited a young man in Accord green with a leather satchel slung across his chest.

He was perhaps twenty and not built for running, though running was plainly his work. His hair was the colour of wet straw and cut too short at the back by someone in a hurry, and his face was a boy's face with the beginnings of a man's jaw under it, spotted along the chin where the razor had gone badly. He was narrow in the chest and soft through the middle, his green coat pulling at its one fastened button. Rain had soaked the shoulders dark and his boots through. A brass Visitation token hung at his collar, and the ink on the fingers of his right hand matched the ink on Harrowgate's cuff.

"Master Talvasciel." He held out an envelope sealed in green wax, the oval of the seal exactly the size of the burn on the back of a certain hand. "The Examiner's compliments. She asks me to say she'll call for it at first bell, sir. She said you'd know what it was."

Crale watched him hand it over with no expression at all.

"Visitation runner," she said, when he had gone clattering down the corridor. "He came in by the lodge a quarter hour ago and I let him, because it wasn't last bell yet." She lifted the lantern an inch toward the dispensary's back corner and let it fall again, and her eyes went once along the floor between the door and the back bench. They stayed a beat on the dry flags. "Last bell's in an hour. I'll be at the end of this corridor when it goes."

She went. Her keys rang once at every step.

The room had half emptied. Imogen Pell had stopped by the doorframe on her way out, in front of the roll he had nailed there at a child's eye height. She ran one finger down the names, slowly, and stopped at the fourth day's ink. Her finger rested there. Then she took it away, and wiped the tip of it on her skirt, and was gone into the corridor.

Hob Pryor went out after her with his brother at his heel. At the threshold he half turned, as if there were something else about Cullen Pit, and then he thought better of it the way he had at the start of the evening, and the corridor had him.

Joan Aldery had not moved from the bench by the bucket.

She had put her pencil away and folded the pamphlet in half and in half again, into a hard little square, and she held it in her right hand. Her left lay in her lap, palm down, the mended cuff turned under so that the black thread did not show. She waited until the porters' boots had gone past the turn of the corridor, then stood and came as far as the second bench, and stopped there with the lamp between them.

"Sir." Her voice was lower than it had been in the lecture, and very even, the voice of a clerk reading a figure back to a manager who has already decided not to believe it. "The stair was at the back of a draper's on Tallow Street. I've been down it a hundred times since, and it won't tell me anything." Her thumb worked at the folded square. "I thought perhaps it tells different things to different people."

# Rovhen — The First Lecture

*Aetherion Academy, the old dispensary. The final lecture of the evening.*

---

The room had warmed. Wet wool steamed off the porters' shoulders and the widow's bonnet, and the steam carried the smell of the docks in with it, tar and river mud and the cold iron of chain. Lamp oil, chalk dust, the camphor in the shelving. Rain ticked on in the downpipe, and somewhere overhead a chair dragged across boards and stopped.

Rovhen stood at the open end of the horseshoe with the slate at his back and said nothing.

He let the silence run. It was Edmund's trick and it had never once failed him. People told you who they were in the first quiet: who filled it, who waited it out, who looked at the door. The elder porter cleared his throat and then thought better of it. Joan Aldery folded her hands on the pamphlet and the tall girl's chalk stopped moving on her slate. At the back, the white-haired girl had turned to a fresh page and written something at the top of it, and her pen stood upright in her fingers, waiting.

"Before we begin," he said, "you should know who is standing in front of you. Then, if you want to check up on me, you'll know where to look."

His voice came out lower than he meant it to. He let it stay there.

"My name is Rovhen Talvasciel. Most of my life I was an inquiry agent. The first part of it I spent as second to a man named Edmund Lambert, the last part on my own, and people who keep lists used to put me near the top of theirs. The Board has my licence now. It was struck through eleven months ago, in Stannvaard, at my own request." He rested two fingers on the edge of the nearest bench. "I gave that work up to do this one. What he taught me, I'll teach you. Mostly it comes to this: how to stand in a room and have the room tell you what happened in it."

He turned and wrote on the slate. The chalk squeaked on the downstroke and he let it.

AETHER.

"Tonight, the ground under all of it. What aether is. The kinds of ground it makes, and the tempers it keeps. What it does to matter, and what it leaves behind in a room once the hand that used it has gone home."

He crossed to the door and lifted the ladle off the bucket. The chain rattled against the rim. He dipped it, full, and held it up where the lamps could get at it.

"This is the bucket," he said. "Aether. It fills every room you will ever stand in, this one included. It belongs to nobody. It has no opinions." He tipped the ladle back and the water went into the bucket with a slap. "And this, once you've drunk it, is Essence. Same water. It tastes of you afterward, and anything you do with it tastes of you too. That taste is what you'll hear called a signature. Two people can draw from the same bucket in the same room and leave two entirely different messes." He hung the ladle back on its hook. "Your whole work is telling the bucket from the ladle."

Joan Aldery wrote that down. Her pencil was down to a stub and she held it with three fingers, the way clerks do.

"First question in any room. How much is there." He walked to the meter by the door and tapped its glass with a knuckle. "The Board counts it for you, in the towns, so that it can bill you. That needle tells the Board how much the main is feeding this room. It has not moved all evening." He looked at it a moment longer than he needed to. "Maybe the room is drawing nothing. Maybe the meter's broken, or maybe someone would like very much for it to read nothing tonight, and has seen to it. You won't know which from the needle. An instrument gives you a number, and only you can decide whether to believe it."

He came back to the slate.

"Out of the towns there is no meter, and you use what you're standing in. Four depths, and you will learn to tell them by your own body before you can tell them any other way." He wrote them down the left side, one under another. "Ambient. That's most of the world, most of the time. A trained adept can tune to it. Anyone else feels faintly uneasy there, or faintly calm, and blames the weather. Active concentration. The ground keeps what happens to it. Stay long enough and it will keep you, too, whether you asked or not. Veil-thin. Time goes wrong. You see things that aren't standing where you're looking. Clocks and pulses disagree." He tapped the last word. "Core. If you ever find yourself in one, you stop taking notes and you walk out the way you came, and you do not stop to ask the ground's permission."

"How would you know," the elder porter said, "which one you're in? Sir." He had a voice like a coal chute. "If it's the first time."

"You wouldn't. You'd know afterward. That's why you write the time down when you arrive and again when you leave, from a watch you trust, and compare them." Rovhen set the chalk in the tray. "I have walked out of a cellar in Stannvaard that I was in for a quarter of an hour by my watch. The lamp I carried in had burned an evening's oil."

The younger porter looked at his brother. His brother kept his eyes on the slate and did not look back at him.

"Second question. Which way it came." Rovhen drew three columns across the slate and headed them. WILD. SPIRIT. MEDIUM. "Every working, and every place that works on its own, acts from somewhere and arrives somewhere. Find out which, and you know which of your witnesses is lying to you."

He put the chalk to the first column.

"Wild. The working took what was already there and moved it. The stone was there, the water was there, the iron was there, and something shoved it about. Quarry country, mostly." He wrote under the heading: *the ground lies. The body tells the truth.* "I once worked a well at a quarry village in the north that had closed up overnight. The walls had grown inward, the way a throat closes when it swallows. The village swore it had always been that narrow, and the stone agreed with them down to the tool-marks. My knees did not. I came out of that well sick to my stomach for no reason I could find, and I trusted my stomach, and I was right." He tapped the phrase once. "In Wild ground, your body is your instrument. The room has been rewritten, and it will lie to you with a perfectly straight face."

"My da's pit went like that," the elder porter said. It came out of him flat. "Cullen Pit. They said the seam shifted." He rubbed his thumb along the grain of the bench. "It shifted shut."

Rovhen let that sit in the room for the space of a breath, which was the most he had to give it.

"Then you already know more than most of the Guild's inspectors," he said, and moved his hand to the second column.

"Spirit. Here nothing was moved. The thing was made, inside the one who did it, and put out into the world from there. The fire was not in the room until it arrived." He wrote: *the room tells the truth. The body lies.* "A clerk at a counting-house in the Lower Wards, burned at his desk. The ledgers under his arms had kept their colour. The room looked like any room. I stood in it and felt nothing at all, and I would have signed my name to that." He paused over the slate. "Edmund made me stand in it again, the next morning, with my eyes shut, and describe what I had actually seen. That is when I noticed the inkwell had boiled dry." He tapped the second phrase. "In Spirit work, the room is your instrument. Your own body will tell you everything is fine."

The tall girl had stopped writing. Her eyes were on the slate now, and on him, and they went from one to the other in a steady measured count.

"Medium." He wrote the last phrase slowly: *nobody tells the truth.* "Water, mostly, and light. The working goes both ways at once. It changes the thing, and the thing changes you while you're looking at it. I spent a night in a flooded sluice-keeper's cottage with Edmund, and we both came out of it certain of things that had not happened. It took us a week to find the ones that were ours." He set the chalk down. "In Medium ground you bring a second witness, you write everything down before you speak to them, and you don't trust them either."

Rain rattled the tall window in a gust and eased again, and one of the lamps guttered and settled.

"Last question tonight, and it's the one people skip." He turned from the slate and put his back to it, the chalk hanging loose in his fingers. "Whether the place asked."

For the first time that evening, nobody in the room wrote anything down.

"Clean ground is choosy. It reads whoever walks into it, and it lets them in or it lets them be. You come out tired and none the worse. Ground that has been used badly stops asking: drawn on and never paid back, stripped, or soaked in a war nobody ever cleaned up after. That kind of ground just takes. It pulls at you whether you fit it or not." He looked along the horseshoe, bench by bench. "You'll know it by the room. It feels owed. If a room ever feels as though you owe it something, do your work and get out of it. Edmund put that one in the pamphlet in his own words. Read them tonight. Read them again in the morning."

"And a man, sir?"

Joan Aldery had not put her hand up. She was sitting very straight, her stub of pencil flat under her palm on the bench.

"A man found at the foot of a stair," she said. "With the room around him looking ordinary. How would you know it was the stair?"

The porters went still. The rain filled the space.

"You would look at the stair first," Rovhen said. "The treads, the rail, the lamp, the angle he lay at. The room first, the law second, the hand last." He held her eyes. "And you would not decide it was the stair until the stair had told you so."

She nodded once and wrote something short. Her hand did not shake. It pressed hard enough that he could hear the pencil through the paper.

The tall girl turned her slate over on her knee, writing side down, and laid her palm flat on it.

"Imogen Pell, Professor. Class S." Her voice was clear and carried, pitched for a larger room than this one. "Will the class be examining the arena? Everyone says the Visitation has put a chain on the north gate." She did not smile. "And that you were in the Visitation Room at noon."

## Author notes

### Isaac's dialogue

Rovhen's opening keeps Isaac's beats in order (his name, once one of the best, giving that life up to teach, tonight's subject the observation of aether), rewritten in his register: plain, low, exact, crediting Edmund.

"Types and cultures of aether" became three canon axes, taught as an investigator's three questions:

| Question | Canon system | What Rovhen teaches |
|---|---|---|
| How much is there | The Density Scale: Ambient Saturation, Active Concentration, Veil-Thin Nexus, Wellspring Core | Tell the depth by your own body; log the time in and out |
| Which way it came | The three Encounter Types: Wild, Spirit, Medium | Wild: trust the body. Spirit: trust the room. Medium: trust nothing. (His framing, derived from canon) |
| Whether the place asked | The Attraction and Obsession axis | "The room feels owed," tying back to Stolen in Edmund's Sort |

"Aether is the ocean, Essence is your water" is canon Primer text, rendered as the bucket and the ladle.

In the prose once, as Rovhen sees her: a fresh page, her pen upright. No words, thoughts or new actions.

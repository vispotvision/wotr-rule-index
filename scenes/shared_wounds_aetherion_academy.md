# Shared Wounds — Aetherion Academy

*Aetherion Academy. The broken cold room under the south range. Continuous.*

The bullet came out of the dark at a little under nine hundred feet a second, and Mu-jin saw it the way he saw everything with the lens off.

A pellet of soft lead the size of a fingertip, spinning, pushed ahead of a short white tongue of flame from the muzzle of a nickelled pocket revolver held in a man's left hand. It crossed the vault toward the bridge of his nose. He watched it come. His body did not know about it yet. The Arrears were running his nerves a second and more behind the world, and the bullet would be in his skull before his skull heard the shot.

Then there was a line in the air.

It was thin and bright and perfectly straight, there and gone in less time than a lamp takes to flicker, and it went through the revolver in Velder's hand. The receiver slumped, glowing, and the gun came apart in two pieces. And the bullet stopped. It hung in the air a hand's width from Mu-jin's face, still spinning, held there by a pressure of light he could feel on his skin without understanding it. Then it went back the way it had come. It turned in the air and drove into Velder Crace below the ribs on the right side, and the captain folded over it.

At the edge of the hole, in the grey light falling through the hail, a dark cloak settled as if whoever had worn it had only just stopped moving.

The shot reached him after that, a flat crack off the vault ribs. The cut came after it, a sound like silk drawn quickly through a ring. The heat of the stopped bullet on his cheek came last.

---

Eleven seconds earlier, the last lamp in the vault had begun to go out.

It went quietly. The flame shrank to a blue bead on the wick and the bead shrank, and the sound of the room went with it, the hiss of the hail, the drip from the broken ribs, the faint wet rattle of Velder's breathing against the pier. Malphas had opened his good hand and let the lens fall, and the null was coming out of him. Nihiloth, his own, the one law no assay could strip because it had never been borrowed. Where it reached, things stopped being true. The air was going first.

The next few moments were not going to be good ones for Malphas.

Mu-jin's essence came out of him like a furnace door kicked open.

There was nothing left in it to hold back. There had been, all his life: the court's careful voice in his head, the Accord's four assessments, the old habit of apologising before he corrected anyone, the long patient way he taught. The crucible had taken all of it. It had taken everything that was not his, and it had taken it into the walls of his own bones, and what was left at the centre of him was the stone and nothing else. The stone did not apologise. The essence that poured out of him was pure in the way a bead is pure at the end of an assay, after the colours have run across it and finished. There was no base metal left in it to burn.

It met the null half-made.

The blue bead on the lamp-wick flared back up into flame. The hiss of the hail came back. The air stayed. Malphas's Nihiloth had only begun to come out of him, and an unfinished Domain is only an intention, and Mu-jin's essence went through the intention like a fist through wet paper. The null tore along its edge and went back into Malphas, unspent, and Malphas staggered.

And with the essence came Lapis, clean, at full.

Every reaction in fifteen metres felt it: the rust on the cot frames bloomed faster, and the meltwater on the flags began to steam. The smell of struck matches came up so strong it stung, and Mu-jin knew, with the part of him that was still a teacher, that he could no longer tell where his field stopped and the room began.

---

Three floors up and a long walk north, in the Grand Mage's office, Niran Yukari looked down.

The Grand Mage had told him to stay, and he had stayed. He had been standing at the window with his hands clasped loosely in front of him and his weight forward, listening past the tower to the two Crystals under the south range, the hot singing one and the flat one. He had heard the hot one stagger. He had heard it go down. He had been counting, quietly, in the order of work: what he could reach from here, what he could not, and what it would cost the Grand Mage's plan if he broke it.

Then the hot one stood up and the floor under his feet changed.

It came up through the stone of the whole Academy, through the foundations and the old district main and the tower's own footings, into the soles of his feet. Cymorath had read ten thousand Crystals in his life. Never one like this. There was no second voice in it. Every practitioner he had ever listened to carried something under the main note, a teacher's habit, a family's expectation, the residue of what they had drawn and what they had borrowed and what they had been told they were. This one carried nothing. It was one tone, struck clean.

*Was that the Open Crucible? Hold on to nothing that is not yours, and what is left is that?*

He thought of his father pushing his reading-lenses up into his hair. He thought of Senri's words on the stone. *Focus on the innards, and you will understand the outer world.*

The ink in the Grand Mage's inkwell had stopped shivering in rings. It had started to steam.

---

At the broken low wall behind the south range, where the infirmary's rosemary bed had been an hour ago, Agnes Tull had her arms spread wide and her keys jangling, and she was walking backward into a crowd of students as fast as her legs would take her.

"Back. Back. Back, I said, I don't care whose son you are." The gap in her teeth put a whistle in every s. "There's a hole in the ground and a storm in the sky and a smell coming up out of it that would turn milk, and if any of you go over that edge I'm the one answers for it, so back."

A first-year in grey had got onto the rubble on his hands and knees to look down. Agnes took him by the collar and lifted him off it like a kitten.

Behind her the hole breathed. Steam came up out of it in a long slow column into the hail, and inside the steam there were little lights, white and very bright, too many to count, popping like fat in a pan. A wardsmith in a leather apron had his slate out at the rim and was trying to take a reading, and the needle on his gauge had gone round once and was going round again.

"What's it say?" Agnes demanded.

The wardsmith looked at the needle, then at her. "It says somebody down there just stopped being polite."

On the infirmary steps, Wat Pellow stood with Matron's chit still crumpled in his fist and watched the little lights go up through the hail. He did not know what they were. He only knew he had carried a message to the Grand Mage an hour ago about a girl with cellar-cold hands, and that he would be telling his grandchildren about this morning if he lived to have any.

---

Mu-jin lifted his left hand into his coat.

The case was gone, rotted to green powder on the flags. The coat was not. A coat that has been lined by an alchemist for twenty years has pockets inside pockets, and in the deepest of them, sewn shut against exactly this kind of morning, there were six small vials of very fine powder. Grey, silver, near-black. Aluminium, magnesium, iron filings ground finer than flour. He had carried them for a decade as the closing demonstration of a lecture, the one he gave every first-year class on the last day of term. It was the lecture on surface area. A lump of metal sits on a bench for a hundred years. Grind it fine enough, throw it into the air, and every grain is a surface, and every surface wants to burn.

He tore the lining open with his teeth and threw all six.

The glass broke in the air over Malphas's head. The powder went out of it in a bloom, a glittering grey-silver cloud that spread through the vault in a heartbeat, and in the heartbeat after it Lapis touched every grain at once.

It was not one explosion. It was thousands.

Every grain of metal in the cloud met the air around it and burned, all of them, separately, in the same instant. A dust cloud in a flour mill does that on its own when a spark finds it; it is why mills blow apart. This one did not wait for a spark. It went off as a storm of tiny white points, each one a star the size of a pinhead, popping and crackling through the whole cloud like a field of sparklers lit together. The light was blinding. The heat came off it in a wall, and the noise was one continuous ripping crackle that went on and on.

Where the cloud was densest, down along Malphas's right side, the little explosions ran together into a sheet.

The sheet sheared into him. It went along his right flank where the acid had already opened him, from armpit to hip, and the skin and the wool and the crust of the old burn went with it. He was flung backward off his feet. He struck the row of empty cots and went over them in a tangle of iron and blanket, and came down on the black floor with his coat on fire.

Behind Mu-jin, Velder Crace had got his left hand into his boot.

The captain had been watching since Mu-jin dragged him out of the gas. He had watched the man who saved his life close his wounds, and stand up from death, and take everything his lord had ever stolen and pour it into his own bones, and he had made up his mind. The pocket revolver came out nickelled and stubby, a five-shot rimfire small enough to hide in a palm. He brought it up in the wrong hand, wavering, and thumbed the hammer back with what was left of the right. He fired.

The bullet came out of the dark at a little under nine hundred feet a second.

And in that same instant, Malphas's own breath caught fire.

---

The hydrogen sulphide had been lying around Malphas all morning. It had come out of his mouth and his Black Ground and pooled in the coffin niches and along the low walls, heavy and thick, four parts in a hundred and climbing. Hydrogen sulphide burns. It burns slowly on its own, with a soft blue flame, if something lights it.

Lapis lit it. Lapis did not make it burn slowly.

Every reaction in the vault went at once, a million, a billion times its natural speed, and the gas that had been Malphas's oldest weapon went up around him in a single blue roar. It came out of the niches and off the floor and out of his own coat, out of his own lungs, a sheet of cornflower-blue fire that stood up round him waist-high and burned hot and choking, throwing off the white sulphur smoke that makes the eyes run. He was standing in the middle of his own breath when it lit.

And the bullet stopped in the air in front of Mu-jin's face, and turned, and went back into the man who had fired it, and the cloak at the edge of the hole settled.

Mu-jin stumbled forward.

His legs were dead from the knee down and his bones were full past bearing, and he went forward anyway, three steps, four, the dead calves taking his weight like the stumps of a broken table. He did not look behind him at Velder, or up at the hole. He looked at the man burning in front of him.

Malphas screamed.

It was the first sound he had made all morning that he had not chosen. He was on his knees in the black among the overturned cots, and the blue fire was in his clothes and his hair and the crown of spurs. The spurs were pale bone and they did not burn, but the scalp they grew from did. He beat at himself with his good hand. The fire went out where he struck it and came back the moment his hand moved on, because the gas was coming out of him. It was in his breath and his skin and the Black Ground under his knees. He was the fuel.

Mu-jin's face changed.

Whatever had been gentle in it was gone. The teacher who said *now, now* when he gave ground, who looked pleased for one full second before he remembered to look severe, who apologised for explaining: none of that was on him now. He swayed. Left, and right, and left again, a slow pendulum, blood running from his mouth and his nose and the cracked corners of his lips, his white-blond hair dark with it at the temples. He stood in the steam coming up off the floor like a man standing in a forge with the doors open, and he looked at Malphas the way an assayer looks at a sample. He did not look at him as a man at all.

*Reserve 471,400,000 EU.* Lapis at full across fifteen metres had cost him ten million in a breath, and he did not feel the loss of it, because nothing reached him on time any more.

He did not stop.

Every reaction in the field ran a billion times faster, and the field did not end at the skin. The reactions in a body are reactions. Malphas had shown him that an hour ago, crouched over him on the floor, fermenting the sugar in his blood. *The thinking you are so proud of runs on sugar and salt and water, like everything else.*

So did Malphas.

His body began to burst.

It started with the burns. Where the blue fire had touched him, the skin did not blister slowly over an hour the way a burn does. It blistered in a heartbeat, the fluid under it boiling up, and the blisters split and wept and split again. The water in his tissue was reaching the boil. The small vessels under the skin, already weakened by heat, gave all at once. Blood came out of him in a fine dark spray from a hundred places, from his scalp around the spurs, from the backs of his hands, from the split along his flank. It came out of his nose and mouth in a gush. His eyes went red to the rims as the vessels in them broke.

And Mu-jin bled with him.

Lapis did not ask whose body a reaction belonged to. It never had. Speed every reaction in fifteen metres and you speed the ones in your own flesh, and Mu-jin's flesh was a ruin: burned, rotted, gassed and half drowned. The gangrene in his calves ran up past his knees in a slow green tide. The burn on his thigh split open and wept. His own nose and gums bled, the small vessels giving all at once as they had in the other man, the same vessels, the same failure. His lungs, half full of fluid, filled faster. A catalyst speeds both ways at once, and nothing was holding him together with a seal any more.

They mirrored each other across six feet of burning floor. Two men in the steam, both bleeding from the mouth, both burned down the right side, both with their legs going under them. One was on his knees in his own blue fire. The other swayed upright in a cloud of struck matches. Mu-jin could not have said which of them was winning, not then, not with the floor steaming and the blood in his mouth and his bones full of another man's theft. He knew only that he was the one standing.

Malphas lifted his head.

The blue flame had gone down to a flicker in his coat and his hair. His face was a ruin of split blisters and blood, the crown of spurs standing out of it white and wet. His cooked left hand lay curled in his lap with the lattice black. He looked up at the man swaying over him, and he smiled, and blood ran out between his teeth.

"There," Malphas said. His voice was a wet rasp, almost tender. "Now we match."

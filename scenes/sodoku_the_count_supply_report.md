# The Count

*Kharven-Seat, the north gate, continuous. Night, the eleventh year.*

---

She came off him in stages.

First the fists, which had to be talked out of the fur one finger at a time by their owner, and which left four vertical channels in the pelt that stayed there. Then the forehead, peeling off the breastplate with a small wet sound. Then the whole small weight of her, stepping back two paces onto her own feet on ground that had frozen solid in a ring three yards wide while she stood on it.

Sodoku's breastplate was streaked from collarbone to sternum. Tears, snot, and a rime of ice already forming over both where the Glacium had taken the heat out of them, and Hild looked at the mess she had made of a Titan-proof cuirass and something went through her face that he recognized before he could stop recognizing it. Mortification. The specific eleven-year-old kind, arriving three seconds behind the feeling that caused it.

She scrubbed her sleeve across her nose. The mail hem swung against her thighs.

Then the wall went back up.

He watched it happen from two feet away and he had never seen anything built that fast. The jaw set. The shoulders came back. The chin came down half an inch so that she was looking at people through the top of her eyes, which was a posture nobody teaches a child and every child on a broken throne learns anyway. Her breathing steadied in four counts. The frost stopped spreading, hesitated at its outer edge, and held.

*He is taller than the stories. The stories never said he would be tall.* Hild kept her hands at her sides where they could be seen. *He is going to look at the yard next and see that we have not swept it, and I told Robin to sweep it, and I am going to hear about the yard for the rest of my life.*

Her eyes went past his shoulder.

Sodoku knew what she had found without turning, because there was only one thing standing behind him worth that particular stillness. He watched his daughter run the comparison across his brother's face, feature by feature, the way a factor checks a delivery against a manifest. Same architecture. Same height off the ground. Crimson where he was dark, purple where he was pale, and the whole of it hung on a frame that carried itself in a register she had clearly filed under something familiar, because her eyes flicked once toward Lambert and back.

Whatever conclusion she drew, she kept.

The wind came through the open gate and took the last of the warmth out of the yard.

Lambert had waited through all of it without shifting his weight, and when he answered the question that had been put to him he answered it at the speed of a man who had prepared the figures four days ago and had been holding them since, which was the entire function of his office and the only apology he had ever offered for existing.

"Three thousand two hundred inside the walls." His voice did not rise for it. "Eleven hundred bushel of rye, four hundred of barley, ninety of oats and a granary floor I would not swear an oath on. Forty barrels of salt pork, which is eleven fewer than the ledger says, and I know which eleven and I know who took them and I have chosen the timing of that conversation deliberately. Sixty-one sound horses. Eight hundred and forty men capable of a day's march in this weather, and I want the phrase *capable of a day's march* understood as the low estimate it is."

He turned his hand over. The burn scar caught the tallow light.

"At the ration we are currently issuing, one hundred and sixty days. Call it five months and a fortnight and be pleased about it. At a true ration, the ration a man needs to swing an axe in cold like this, ninety." The thumb went once against the index finger and stopped. "We are issuing two thirds. We have been issuing two thirds since the equinox."

Snow ticked against the gate leaves.

"The timber towns eat full," Lambert said. "Understand that before you form an opinion about it. Ironwood is the only thing this kingdom still sells that anyone still wants, and a man cannot fell ironwood on two thirds, and if the felling stops then the last thing we can trade stops with it. So Brackenhold eats and Whitmoor eats and Cold Cross eats, and the people inside these walls go to bed on a heel of rye and a mug of hot water with a pork rind in it, and they have been doing that for four months, and they do it without a great deal of complaint because they are northern and because complaining does not fill anything."

*And because they have decided the child is worth it, which is a resource that does not appear in any ledger I keep and which I have been spending faster than I can replace it.*

"The Thin Weeks came early this year," Lambert said. "They came in the second month instead of the fourth. Nobody in this city has used the phrase correctly since, because the phrase assumes an end."

He let that stand a moment, and Sodoku understood that the old man was building toward something and was giving him room to arrive at it first.

"The death-house has not been staffed in four years," Lambert said. "There is no one to keep the Waiting. The families keep it themselves now, in their own front rooms, with the body on the table and the window open and the children sleeping in the same house for three days because there is nowhere else to put them. Ada Foss kept her husband three days in a room ten feet by twelve with four children in it, and she did it correctly, and when I asked her afterward whether she wanted the Crown to find her a room she told me the Crown should find her a granary." His face did nothing. "We have lost the trick of burying people properly, your highness. It went faster than I would have predicted. Four years, and a thing this kingdom did for six hundred years is now performed badly by amateurs in their own kitchens."

The tallow flames leaned in the draft and straightened.

"Then tell me what is missing that should not be missing," Sodoku said, because his brother had asked for that too and Lambert had not yet given it.

Something moved behind the heavy lids.

"Grain is missing," Lambert said. "That you know. What is not missing is inscription substrate. Stannvaard has cut our grain allocation by three quarters over two years and has delivered inscription substrate on schedule, in full, every quarter, for eleven years. Through the collapse. Through the factional fighting. Through two winters when the corridor was closed to everything else moving on wheels." He paused, and it was a pause with weight in it. "I have written to the Ore Council four times asking them to explain the discrepancy. They have answered three of those letters promptly and courteously and without ever once addressing the question."

Sodoku turned that over and set it down where he could reach it later.

"They want us armed."

"They want us armed and they want us hungry," Lambert said. "An armed frontier that can feed itself is a neighbour. An armed frontier that cannot is a tool." The thumb resumed against the index finger. "I have been unable to decide, over eleven years, whether that is the Dawi being Dawi or whether somebody has been standing at the Ore Council's elbow the entire time explaining to them what a useful shape looks like. I dislike questions that have two answers of equal quality. I dislike them enough that I have stopped sleeping properly."

He stopped there.

The stopping was too clean. Sodoku had spent his adult life in rooms where the shape of a silence carried more freight than the sentences on either side of it, and this one had a corner in it, and he read the corner as the man's dead wife. Eleven years of not sleeping. A name he had not said out loud in front of the girl. It fit so well that Sodoku did not examine the fit.

"Though do not mistake me," Lambert said, and his eyes went to Hild and came back. "She has eaten. Every day. Full ration, meat twice a week, and I have fought her over it in that hall more times than I have fought her over anything else in her life, because she wants to eat what her people eat and she is entirely correct that it would be the honourable thing and she is entirely wrong about what it would cost us." He said it flatly. "She is eleven and she is not finished growing and this kingdom has exactly one thing that people will still walk three days through compromised territory to stand near. I will not have that thing die of a principle. She has called me a coward for it. She will call me worse before she is done. I have written it in the ledger under acceptable losses."

Hild's ears had gone red. She did not look away from the middle distance.

*Say it in front of him. Say it in front of all of them. Old man, I hope your knees hurt.*

"I am glad you are back," Lambert said. "That sentence is not sentiment and I would like it received as it is issued. This land needs you. I need you. I have been running a kingdom out of a lockbox and a memory for eleven years and I have run out of both, and if there is going to be a war, and there is, then somebody has to stand at the front of it who the Banner Houses will follow without first requiring an argument."

"You will get one argument," Sodoku said.

"I expect several."

"You will get one from me. The rest will come up out of the Houses." He looked at the patched course in the wall. "Six of them, and every one has spent eleven years learning that the Crown is a thing you survive rather than a thing you serve. I am not walking into that hall assuming an oath transfers. Oaths expired with my father. We will be re-swearing this kingdom one house at a time and paying for each one, and I want to know what we have to pay with before I stand in front of the first of them."

"That," said Lambert, "is the correct order of operations, and I have been waiting eleven years to hear somebody say it in that order."

Sodoku turned his head.

Emira was still standing where she had come to a halt, and the aura she carried had gone down into its dormant state, and she had said her piece, all of it, the way she always did: straight through, no gaps, nowhere for him to put a word until she was finished. He let a breath go out of him. It steamed and the steam went sideways off the wind.

"You are right about the debt," he said. "You are wrong about which of us is carrying it, and I am not going to argue that with you in a wind at this hour, so I will concede the ground and take it back later when I have somewhere warm to do it from."

He picked a stone up off the gravel with his bare hand and weighed it and let it drop.

"And I never said I did not need it. I said you owed me nothing. Those are two sentences and they only sound alike to somebody who has already decided which one she is going to be angry about."

Yoko's ears came around. She had absorbed the sister-in-law remark without any visible reaction at all, which anyone who knew her understood to be the reaction.

"I am not throwing myself into anything," she said. "I have a child asleep on the third sledge and a husband with a documented tendency to walk toward large problems in order to see them better. My entire function on this expedition has been to stand behind that tendency and describe what it is walking toward." Her tail moved once. "I would appreciate the assistance."

*She is teasing me, which means she has decided I am family, which is a promotion I did not apply for and will not be returning.*

"As for quiet," Sodoku said.

He looked at the gate lamps. Tallow, guttering, throwing that thick yellow light that never reaches a corner.

"Quiet is what a thing sounds like when it is not currently pointed at you. I never saw a body. Neither did you. Neither did anyone we can name." He said it without heat. "I will not build a plan on a corpse nobody has produced. If he is dead then the world is a smaller and better place and I have lost nothing by having been careful. If he is not, then we walk in expecting weather and we are not surprised when it snows."

Beyond the yard, three streets up, past the broken fountain and the black stumps of the counting-house, a lamp burned in an upper window of a house that had no business having lamp oil.

Sodoku found it without meaning to. Oil, not tallow. The flame stood straight and white and small. He held on it for perhaps two seconds.

The lamp went out.

The flame did not gutter and nobody carried it back from the glass. Somebody had put a hand over it, and the hand had been quick, and Sodoku stood in a ruined courtyard with his daughter's frost around his boots and filed the window and could make nothing whatsoever of it.

Taulagi had seen it too. The old man's chin had come up perhaps a quarter of an inch.

*Third house past the fountain. Second floor. Left of the chimney.* Taulagi's hands stayed exactly where they were. *I will find out who lives there before I sleep, and I will not be telling anyone that I intend to.*

"Please come this way."

Hild's voice came out with the wall behind it, and it carried, because she had learned to make it carry in a hall full of men who wanted her to be small.

"It is cold and you are all standing in it, and half your column has been standing in it since the gate came off the bar, and there is nothing at this gate that cannot be discussed indoors." She caught herself. Adjusted. Came down half a register. "The palace is not habitable. The east wing has no roof and the wells under it are fouled. I have been living at the Hallenfeld factor's house on the low street, which has a kitchen that works and a table long enough for all of us, and it is nine minutes' walk and the walk is uphill and I am telling you that now so that nobody complains about it later."

She turned to Lambert.

"Edward, you will accompany us. We plan tonight." The chin came down again. "While we can."

Lambert bowed shallow.

"Your grace."

The girl put her hand into the pocket of the slate overdress and closed it around something and Sodoku watched her do it. He knew the shape through the wool before he knew he knew it: a river-smoothed hearth stone, palm-sized, the kind that goes into the fire at supper and comes out wrapped in flannel and goes to the foot of a bed. Somebody had given her a night-stone. She had been carrying it since the messenger came, gripping it through her clothes, and it had gone stone-cold hours ago and she had not put it down.

She started walking.

She got four paces and stopped and did not turn around, and stood there in the frozen ring with her back to all of them, waiting, and would not ask.

Sodoku crouched, picked his gauntlet up out of the gravel, and went after her.

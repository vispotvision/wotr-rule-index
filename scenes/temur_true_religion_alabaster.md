# The True Religion

*The Hallenfeld factor's house, the low street, Kharven-Seat. Late that night.*

---

Warmth got into the armour before it got into him.

That was the order it always came in. The Glacium plates had been holding polar temperature for eleven days and they gave it up grudgingly, sweating in the kitchen air, beading water along the lames of the fauld and dripping it onto flagstones that had been swept but not scrubbed. Under the metal his gambeson stank. Wool and old sweat and the ammoniac sharpness that eleven days in harness produces in any man regardless of bloodline, and over that the smell of the room itself: peat smoke, boiled rye, hot iron off the range plate, wet wool steaming on the drying rail, and something scorched at the bottom of a pot that somebody had scraped and put back on the fire anyway.

Temür sat on a bench built for a factor's clerks and started on the buckles.

Pauldrons first. Then the gorget, which came away with a sucking noise and a rush of trapped heat that fogged in front of his face. He set each piece down on the flags in the order he would put it back on, because that was a habit his father had beaten into him at nine years old and because a man who has to hunt for a vambrace in the dark has already lost whatever is happening in the dark.

The fire cracked. A door banged somewhere in the back of the house and a woman's voice called up the passage asking how somebody's stack was, and a man's voice answered that it was four ricks and all of it green, and the woman said something short that got a laugh out of two people.

Green wood. In the second month.

"Whatever he decides in his personal life is his business," Temür said. "He is thirty-one years old and he has never once taken advice about a woman, including from our father, who tried twice and stopped." He worked the left couter free. "If he wants to keep a demon in his room he will keep a demon in his room, and if it becomes a matter that touches the Crown then it stops being personal and becomes mine, and he knows exactly where that line sits because we drew it together when we were still children with nothing to divide."

The couter went down on the pile.

"He is here. That is what I asked for. I did not attach conditions to it."

Across the kitchen, Hild had planted herself in front of Yoko with her hands behind her back and her chin at that same borrowed angle, and Temür made the mistake of reading the posture as curiosity.

"So you must be the lovely woman who has kept him safe," Hild said.

The tail moved once behind Yoko and stilled.

"I try." She looked down at the girl and did not crouch to her height, which Temür noticed, and which Hild noticed harder. "Your father is arrogant. He does not listen. He walks toward large problems in order to see them better and he calls it assessment." A pause exactly one breath long. "I have assumed it was the prince in him. I have no evidence. I have not met enough princes to make a comparison, and one is a poor sample."

*She did not lie to me.* Hild kept her hands where they were. *Everyone lies to me. Osric lies to me kindly and Pietro lied to me cruelly and Edward tells me the truth in a way that is built to make me act, which is a third thing. This one just answered the question.*

The girl looked at her a moment longer, and Temür watched his daughter take the full inventory. Silver-white at the roots of the fur ruff where the winter coat had come in thick. Dark grey through the crown. A face with too much bone in it to be soft.

Fern would have liked her.

Fern would have liked her and been jealous of her in the same breath and would have said both out loud at the table, in front of company, because she had never in her life been able to hold two feelings without narrating them. He had heard her do it about a Hallenfeld factor's wife, a horse, and once, memorably, about a bowl.

*The child is testing whether I will lower myself to her height, and I am not going to, because she is the only person in this house who has been talked down to since she could stand and I refuse to be the twelfth.* Yoko's ears stayed forward. *She will work out what that means on her own. She is quick. She has already worked out three things about me that her father did not notice in a year.*

"Enkhtuya." Temür got the last strap on the cuirass and the front and back plates came apart and the cold air in the gap between them made him grunt. "I am glad you are safe. I have wanted to say that for four days and there has been no hour in which saying it would not have sounded like a preamble to an order."

He set the breastplate against the bench leg.

"Robin went south nine days ago. Fast horse, no escort, sealed pouch, and instructions to burn it if he was stopped by anyone wearing anything at all." He rubbed his forearm where the vambrace had been. "He carries a request for aid to the Holy Sea of Alabaster."

Lambert's head came up.

He had taken the chair nearest the range, coat still on, and had been warming the burned hand against the iron in a way that suggested it hurt him in cold and that he had spent eleven years declining to mention it.

"You expect the Alabasterines to answer that."

"Yes."

"Your highness." Lambert set the hand flat on his knee. "I have written four letters to the Ore Council in eleven years. I have received three replies. All three were courteous. None of them contained a fact. Alabaster is farther, poorer in grain than Stannvaard, and has never once moved a granary north for a kingdom that was not in its charter. I would like to know what you think you are holding that I have not been able to see from this chair."

Temür took his time.

The fire went down and somebody in the back passage put a log on it and the log was green and hissed.

"The Alabasterines keep our traditions," he said. "Ours, and they have never once pretended otherwise in a document that carried a seal. The Holy Sea's rite for testimony descends out of the same body of practice my mother spent nineteen years teaching, and she taught it in the Holy Sea's own halls, in a language she never learned properly, to men who wrote it down and filed it under a heading she would not have recognised." He looked at the fire. "She was not a missionary. She went because the Fusi Vā was dying and Alabaster was the only place that would sit still long enough to be taught. She did not do it for Kharven. Kharven inherited it anyway."

"And she is dead."

"She is dead," Temür agreed. "Along with the last four people who could bind an oath into anything harder than breath. There is no Fusi Vā in this kingdom. There has not been one since she died. Six Banner Houses swore to my father in a rite that made the swearing structural, and when the rite died the oaths went back to being words, and words came apart in a winter." He rolled his shoulder in the socket. "Ask yourself why the Houses fragmented along exactly the lines they fragmented along, and then ask yourself what was holding those lines before."

Lambert said nothing for a moment. The thumb had stopped.

*He has put his finger on the load-bearing member and he has done it in one sentence, which nobody in this kingdom has managed in eleven years including me, and I have been sitting in the ledger the entire time looking at the grain.*

"Stannvaard," Temür said.

He said the name and let it sit in the room and picked up a rag and started on the water beaded across the breastplate, because the plates spotted if you left them.

"Cut a kingdom's grain by three quarters and deliver its inscription substrate in full for eleven years, and you are not trading. You are dressing a war measure in a ledger so that nobody has to write the word down." The rag went around the neck of the plate. "Ask what my father would have done. He would have taken four thousand men down the corridor and stood them at the Ring gate and asked the Ore Council to explain the discrepancy in person. He would have got an explanation. That is what men mean when they say he was vicious. They mean he was fast."

"And you propose to be slow."

"I propose to be correct first and fast afterward." He turned the plate over. "But do not mistake what I think of them. What Stannvaard is doing is a proposition of war conducted with a quill, and it is a disgrace to the standard the Scale claims to hold, and I am willing to say that out loud in a room with a Measurewright in it."

"They do not worship," Lambert said. "Sanctum Lux. Whatever you are about to build on, they will not stand on it with you. The first line of their catechism is a refusal. They strike the word church out of every filing that carries a seal, they call the Archons crowned principles, and they call anyone who kneels to a measure a man who has already agreed to be lied to. I have read the Catechism of Equal Breath. Twice, and the second time was to be sure I had understood the first." His voice stayed level. "You will send them a letter about the True Religion and they will file it as a category error and answer it courteously and it will contain no facts."

"I know what is in their catechism."

Temür set the rag down.

"I am not sending them a sermon. I know they do not kneel. I respect the refusal more than I respect most prayers, and I will tell any Lector who asks me that a body which declines to be adored is the only body in four worlds I would let near my kingdom's accounts." He looked up. "Here is the thing they will not enjoy hearing, and it is true, and it is why I think it will move them.

"The Büri did not build the Scale. We are one of the things it was calibrated on.

"The Works are older than the charter. Older than Izayoi, older than the Great Spirit War, older than anyone's chapter hold. They were written before time and they are the meanings stripped down to the bone, and the Archons are those same meanings crowned. Sanctum Lux measures against a reference that descends out of that. So does my blood. My family has carried Ajiin in the eye for longer than that order has existed, and it did not come to us through a charter, and no one issued it to us, and there is no procedure by which it can be revoked." He said it flatly. "That is what the labours are. The Works are the labour and the Archons are what the labour became when it needed a sovereign to keep it running. My mother spent her life on the difference. You people see the settlement and the blood in the corridor and you call it savagery, and you are entitled to, and I have never once argued it. Do not confuse it with what we believe. We hold to the high religion of the labours. It is older than the north, it is older than the Ashen Crown, and it does not require anyone's permission to be true."

The kitchen had gone quiet. Somebody in the back passage had stopped talking.

"I can freeze the water in a man's chest from nine paces," Temür said. "I can put a verdict through plate. Every one of those is a physical thing and all of it is the smaller half. The power that held this kingdom together was a woman in a hall with her hands open, binding six oaths into something that would still be load-bearing when everyone in the room was dead, and she could do it because she understood what nature is at the level where it can be spoken to. She is dead and the binding is gone and here we are, three thousand two hundred people on two thirds and green wood."

He let a breath out.

"But you are right about the letter."

Lambert's eyebrow moved one degree.

"None of that goes to Alabaster," Temür said. "You are correct and I will not waste a courier proving you wrong. What goes to Alabaster is testimony. Their Wellspring is testimony, their whole apparatus is built to weigh a sworn account, and I am sending them a sworn account: three thousand two hundred inside the walls, one hundred and sixty days at two thirds, ninety at a true ration, and a substrate delivery arriving on schedule through two closed winters. Signed. Sealed. Attestable. Let their chapter hold read it and let them forward it to Altherion, because a body that exists to measure consequence has to weigh a starvation conducted through commerce or admit out loud that its instrument does not reach a ledger."

Lambert sat back in the chair.

"That," he said, "is the letter I would have written, and I would have taken four days to arrive at it, and I would have arrived by the wrong road."

"You arrived at it in this room in nine minutes."

"I arrived at it by disagreeing with you. That is a different skill and a cheaper one." He flexed the burned hand. "I will want the numbers checked against the granary floor before anything is sealed. I do not trust that floor and I will not swear to a figure I have not stood on."

"Do it tonight."

"I intend to do it before I sleep, your highness, and I want it on record that I said so first."

Temür stood, and stretched his back until something in it moved, and while he was upright he found himself looking at the shelf above the range without having decided to.

Sixteen jars, most of them empty, one of them holding rendered fat. And at the end, standing apart from the rest with its wax seal intact, a squat brown bottle of burn-salve. Properly made. Rendered clean, no separation at the neck, the kind of preparation that takes an alchemist with a graduated glass and nine hours and a reagent supply.

In a house with tallow in the lamps.

Temür looked at it for four seconds and could make nothing of it at all.

Hild had gone to the range and put both hands flat on the warm iron and stood there with her shoulders up around her ears, and after a moment she took the night-stone out of her pocket and set it down against the plate to heat.

"Sit down and eat," she said, to the room, in the voice she used in the hall. "All of you. There is rye and there is pork and there is enough tonight because I said there is enough tonight, and Edward is going to tell me tomorrow what tonight cost, and tomorrow is when I will care."

She dragged a bench out from the table with both hands and both feet and got it into position, and sat.

Temür came and sat down beside her.

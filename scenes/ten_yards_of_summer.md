# Ten Yards of Summer

*The Kessen ford in spring flood, the hour of the dog; Kharven-Seat, the next morning; the cedar wood on the Kessen road, the second evening*

---

He had a heartbeat, and he spent it looking.

Afterward Shōki could never say why. The law did not let go of him to do it; the law had both his hands on the old man's chest and the book half closed, and the black hand on his soul was pulling north as hard as it had ever pulled. But the old man had said *look*, in that scalded, gentle, physician's voice, and three hundred years ago a woman with copper in her hair had said the same word to him on a bridge over a different river, pointing at a heron standing on one leg in the shallows, laughing at him because he had walked the whole length of the bridge with his eyes on the boards. *Look, Shō. You never look at anything that isn't owed you.* The word went into him by that old road, the one he had burned at Gisei and thought was gone, and his head turned on his neck before he had decided anything, the way a headsman's head turns at the sound of the drum.

So he looked outward. For the first time in three centuries his eye went out of himself and stayed out, and it found the girl.

She was very small. She had on a quilted spring jacket the yellow of a festival lantern, printed all over with red plum blossoms, and the flood had her by it and was rolling her down the green water toward him, face up and then face down and then face up, her arms going, her mouth open on nothing because the river was in it. Behind her on the bridge the brass lamps swung on their chains and threw their gold light across the spray, and her mother was running along the planks with both hands out, screaming a name he did not catch. Ten yards. Eight. Below her, round his own waist, the Kessen was boiling white and loud, *GOBOBOBOBOBOBO*, a pot left on a forge, and in three more heartbeats that yellow jacket would be in it and the child inside it would be cooked like a crab.

Shiin. Everything on the bridge went quiet at once, the way a crowd goes quiet when it sees what is about to happen and cannot stop it.

His left hand came off the old man's chest.

It was the quench hand, the swordsmith's hand, the one drawing the Karmic Fire's heat back out of Raizan as fast as the right hand poured it in, and the moment it lifted, the moment there was nothing pulling the fire out of the old man any more, the fire had nowhere to go but out through him. Shōki knew it as he moved. He moved anyway. *Ba.* He leaned into the flood with his whole long body and the river roared against his ribs, and his hand went into the green water to the elbow and closed on yellow cloth.

*Jūūūū.* The jacket smoked the instant he touched it. His fingers found her wrist under the sleeve, thin as a reed, and the skin of it blistered under his palm with a sound like fat dropped on a griddle, and the girl, who had swallowed half the Kessen and should not have had breath for anything, screamed. It was a terrible sound. It was the first sound in three hundred years he had heard as a sound and not as a debt, and it went through him from the ear to the heel. He lifted her out of the boil in one sweep, up through the steam into the cold spring air, the water pouring off her quilted jacket in a sheet, and he did not hold her. He could not hold her; his hands were forge iron. He threw her, underhand and soft as he had ever thrown anything, out across the last of the white water toward the bank, and she went through the air in a little yellow arc and came down in the cold shallows below the bridge stair with a slap, *bashan*, into the reach of her mother's arms.

The mother had her. The mother was on her knees in the shallows with the child clutched against her and her face lifted toward him, and the face was the white of a plate.

Behind him, the old man died.

What was left of the Karmic Fire went out of Raizan Tenrai Moto through every vessel he had. Shōki had burned most of the old man's account down already, four hundred and twelve and less and less, and what remained was too little to take the ford. It was enough to take the man. The blood came out of him all at once as a red steam, out of the scalded hands and the broken cheek, out of the eyes and the nose and the mouth, out of the pores of his skin in a fine hot mist that hung over the flat stone and turned the steam pink, and it came down on Shōki like a rain. It ran down his face. It ran into his braids and down his crazed white arms and over the cracked Covenant Scar on his chest, and for once it did not hiss on him. It was too hot already to hiss. Under it, the old man's skin withered. It drew back on his bones the way a fig draws back on its stone when it is dried in a kiln, the long fingers going thin and brown and spotted, the face hollowing under the cheekbones until the courteous old smile was a dry seam, and the slow spokes of light that had turned in his pale eyes for seventy-seven years went round one last time and stopped where they were.

Raizan lay back. He did not fall. He leaned back off the flat stone into the arms of the river with his hands open on his chest, the way an old man settles back into a bath at the end of a long day, and the Kessen took him gently and held him there against the stone and ran on round him, pink, then clear, then green.

*Look,* he had said. Shōki had looked. He stood in the river covered from the brow to the waist in the old man's blood and found that he could not stop.

He could feel.

That was the thing the look had done. It had opened the old road a crack, and something had come up it. Forgiveness could never have come up that road; forgiveness would have emptied the whole bank into his own frame at once and burst him like a kiln with a wet pot in it, and HAKANASHI knew that and would never let it near him. What came up the road was the other thing, the thing on the other side of the same door. Wrath had been a law in him for three hundred years, cold and exact as a register, a sentence he carried out because a sentence must be carried out. Now it was a feeling. It came up hot from the pit of him and filled his chest and his throat, and it had other things tangled in it like weed in a net: hatred, sharp and clean as a new blade; anger, which is wrath that wants to be seen; and under them, slow and heavy and enormous, grief. He was grieving. He stood in the river with the old man's blood cooling on his lips and grieved for a woman three hundred years dead, felt the size of the place where she had been, which had been only the hottest part of him for so long that he had forgotten it had a shape. And under even that, last and worst, regret: for the yellow jacket and the blistered wrist, and for every chest he had ever counted without once lifting his eye from the boards.

*A man who learns to love takes on the risk of hate, Shō.* Tsuyu had said that to him too, in their first year, in her father's copper-roofed house, with her feet in his lap. *It's the same door. You don't get to open it only one way.*

He had opened it. Gisei had burned the door off its hinges and he had thought that was the end of doors. And now an old Tenrai with a needle in his sleeve had put a word into it and leaned it open again, and what came through first was hate, all of it, three hundred years of it at once, alive.

The black hand closed on his soul, hard, harder than it had ever held him, because now it had something to grip. It pulled north. North was the Seat. North was the Hall of Verdicts and its seven chairs, and the heirs of the seven, and in the red chair, where nobody had sat in the long years of the war, somebody sat now. He could feel it the way a compass needle feels the pole: a weight at the top of the world, gold and slate gray, ringing on one note.

Around him the Kessen boiled, *gogogogogogo*, and his body thrummed with it, every crack in his skin lit ember-orange from inside. On the bank the mother was carrying her daughter up the bridge stair two steps at a time, and the girl was looking back at him over her mother's shoulder with river water in her hair and his handprint on her wrist, and she was not crying any more. She was only looking. Shōki found he could not bear it, and turned his back.

The bedframe had split again in the night. Muken sat on the edge of it in the gray dragon kimono with his elbows on his knees and looked at the long new crack running through the oak where his hands had been, and at the old cracks beside it, and at the iron bands the carpenter had put on after the third time, and thought he would have to have the whole thing made out of stone.

The east tower of Kharven-Seat took the sun first in the mornings, before anything else in the north, and it was taking it now. It came in level through the tall windows and laid long gold bars across the floor and up the wall, across the chest with his mail on it and the rack where Tsugigane hung in its red-laced scabbard with the white cord at its guard, and below the windows the whole Seat was waking up in its colors: the blue slate roofs going silver with melt, the brass lamp-stems on the Guild rounds ticking and steaming as the morning wardens took the flames down, *tik, tik, tikitik*, the market bell going in the lower town and the smoke of a hundred bread ovens standing up straight in the still air. Out past the walls the ice was going out of the Marrow in great white plates that turned over in the black water and showed their green bellies to the sun. Spring. Six months since the war, and the north was coming out of the snow like a man coming out of a fever, weak and surprised to be alive.

He had dreamed of heat, for once, instead of the cellars: a forge door left open somewhere very far south, and a long road, and something walking up it.

"You've got that face on again."

Amelia Greymane was at the low table by the window with the tea things, where she had been for the last quarter hour, pretending she had come up the tower stairs because the tea needed carrying and not because her brother had sent her. She was in her court clothes, which on her meant the oxblood cuirass with fewer knives on it and the longsword at her hip instead of the axe, and the sun was on her hair, which was gold, bright and warm and cut short and ragged at the jaw, with the one thin Shieldwarden braid drawn back over her ear. She poured him a cup. She did not ask if he wanted it. She had worked out within a week that he never wanted anything until it was in his hand, and she had stopped asking.

[flat northern Kharven, dry] "Here."

He took the cup and she did not let go of it at once; she let go of it and then put her hand on his face, the left one, the ruined one, the knuckles ridged white and the two little fingers bent toward each other like old men on a bench. Three young lords had picked that hand up and looked at it as if they had picked up a dead bird. She laid it flat against his cheek as if it were a perfectly ordinary hand, and it was warm, and callused shiny from the axe-haft, and it smelled of axe oil.

"You should take that scowl off your face," she said. "It's a bad one. It makes the servants walk round the long way to the stair."

[young northern Kharven, a salt-gallery cutter's vowels under a king's manners, embarrassed] Muken blinked at her. He felt the heat come up his neck, which was a thing that happened to him now, which had not happened to him in eighteen years of salt galleries and free companies and war, and which he did not know what to do with. The tea in the cup trembled. So did the lamp on its chain over the table, very faintly, *tinnnn*, because his voice when he answered came lower than he meant it to.

"What have I told you about this?" he said. "I'm a king. You can't go about babying me. It's not dignified."

He said it to the room at large, with great sternness, as if a hall full of Banner lords were listening and it mattered very much what they thought. There was nobody in the room but her. There was nobody on the stair; Lorn had the stair, and Lorn would rather have cut off his own hand than come up it uninvited. The only witness to the king's dignity was a gull on the windowsill, eating a crust.

Amelia looked at him. Nothing moved in her face, and nothing moved, and then something did, the way ice goes on a pond: the corner of her mouth first, and then the rest of it, and then a laugh came out of her that she plainly had not given permission to. It was a short, surprised, girlish sound, and she put the back of her ruined hand over her mouth to stop it and it got out round the sides.

"You are very strange, Muken," she said, through the hand. "Do you know that? You are the strangest man I have ever met." She took her hand back off his face and pointed a crooked finger at him. "If you tell Godfrey I laughed I'll put the axe through your bedframe. Not that it'd notice."

"It wouldn't," Muken admitted, and looked at the crack, and she laughed again.

The knock came then. It was Godfrey's knock, three hard ones and then the door opening before anyone had said come in, because Godfrey Greymane had been Hand to the King for six months and had given up waiting at doors about a week into it. He came in in his gray cloak with the ridge device on the clasp, long and straight and black-haired, with a paper in his hand and his face set in the way it set when he had read the paper twice. He saw his sister at the low table and the king on the edge of the split bed and the cup of tea between them, and something went across his face that in a lesser man would have been a grin, and he put it away.

[measured northern Kharven, an aide-de-camp's evenness] "Sire. My lady." He held up the paper. "This came up the beacon line from Kessen in the night. The Tenrai factor's office, in their own cipher, and a Tenrai rider behind it at dawn with his horse blown under him. I've had the rider fed. He's not making a lot of sense."

Muken put the cup down.

"Raizan Tenrai Moto is dead," said Godfrey. "At the Kessen ford, at the hour of the dog. The Tenrai house at the top of the town is burned to the beams. The rider says the whole ford boiled. He says the old man was killed by a man in irons who was on fire and did not burn." He looked at the paper. "He says it more than once. He says the man stood in the river and the river boiled round him, and the old man's blood came out of him as steam. And then the man walked out of the river and up through the town and took the north road, and the snow has gone off it behind him."

There was a silence in the tower. The market bell had stopped. Even the lamp-stems seemed to have stopped ticking.

Muken knew the name. Everyone in the north knew the name, the way children in the north knew the name of the White Maw and the Droval and the thing under the ice: Raizan Tenrai Moto, the Doom Breaker, the old man in the physician's coat who came to a house at breakfast and was gone by the second cup, and the lord of the house was found at his go board with his heart stopped and a single bead of blood at the nape. Kagenori Tenrai's own great-uncle, it was said, though nobody said it at the Seat. The man the Tenrai sent when they did not mean to lose.

[low, the lamps beginning to tremble on their chains] "Someone beat the Doom Breaker," he said. "Someone was able to kill Raizan Tenrai."

"Someone boiled him in a river, sire."

Muken got up and went to the window. Below him the Seat went about its morning in its blue and silver and smoke, and he did not see any of it. He was seeing the place made of threads, and the Bound Flame kneeling in it under the weight of the cracked Bell, the great gold shape of Faisal bowed with one knee to the ground, and he was hearing the slow warm voice that never left a sentence unfinished. *There are older things than your house walking under the world, Muken. Some of them were men once. When the threads begin to twist, do not go out to meet them alone. Send for me, and I will not let you fall.* He had felt the threads twist. Two weeks ago, in the gray before the first bell, he had woken with his hands on this same bedframe and the oak splitting under them, and for once it had not been the dream: something in the weave of the north had pulled out of true, malformed, knotted, the way a salt face goes wrong when there is a void behind it and the cracks begin to run in the wrong directions. And far down the south road, closer every night, something walking toward him with heat coming off it like a forge door left open, stopping every few miles, and coming on.

He had told himself it was grief. Faisal had told him the Eye would not rest, and that grief came up through it at night.

It had not been grief. The heat behind his left eye knew better, and the gold seam in his iris was warm as a coal.

"Get me a scouting party," he said. "Small. Riders, not foot. Twelve of Lorn's, and Lorn. I'll go down the Kessen road myself and look at this."

[a breath, very patient] "Sire," said Godfrey. "Let the Tenrai send their own men to look. Let the Kurenai send theirs. Let them say all of it, and come up the road and tell you. You can be in a hurry afterward."

"The Tenrai will send twenty men down that road and none of them will come back up it, and Kagenori will sit in his gold chair and tell me it was a house matter." Muken turned round. "And if it's walking north, Godfrey, it's walking here. To this tower. I'd sooner meet it in a wood than in a hall full of people who can't get out of its way."

Godfrey looked at him a long moment, and then put the paper inside his cloak. "Lorn," he said. "Twelve riders. First bell."

"Let me go with you."

Amelia had stood up from the low table. She did not say it loudly; she never said anything loudly. She stood by the window with the sun on her and her feet set apart the way they set when she went into a line, and said it as if she were giving him the count of shields on a wall. [flat, very steady] "Allow me to accompany you, my lord. I'll be honest with you, because I don't know how to be any other way. You're about to tell me no. You're about to tell me no because you've decided you'd like to keep me in this tower like a jar of something on a shelf, where nothing gets at it. I've been kept on a shelf. I didn't like it the first time."

Muken looked at her. He did want to keep her in the tower. He wanted it so badly that his Eye, without being asked, ran the gold lines over her where she stood, the crack in the second lame of her left pauldron under the fur, the old break in her nose, the knuckles, every place the world had already hit her and found her and would find her again, and the field he could not switch off came back with what it always came back with when he turned it on her: nothing with a hook in it. A solid, quiet weight, like a stone in a river. She wanted what she said she wanted. She wanted to stand in front of him.

That was exactly what frightened him.

"You should let her come, sire." Godfrey was at the door with his hand on the latch, and the grin had got out after all; it was a small one, under the long gray Greymane face, and he did not try very hard to hide it. "It'd be good for the two of you. A few days on a road. Nothing like a road for finding out what you are with somebody." He opened the door. "And she'll go anyway, and follow you at a mile off, and then I'll have to explain to the Shieldwardens why their lieutenant is sleeping in a ditch."

"I would," said Amelia.

"She would," said Godfrey, and went out, and they heard him going down the stair two at a time, whistling.

Muken stood in the gold light with the cracked bed behind him and the woman with the ruined hand in front of him, and the seam in his eye burning, and the lamps on their chains still swinging very slightly from the last thing he had said in his low voice, and he was more frightened than he had been on the stair at Halcrest. Something had killed the Doom Breaker in a river. Something had made a ford boil. And it was walking up the road toward everyone he had left.

"First bell," he said.

Amelia nodded once, flat, a lieutenant taking an order, and then the corner of her mouth went again, and she picked up his cold tea and drank it.

There were seven chairs in the Hall of Verdicts, and at the hour of the snake on that spring morning nobody was sitting in any of them.

Kiyome Byakuya stood beside the white one, because she always stood beside it, and watched the light come down. The Hall took the morning through a row of high narrow windows under the roof-beams, and the sun fell through them in long slanting shafts full of turning dust and lay across the seven chairs one after another as it climbed: the gold of the Tenrai, worked so thick with thread on its banner that it threw the light back like a shield; the indigo of the Amagiri, deep as dusk on a lake; the raw copper of the Akagane, warm as a banked forge; the silver of the Shirogane; the near-white of her own house, pale as a church; the black lacquer of the Kurenai, which drank the sun and gave nothing back; and at the head of them all, raised on its own step, the red. Red enamel over near-black steel, with the lion's head in a boss at the crest, a red so deep and live under the shafts of gold that it looked wet. Her sister's son had sat in it for a month. The dust on its arms was gone.

On the dais step below it, Hayato Amagiri was drawing a road in the dust with one finger.

He did it without knowing he did it. He was a slight, apologetic man in a blue-gray traveling coat with the indigo of his line showing only at the cuffs, and his finger moved in the gray film on the step the way it moved on every table he had ever sat at: a long line, and a bend, and a short cross-stroke where a river would be. The beads at Kiyome's temples clicked softly when she turned her head to watch it, *kchik, kchik*, like the first of a rain on a slate roof.

[soft, apologetic, an old Japonic register worn smooth by Common] "Forgive me, my lady. My lord." Hayato did not look up from the line. "The post-rider at the Hōsen relay came in at dawn. He changed horses at the Kessen crossing yesterday noon and rode through the night. He says the snow's gone clean off the north road below the crossing, as if it never fell. A strip of it, ten yards wide, as if somebody had drawn a line of summer straight up the middle of the road. The verges are white on either side and the road's bare and steaming, and the grass is coming up green in it." His finger stopped on the step. "He says it's moving. A few miles a day. North."

"Ten yards," said Tetsugen Akagane.

He was standing beside the copper chair with his arms folded, in the short copper-ochre shoulder-cape of his house over white, and his face was the face it had been for forty years: twenty-five, smooth, the black hair curling untidily over the brow, the warm gold eyes. The voice that came out of it was a cart going over a bridge. The copper pulse in his aura kept slow time with his heart under the lamps, *thum, thum*, and she could feel it faintly through the soles of her feet.

[deep, flat, old northern iron] "Snow won't lie within ten yards of him. It's in the copper book. It was in it before my grandfather's grandfather was born." He looked at the black chair, and then away. "We all know what this is."

"Yes," said Kiyome. "We do."

She found that her voice was quite clear. That was always the way with her. Her voice went clearer and calmer the worse things were, up to the moment the rings in her eyes turned, and then everyone in the room could see her break, and mend.

[clear, level, the old high register of the house] "My grandmother told it to me over the lamp, the year I swore," she said. "The morning the seven chairs sat in judgment on a woman of the copper line, for a lord found dead at a feast with his heart stopped. They voted her death. Her husband was the Kurenai headsman's first hand, and he would not take up the blade, so they put him in the condemned's irons beside her and made him watch another man take her head. And when it was done he broke the irons." She looked down the Hall at the black chair. "He looked inward instead of out, at the block, and what he saw there kept him. The Kurenai struck his name. The Holy Sea filed him with the demons of the First Eon. My grandmother called him the Unburied. Everyone else called him what the Kurenai called him." The beads clicked. "The Sin of Wrath has come back to the Hall of Verdicts. Shōki has returned."

Nobody said anything for a while. The shafts of light moved a finger's width along the chairs.

"His wife was Tsuyu," said Tetsugen. "Akagane. A copper girl." He turned his scarred hands palm up and looked down into them, the way he did when a thing weighed on him, as if there were writing there. "She's in the book. Every Akagane child reads her name. We were told she poisoned a lord and shamed the line. When I took the chair I read the Hall's own record of that morning, and the Hall's record says the lord's heart stopped with a needle at the nape." He looked up. "A needle at the nape, Kiyome. Three hundred years before Raizan Tenrai was born, the Tenrai had a man who did it the same way. And yesterday he walked into Raizan's house."

"He's not coming for the Tenrai," said Hayato quietly. His finger had begun to move again in the dust, up the line of the road, north, north, to the top of the step where the red chair stood. "He's finished with the Tenrai. He walked out of Kessen and took the north road. Every chair in this Hall voted that morning, my lady, or had a forebear in it that did. And every chair in it has an heir standing in it now." The finger reached the top of the step and stopped against the foot of the red chair. "Two hundred miles. On foot, a few miles a day, stopping. Call it a fortnight, if he keeps walking. A great deal less if he stops walking."

Kiyome was looking at the red chair. She was thinking of her sister's son sitting in it with his elbow on the arm and his chin in his hand, scowling at the seven lines as though they were a salt face that had a void behind it, and of the way he had looked at her at Kharven's Foot with Kasane's jaw and Gimbzo's eyes and no idea in the world who she was. She had kept an oath for sixteen years with nothing at the end of it. Now there was something at the end of it, and it was eighteen, and it was in a tower at the other end of the Seat, and a furnace was walking up the road toward it.

Her lips had begun to move. She did not know until Hayato looked at her, and she saw in his face that he knew what the words were. *The red line before the white, until the end of the red line.*

[gentle, frightened, apologizing even now] "My lady," said Hayato. "I know you want to protect him. He's your nephew. I'd want the same, in your place." He wiped his finger on his coat. "But I've done the sum three ways and it comes out the same every time. He's Eleventh Stage. So are we, the three of us, and nobody in the north knows it but the three of us, and it doesn't help. He's had three hundred years at Eleventh and he's Rot-Born, and every blow we land goes into his bank and comes back out at whoever landed it. My mist won't hold within ten yards of him; it'll burn off the road like fog off a valley at noon. Your light wants dry air, my lady, and he walks about in his own steam. And my lord Tetsugen's art wants a heart to slow, and his is running at forge heat." He swallowed. "Even with the three of us, I don't think we can defeat him. I don't think anybody in the north can."

Kiyome looked down at the floor between her feet. The rings in her eyes turned, once, slowly, silver as winter ice, and for a breath every person in the Hall could see her break.

And then mend.

"That's as may be," said Tetsugen.

He unfolded his arms. He came down off the copper chair's step and across the floor of the Hall, through the shafts of dust and light, in that unhurried old soldier's walk that did not belong in a young man's body, and stopped in front of Kiyome and looked at her. The copper in his aura had come up a little, warm red-bronze round the edges of his white sleeves, *thum, thum, thum*, quicker now.

[low, the rumble coming up through the floor] "That's as may be, and the boy's sum is a good sum, and I'll not argue with it. But it doesn't mean we give up and go home and wait for him to walk in here and sit down." He jerked his chin at her. "You're the Owl. Naevra Yukari called you that on her own gate, and she's never wrong about a person. And she called me the Fox, and she's not wrong about that either, more's the pity. The Owl and the Fox. The two times in the whole history of this house the Moto knew peace among themselves, it was under an Owl." He put his scarred hand flat on his own chest, the Stark way, the way he had at the woodpile. "I'll not obey that boy. I said so. I'll follow him. And this is where following him goes, Kiyome. We have a duty to our king. That's the whole of it."

"I can't stand in his place," he went on, before she could answer, and for the first time in all the years she had known him the old iron voice had something under it that was not iron. "That's what frightens me. I can stand in any man's place in the north and tell you what he'll offer. I stood in Karogane's, and the Keld's. I can't stand in a furnace. He's not buying anything. There's nothing he'd take." He breathed out. "But I know four things about him from the copper book, and I'll give them to you for nothing. He can't abide the cold, and he can't abide the wet. His quench does nothing to copper, which my line has known for a thousand years, because a copper blade comes out of the water softer and not harder. And he answers every blow. Every one, from anyone, before he does anything else. A hanging judge can't leave a case open on the table." He held up a fourth finger. "And the boy's sum leaves out the last thing. His bank has a top. A quarter of his reserve over the full, the copper book says, and past that line it goes into his own frame and cracks it. Every blow we land goes in the bank, Hayato says. Good. Three of us at Eleventh can put a great deal in a bank." His young face, very briefly, smiled. "That's not a way to win. But it's a way to steer."

Kiyome looked at him for a long moment. Then the corner of her mouth lifted, and the beads at her temples moved before she did.

"Copper, then," she said. "And cold. Seven lines forgive us."

The door at the end of the Hall opened.

It was Godfrey Greymane, in his gray cloak, and he came in quickly and then stopped, because he had not expected to find three of the seven lines standing among the empty chairs with their faces like that. He had a paper in his hand. He was pleased about something; it was still on his face, going out of it as he looked at them.

"My lady," he said. "My lords. I came to tell you before you heard it in the corridors. His Majesty's ridden. At first bell, with Lorn Stark and twelve of the Stark riders, and my sister." The pleasure had gone entirely now. "South. Down the Kessen road. To look at what killed the Doom Breaker."

The rings in Kiyome's eyes turned, and this time they did not mend.

"First bell," said Hayato. His finger had gone still on the dust of the step; it lay on the line of the road, halfway down it, where two things coming toward each other would meet. [a whisper] "Two days and a road. He's already half a day on it."

Tetsugen was already walking. "Horses," he said, to nobody, in the voice that had carried across the Sennen road. "Now. And find me every copper blade in this keep."

The Tenrai house at the top of the ford town was still burning when Shōki came up the hill.

It had been a long two-story hall of red pine lacquered black, with its eaves turned up at the corners and hung all round with brass lamps on the Guild round, and a gate painted the Tenrai gold. The gold was blistered off the gate now in long curling peels, and behind it the hall was a black ribcage open to the evening sky, the beams standing up out of their own ash still glowing along their undersides, orange, then dull red, then orange again when the wind breathed on them, *pachi, pachi, pachi*. The brass lamps had melted on their chains and hung in long bright drips like icicles made of gold. Somewhere in the garden the blue flame from the lamp oil was still running along the edge of the koi pond, low and pretty, and the fish had come to the surface and lay there white. The wardens had walked off their rounds at the first smoke; the whole top of the town had gone silent the way a district goes silent when its keepers walk, no hum, no tick, only the fire talking to itself, and the dogs.

The townspeople had gone into their houses and barred their doors. He could feel them behind the shutters as he passed, a long street of small cold shapes of body heat in the dark rooms, holding still, the way mice hold still when the cat walks by the wainscot. TSUMI-YOMI counted them without his asking. A cooper with a dead apprentice on him from a drunken night ten years gone. A woman with nothing. A child with nothing. The count went past him like the doors, and for the first time in three hundred years he felt it go past, felt the nothing on the child's chest and the woman's, and hated that he felt it, and walked faster.

There was a stone horse-trough at the gate of the burned house, full of black water with ash on it. He went down on his knees beside it and put his face in it and drank. He drank like a horse, the way he always had to after a fight, great dragging swallows, and the water boiled in his mouth before it reached his throat, *GOBOBOBOBO*, and the steam came up round his head in a white column, and he drank the whole trough down to the slimed stone at the bottom and sat back on his heels on the cobbles with the old man's blood sliding off him in pink runnels and his chest heaving.

Then the heat he had taken out of Raizan Tenrai Moto began to go to work.

He felt it go, and could do nothing about it, and did not want to. The Karmic Fire had gone into him through the quench hand, the heat of four hundred and twelve men's deaths drawn out of the old man's body before the girl, and it had not been spent; it was banked in his tissue like coals under ash, and his body knew what to do with it. It went to the cracks. The river had shattered him in The Ford, quenched him like a blade thrust white-hot into cold water, and the glaze of his skin had crazed and flaked off black across his arms and his chest and his neck and his face, and the Covenant Scar had cracked through and wept. Now the banked heat came up into every one of those cracks from underneath, and the cracks lit, ember-orange and then yellow, *jiririririri*, a sound like a filament overdriven, and closed. They closed the way glaze closes on a pot that goes back into the kiln: running together, smoothing, the black flakes falling off him onto the cobbles and the new skin under them coming up pale and crazed and lit from inside like the old, and hard. The Scar on his chest knit shut with a sound like a hot iron going into a barrel. His hair, which the river had flattened to his skull, lifted off his scalp in the updraft of his own heat and stood out white at the roots. The chain stubs on the Hall Irons at his wrists glowed cherry-red, and the aura came back up round him from the soles of his feet, Caloria orange, roaring, *GOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOO*, a blast furnace with its doors thrown open, and round the edge of it the black-violet rim of Vantabriel, thin now, a line. The bank was empty. He had spent the bank on the old man. He would have to fill it again, and he knew where.

It was wrath that did it. His Regeneration was a good one and Exuroth had tempered his lattice to hold what lesser vessels could not, but those were only the ways. What drove the heat into the cracks and held it there till they closed was the thing that had come up the old road when he looked, hate and anger and grief, hot and alive, filling him the way the fire fills a kiln. Three hundred years he had run on a law. Now he ran on a feeling, and the feeling was hotter.

He coughed. Something tore in his chest, low on the right side where the river's steam had gone down into him at The Ford, and he leaned over and spat. It came out of him bright red and smoking and hit the cobbles with a hiss, *jū*, and lay there and steamed.

He wiped his mouth on the back of his wrist, on the iron, and stood up.

The black hand pulled north. The road went up out of the valley past the burned gate and over the shoulder of the hill and on, white under the evening, and at the end of it was the Seat, and the Hall, and the seven chairs, and the red one, and the weight in it ringing on its one note, slate and gold. He did not know the name. He did not need the name. The Kōkan had sat at the head of that Hall the morning the chairs voted, and a Kōkan sat at the head of it again.

[a hanging judge reading sentence over the roar of a foundry floor; an old Japonic court accent gone hoarse with steam] "I am coming for you," said Shōki Kurenai Moto, to the road. "Kōkan."

His voice came out of him low and cracked and enormous, and the shutters along the street rattled in their frames.

"I will make you feel what I have felt. All of it. I will take your hall and your chairs and your oaths, and every person who has ever stood between you and the cold, and I will burn them in front of you one at a time, the way they made me stand and watch, until you understand." He spat again; it hissed. "What it is to lose. To lose everything. To stand in irons with your hands open and nothing in them." He looked down at his own hands. The left palm still had the shape of a small wrist in it, a ghost of heat, and he closed it into a fist so hard the iron creaked. "And then the rest of them. Every hand that was raised that morning. Every heir of every chair. Every one who ever wronged me. Entered. Every one."

He started walking. Behind him the burned house settled on itself with a long groaning crash, *GASHAAAAN*, and sent up a great spray of sparks into the dark, orange and gold, like a festival.

In front of him, the snow drew back off the road.

The cedar wood came down to the Kessen road on both sides about a day and a half south of the Seat, where the road went down off the high ground toward the river country, and Lorn Stark had always liked it.

It was old wood. The cedars stood up out of the snow as thick as a hall's pillars and so tall that the tops of them were still in the sun when the road at their feet had gone blue with evening, and their bark was red-brown and soft and stringy and came away under the hand in long ribbons that smelled of a clothes chest. The snow lay deep under them in the shadows, blue and hard and crusted, but the spring had got in at the edges, along the road and in the clearings where the sun came down: there were snowdrops in the black leaf-mold at the trunks' feet, and the first purple crocus, and moss coming up green as new paint on the stones, and a jay somewhere ahead screaming at them for riding through its wood, *skraaak, skraaak*. The horses' breath went up white. The twelve Stark riders came down the road behind in pairs, in their bear-fur and their gray, with the wolf on their breasts and the clear crystal braids the Hollenmark women had made them swinging at their collars, and Rob was singing something filthy about a miller's wife under his breath, badly, which meant he was frightened.

Lorn rode at the front with the king, because that was where he rode.

The king had been quiet since the Seat. He sat his tall gray horse in the red-laced harness with Tsugigane across his back and the white cord at its guard, and he did not talk, and every mile or so his head would turn to the south before anything had moved there, and then turn back. Lorn did not ask about it. He did not read people; he had never been any good at it. He watched them. He watched the king's left hand on the reins, which kept closing, and the king's left eye, which every so often went to a gold seam in the evening light like a crack in a lamp.

The Greymane girl rode on the king's other side. She had the axe on her back and the shield on her saddle, and she had been talking to the king for the last two miles in that flat lieutenant's voice of hers, about nothing, about the footing on the road and the state of the Stark horses and a smith in the lower town who had tried to charge her half a month's wage to rivet a pauldron. She was doing it on purpose. Lorn could see that much. She was standing in front of the king with it the way she stood in front of everything, and once the king had laughed, a short surprised sound, and Lorn had looked at the floor of the road three feet in front of his horse and felt the corner of his own mouth go.

[plain northern Kharven, flat] "Your Majesty's going to tell me the pauldron'll split on the next hard blow," Amelia said.

[a cutter's vowels, tired, fond] "It will split on the next hard blow."

"Then I'll mend it after the next hard blow. That's when you mend things. Not before."

"That's a stupid way to run a..."

Lorn's breath stopped steaming.

He noticed it the way you notice a sound that has stopped. The cold had been coming off him all day the way it always came off him, without his thinking about it, the heat going out of everything within a few paces of him and down through his body and out through his heels into the ground: the rime on his beard, the frost silvering the bear-fur on his shoulders, the snow stiffening and squeaking under his horse's hooves, his breath going up clear where every other man's went up white. It was the Stark cold. He had never been warm in his life. And now, between one breath and the next, it was moving.

It was going east. He could feel it the way you feel a draft from a door you did not know was open. The cold was running out of him on the left side, toward the trees, the way water runs out of a cracked pail toward the low ground, and the rime on the left side of his beard was going soft, and wet, and then it was not rime at all; it was water, running down into his collar. His left cheek was warm. His left cheek had not been warm since he was born.

He reined in. *Krrk*, the snow went under the horse's hooves as it stopped, and the creak went out from him in a ring across the road, the sound of a frozen lake taking a weight.

"Hold," he said.

The column held. Rob stopped singing. The jay had stopped screaming. He had not noticed when it stopped; he noticed now that there was nothing in the whole wood making a sound, no bird, no squirrel in the cedar tops, no small thing in the snow, and that the horses had their ears flat back and their eyes showing white at the edges and were trying, all of them at once, to turn their heads away from the east.

He looked east, into the trees, the way his father had taught him to look at a wood, at the snow under the trees before the trees themselves. Woodcraft. Which ice holds, which wind brings snow, where the deer go. Eighty paces in, maybe ninety, there was a great cedar, older than the others, its trunk as wide as a cart, and the snow was gone from round its foot. Gone in a ring ten yards across, as if somebody had drawn a circle of summer on the ground with a stick, and in the ring the black leaf-mold lay bare and wet and steaming, and the snowdrops in it had come into flower and gone over and died in the space of an afternoon, and lay brown and flat. The bark on the near side of the cedar was smoking, very gently. There was a dark shape in the long ribbons of it at the height of a man's shoulder, where something had leaned.

[young northern Kharven, puzzled, the lamp-low note gone out of his voice] "Lorn?" The king had turned his horse. His left eye was gold at the seam, burning; he was looking where Lorn was looking, and Lorn could see that he could not see anything. "What is it? I don't see anything. I can't feel anything out there."

He would not. Lorn knew it without knowing how he knew it. The king's Eye read cracks at fifty paces and the king's other thing, the field, marked the men who meant him harm at sixty. Whatever was standing behind that cedar was standing just outside the reach of both, the way a man stands just outside a sword's reach when he knows exactly how long the sword is. And it had not counted on a Stark. It had not counted on a man whose whole blood was cold, who did not need to see a fire to know where it was, because the cold in him ran toward it like water downhill.

Lorn put his right fist to his breastbone, once, hard. Then he got down off his horse.

He walked forward three paces onto the bare road with his hands still at his sides, heavy, even, unhurried, the way his father had walked across the Hall of Verdicts to the black floor, and stopped, and set his feet. The Stark sword was at his hip. He did not draw it yet. He looked at the smoking cedar.

[plain, low, a voice that does not waste words] "You might as well come out," said Lorn Stark.

Nothing moved in the wood. The steam went up off the bare ground round the cedar's foot in a slow white column into the last of the sun.

"Lorn," said the king, behind him, very quietly. "Who are you talking to?"

Lorn did not take his eyes off the tree. His jaw had tightened on the left side. The rime was spreading again across the snow round his boots, silver, creaking, and on the right side of his beard it was white as frost, and on the left side, the side toward the tree, it would not form at all; it melted as fast as it came, and ran.

"You cannot hide your presence from me," he said to the tree. "Not from a Stark. You're a fire in a cold house. I've been able to feel you for a quarter mile." He breathed out, and his breath came out clear. "And you stand like a Kurenai."

He knew that stance. He had seen it a month ago at the foot of the dais, in the woman in black and ash who had carried the blade to his father's neck. He had seen it in his own sleep every night since.

For a long moment nothing happened. Then something stepped out from behind the cedar.

*Don.*

It was only a step. It was a man's foot coming down on the bare black ground, a long lean man in a ragged black coat with iron at his wrists, and the sound of it was nothing, a soft wet thud in the leaf-mold, and the whole wood felt it. The snow on the cedars' lowest branches came down all at once in long soft falls, *zazazazaza*, the length of the road, as if every tree had shuddered. The heat came out from behind the trunk with him like a forge door thrown open, a wall of it rolling across the eighty paces and over the road, and the Stark riders' horses screamed and reared, and Rob went off his backward into the snow, and the frost round Lorn's boots, the cold of his whole blood, his whole house, went back from it with a long hissing sigh, *shuuuuuu*, like a lake going out from a fire on its shore. He felt the sweat start under his father's mail for the first time in his life. His knees wanted to bend. He did not let them.

The man came out into the evening light and stood in his own ring of summer. He was very tall, and narrow as a drawn wire, and his long black hair stood off his skull in the updraft of his own heat, white at the roots. His skin was pale and crazed all over like glaze on a fired pot, and every crack in it was lit from inside, ember-orange, and his black coat and his face and his braids were stiff and dark and flaking with old blood, a great deal of it, not his own. The air round him shimmered so that his outline ran like a reflection in water. There was a fresh red smear at the corner of his mouth, and as Lorn watched he turned his head and spat into the snow beyond his ring, and the snow hissed and made a little smoking hole.

His right eye was a cooked white thing, clouded like a boiled egg. His left eye was dark, and it was looking past Lorn, past the riders, past the Greymane girl who had come off her horse without anyone telling her and was already stepping in front of the king with her shield coming up off the saddle in one long movement, *gakon*, the rust-and-gold of her aura rising off her shoulders like heat off a hearth. Fine rings had begun to turn in the dark of that left eye, and a red capillary light was filling them, and it was looking at the king's chest the way a clerk looks at the last page of a ledger.

[a hanging judge reading sentence over the roar of a foundry floor; an old Japonic court accent gone hoarse with steam] "Kōkan," said the Sin of Wrath.

The word went through the cedar wood like a bell. The jay, somewhere far off, began to scream again.

# The Bearer of Weight

## The Yukari · After the Seikai

---

The tablet stood at the back of the hall where his father had set it, and Naiser sat in front of it in the dark.

It was a single slab of the valley's limestone taller than a man, grey with a warmth of honey in it where the light caught, dressed flat on the face and left rough on the sides, so the marks of the quarry chisel still ran down its flanks in long shallow furrows. His father had cut it out of the hill behind the gate with his own hands, over the course of one whole winter, and had carried it down on a sledge with six men pulling and himself walking beside it with one palm laid on the stone, as if it were a horse that might bolt. He had carved the face himself as well. There was only one line on it, set at the height of a seated man's eyes, so that whoever read it had to be sitting to read it properly, and the letters were deep and plain and unornamented, cut with a patience Naiser had never once seen his father spend on anything else.

*Master the innards, and the outer world is yours to command.*

He had read it ten thousand times. As a boy he had read it as a promise. As a young man he had read it as a boast. Tonight, sitting on the cold floor with his knees drawn up and his forearms resting on them, he read it as an accusation, and he could not stop.

The hall was unlit. Someone had set a lamp at the door when they carried him in from the field, and he had put it out with a word without turning round, and nobody had come back to light it. It did not matter. There was light enough. It came in through the high windows and the open doorway behind him, and it was the wrong colour.

Outside, the courtyard was full of the dead.

The bodies were laid out on the far side of the wall, under cloth, facing east as the dead of the house were always laid, rows and rows of them, with lamps between. They were the house's worked lamps, brought down from the halls in their cedar hoods, and they burned a clear honey-gold that did not flicker, a line of small steady suns laid along the rows. Above them the valley went on being beautiful, which was the hardest thing in it to bear. The cedar halls stepped down the slope on their limestone footings, their beams gone red-brown and resin-dark with age, their bark-shingle roofs furred silver-green with lichen, and the great cedars stood over all of it black against a sky the deep clear violet of the hour after dusk. Under the floor of this hall the stream ran as it had always run, cold over its stones, talking to itself in the dark. These were the others. Since the battle had ended at noon they had been coming back into the courtyard in ones and twos, the shapes of the men and women who had died that day, faint as breath on cold glass, a soft silver that gave no warmth and cast no shadow. They did not speak. They did not look at anyone. Each of them did one thing, over and over: a spearman turning to shout a warning, a girl of the archive lifting her hands to her face, an old man going down on one knee to pick up something he had dropped. They did it, and faded, and did it again. The living walked round them with their eyes on the paving.

That was what the Seikai had done. That was the wound the war had left in Ashura.

She who had given awareness the power to feel, the Epoch whose patience was the reason the Soul Plane could hold a memory at all, had been struck, and the place in her where the dead were kept had split along a seam, and now the memories were leaking out of her into the world of stone and weather. The dead could not rest because the place that held their resting had come open. And it went the other way as well. He had heard it in the camp before they brought him in, in the voices of the soldiers sitting by the wagons with their heads in their hands: *I can't remember her face. I had it this morning. I can't remember it.* Memory was running out of the living like water out of a cracked jar, and running into the courtyard, where it walked.

Naiser looked at his own hands on his knees.

The blood on them had gone dark and tacky at the creases and cracked to a dull brown across the backs. It was not his. Very little of it was his. It was on his cloak as well, the long dark founder's mantle with its high collar and its old stitched motifs belonging fully to no house, stiff with it from the hem to the hip, so heavy on the right side where he had knelt in it that the cloth hung like a wet sail. It was in his hair. He could feel it there, drying, pulling at the roots.

His hair fell to his waist. It had not been cut since he was nine years old.

He was tall even sitting, and long in the limb in the way of his mother's people, with the elven line in him lengthening every bone and the denser blood beneath it packing the length with a weight that people did not expect until they tried to move him. His skin was dark and warm, a dusky brown that the silver light from the courtyard clung to instead of washing out. His face was the kind that would have been called beautiful if it had been less severe: high in the cheekbone, narrow at the jaw, a straight hard mouth, and under the eyes the faint blue shadow of a man who had not slept in four days and would not sleep tonight. His eyes were dark, near black, with a violet-black ring round the pupil that never quite went away. Fine pale lines ran under the skin at his temples and down the backs of his hands, branching like roots seen from beneath the soil, the marks of the thread-sight that every bearer of the Yukari name grew into whether he chose it or not. At rest they were the colour of old scars. Tonight they had not stopped glowing faintly since the battle, silver going violet at the edges, and he could not make them stop.

His longblade lay on the floor beside him, sheathed. He had not cleaned it. He would have to.

In the Yukari, when a fighter lost a battle, he cut his hair.

It was a small custom and an old one, older than the archive. The house did not make speeches about defeat. It did not hang its banners upside down or tear its clothes. It simply took a knife, the morning after, and cut off a length of hair at the back of the neck and laid it on the fire, and went about its work with a bare nape until the hair grew back, and everyone who saw that nape knew, and nobody spoke of it. A child who lost a sparring match in the yard was given a knife and shown how. Naiser had been nine the last time. He had lost to a cousin two years older, flat on his back in the dust with a practice blade at his throat, and he had cried with fury in front of the whole yard, and his father had come and stood over him and said nothing. Then his father had knelt, and taken the knife, and cut the hair himself, very gently, and put his hand on Naiser's head afterward, the way the elders did, and still said nothing.

He had not lost since, in the yard or in the field, in all the years of the war.

He had not lost today. That was the trouble.

He had won. The Seikai were gone. He had broken their working at its root and broken the people who held it, one by one, through the long morning, and when the last of them went down on the slope above the river the seam they had been prising open in Ashura had stopped widening. He had felt it stop. He had felt the whole Soul Plane shudder like a struck bell and go still. And there had been a moment, kneeling in the mud with his blade buried to the guard in the last Seikai standing, when he had thought: *It's done. It's over. We held.*

Then he had stood up, and turned round, and seen how many of his own people were lying behind him.

There was Seikai blood in him. He had always known that. The house had never let him forget it, and neither had the Seikai, and it had made no difference at all on the slope above the river. He did not know yet whether that was the worst thing he had learned about himself today or only one of several.

He looked up at the line on the tablet. The silver light from the doorway moved across the letters as the dead in the courtyard faded and came back, faded and came back, so that the words seemed to breathe.

*Master the innards.*

He had mastered them. That was the cruelty of it. Everything inside him that could be made to answer had been made to answer. He had spent his whole life teaching two warring bloods to kneel down into the same posture, and they had knelt, and the discipline had held through every hour of the battle. There had been no slip, no tremor, no wasted stroke. He had been exactly what his father built him to be.

And the outer world had not obeyed. The outer world was lying under cloth on the far side of the wall.

He whispered it. He had not meant to say it aloud.

"Have I failed you, Father?"

The hall gave it back to him, very small.

---

He heard the footsteps before they reached the door.

He knew the step without turning, as he knew his own pulse: light, unhurried, a little uneven since the winter she had broken her ankle on the archive stairs and never let it heal quite straight, the left foot coming down a breath softer than the right. Barefoot on the stone. She always went barefoot in the house. And under the sound of her feet, the other thing, the thing that was not sound at all, the faint pale tug at the edge of his thread-sight that meant her, and only her, as surely as a voice.

Naevra stopped in the doorway. For a moment she did not come in.

He could see her without looking, the way he could always see her, and when at last he turned his head she was exactly as he had seen. Tall for a woman, nearly his height, and slender to the point of seeming insubstantial, a long pale line of a person who stood as if she were always listening to something just past the edge of the room. Her skin was porcelain-pale where his was dark, and the silver light did not cling to it; it went through it, almost, so that in the doorway she looked half like one of the dead herself. Her hair was white. It had been white since the day she was born, white as old parchment, straight and long, worn loose down her back tonight because there had been no time to bind it. Her eyes were silver-white, and when she was reading, as she was reading now, without meaning to, they took on a faint lavender cast and the air around her face filled with threads so fine they were visible only when they caught the light, hanging like cobweb in a barn door.

She wore a plain shift of undyed linen with the sleeves pushed up past the elbow and a stained apron over it. The apron was the archive's apron, meant for ink, and it was not ink on it now. There was a smear of someone else's blood down her left forearm where she had not wiped it, and dirt on her knees, and her feet were filthy from the courtyard.

Her lips were pressed thin. They went that way when she was holding something down. She had watched what this war did to him. She had watched it through every season of it, the way she watched everything, and she had read it in his threads from three rooms away on nights when he would not open his door, and she had never once told him what she saw there. He did not have to read her to know what was in her face. The pain of it. The long grief.

"Naiser," she said.

He did not answer. She came in. Her bare feet made almost no sound on the stone, and she stepped round his blade where it lay without looking down at it, and sat beside him on the cold floor in front of their father's tablet, close enough that her shoulder touched his arm.

"We have to prepare the funerals," she said quietly. "I know this is hard on you, brother."

He closed his eyes.

"I know," she said again, as if he had argued.

Then she reached over and took his arm in both her hands. She did not ask. She took his wrist and his bloody hand, and drew it across to her, and pressed his palm flat against her chest, over her breastbone, where he could feel her heart going under the linen, much too fast. She held it there with both hands over the back of his, the way she had when they were children and he woke from the dreams and she would hold his hand to her heart and say, *Feel that? That's still going. So are you.* She had sat with him like this when they were small, a year apart and she the one who would not cry, and he had hated her for it then.

She wanted to cry. She had wanted to cry for hours, he thought; he could feel it in the hitch of her breathing under his hand. Then he heard the small wet catch in her throat and felt her shoulders begin to shake against his arm, and understood that she was not wanting to any longer. She was crying. She was doing it almost without sound, the way the house did everything, with her eyes open and her chin up and the tears simply running down.

"How many?" he said.

"Don't."

"How many, Naevra?"

She was silent for a while. Out in the courtyard, the spearman turned to shout his warning, and faded, and turned again.

"Four hundred and eleven," she said. "So far. They're still bringing them in from the river. Seradris thinks it will be closer to five hundred by morning."

He said nothing.

"Some of them," Naevra said, and her voice went very careful, "some of them, the families are already forgetting. The memory is going out of them as fast as it's going out of her. Kaeshin's mother came to me an hour ago and asked me what her son's calling-name was. Her son, Naiser. She sat with me in the archive and wept because she couldn't remember what she used to call him at supper." She drew a breath. "So we have to do it tonight. All of them. Every name read out over every one of them before the light comes, while there's still someone who can say it. If we wait until they're forgotten we'll be burying strangers."

He opened his eyes and looked at the tablet. *The outer world is yours to command.*

"And then?" he said.

She pressed his hand harder against her chest.

"And then," she said, "we go on. There's nothing else we can do. We can do nothing but move on."

---

It went into him like lightning.

It went in at the place where her heart was beating under his palm and ran up his arm and into his chest and split there, and he felt the two bloods in him jolt out of step for the first time in years, the old treaty between them lurching like a cart losing a wheel. The thread-marks at his temples flared. In the courtyard the silver dead flickered all at once, every one of them, as if a wind had gone through them.

*Move on.*

Move on, after all of it. After his mother, whose voice he had already lost somewhere in the last four days, so that when he reached for it now there was only a soft blank where it had been, like the pale square on a wall where a picture used to hang. After his father, who had walked into the dark four winters ago, when the war turned to its worst, to hold a door the Seikai were forcing, and had held it, and had not walked back. After the cousin who had put him on his back in the yard when he was nine, dead on the river slope this morning with half his face gone. After four hundred and eleven names, and five hundred by morning, and a mother in the archive who could not remember what she had called her son at supper.

Move on. As if the world were a road and the dead were stones on it to be stepped over.

He did not want to move on. He wanted to take hold of it. He wanted to take the whole broken world in his two hands, the cracked jar of Ashura with its memories running out, the seam in the Soul Plane, the slope above the river, the courtyard full of silver ghosts doing the last thing they ever did over and over until the end of time, and he wanted to *shape* it. To bend it back. To make it into something that did not do this. The want rose up in him so hard and so fast that it frightened him. Grief he knew, and this was older and colder and very much larger than grief, and it sat in the space between his two bloods like something that had been waiting there his whole life for a door to open.

He tilted his head down. His hair fell forward over his face, long and black and heavy with the dried blood in it, and closed over his eyes like a curtain, and for a moment he was alone behind it.

And he remembered his father.

It came back whole, as if it had been waiting. The voice was going too, he could feel it going, but the words had stayed. The cold morning at the gate, and the frost on the flat stone, and his father in his travelling coat with the pack already on his back and the door to the dark standing open behind him, a long seam of nothing in the air above the hill, humming. His father had put both hands on Naiser's shoulders. He had looked at him a while without a word, which was how he said most things. Then he had spoken, quietly, so that the others at the gate would not hear.

"Do not be afraid, my son. All things will come. Your destiny is here, in the now. You are the light." His father's hands had tightened. "You are the Bearer of Weight. You are the guide I leave behind for our people."

And then he had let go, and turned, and walked into the seam, and the seam had closed behind him like a mouth.

Naiser had wanted to shout after him that it wasn't fair. He had been grown by then, a man, a fighter of the house with long hair down his back, and he had stood at the gate with the frost burning his feet and wanted to shout it like a child of nine flat on his back in the yard. *It isn't fair. You can't. You can't leave it to me.* He had not shouted. He had stood there with his mouth shut and his hands at his sides, because it was war, and in war nobody asked whether a thing was fair.

He wanted to shout it now. He held it behind his teeth. It was still war.

Under his palm, Naevra's heart stumbled, and her breath stopped.

She could feel it. He knew she could feel it, the same way she had always felt it, through the threads: the rage going through him like a storm through a forest, the two bloods out of step and grinding, the cold vast want sitting in the space between them with its eyes open. He could feel her reading it. The fine white threads in the air round her face had drawn tight, every one of them, the way a spider's web goes taut when something heavy lands in it. Her hands on the back of his had gone cold.

"Naiser," she whispered. "What is that?"

He did not answer her. He did not know.

He took his hand back from her chest, gently. She let it go. He reached down and picked up his longblade from the floor beside him, and drew it a hand's width out of the sheath, and the narrow severe steel came out still dark with the morning's work along the edge.

"Naiser," Naevra said again, sharper.

He gathered his hair at the back of his neck in his left fist. All of it, the whole black length of it, heavy with blood. He drew it tight, out and away from his nape, and laid the edge of the blade against it just above his fist, and cut.

It took two strokes. The hair was thick and the blood had made it stiff. On the second stroke it parted all at once and the weight went off his head so suddenly that he swayed, and he sat for a moment with the severed length of it hanging from his fist like a dead thing, longer than his arm.

Naevra made a sound he had not heard her make since their father went into the dark.

"You didn't lose," she said. "Naiser. You *won*. The Seikai are gone. You held the seam. You didn't lose."

"Four hundred and eleven," he said.

He got to his knees in front of the tablet. He laid the hair across the foot of it, on the cold stone, under the line his father had carved at the height of a seated man's eyes. The silver light from the courtyard moved over it. Then he sheathed his blade, and stayed there on his knees with his hands flat on his thighs, and looked up at the words. The back of his neck was bare and cold. He could feel the air of the hall on it for the first time since he was nine years old.

*Master the innards, and the outer world is yours to command.*

"He was wrong," Naiser said. "Or I haven't finished."

Naevra watched him, and the stream went on talking under the floor. The threads round her face did not loosen. Whatever she was reading in him, she was still reading it, and she did not say what it was, and he did not ask.

At last she got up. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, both cheeks, hard, the way their father used to wipe frost off the flat stone above the gate before a reading, and left a pale streak of someone else's dried blood across her cheekbone without noticing. She held her hand down to him.

"We have to go," she said. Her voice was steady again. She had made it steady. "Seradris and the others are waiting for us."

He looked at her hand. It was shaking very slightly.

He took it, and stood.

Out in the courtyard the spearman turned to shout his warning, and the girl from the archive lifted her hands to her face, and the old man went down on one knee for the thing he had dropped, and none of them would ever finish. Beyond the wall, five hundred lamps were burning gold between the rows under the black cedars, and the first stars had come out over the ridge, white and very sharp. Naiser walked out through the doorway with his sister's hand in his and his nape bare to the cold, and the dead in the silver light did not look up as he passed between them, and he did not look away.

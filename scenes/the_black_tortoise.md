# The Black Tortoise

*Aetherion Academy · the Crucible floor, Hiromi Mahuo's bout, after the palm*

---

The hum went sharp, and Hiromi knew what it meant before the second note confirmed it.

He had been breathing with the Lion for so long that its drone had become a part of his own body, the way the creak of a ship becomes the sailor's, and when the pitch rose by a hair he felt the rise in the hollow under his breastbone like a missed stair. Then it rose again. Somebody had a hand on his summons. Not on its ownership and not on its recognition; the boy had learned that lesson in the hardest way there was. On its pulse. On the fine trembling of packed Aether that made the haetae's body cohere and sing, the oscillation itself, the one part of the Lion that was, in the plainest sense of the word, moving.

Of course it was. Hiromi felt the grin come back to his face with a slow, rueful warmth. He had built the beast out of motion and then stood it in front of the Paragon of Motion and invited him to look.

The nosebleed had stopped some time during the held breath. He found the blood dried on his lip when he licked it, a crust and nothing more, and the grey that had crept in at the rim of his sight while the third call landed had drawn back to a faint dusk at the very edges, like the last of a fog lifting off a field. He was tired. He was hungry, which he would deny to anyone who asked. His legs were his own again. A summoner of thirteen who had made three calls of Lion's Bane before the sand had properly settled ought to have been on his back, and he was not, and he did not intend to be for some while yet.

The hum sharpened a third time and the doubled paw on the boy's shoulders flickered.

---

He listened to what the boy was doing, and as he listened he understood it, and understanding it pleased him so much that for a moment he forgot to be anything but pleased.

Kain was detuning the Lion. Every bell has a note it wants to ring at; strike it and it rings there and nowhere else, and the summoned body of a haetae made of the room's own Aether was a bell of that kind, a great standing pattern of vibration with a note of its own, the note ECHO had built on and Hiromi had breathed with. The boy had taken hold of that note and was pushing it, a little at a time, off its own pitch. A bell made to ring a fraction above its note does not ring cleanly. It fights itself. Hiromi could hear the fighting: underneath the drone, a slow throb had begun, loud and soft, loud and soft, the wavering that two nearly matched notes make when they are sounded together. Tuners of organ pipes listen for exactly that pulse and call it beating, and they turn the pipe until it slows and stops. Kain was turning it the other way. The beat was his work, and the Weight on his shoulders was pulsing with it, heavy and light and heavy, as each portion of the Lion fell a fraction out of step with the whole.

It was clever. It was precisely, beautifully clever, the cleverness of somebody who had listened to Hiromi take his reversal apart and declined to argue with a single word of it, and gone instead underneath the argument to the machine the argument was standing on.

But a bell does not like to be pushed off its note, and the boy was about to learn what it does about it.

Push a swing in time with its swinging and every push adds; that is resonance, and it was what ECHO had always been. Push it against its own swing, or a fraction early, or a fraction late, and part of every push goes into the swing and part of it comes straight back into the arms that pushed. A driven thing that is driven off its own pitch pays back the driver on every half of every cycle. The energy sloshes. It goes out on the stroke and returns on the backstroke, and the further from true the driver drags the note, the more of every effort comes home into the hand that made it, and the less of it stays where it was sent. Engineers who drive great pumps and the shafts of mills know this well enough to have a word for the useless returning part, the part that does no work and only heats the windings, and they hate it, because it burns out the machine that makes it.

Kain's machine was himself. Every degree he forced the Lion's note out of true came back into his pathways, the channels his Essence ran through to make the forcing, and it came back hot. Hiromi could not see that heat. He could feel it, because GRASP was still open through the Lion and the whole floor of the Crucible still lay on a slope with Hiromi at the bottom of it. Every draw the boy made to push on the Lion had to climb that slope at a third again of its proper cost, and under the Weight's lid besides. Hiromi felt each draw labour up toward him as distinctly as a man feels a cart being hauled up the hill outside his window. They were getting heavier. Each time the boy reached for the Lion's pulse, the draw he made to reach with was slower to climb and thinner at the top than the one before; more of it was going astray in the climbing, bleeding out into the boy's own channels as waste. Whatever fraction of his effort Kain's soul usually turned into work, that fraction was falling, draw by draw, and every lost part of it was going into the pathways that carried it.

And there was the other thing, the thing the boy could not reach at all.

The haetae was a judge. It sat at the gate and knew whoever came up the step, and the paw it had laid on the boy's shoulders was not only a weight. It was a verdict in Fate, a recognition that the one beneath it had come before the guardian. Every time Kain put his hand to the guardian's own body to unsettle it, he came before the guardian again. He came up the step a second time, and a third, and the verdict knew him each time and set itself down a little harder, because a man who has been judged and then lays hands on the judge has added to the charge. Hiromi had read once, in a paper on the strange behaviour of copper rings dropped past a lodestone, that nature resists any change in what flows through a loop, and resists it in exact proportion to how fast the change is forced: drop the ring slowly and it falls almost freely, drop it hard and it brakes in the air as though caught in honey. The judgement worked like that. The harder Kain pressed his power against the Lion, the harder the invisible force pressed back on him, and that force lived in Fate, in recognition, in the country where Hiromi was a throne and the boy had no standing at all. Motion could detune a bell. It could not reach the verdict the bell had given. It was the one spiritual force on the field the boy's art had no handle on, and every time he worked on the Lion he leaned more of himself into it.

Hiromi watched it in the room, because the room was the only honest witness. The draw-gauge on the far wall behind the boy's mark, which had jerked up off its stop, was jerking about now in a way gauges were not built to: up, down, up, chattering in its glass as the draws behind it stuttered. The air above the boy's shoulders, beneath the flickering paw, had begun to shimmer the way air shimmers over a stove-lid.

*He'll stop,* Hiromi thought. *He's too clever not to. He'll stop the moment it's cost him enough to give him an opening, and then he'll move.*

He was right about that too.

---

The boy moved.

Hiromi saw the first step begin, the bare right foot lifting from the stone where it had stood since the bell, and then he did not see the rest, because there was no rest to see; there was a body on the far mark and then there was a disturbance in the air coming straight down the Crucible toward him at a speed his eyes refused to divide into parts. Everything in that small frame had gone one way at once, ankle and knee and hip and spine and shoulder and breath all firing in a single sequence, the way a whip's whole length dumps its speed into the last inch of the lash.

Two things happened in the Lion when the boy left the mark, and Hiromi felt both.

The first was that the haetae steadied. The hand on its pulse had let go, because the boy's art was all being spent on the boy now, and the moment the driver went away, the bell went home to its own note. Hiromi heard the beating slow, and slow, and stop; heard the drone settle back into a single true pitch; felt the beast's body draw itself together again as each portion of it fell back into step with the whole. A pattern that is in step with itself is a pattern the world can believe in. The world believed in it, and the Lion seemed to gain back some of its mass as it sat there, its haunches settling deeper into the broken floor, the slabs under it groaning.

The second was that Hiromi understood he could not keep it like this.

The boy had found the Lion's pulse once. He would find it again, and faster, and the Lion's pulse was the Lion's body, and the Lion's body was the thing carrying the slope and the lid and the verdict. A summons made of moving Aether would always have a pulse, and a pulse would always be something a Paragon of Motion could put his hand on. So Hiromi began, very slowly, to take the Lion out of the Material.

He did not dismiss it. Dismissal would have dropped everything it carried at once, the Weight and the slope and the second remembering of the paw. A load dropped all at once falls on someone. He drew it out of its body the way a careful man draws a cork out of a bottle that has been shaken: by degrees, so slowly that nothing inside it would know it was being moved. There is a law, set down in the papers he was not supposed to understand, that a thing changed slowly enough keeps the shape of what it is all the way through the change; hurry the change and it scatters into everything it might have become, but ease it across and it arrives on the far side still itself. He eased the Lion across. The mass that had come back into it when the boy let go, he let go of himself, a little and a little. The gold of its flanks thinned from metal to glass, from glass to the colour of light through honey, from honey to a brightness with no surface at all. Through its chest he could see the cracked slabs behind it. Its claws no longer dented the stone they rested on. Its weight left the floor, and the floor, relieved of it, settled with a long grinding sigh.

What remained was everything the Lion had ever been except a body. The pattern of the haetae at the gate of Sum-gol, held in Soul. Its recognition, its ownership, its judgement, held in Fate. Its presence. A melody survives the silencing of the bell that played it, if someone is still holding it in his head, and Hiromi was holding this one; **[Ma]**, Recall, was nothing if not the art of keeping what is no longer struck. A spirit has no packed Aether to tremble. It has no note that can be pushed off its pitch. Whatever the boy put his hand on next, it would not be the Lion's pulse, because the Lion no longer had one.

And the relief of it came into Hiromi's body like breath into a held chest. A summons in the Material is a summons the room has to feed and its maker has to hold up, and GRASP had been holding up four hundredweight of cat several times over through a relay for the whole of the bout. Lifting the Material out of it lifted that. The faint dusk at the edges of his sight drew back further. The heat in his chest dropped from white to a dull, workable red.

He needed that. The boy was nearly on him.

---

Hiromi had not waited for the boy to arrive before answering him. He had answered on the exhale.

He had known since the hum first went sharp that the boy would come in person at the end of it. A boy who had stood still through everything thrown at him would move only when he had a reason, and the reason would be Hiromi himself; it would be Hiromi's body, and Hiromi's rhythm, and the four-count breath he had been taking in plain sight of the whole Crucible. So on the out-breath before the boy's foot left the stone, Hiromi had reached down into the Firmament, past the Lion's star and the bright familiar points near the surface, down into the deep dark water at the bottom of everything he had ever understood all the way through. And he had called up from the depths the oldest guardian he knew.

The Black Tortoise of the north. Hyeonmu.

It was painted on the north wall of the ancestral shrine at Sum-gol, a black tortoise with a serpent coiled about it, the guardian of the cold quarter, of deep water and winter and of everything that is put away to be kept. As a small boy he had stood in front of it for hours. Later he had gone further, as he always went further: he had kept a river snapping-turtle in a basin in the east courtyard for a year, and when it died he had taken it apart on the kitchen table to his mother's loud and lasting disgust, and he had learned that a tortoise's shell is not armour that it wears. The shell is its skeleton. It is the ribs and the spine grown outward and fused into a single domed vault of bone, roofed over with horn, so that the animal's armour and its frame are one structure and there is no joint between them for a blade to find. A dome turns a blow at one point into a squeeze spread over the whole curve, the way an arch turns the weight of a bridge into a push along its own stones. In the cold, its heart beats once to a count of ten.

It rose out of the sand in front of him as if out of deep water. The floor between Hiromi and the oncoming boy went dark, a black slick spreading across the scored sand in a disc as wide as a cartwheel, and up out of the dark came the vault of the shell, plated in scutes of black horn edged with dim green light, each plate ridged in growth-rings, the serpent's coils lying in a dark rope around its rim. Hiromi did not give it a body. There was no time and he did not need one. He gave it only the shell, stood on edge between himself and the boy, a round shield taller than he was, and he gave it one word of **[Ath]**, and the word was *Keep.*

The boy's palm arrived.

It came exactly where Hiromi had known it would come and exactly when: to the centre of his chest, at the bottom of the exhale, as his weight drifted the last fraction backward, so that the strike ran with his own sway instead of against it. It was so precisely chosen that, even with the shell rising to meet it, Hiromi felt a flash of pure professional admiration. A push that runs with the way a body is already going is a push AXIS has no quarrel with. A top has no grip along its axis. The boy had read the spinning top off the cracked slab beside him and gone looking for its axis, and he had found it, and it was a very good find.

But the palm met the shell first, a finger's breadth from Hiromi's coat.

And the shell kept it.

Hiromi had built Hyeonmu's shell on a thought he had been turning over since he was eleven, standing on the platform of the southern line watching the great engines come in. A heavy engine stopping by its brakes throws away everything it was. The whole of its rushing goes into the brake-shoes as heat and is lost, smoking, into the air. But a machine could be made, and in the far south men were trying to make one, that stopped by turning its own rushing back into stored force, winding its motion into a spring or a charge instead of burning it, so that the stopping itself was a kind of saving. And a shield could be made that did not fight a blow at all, that took the wave of it in so perfectly matched that none of it rebounded, the way a heavy rope tied to a rope of the same weight passes a ripple along without the smallest jerk at the knot. Nothing bounced back off Hyeonmu's shell. Nothing broke against it. It received.

The boy's strike was motion, but it was not only motion. It was motion that the boy's soul had made: the whole body accelerated by his art into one coherent line and driven into the palm, the continuation he had poured into Hiromi's sway. That continuation had come out of Kain's own Essence, worked by his own Archon, and when it struck the shell, the shell recognised it for what it was underneath its speed. It translated it. The motion went into the black horn as motion and came out the far side of the meeting as Essence, drawn down through the ringed plates into the vault of bone, and there **[Dom]** closed over it like a lid over a strongbox. Hiromi's Dominion could not have stopped the boy's movement; the boy was right about that, and always had been. But it could hold what that movement turned into. Essence obeys conviction, and Hiromi's conviction about what was in his keeping was absolute.

And Wyther could not object. That was the part that made him want to laugh again, and he very nearly did. Continuance through Change. The boy's own Archon had no quarrel with a motion that became something else and went on existing; that was the Archon's whole creed. The strike had not been stopped. It had not been destroyed. It had changed, and it continued, in the vault of the Black Tortoise's shell, as stored Essence under Hiromi's lock.

What reached Hiromi's body was his own sway, nothing more: the backward drift he had begun himself at the end of his breath. He let it carry him. His boots slid half a pace back across the sand and he caught his weight on his rear foot, knee bent, and stood up straight again.

The shell hummed, very low, lower than the Lion had ever hummed, a note so deep it was almost below hearing, the sound of a heart that beats once in a long while. The green light along the edges of its scutes had brightened. Down in the vault of it, under the lid, something it had been given turned over slowly, like a coal in a banked fire.

The boy stood a pace away, at the end of his own extended arm. His palm still rested against the black horn.

---

The Lion had finished crossing.

Hiromi felt it go over the last of the threshold behind him and come to rest on the far side, out of the body entirely and into presence, and in the instant it came to rest, it spread.

He had wondered, during the long slow drawing-out, whether the Weight and the slope would survive the Lion losing its body, and he had found the answer in the walls. The Crucible was warded. Ward-lines ran under the sand and up into the barrier and round the whole raked oval of the floor, inscribed into the bedrock by people who had spent their lives making sure that what happened on this floor stayed on it, and the effect of all that care was that the Crucible was a sealed vessel. The Aether inside it was a fluid shut in a jar. Any boy who had ever pressed on a stoppered bottle of water knows that a press at one point of a sealed fluid is felt undiminished at every point of it at once. A body is a point. A presence in a sealed vessel is the vessel. While the Lion had a body, its Weight had fallen off with distance like everything Hiromi owned. Now that it had none, the pressure it carried was the pressure of the whole jar.

The spirit of the haetae cast its presence across the Crucible.

It showed in the room first. Every lamp flame on every barrier post around the whole long oval of the floor shortened at once and burned blue at the root, all of them, the near and the far, as if a single hand had turned down every wick together. The dust still hanging over the wreck sank in a single grey curtain and lay down. Every draw-gauge set into the walls swung round on its pin and pointed at Hiromi, and stayed. The ward-lines under the floor took up a deep groaning note, the sound of a ship's timbers when the sea leans on them, and the faint glow of their inscriptions came up through the cracks in the slabs in long pale seams.

Then the tiers. The presence did not reach the stands with any weight; the wards held that back. But it reached them the way a storm reaches a house before the rain, as a feeling, and the feeling was being looked at. The roar of the crowd, which had been building since the boy took his first step, faltered and failed. In the front rows people sat back hard in their seats without deciding to. A man halfway out of his place sat down again. Somewhere high on the northern tier a dog that someone had smuggled in under a coat began to whine, steadily, and would not be hushed. Down on the floor, there was no longer anywhere the Lion was not: no far mark and no near mark, no ten paces beyond which a draw ran easy, no corner of the oval where the slope gave out. The boy could run as fast as his art would carry him. Everywhere he ran would be the step before the gate.

Hiromi felt the cost of that, and it was real, and he could carry it. He stood with his weight back on his rear foot and his chin up and the dried blood cracking on his lip, and he looked at the boy a pace away with the brightest, most delighted face he had worn since the bell.

"You found my rhythm!" His voice came out at its full size and rang off the barrier. "Good! It's yours. Have it. I don't need it anymore!"

He jerked his chin toward the vast dark shell between them, at the palm still laid on its plates.

"Hyeonmu doesn't keep time. Its heart beats once while you count to ten." He grinned. "And it keeps everything it's given."

The shell's low note sounded once, under his words, very deep, like a heart in cold water.

"Of course."

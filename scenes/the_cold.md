# The Cold

*The Pit, and after it the western shield above Kharven, the first snow-hour of the morning*

Shōki heard a voice that felt familiar.

It came from nowhere he could point to, from under the roar of the vents and the slow boil of the river and the hum of the goblin's thickening air, and it said his name. Only that. *Shōki.* A woman's voice, low and warm, with a little laughter held back behind it, the way a voice sounds when it is about to tell you to lie down before you set the bedding on fire.

Nobody had said his name in three hundred years. The Kurenai had struck it. The Pit had no use for it. He knew the voice. He knew he knew it, the way a man knows the shape of a room in the dark, and when he reached for whose it was there was nothing there to reach with. He had burned that on the black floor. He had given the Plane the part of himself that would have known her, and the Plane did not cheat the exchange, and so the voice came to him with all its warmth and none of its face, a letter with the name cut out of the bottom.

It did not calm him. Nothing calmed him. It went into the heat with everything else and the heat took it and rose.

He came on.

His fist was moving at its right pace now, the dragon's mouth on the end of his arm shut and burning at the back of the throat, and the ring of his Domain stood round them both in its kiln-orange light with nowhere for the heat to go but up, and up, and up. The goblin's pane of glassy air had sagged into a long clear drip. The velour on Vaashuuk's sleeve was still smouldering. Shōki took a step across the yellow-hot obsidian toward him, and another, and the third step was nearly the speed of a man walking, and the fourth would be the speed of a man killing.

Vaashuuk's yawn finished.

His mouth closed with a small wet click of the gold canine against the bottom teeth, and he looked at Shōki over his two claws, and the look was not lazy at all.

"Sloth's mostly dull, handsome," he said, quite quietly. "Mostly just lyin' there. A big dull lump of not-botherin'." He took the claws out of his mouth. "But you gotta put a little spark of enthusiasm in it. Now and then. Just a little one. Or it don't take."

The fourth step did not land.

Shōki's influence over the world around him began to sputter. He felt it go the way a man on a long rope feels the rope go slack, from the far end first. The ring of ABI, a hundred and fifty yards of kiln-light, flickered at its edge, and the edge was not where it had been. Pieces of it had stopped. Out at the boundary, where the heat had been piling against the wall it could not cross, there were patches where nothing was happening at all, holes in the orange like holes burned in paper, and in each hole the world had simply ceased.

He saw the first one up close a heartbeat later, because it was in front of his face.

A drop of molten obsidian from his own stamp had been hanging in the air there, glowing, falling at the slow pace of the thickened field. Now it was not falling at all. It was not glowing. It hung where it was, a bead of black glass the size of a plum, perfectly round, perfectly dull, so still that it seemed to have no surface, as though a hole had been cut in the air in the shape of a drop and painted black. And around it the air itself had gone strange: a crystal of nothing, a faceted pocket a hand across, its planes catching the kiln-light at angles that did not agree with each other, as if three different windows had been set into the same frame.

Then there were more of them.

They came out of the goblin like frost comes across a windowpane, but in jagged pieces, scattered, wrong. A coil of steam off the river stopped and stood in the air as a white glass ribbon that would never finish its curl. A tongue of his own purple fire, flaring off his shoulder, went still in the middle of its leap and hung there as a twisted violet sculpture with every flicker caught in it like an insect in amber. Half a bubble on the boiling river stopped in the act of bursting and became a dome of rose-colored crystal with a ragged hole in its top. All around them the Pit was coming apart into pieces of perfect stillness, malformed and beautiful and dead, each fragment sealed off from the next along clean flat planes that cut through steam and stone and light alike, and where the planes met there was a sound, very faint, like ice settling on a pond in the night.

Shōki understood it a moment too late.

Before, the goblin had made the world thick. He had slowed the flow of things across the room and left the small shaking of things where they stood alone, and that was how the heat had beaten him. This was the other thing. This was the stillness going all the way down. In those fragments even the shaking had stopped. There was no heat in them because there was nothing in them moving at all, not a mote, not a tremor, and the fire Shōki poured into the field went into those still pieces and simply ended there, the way water ends at the bottom of a well.

His dragon's mouth opened. The furnace at the back of it roared and went out.

A fragment had closed around his right hand.

He saw it. The maw, scale and teeth and furnace, was sitting inside a pane of nothing that cut his forearm off two inches below the fused cuff in a plane as clean as a headsman's stroke, and the part of him on the far side of the plane was there and was his and could not move. He could not feel heat in it. He could not feel anything in it. It was not cold. It was not anything.

"Ouuuuu," said Vaashuuk, a little breathless. "Hurts, huh. Hurts me too." And it did; Shōki could see it. The goblin was gray round the mouth, and sweat stood on his upper lip under the painted beauty mark, and his claws were shaking. "This is why I don't do it. Takes the stuffin' outta me. Captain's gonna owe me a week off. A month." He swallowed. "Okay. Hold still. Not that you got a choice."

The stillness was spreading down, under them. Shōki felt the slab beneath his boots go quiet, the yellow glow of the obsidian stopping in a single instant into dull dead black. He looked down. There was a plane running through the rock beneath them, a clean flat face of nothing, and beyond it, through it, below it, there was something the Pit did not have.

There was blue.

The fragments touched the floor of hell, and the floor of hell broke along their faces like a frozen lake breaks along its cracks, and the two of them fell through.

---

Cold.

It went into him all at once, like a hand closing round his chest. He was on his knees, and under his knees was snow, deep and dry and squeaking, and above him was a sky so blue it rang, a pale enormous winter blue from one edge of the world to the other, with a sun low in it the color of new brass, and the light came off the snow in a white blaze that made his one eye water. The air smelled of pine resin and woodsmoke and nothing else. No sulfur. No incense. No vents. In the silence he could hear a crow, a long way off, and the soft tick of snow settling off a branch.

And in his chest, something moved.

He did not recognise it at first. It was a single heavy thump behind his breastbone, under the Covenant Scar, under the Crystal, a blunt fist knocking once on the inside of a door. Then again. Then again, faster, finding its own beat, and the blood went out from it down his arms and up into his throat and into his face, hot, *his*, moving, and he understood it was his heart. It had not beaten in the Pit. He had not noticed it stop. Three hundred years on the slab without a pulse, and it had taken one breath of winter to remind it what it was for.

His right hand was his own again. The pane of stillness had stayed below with the rest of the Pit. His hand was a hand: five fingers, bronze skin crazed with ember lines, the cuff fused above it, the three dangling links. The dragon had gone back into whatever part of him it lived in.

He knelt there a long moment. Then he put his hand down into the snow.

It was so cold it burned. He pushed it in to the wrist, to the cuff, feeling the snow pack against his palm and between his fingers and creak, and then he closed his hand on it and lifted it and watched it go. It did not even reach the ground as snow. It ran out between his fingers as water, and the water hissed on his skin and went up as steam, and the steam rolled off him in a thick white cloud, off his shoulders and his back and the crown of his head, until he knelt in a fog of his own making with the blue sky going pale through it.

*Cold,* he thought. *I had forgotten cold.*

The heat of him was already working outward through the snow. A ring of it went soft around his knees, then gray, then wet, then gone, and under it was black earth and frozen bracken, steaming.

---

Fifty yards down the slope, Vaashuuk Snorr-Khal lay on his back in a snowdrift with his arms and legs spread wide and laughed until he coughed.

He had landed badly. He had landed on his pompadour. There was snow in his collar and snow down the back of his green silk shirt and snow, unforgivably, inside one boot, and the scorched sleeve of the plum coat was still giving off a little thread of smoke into the bright air. He did not care. He was out. He had done the hard thing, the big thing, the thing that took the stuffing out of him, and it had *worked*, and the Captain was going to owe him a month and possibly a new coat, and the sky was so blue.

He lifted his head out of the drift, shook it like a dog, and looked downhill.

Below him the slope ran down through scattered black pines to a valley, and in the valley there was a road, and on the far side of the road, on a ridge of gray granite, there was a watchtower of black basalt blocks, squat and square and ugly as a fist, and on top of the tower, snapping in the wind, there was a banner.

He knew it. Every goblin from the deep yards knew it before they knew their own mother's face. Iron-red cloth, and on it a black basalt fist, closed, the knuckles forward. The Iron Mandate of Undaar-Keth. The Empire in the North, the goblins in the yards called it when there was no hobgoblin in earshot, and the Imperium when there was one and they wanted to make him smile.

Vaashuuk looked at it for a long time.

The hobgoblins had always looked down on the lesser kinds. That was the word they used in the decrees, when they used one at all: *lesser*. Orcs were for the walls and the northern perimeter, to be spent against whatever came off the tundra hungry. Goblins were for the workshops. Goblins were small and quick-fingered and cheap, and the whole western shield ran on what goblin hands made: the gauge-housings and the standpipe valves, the glyph-cut fittings for the draw-mains, the little brass hearts of half the magitech in three kingdoms, filed and soldered and tested in the canyon workshops by goblins who were paid in bunk space and soup. His mother had made valve-seats until her eyes went. His father had worked the bellows until the bellows-roof came down. Hobgoblins called it the natural order. Goblins called it the shift.

And here was Vaashuuk, the goblin who did not work. Who had never made a single valve-seat in his life and never would. Who lay down. Who was, as far as he knew, the laziest, least enthusiastic, most thoroughly unbothered creature the goblin race had ever produced, lying in the snow in a stolen coat under the fist of the people who had built an empire on goblin hustle, with his hands folded on his belly.

It was the funniest thing he had ever seen.

He laughed again, long and wheezing, with his head back in the drift and the snow melting into his hair. He laughed until his ribs ached and a crow took off from a pine above him in disgust.

"Ouuuuu," he said to the banner, wiping his eyes. "Look at you. Still flappin'. Still workin' so hard." He settled back into the snow and crossed his ankles. "It's fun to be back."

---

The village was at the bottom of the valley where the road crossed a stream on a hump of stone bridge, and it was small and bright and full of people.

It was the kind of place that had been on the border so long it had stopped noticing which side it was on. The houses were timber, low and long under deep snow, their eaves painted the dark red the northern clans used to keep the winter out, their doors hung with straw charms and little brass bells, and above the doors on the main street hung lanterns of oiled paper in yellow and orange and a green like new leaves, unlit in the daylight but swinging in the wind. Smoke went up straight from every chimney in the still cold. There was a smith's forge going at the far end, the clank of it coming up the street with the smell of hot iron, and a woman sweeping snow off a step with a broom taller than she was, and two boys dragging a sledge of firewood and arguing about it, and a black dog with one white ear that followed Shōki twenty yards with its hackles up and then thought better of it and went under a cart.

People looked at him. A bare-chested giant with white hair, burned dark and crazed with ember lines, irons on his wrists, steam coming off him in the cold like a horse after a race. They looked and looked away and went inside and shut their doors, and the little brass bells on the doors rang as they shut.

He was hungry. He had not been hungry in three hundred years either.

The smell found him before he found the stall: pork bone boiled white, and ginger, and soy, and spring onion. It was a lean-to off the end of a timber house, hung with a short blue cloth curtain split in three, the kind a noodle seller hangs over his counter, and behind it a man with a kerchief on his head was standing over a great black iron pot with a steam-cloud rolling off it. There was a bench in front of the counter and a table at the end of it, a plank on two trestles, with a pot of chopsticks on it.

Shōki sat down at the table. The bench groaned under him.

The noodle man looked at him for a long moment, at the irons and the scar and the steam, and something went out of his face. Then he put a bowl down in front of him. A deep red-lacquered bowl, chipped on one side, with the broth in it cloudy and gold, a coil of noodles, two slices of pork with the fat gone clear, half an egg with a soft orange yolk, a heap of green onion and a square of dark seaweed leaning on the rim. He set it down and went back behind his steam and did not come out.

Shōki ate.

It was very good. He had not known how good food could be. He ate with his face over the bowl and the steam from it mixing with the steam from him, and the broth was hot and salt and rich and went down into the place under his breastbone where the heart was still beating its new beat, and for a moment, a few breaths, nothing was rising in him at all.

"Those marks," said someone beside him.

There was an old man at the other end of the bench. Shōki had not seen him sit. He was very small and very old, bundled in three coats and a fur hat with the ear-flaps down, with a face as brown and creased as a walnut and a long white mustache stained yellow at the ends from his pipe, which he was holding, unlit, between two knuckles. His eyes were milky blue at the edges, still sharp in the middle, and they were on the Covenant Scar.

"I've seen those," the old man said. "Once. When I was a lad. My father took me down to the Seat for the assize, and I saw them walk the condemned down the black floor. The Kurenai." He said it carefully, in the old court way, the way his father had. "They had that mark. Just there." He touched his own left shoulder through the three coats. "Only theirs were red."

Shōki put his chopsticks down.

"Mine was," he said.

The old man looked at him a long moment, and then, very slowly, he nodded, as if a thing he had been told as a child and not believed had just walked in and sat down at his table and asked for noodles.

"The Moto," said Shōki. His voice was low and rough, and the noodle man behind the steam went very still. "The seven lines. The chairs. Who sits them."

"Who sits them." The old man turned the pipe over in his fingers. "Well. Nobody sits them the way they did. Not for a while now. The Kōkan put them all under the one crown. Years back. The boy did. Muken." He said the name the way the north says it, with a kind of pride and a kind of wariness both. "Came up out of nowhere, a sellsword, the Kōkan blood come back. Unified them. The seven lines kneel to the red chair now, or did, the way I heard it. One house, one law, one crown. Built it all up north in the Seat." He shook his head. "My father wouldn't have believed it. Seven chairs that couldn't agree on the weather for a thousand years, and one boy made them kneel."

Shōki sat very still.

The Kōkan.

The crown line. The red chair, that had sat above the other six and ranked them and ruled them and called it law. The Kōkan, whose judgment had stood over the Hall the morning the seven chairs voted Tsuyu to the black floor. Their hand had been on that verdict. Every hand in that Hall had, every chair, and over all the chairs the red one, which had not spoken against it, which had let it pass, which had ranked a Kurenai's wife below whatever bargain was being made that morning in the corridors and closed the matter.

And now there was one crown. One house. One law.

*They put them all under it.*

The anger came up.

It did not come as it had in the Pit, in a column of fire. It came out of him the way the steam came off him, through every crack in his skin at once, and it was not heat. Or it was heat, and something else with it. It went out of him into the cold bright air as a haze, a faint shimmer the color of a bruise, purple-black at its heart and fever-red at its edges, and it did not rise like steam rises. It spread. It went low across the snow and under the doors and in through the chinks of the shutters and down the street, slow and thick, and wherever it touched it stayed.

The noodle man behind the steam made a sound.

He had put his hand flat on the counter. His face had gone red, a deep hot flushed red from the collar up, and there were lines in the red, fine dark branching lines like the cracks in fired glaze, spreading up his throat and across his cheeks as Shōki watched. He swayed. The kerchief came off his head and his hair was soaked with sweat in the cold. He looked at Shōki with eyes gone glassy and bright, and opened his mouth, and what came out of it was a hoarse furious shout with no words in it at all, and then he went down behind the counter with the pot, and the broth went across the floor in a hissing gold sheet.

It was the body's own fire turned against the body. Shōki knew it the moment he saw it, the way he had known his own wounds close in a hiss when he was a boy. His heat was going into them, his rage riding on it, and their own blood answered it the way blood answers a wound: it inflamed. Everywhere at once. The fever climbed and would not break, the flesh swelled and reddened and burned, the body's guard turned on the body it was guarding and did not stop, could not stop, because the thing it was fighting was in every part of it and was not a sickness at all. It was him.

The old man beside him coughed. Once, then again, harder. The pipe fell out of his fingers onto the plank.

Out in the street, someone screamed, and then someone else was shouting in a voice gone raw and senseless with rage, and there was the sound of a door being broken and the crash of a lantern and the clank of the smith's forge stopping in the middle of a stroke. The black dog with the white ear was howling under its cart. The two boys with the sledge had stopped arguing. The woman with the broom was on her knees on her own step with her hands over her face and the red climbing up through her fingers.

It went from house to house, down the street, over the bridge, across the stream, the way a fire goes through dry stubble when the wind is behind it, and he did not stop it, because he did not know how, and because some part of him at the bottom of the heat did not want to.

The old man was looking at him. His face had gone the deep red of the others and the glaze-lines were across his forehead, and he was breathing in short hot rasps, but his eyes were still sharp in the middle, and he was looking at Shōki without fear.

"The boy's..." he said. "Muken's... you'd have to go north, to the Seat, to find what's... his sons..." He coughed, and the cough took the rest of it, and he leaned slowly sideways against the post of the lean-to and was still.

The street went quiet after a while.

Not all of it. Somewhere a child was crying, thin and far off, down past the bridge, where the haze had gone thin before it reached. Somewhere a man was calling a name over and over in a cracked voice. The bells on the doors rang in the wind. The lanterns swung, yellow and orange and new-leaf green, over a street full of shapes in the snow.

Shōki sat at the plank table with the red bowl in front of him and his heart beating in his chest.

He had known this. He had known it for three hundred years in the Pit without having to look at it, and now he looked at it. Wherever he went, death went with him. It came off him like heat comes off iron. It was the anger, and the anger was not something he did; it was what he was now, what he had made himself on the black floor, and it leaked out of him into the world as a presence, a weight, a wrongness that bent the very grain of things around him the way a heavy stone bends the cloth it is laid on. The world could not hold him without warping. He had always known it would come to this. He had chosen it.

*Muken Moto,* he thought.

He would find him. North, the old man said. The Seat. He would walk into the house of the red chair and stand in front of this boy who had put the seven lines under one crown, and he would read his chest, the way the Kurenai read every chest, and he would decide. He would be the one to decide, this time. Whether Muken Moto was what the family needed. Whether there should be a red chair at all. He was done. He was done letting the Kōkan choose the road for his people and call it law, done letting one house rank every other house and stand over the black floor while the chairs voted and the blade fell.

That was why she died.

That was why his family died, all of them, the line that would have been his, the children they had talked about at night in the dark with the camphor in her collar and her cold feet against his shins. Because one house sat above the others and judged, and nobody judged it.

He stood up. The bench, relieved of him, sprang back an inch.

There was something on the table under the old man's elbow. It had been there the whole time, a little thing, half under the fallen pipe, and Shōki had not looked at it. He looked at it now.

It was a chapbook. A cheap little pamphlet of folded paper the size of a man's palm, the kind pedlars carried in a tray and sold for a copper to children at fairs, printed in blurred black ink on gray paper, with a woodcut on the cover colored by hand in red and orange. The woodcut showed a huge man, almost round, with a kettle for a head and steam pouring out of the spout, and chains on his wrists, sitting in a puddle of his own tears that were boiling. Under it, in big friendly letters, it said:

**OLD FURNACE AND THE HEADSMAN'S WIFE**
*A Tale for Wicked Children, With a Moral*

He picked it up. The paper was soft from handling. Some child had loved it. There were thumbprints on the corners and a jam stain on the back.

He opened it.

> Once upon a time there was a great big headsman with a great big temper, and he was so cross ALL the time that steam came out of his ears!

There was a picture of the steam coming out of his ears.

> One day the Lords said his wife had been very naughty, and they had to chop her. And the headsman was SO cross that he cried and cried, and his tears were so hot that they boiled him all up like a kettle! WHEEEE! went the kettle. And now he lives at the bottom of the hole, and he is still boiling, and if YOU lose YOUR temper, Old Furnace will come up out of the hole in the night and boil YOU too!

At the bottom of the page there was a rhyme, for skipping, with the beats marked:

*Old Furnace, Old Furnace, with irons on his hands,*
*He cried for his lady and boiled up the lands.*
*He cried and he cried till his eyeball went POP,*
*So mind what your mam says, or you'll get the CHOP!*

The last page had the moral, in a little box with a border of printed flowers: *Children who keep their tempers do not become Kettles.*

Shōki stood in the street of the dead village with the steam coming off him into the blue air and read it twice.

They had made her into a joke for children. They had made the morning on the black floor, and the irons, and the camphor and the copper hair-pin and the sound of the blade, into a thing for skipping in the snow. His eye. His grief. *WHEEEE.* Somewhere in the north there were children who sang it at each other, laughing, and mothers who used it to frighten them into bed, and none of them, not one, had ever been told her name.

He closed the chapbook very carefully, along its crease. He did not burn it. He put it inside the knot of the white robe at his hip, against his skin, where the heat would keep it soft.

Then he turned north, toward the Seat, and walked out of the village, and behind him the snow did not settle on the road where he had been.

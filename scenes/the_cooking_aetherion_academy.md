# The Cooking — Aetherion Academy

*Aetherion Academy. The broken cold room under the south range. Continuous.*

"I name no one."

Mu-jin said it to the room, and he said it loud enough for the storm to hear. Above the hole the dark drew in on itself and turned, and the tick in his chest slowed, and he felt the bear's attention settle on him like a hand on the back of the neck. Raga would wait. Raga always waited for a name. The heavier cost of the bond had always been the naming: deciding aloud, in front of witnesses, who would die. He had paid it at Rimward and on the Ashgate road. He would not pay it with a twelve-year-old.

"Now, now," he heard himself say, softer, the old phrase he used when he was giving ground and wanted the student to know he knew it. Then he walked into the gas.

Lapis went before him. The yellow layer beyond his fifteen metres met the edge of his field, and where it met, it burned without flame. The hydrogen sulphide gave up its sulphur in a falling yellow snow and its hydrogen as a thin damp sweat on the stone, and the air behind his steps came clean and stinging and smelled of struck matches. But the sulphur fought him. Every grain that fell had touched the stone first, settled on it, choked it a little before it dropped, and the clean bubble around him shrank as he went, from fifteen metres to twelve, to ten. Each step cost ten times what the last had, and the match smell flickered like a lamp in a draught.

*Reserve 612,500,000 EU.* The ledger arrived plain and late.

He reached Velder at nine metres from Malphas, well inside the reach a careful man would have kept from him, and knelt.

The captain lay on his face with his ruined hand trapped under his chest. Mu-jin turned him by the shoulder. Velder's face came up out of the yellow grey and slack, a string of spit at his lip, his eyes rolled half back, and he was not breathing. Mu-jin hauled him up by the coat and set him sitting against the nearest pier, head well above the gas, and struck him once flat-handed across the chest. Velder's body jerked. He dragged in a breath that whooped like a child's after crying and coughed it out yellow, and dragged in another.

The hand. The thumb and first two fingers were gone at the knuckles and the stumps were pumping. Mu-jin closed his own hand round the wrist and let Lapis go into the blood. Blood wants to clot. It is always one step from clotting, held back only by the body's careful chemistry, and he let it take that step all at once. The stumps sealed in a dark glossy crust that formed under his fingers in a breath. *850,000 EU.* The bleeding stopped.

Velder's eyes found him. They focused, slowly, on the white-blond hair and the empty socket of the lens and the hands that had just closed his wounds, and something moved behind them that Mu-jin was too tired to read.

His vision had begun to whiten at the edges. He did not see the stubby nickelled shape of the pocket revolver holstered inside Velder's left boot. His eyes passed over it on the way back to his feet, and the read did not catch.

"You stopped," Malphas said, very close, "to save my student."

He had come on while Mu-jin knelt. He had not hurried. He stood six feet away with the girl and the carter at his shoulders, and Mu-jin was inside his reach.

"I would have done the same," Malphas said gently. "That is the trouble with us."

The reagent case on Mu-jin's hip began to bloom.

It started at the brass clasp, a spot of orange no larger than a fingernail, and it spread the way frost spreads across a window on a hard night, branching, feathering, racing outward along every hinge and rivet and fitting the case had. Brass went green, then black, then fell to powder. The iron hinge pins went orange and swelled and split. Mu-jin heard the clasp go with a soft dry crunch like a beetle under a boot. The case fell open, and a year's careful work tumbled out of its velvet across the black wet flags.

Glass broke, three vials of it.

A vial of purple crystals and a vial of clear syrup had shattered side by side. Mu-jin saw them mix and knew what they were before he had finished seeing it: permanganate and glycerine, kept in separate pockets of the case for exactly this reason. He had filled those pockets himself. The purple bled into the syrup. For three heartbeats nothing happened, and then a thread of white smoke rose from the puddle, and then it hissed, and then it burst into a hot lilac flame that ran up the spilled oil of a third broken vial and onto the hem of his coat.

He watched his coat catch fire and felt nothing.

The flame ran up the wool to his knee, to his thigh, licking blue and orange, and the Arrears held the pain back from him like a hand over a mouth. He saw the cloth blacken. He saw the skin of his thigh redden through the burning hole. He beat at it with his palm and the fire went out under his hand, and still he felt nothing, and he knew exactly what was coming.

The floor moved under his knees.

He had made the brine himself. He had poured the minerals out of the stone into the meltwater to give the black lightning a road to ground, and it had worked, and it had left the flags around him soft and wet and full of dissolved salts. It had left him the softest, wettest ground in the vault, and the Black Ground came into it the way water runs into a footprint. The flags under him went dark. The stink of a pond bottom rose round him, sulphur and black mud. And the hands came up.

They came up through the floor on either side of his knees, two of them, black and wet and broad as the wheel of a handcart, and they closed on his legs below the knee. In the centre of each palm the brassy needles of iron sulphide stood out in sprays, and the needles went in. He saw them go in through his trousers and into the meat of his calves, the gastrocnemius on both sides, a dozen thin bright points in each leg, and he felt the grip of the hands a full second later, and the needles a second after that, all at once, like a mouthful of teeth closing.

That was not the worst of it. The worst of it was the needles were hollow, in the way a root is hollow, and through them the rot went into him.

He watched his own legs begin to go over. Around every puncture the skin paled, then greyed, then took on a faint green-black bruise that spread outward in a slow ring, and the smell came up out of it, sweet and thick, the smell of a field hospital's back room in summer. Gangrene took days. Under his eyes it was taking seconds. The tissue was dying from the inside out, the way meat goes in a warm larder, and it did not hurt yet. It would.

The burn on his thigh arrived.

It came all at once, the whole fire delivered late, and it went through him like a blade through cloth, and he heard himself make a sound he had not made since Rimward. His hands went flat on the black floor. He held there on his knees with the hands gripping his rotting legs and the burn screaming in his thigh, and he looked up.

Malphas had crouched in front of him, forearms on his knees, the way he used to sit on the bench by the archive gate.

"Your blood is full of sugar," Malphas said conversationally. "So is every man's. It is what the muscles burn. Did you know that when a muscle burns it without enough air, it turns it to acid? Every runner knows it, at the end of a race. The legs fill, and they fail." He tilted his head. "I can make it run without air."

Mu-jin's thighs locked.

The cramp started in the quadriceps above the knee and went up into his hips and down into the calves the hands were holding, every fibre seizing at once, hard as wood, the acid flooding them faster than any blood could carry it away. His legs went from under him. He went down from his knees onto his hip in the black, and the hands came with him, holding, and the needles tore in the turning, and he lay there half on his side with his legs knotted under him like a dead spider's.

*Reserve 611,650,000 EU.* Plenty. He had plenty. It did not matter. Nothing Malphas was doing was a working he could out-pour. It was chemistry. It was his own body's chemistry, the sugar in his own blood and the acid in his own muscles, taken over and run the wrong way. And Mu-jin's Resilience, the S-Grade Ward that would turn a discharge of forty billion joules, had never been asked to stop a man from fermenting him.

He made himself read.

The lens was gone, somewhere in the black. He did not need it. He looked at Malphas the way he looked at a failing Draft, the old reflex, and he found the lattice hand resting on the man's knee six feet away, the cold threads running from it to the two standing carriers and the chained boy. He found the man's Ward, thick, two layers by any reading. And he found the road through it.

Pervium. He had seen it before. Now he took it.

"*Falsum ardeat,*" he said through his teeth, and the dead right arm that had hung at his side for years lit from shoulder to wrist with written glyph. Lines of Parunic came up white through the sleeve, rising off the skin, nine arms of script reaching from his dead side as they had on the Ashgate road. "*Verum maneat.*"

The Calcination went into Malphas without touching him.

It did not break the Ward. It crossed it, the way a particle sometimes crosses a wall it has no right to climb. The chance of that shrinks with every layer, and he paid for every layer. *85,000,000 EU.* It left his Crystal like a tooth pulled at the root. Half of an honest fire arrived on the far side of Malphas's Shell, inside him, at the place where every thread met.

The lattice on Malphas's left hand lit from within.

For one heartbeat Mu-jin could see the bones of the man's hand through the skin, dark against the glow, and the raised lines of the lattice burning white along them. The smell of cooked meat came off it. Malphas's hand jerked open, and the two smallest fingers curled into charred hooks and stayed curled. The threads flickered. The carter's thread held. Ansel's thread held. The girl's thread went out like a snuffed wick.

She folded.

She did it gently, from the knees, the way a girl sits down in a meadow, and she lay on her side on the black floor with her hands in her lap where they had been all along. Her face was still. For the first time since Mu-jin had come down the stair it looked like a face that someone had lived behind. Then her breath came out of her, a long last exhalation, and for the first time it fogged white in the cold air. It hung over her, and dispersed, and did not come again.

Her share of the overflow came home.

Mu-jin saw it go. It went up the snapped thread into the burned lattice like water hammering up a pipe when every valve on the line slams shut at once, and it hit Malphas's Crystal in a single blow. Malphas made a sound Mu-jin had never heard a man make. His back arched. His head went back. And out of his scalp, through the black hair and the grey at the temples, a crown of pale spurs cracked up into the light, uneven, the way something grows rather than the way something is worn. Bone, or crystal, or both. His Crystal had gone past its rating, half again what it was built to bank, and it was Overgrown, and the Overgrowth had nowhere to go but out through him.

Black crystal crazed across his temples like ice across a pond. The frost exploded off his ruined hand in a white puff. And he stayed on his feet.

He stayed on his feet, swaying, breathing hard through his teeth, with a crown of spurs on his head and his two smallest fingers cooked to hooks, and he looked down at Mu-jin. He was not angry. Whatever had been patient in his face had gone somewhere else.

"There it is," Malphas whispered. "There is the teacher." He touched the spurs with his good hand, wonderingly, and the fingers came away wet. "Do you know what you've done? You've filled me."

He crouched again. Slower this time, and closer.

"Let me show you what I can do when I am full."

Mu-jin felt his own blood change.

It began as warmth, a flush in the face, a looseness in the jaw. He knew what it was within a breath and could not stop it. The sugar in his blood, the same sugar that had been feeding his cramping legs, was turning into something else in the vessels themselves. It was being fermented where it ran, the way yeast turns the sugar in a vat, the way a body with the wrong yeast in its gut brews alcohol out of its own bread. The warmth climbed into his head. The vault swam, a long slow tilt, the way a ship's deck tilts.

He tried to read. He had read every room he had entered since he was fourteen. It was the one thing he had never once failed to do, the one stat he lived inside.

The read would not come.

The glyph chains he could always see were there and blurred, doubled, sliding over one another. The threads from the lattice were a smear of cold light. The numbers of his own ledger arrived in him and would not hold still, *six hundred and something, five hundred and something,* and he could not make them add. He knew he was drunk. He knew it the way a man knows he is drowning, from the inside, with all the understanding in the world and nothing he can do with it.

"Your Gnosis," Malphas said, very gently, "is the best I have ever seen. I sat beside it for years. I learned more from the air around you than from any book in the Division. And it is chemistry, Teacher. The thinking you are so proud of runs on sugar and salt and water, like everything else." He leaned in until his face was a hand's width from Mu-jin's. "I am taking away the only thing you ever were."

He breathed out.

The miasma went straight into Mu-jin's face. There was no field to scrub it now; Lapis was choked and drunk and failing. The rotten-egg stink filled his nose and mouth and went down into his lungs, and he gagged on it, and then the smell vanished. That was the worst sign there was. A hundred parts in a million, and the nose goes dead. The lining of his lungs began to burn, the fine sacs of the alveoli flooding, and the burn would arrive late, like everything else, and he could not tell any longer how late.

He coughed. Black fluid came up with it, from somewhere, and ran down his chin.

His hand, scrabbling in the wet black beside him, closed on something cold and round. The lens. The single round lens on its thin chain, the one thing he had taken from the court that had ever been his. He got his fingers round it.

Malphas's good hand closed over his and took it away.

Malphas straightened, slowly, and stood over him with the lens held up to the grey light from the hole. He looked through it. He turned it to the storm, to the chained boy steaming in the field, to the girl lying still on the floor with her last breath gone. He turned it, at the last, down onto Mu-jin.

Against the pier behind them, Velder Crace was watching with his clotted hand held to his chest, breathing yellow, alive.

Overhead the storm turned, and the tick went on in Mu-jin's chest, waiting for a name.

"Tell me, Teacher," Malphas said, looking at him through his own lens. "What do you see now?"

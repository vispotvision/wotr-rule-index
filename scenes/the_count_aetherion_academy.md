# The Count — Aetherion Academy

*Aetherion Academy. The old cold room under the south range. Continuous.*

Mu-jin's presence came up out of him like a tide coming over a bar.

It did not start as a push. It started in the lamps. Every flame in the vault lay down flat at once, all of them pointing away from him, and held there, bluish and thin, a row of knives laid on a table. The frost Malphas had been breathing onto the walls for an hour cracked off the dressed stone in plates and came down in a dry rattle. Dust lifted from the floor, every grain of it, and hung at the height of a man's knee. Then the wave folded on itself and turned, a twisting shock that went through the room the way a slammed door goes through a house, and it took Ansel off his feet.

The boy went backward through the air without a sound and struck the end of the row of cots. The cot went over. He went over with it, rolled once on the flags, and came up onto his hands and knees with his head tilted, listening to something.

Velder Crace had both palms pressed to the table. Sweat stood on his upper lip and ran from his hairline into the burn scar, and he swallowed hard twice against the sour rise in his throat. The Pressure of a man that far up the ladder did not hurt. It only told the body, over and over, that it was in the wrong room.

Malphas stood in it with his hand still lifted where the boy's hair had been, and the frost in his knuckles did not even stir.

Mu-jin dragged one finger down the open page of the grimoire. The ink under it woke as his nail passed, line after line of Parunic lifting off the paper a hair's breadth and standing there, faintly luminous, a page written in the air above a page.

"You leave me no choice."

He felt the floor answer before he saw it.

Under the Academy, under its foundations and the sealed conduit and the old district main the Guild had laid two centuries ago, the ground ran with current the way a body runs with blood. Nobody saw those veins. They were drawn in the Survey's books as dotted lines and nobody who had not stood on one at the wrong hour believed in them. Now they came up through the flagstones as light. Great slow bodies of aether rose through the stone in columns as thick as the vault's own piers, and as they reached for the circle forming at his feet they began to turn. Each one spun tighter as it drew in, the way water spins up as it narrows toward a drain, the same mass made faster by being made small, until there were five small typhoons standing on the flags of the cold room, each the height of a man, each roaring under its breath, and every lamp flame in the vault had swung around to point at them.

"Infinite Summoning Arts." His voice carried to the back row. It always had. "Divine Thunder Bear. Raga."

And then the old words, the only ones the bear had ever answered, with the name in the same breath, because Raga would not touch what had not been named aloud by a voice he knew.

"*Numera intervallum. Veniat sonus.* Malphas."

The air began to crackle.

It started as a hiss, the sound of rain heard through a wall, and grew into a dry continuous crackling that came from everywhere at once. Blue light rose off every edge in the room. It stood off the iron frames of the cots, off the rims of the lamps, off the buckle of Velder's belt, off the frost on the floor, a pale violet-blue that was the air itself giving up its electrons where the field was strongest. The smell came after it, sharp and clean and wrong for a cellar, the smell of a high field after a storm. Mu-jin's hair lifted from the cloth that bound it. The scars along his forearm prickled.

He counted. He had always counted. Four seconds. Five. The interval was how far away the bear had been when he called, and there was no hurrying it, and Malphas knew that too, and stood and watched and let the count run because there was nothing he could do to it.

Seven.

The ozone reached them a breath before the bear did.

Raga came into the cold room the way weather comes into a valley, all at once and from no particular direction. The black of him filled the far half of the vault, a black that took the lamplight and gave none of it back, guard hairs standing in dense ranks along the hump of a shoulder wider than a cart. He was too big for the room. The rack came in last, pale grey and branching like storm-stripped driftwood, and the outermost tines scored the vault ribs as he raised his head, ploughing two white furrows through dressed stone and bringing down a sift of grit. Every tine carried a standing corona. Green motes came off the points and drifted upward against the falling grit, slow, the colour of his eyes.

The left fore was not flesh. It had not been flesh since the orchard at Sum-gol. A mill shaft of black oak ran where the bone had been, banded at the wrist with the iron tyre of a cartwheel, and the hide had grown back over the oak in places and failed to in others, so that the old timber showed through like bone through a wound. Over the left eye the three scars ran deeper now, and the green point under them burned a shade dimmer than its twin.

Raga faced Mu-jin first. He always did. The flat green points arrived on his summoner a moment after the great head had turned, and held there, checking something nobody had ever named.

Then he faced Malphas.

The tick began. It was not quite a sound and not quite a change in the air, a low regular beat under the crackle, keeping time with nothing, and Mu-jin felt it settle into his own chest where it would stay for days.

The Arrears came with it. His own body went late on him. He watched his left hand close on the grimoire's spine and felt the leather a moment afterward, as though the hand were somebody else's and the news of it had to walk to him. The migraine Sight Through the Glass had opened behind his eyes went quiet, and he knew it had not gone, only that it was on its way. He had agreed to that, once, at first recognition. He did not get to agree again.

A fifth of everything he held had gone out of him to buy those seven seconds. He felt that late as well. It arrived as a coldness in the hands, and a lightness behind the breastbone, the feeling of a cellar after the winter's wood has burned.

Raga moved.

There was no wind-up. The right fore came across the vault in a single diagonal arc from high left to low right, five claws each as long as a man's forearm, and the speed of it cracked the air the way a drover's whip does at the end of its throw, a flat hard report that hit the walls and came back. The bear's whole mass went into it, rotated through the hump and down the straight heavy bones of the shoulder, and the corona on the rack flared green as the charge on the hide went with the limb. It was aimed at the one thing in the room that had been named.

Malphas did not move his feet.

He moved his left hand. Mu-jin saw it with the lens off, the way he saw everything with the lens off. The cold thread that ran from the lattice to the boy's sternum went taut as a bowstring, and Malphas poured himself down it. Aether went into Ansel in a flood, more than a body that size had any right to hold, and the boy came up off his hands and knees as though a rope had caught him under the arms. It lifted him across six feet of floor and set him in the arc, chest square to the bear, arms loose at his sides.

The claws went into him.

They entered below the left collarbone and came out above the right hip. Between, nothing held its shape. The first claw opened the chest wall and broke the ribs across their shafts, the second and third went through the lung and the heart behind it, the fourth unseated the liver from its ligaments, and the fifth took the abdominal wall away entire. The aether Malphas had poured into him hardened at the moment of contact, every channel in the small body stiffening at once, so that the boy met the claws rigid as a board and took the whole weight of the blow that had been meant for the man behind him. Blood came out of him in a burst. It was dark and thick and cellar-cold, and it struck the flags in a fan and did not steam.

Ansel felt none of it. His face did not change. His eyes stayed perfectly ordinary, open, attentive, the eyes of a boy waiting to be told when his mother was coming. Then the rest of the strike's momentum arrived and he left the floor. He went the length of the vault loose and turning, arms flung, the way a straw doll goes when a dog shakes it, and hit the far wall below the faculty gown on its hook, and slid down it, and began, without any hurry, to get up.

*He took it for him.* Mu-jin felt the thought land a beat late, the way everything was landing now. *He made a twelve-year-old into a shield, and the shield stood up.*

Raga did not strike a target twice. Every summoner knew it. But the claws had never reached the name. The bear's head stayed on Malphas, and the tick in the room did not stop, and Mu-jin understood with a cold, sick clarity that the strike was not finished. It was owed.

He already had the vial out.

It was the length of his finger and thick-walled, stoppered with wax and wire, and the liquor inside turned on itself in a slow blue spiral that never settled, light moving through it like a fish under ice. He thumbed the wire loose.

"Third Alchemical Seal." The words came out flat and fast, the teaching voice gone, each one another piece of the mechanism. "Chains of Lightning."

He threw it overhand at the boy.

The glass broke on the flags at Ansel's feet. The liquor did not splash. It stood up. It went out across the stone in eight lines from the point of impact, and each line rose into a chain, links of blue light as thick as a man's wrist, and the chains went over the boy's shoulders and round his waist and his knees and drew him down. They pulled him flat against the floor and sank their far ends into the stone, and between them, humming, the field closed.

Ansel fought it. He did not know any better. The formula behind his Shell had been built to keep the body moving, and it kept the body moving, and every movement it made inside the field was a conductor sweeping through a magnetic flux. The field answered. It drove current through him in eddies that opposed the motion, a brake made of his own substance. The harder he pulled, the harder the field pulled back, and the more current ran through the wet of him, electrons crowding through tissue that resisted them, and every collision gave up its heat. His fingers went first. The water in them reached the boil and the skin over the knuckles lifted in white blisters and split, and steam came off his hands in thin threads, and still the formula told the arms to pull, and the arms pulled, and the field cooked them for it.

Mu-jin made himself watch. Malphas had written that the living who will not look at the dead are the ones to fear. He would not hand the man that.

Across the vault Velder Crace came off his stool.

The revolver came out of his coat in one practised motion, a heavy top-break of blued steel with a five-inch barrel and the Board's proof-mark stamped bright on the frame below the cylinder, and his thumb drew the hammer to full cock with a click that sounded very loud under the crackle. The rounds in the cylinder had the dull grey look of proofed shot, worked lead, the kind Guild elites carried because ordinary shot slid off anything with a working on it. He brought the sights up onto the centre of Mu-jin's chest across twenty feet of floor.

Malphas stepped into the line of fire.

He did it without hurry, one long stride, so that the muzzle was suddenly pointing at the middle of his back. Velder's finger came off the trigger as though the guard had burned it.

"No." Malphas did not turn round. "I will handle this. I have to make an example."

Velder's breath went out of him in a hiss through his teeth. The muzzle drifted down, an inch, and stayed there, ready.

"If you're sure, my lord." His eyes went to the broken grille on the stair, to the slag still greying on the steps. "It would be a great deal easier to finish this before his allies arrive."

---

Malphas looked up at the bear.

It had been a long time since he had seen Mu-jin's bear. Years. He remembered it as large. He remembered it as an animal. What stood in the cold room now was a weather front with a heartbeat, and the grey rack had more tines on it than he had counted at Sum-gol, and the charge coming off the hide stood the fine hairs up along his own forearms in spite of the cold. The fight with Cozbi must have done this to them both. Something in that war had stretched the man and the beast past what they had been. He could read it in the depth of the corona, in the steadiness of the tick, in the calm way the summoner was letting his own senses run late and not flinching from it.

*But you are not the only one who has grown, Teacher.*

He let his aura out.

It did not come up like Mu-jin's had. It barely came up at all. The Pressure that had flattened the lamps and lifted the dust had nothing to answer it, and a man watching only for force would have said Malphas had none. What went out of him was slower, and lower, and patient as a pond. It went into the aether around him and did not push it. It changed it. The way a starter goes into milk, or yeast into dough, or rot into a bundle of flax weighted under water past the hour, it went in and it began to work, and the aether that had been raw and bright around him went dull, and then dark, and then began to be something else.

That had always been the whole of it, the thing the Necrocursica had never once set down in his own name. He could ferment. He could take what was in front of him, the stone and the air and the aether, and work it the way his own gut works a meal, and let it go over.

The ground began to swell.

The flags round his feet lifted and parted along their joints, pushed up from beneath like the crust on a loaf, and a smell came up through the cracks, sulphur and black mud and the bottom of a pond in high summer, the smell of a place where nothing breathes and everything is still busy. The stone under the flags had gone soft. It was dissolving faster than an eye could follow, the lime in it eaten out from between the grains with a faint continuous fizz. What was left went black. It was the black of anoxic silt, of a retting pond's floor, iron and sulphur bonding where there was no air to stop them. It ran out from Malphas across the floor in a widening stain, and it ran under Raga's feet, and under the bear it rose.

It came up as hands.

Four of them, then six, broad as cart wheels and wet and black, shouldering up out of the floor as though the cold room had always had something buried in it that wanted out. In the centre of each palm a cluster of needles had grown, brassy and bright, crystals of iron sulphide pushed out of the black in sprays of fine straight spines, the way frost grows on a pane. The hands closed on the bear's legs and belly from beneath. The needles went in through the hide. And then all six heaved upward together, one slow tremendous push, and the black under them swelled with gas as they did, and the floor of the cold room rose with the push like dough doubling.

---

Mu-jin saw it go and did not wait to feel it.

He let go of what he was holding. Essence went out of him and turned to raw aether on the way, and he drove it straight down into the flags in front of his own feet. The floor broke. Pillars of light came up out of the stone in a ragged line, white and roaring, each one punching up through the spreading black like a pick through ice, and where they rose the black went grey and fell apart into wet ash, and the stink of sulphur was burned out of the air in a hot clean gust.

It was not fast enough. Raga was already leaving the ground.

The bear went upward. Three point eight tonnes of him, lifted by six black hands and a floor that had risen like bread, went up into the vault rack first. The tines struck the ribs and the ribs gave. The vault opened. Dressed stone came down in blocks the size of coffins, and the old charnel yard behind the south range came apart above them, the turf and the low wall and the infirmary's bed of woody rosemary all going up together in a column of earth and broken masonry, and through it, for the first time in an hour, the cold room saw the sky.

Grey light poured down the hole. Grit and rosemary rained back into the vault, a green smell under the dust of broken stone. And the bear was in the open.

Mu-jin felt the change before he understood it, late, the way everything came to him now. The tick in his chest had shifted, longer between beats. Raga was further off. Raga was up. And Raga, who had come into a cellar he should never have been asked to enter, was standing under the open sky at last.

"Shift." Mu-jin's voice cracked on it and held. "Lightning Bolt."

Above the hole the bear's shape came apart.

It did not break. It spread. The black of him went out across the grey sky like ink dropped in water, rising and widening, and the air over the south range went dark. The temperature in the vault fell. Rain began in the hole, sudden and hard, and then hail, small and white and bouncing on the flags, and somewhere above it all the cloud that had been a bear was doing what every storm cell does, tearing its ice apart in its own updraught until the tops held one charge and the bottoms held the other and the gap between them could not bear it. The green motes came down through the rain in thousands. Thunder rumbled somewhere far up, long and rolling, the sound arriving late, as Raga's sound always did.

The leader came down first. It came in faint steps, a branching violet thread feeling its way down the hole through the hail a few yards at a time, looking for the name.

Then the return stroke.

It came up the channel the leader had opened in a single instant. It was the strike the claws had begun, owed and now paid. Aether condensed past anything the eye could hold, a column of plasma brighter than the noon sun and hotter than its surface, thirty thousand amperes and more forced through a channel no wider than a thumb. The air in its path was torn of its electrons and heated past twenty-eight thousand degrees in a few millionths of a second, and it drove down through the broken vault at the man standing in the black.

Malphas lifted his lattice hand.

"Fermented," he said, almost gently, "Star."

The black on the floor came up off the stone and gathered in front of him. All of it, the hands and the stain and the swollen ground, poured inward to one point over his raised palm and folded there into a mass the size of a man's head, and then smaller, and then smaller, denser with every fold, until what hung in the air between Malphas and the falling light was a ball of perfect black. It drank the lamplight. It drank the grey from the hole. It was the colour Malphas had written into his own confession, the bottom of the last pond in the year his father burned the bundle, and it hung there humming half a tone flat.

From its upper surface, a pale thread of light reached upward to meet the bolt, the way the iron of a church spire reaches when a storm stands over it.

The lightning found it.

For one instant the cold room held two suns. One was white and too bright to look at, and the other had no colour at all, and they touched.

The thunder had not reached them yet.

# The Dead Note

---

*The Sum-gol Post, at the end of the Kessen road, the same evening; the hour the last crows come in*

The kettle was boiling on the brazier, *bogeul-bogeul*, and Hiromi watched the steam go up off it and lie along the underside of the rafters, where the crows sat.

There were always crows at the Post. There were forty of them tonight, black on the black beams, shifting their feet and muttering to each other in the low creaky way crows talk when they have come a long way, *kkaak, kkak*, and each had a little brass tube on its leg and a ring of olive thread above it, the Post's color. Below them the long room ran the length of the house: the sorting tables where the couriers had stood all day under the lamps with their sleeves rolled, the pigeonholes up the whole of the back wall, a thousand little square mouths of dark wood each with a name painted over it in white, and the big doors at the end standing open on the evening. Out past the doors the last of the light was going off the salt pans below the house. In the dusk they looked like a field of broken mirrors laid out flat in the valley, pink and then copper and then gray as the sky went over them, and beyond them the iron hills stood up rust-red against the dark, and down the valley the rice terraces held the last of the sky in their water like a stair of lamps. It was the most beautiful thing Hiromi knew. It was also, he understood now, very small. A valley. A post. Forty crows.

His uncle poured the tea.

He did it the way he did everything at a table, as if it were the most important thing that would happen that day: the pot lifted high so the tea ran out in a long amber thread and fell into the cup without a splash, *jol-jol-jol*, and the cup turned a quarter so the glaze-mark faced the guest, and set down. Hiromi was the guest. He was always the guest at his uncle's table, even when it was only the two of them and the crows. His uncle said a host who forgets to be a host when nobody is watching has forgotten what a host is for.

"Drink," said Cozbi Mahuo. "You've been in the yard since noon. My mother would have my ears for letting you go thirsty." He smiled across the cup. "She's watching, you know. She's fond of this room."

Hiromi drank.

He looked at his uncle over the rim of the cup while he did it, because he had been looking at his uncle all autumn, when his uncle was not looking at him. He was twenty-five. Hiromi knew that because the couriers said it as if it were a thing to be amazed at, the Post-Master, twenty-five, and him what he is. His hair was black and fell ragged over his brow, and where the lamplight caught it it went green as a mallard's head. His coat was the olive of the Post, long and pleated and perfect, the great white fur over his shoulders, and the gold at his ear. He looked, sitting at the low table with the tea, like a lord in a painting. He was the most powerful man Hiromi had ever heard of. The couriers said that too, more quietly, and then looked over their shoulders.

He was also the thinnest man in the house.

Hiromi had watched it happen. In the spring his uncle had eaten at the long table with the couriers and laughed loudest of anyone at their jokes. By midsummer he ate alone, late, a little rice and broth, and left most of it. Now he did not eat at all that Hiromi could see. The fine coat that fitted him exactly had been taken in twice by the seamstress in the village; Hiromi had seen her come with her pins. His face had gone to bones under the skin, sharp at the cheek and the jaw, and the skin itself had a cast to it now, gray-green, like a pond in shade, and under his eyes it was nearly black. He did not sleep. Hiromi knew that because he did not sleep much either, and when he got up in the night to use the pot he could see the light under his uncle's door, always, and sometimes hear him talking in there to somebody who did not answer.

And there were the threads.

Most people could not see them. Hiromi could, a little, because he was a Mahuo and the blood is the blood, his uncle said. They ran out of his uncle's chest, very fine, black-violet, like the threads a spider lets out into the wind, dozens of them, more than dozens, going off through the walls of the house in every direction to wherever they went. When his uncle was tired they shook. Tonight they were shaking all the time, and Hiromi could see where they came out of him, and it looked like it hurt.

His uncle had taken him in when there was nobody else to take him in. That was the plain fact under everything. Hiromi had been eight, and the war had come down the valley, and his mother had gone into the house to get the baby and the roof had come down after her, and there had been a long time after that which he did not think about. And then his uncle had come up the road in the olive coat, very young, with the dead walking behind him like an honor guard, and had gone down on one knee in the mud in front of Hiromi and put his hand on his head and said nothing at all for a long time. Then he had said, *you're mine now, and I'm yours*, and taken him home. He had given him tea when he was thirsty and a bed that did not have soldiers in it and a seat at the sorting tables, and he had given him hope, which Hiromi had not known was a thing a person could give another person, and which there had been very little of anywhere in the valley that year.

But his uncle had lost things too. Hiromi did not know all of it. He knew his uncle had a wife once, and two children, a boy and a girl, and that he talked about them in the present tense. He knew his uncle's mother was dead, and that she was fond of this room. He knew Hiromi's own mother had been his uncle's sister, and that his uncle had come up the road too late for her by a single night, and that he had never once said so, and that it was why he had gone down on one knee in the mud.

Hiromi was eleven. He did not have words for what was in his chest. He had looked for them all autumn and not found them. Anger was in it, at the soldiers and the roof and the war and the houses whose banners had come down the valley, and grief, which was heavier, and something else under both that made him want to hit the practice post in the yard until his knuckles bled, which he had been doing since noon.

He wanted to be stronger. He knew that much.

He put the cup down.

"Samchon," he said, the old word, uncle-on-my-mother's-side. Then he stopped.

His uncle waited. His uncle was very good at waiting. He had a way of waiting that made you feel he would wait all night if you needed him to, and enjoy it.

"Do you think," said Hiromi, and his voice came out smaller than he wanted, "I could go and train with the Yukari?"

The crows went quiet in the rafters.

The quiet was short, and his uncle did not move, and his face did not change at all. But the threads coming out of his chest went still. All of them at once, the way a spider's lines go still when something lands on the web. And the lamp on the sorting table nearest them leaned, very slightly, toward a place in the air beside his uncle's shoulder and away from it at the same time, which Hiromi had seen happen only once before, the day a man came up the road from the Accord with a paper, and the man had gone back down the road faster than he came.

The air tasted of struck flint.

"What," said Cozbi Mahuo, "do you seek to learn that you cannot learn from me."

He did not make it a question. Hiromi heard the place where the question should have been, and it was not there, and it frightened him more than shouting would have.

His uncle heard it too. Hiromi saw him hear it. Something went over the thin face, quick, a man catching his own foot on a stair, and the lamp straightened, and the threads began to tremble again, and his uncle closed his eyes for a moment, and opened them.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I didn't mean it to come out like that." He turned his own cup a quarter on the table and looked at it. "I know it's been hard here lately. I know you've seen it. The revenue from the east hasn't come since the summer, and the couriers haven't been paid in a month, and they come anyway, because they're good people and because they're frightened of me. We're barely holding on, Hiromi. I won't lie to you about that. I don't lie at my own table." He looked up. "But we can't give up our honor to hold on. And we can't give up control. Not to anyone. Not to the Yukari." He said the name gently, the way you set down something hot. "They tell the story of how they killed this house themselves. They tell it well. Their lord offered me Sum-gol back, this spring, as a present, and asked only that every touch of the dead in the south go through his counsel. As if the dead were his to counsel on." The gold eye was steady. "We are summoners, Hiromi. That's what the Mahuo are. We call, and it answers. We're supposed to call things that bring people what they need: a letter from a son at the front, a mother's voice at the door one last time, a soul that's lost its way, carried home. We make people whole. That's the whole of the art, under everything. If I send you to learn it from the people who broke the world's soul in the first place, what do you think they'll teach you to call?"

Hiromi understood him. He understood every word. He did not know how to make his uncle understand him back. All the anger was in his chest and all the grief and the thing under them, and none of it had words on it, and his uncle had all the words.

"But Samchon," he said. "You're breaking."

He said it before he could stop it. He watched it land.

"You're breaking," he said again, because now it was out he could not put it back. "You don't eat. You don't sleep. You talk to people in your room at night and they don't talk back. The threads are coming out of you all the time now and they shake and it *hurts* you, I can see it hurt you." His eyes were stinging and he hated that they were. *Huljjeok.* He wiped his nose on his sleeve, which his uncle did not like. "You're trying to do all of it by yourself. The Post and the dead and the house and me. All of it. And there's nobody else. There's just me. And I can't do anything."

His uncle looked at him for a long time.

Then he reached across the low table, past the teapot, and put his hand on top of Hiromi's head.

It was a long cool hand, too thin, and it lay there the way it had lain on his head in the mud three years ago, and his uncle did not say anything for a while. The crows shifted on the beams. Out past the doors the salt pans had gone dark, and the first lamps were coming on in the village below, small and yellow and far apart.

"You're all I have left," said Cozbi Mahuo.

He said it quietly. He said it in the present tense, the way he said everything about the living he meant to keep, and Hiromi did not know yet that his uncle had a past tense he used for people he had decided would die, or that the couriers listened for it. He only heard that his uncle's voice had a crack in it, very small, and that his uncle, who had all the words, had used only six.

And Cozbi knew. Hiromi saw him know it. The boy had already decided, the way his mother had always already decided, and was only asking to be polite.

Hiromi put his own hand up over his uncle's on his head, and held it there.

"I have to get stronger, Samchon," he said. "I have to. If I don't..." His throat closed and he made it open. "If I don't, then who'll protect you? When you're too tired to protect yourself?"

His uncle's hand did not move.

After a while, he laughed. His big laugh, the one he used on the couriers and the Accord men, filled the whole long room; this one was small and surprised and it came out of him like a cough. He took his hand back off Hiromi's head and looked at it, the way a man looks at something he did not know he was holding.

"My sister," he said, "would like you very much." He picked up the pot, and poured them both another cup, high, so the tea fell in a long amber thread. "Drink your tea. I'll write to the Cedar Enclave in the morning." The gold eye glinted. "By my own post. They'll have to pay the carriage."

*The cedar wood on the Kessen road, inside the dome of ABI; perhaps forty seconds before it falls*

Muken would not go.

He looked at Hayato's hand, the black glove held out to him with its gold bangles, the fox mask over the face smiling its narrow gilt smile, and the gray eyes behind the eyeholes wet, and he wanted to take it more than he had ever wanted anything. *Two days and a road, sire.* The Seat gate. Home. The tower, and the split bed, and the cold tea, and Godfrey on the stair.

The Eye showed him the road behind him. It also showed him his aunt, thrown off the Sin of Wrath's back a moment before into the slush, rolling, coming up on one knee with her hands burned black and her harness smoking and her face bloody, already getting up again; and the copper lord at the foot of the bank with his good hand flat on his chest and his lips blue, holding every heart in the oven to his own; and Lorn over his sword, white with rime from the beard to the knees and shaking now, all over, the cold coming back into him; and Amelia, in front, her red-brown and gold coming up off her shoulders and her heels sunk in the road, her shield up, her pauldron with its hairline in the second lame under the fur. The Eye showed him where every one of them was going to break. It was soon.

He had let go of a hand on a road once. Lorn's arms round him on the ice, and the boy fighting to go after his sister, and the boy losing, and the white hair coming back down the Marrow on a pike.

He had been told, then, that there was nothing he could have done. He had believed it for six months. He did not believe it now. He knew exactly what he could have done. He could have not let go.

"No, Hayato," he said. Low, slow, each word set on the last. The steam in the oven went still around him in a ring. "I'm sorry. I'll not."

Hayato's hand stayed out a moment longer. Then it closed, and dropped, and the fox mask bowed its head to him, once, and Hayato said, very softly, "Then I'll keep the road open, sire. For when you change your mind."

The Sin of Wrath came.

He had the Byakuya katana. Muken saw it as he came: when he had torn the Owl off his back and thrown her he had taken the blade out of its white scabbard at her hip as she went, the way a man takes a cup off a passing tray, and he was carrying it now in his right hand as if he had carried it all his life. It was a headsman's grip. Muken did not know that then; he learned it later. The hand low on the handle, the blade back along the forearm, the point down. He came through the oven toward the king with the Owl's sword in his fist and the Owl's blood on it, and the old steel of it, three hundred and twelve years of Borin Ironheart's work, was going white along its edge in his hand like a bar coming out of a forge.

[a hanging judge, roaring now, the iron dragged on the foundry floor and the furnace under it] "*Kōkan!*"

Muken let his left foot fall.

It was the stance Yusif had given him on the gallery floor at eight, and the Lanternless had worn it into him, and nothing in the Plane of Fate had taken it out: the left foot back and turned, the weight sinking through the heel, the right foot light, the sword coming up from the low guard in both hands. He brought Tsugigane up. The blade was already ringing. It had been ringing since the bank, a single held note, a bell struck once and held, felt in the back of the teeth before it was heard, and now as he raised it he put the word on it.

"*Fure.*"

*Shake.* The word the Kōkan used before it was taken from the line in the cellars, and Faisal gave it back.

HIBI woke in the steel like a held breath let go. The ring went up from the blade into his hands, his arms, his teeth, and Fractura and the Eye went with it, and they found what the ring was for. One thing before him. One crack. He had already chosen it. He had chosen it on the bank. The gold line ran out of his eye across the oven to the back of the Sin of Wrath's neck, where the block had been, and the note in Tsugigane went down, and down, past the note of the Byakuya steel coming at him, past the note of the Hall Irons, down to the note of a crack.

And he heard it.

He had heard dead notes before. In salt faces with rot gone right through them; in the ward-stone over the Seat's gate, lying, from half a mile off on the Marrow ice. A dead crack rings sour against the steady note of the sound thing round it, wavering, the way a cracked bell does. This was that. This was the sourest note he had ever heard. It was the note of a thing that should have ended three hundred years ago at the bottom of a flight of steps in front of seven chairs, and had not, and had been held open ever since, unfinished, the way a door is held open by a man's foot. A death that had been done to a man and never been allowed to finish. It was dead. It had been dead all along. It only had not been told.

*A thing that is already dead may be ended.* His sister, in his head, with her hands on a wound. *And only that.*

He had sworn it. By his honor, before Faisal. Nothing that was not already dead.

This was.

The two blades met.

They met above his head, Tsugigane coming up and the Byakuya katana coming down in the Sin of Wrath's fist in a headsman's stroke, the long straight vertical cut that closes the register, the one stroke a Kurenai is allowed and never a second. Muken saw it coming down at the blade and not at him, at Tsugigane, at the silver thread in its join where his sister's needles were folded into the steel. The Eye saw it in the shoulder and the hips before the edge arrived: the Sin of Wrath meant to break the sword in the boy's hands first, and leave the boy. Then the boy's friends. Then the boy. One at a time, in front of him, the way they had made him watch.

The edges met.

***BWOOOOOOOOOOOOM.***

The world bent.

To Muken it was like standing inside a bell when it is struck. The two blades met at the top of Muken's guard and the force of the Sin's stroke came down through the Byakuya steel into Tsugigane and through Tsugigane into Muken's hands and his wrists and his forearms and his shoulders, all of it, more weight than had ever been put on him, more than the Lawbell, more than the gallery ceiling at Orrel that came down on his father's crew when he was ten, and he did not stop it. He could not have stopped it. He did what Faisal had taught him kneeling under the Bell: he planted both feet and became the wall, SHIGOTO NO KABE, and BARANSU NO SHIGOTO opened a road through his body for the weight to go, down the long bones of his arms and his spine and his legs and out through the soles of his boots into the frozen road, and the road took it.

The road went down. It went down under him in a bowl three paces wide, the frozen earth and the stones and the slush all crushed into a smooth-sided crater in an instant with Muken standing on the bottom of it, sunk to the shin. The ring of frost round them blew outward into fine glittering dust. The steam in the oven was flung away from the two of them in a single expanding sphere, and for a breath the air was clear and Muken could see every burning cedar in the wood standing up out of the dark like candles. He felt both his forearms crack short of breaking, the long bones taking a hairline each from the wrist to the elbow, the way the cost of the wall always took him, a little, every time; and something in his left knee went with a wet pop. Blood came out of his nose in two lines and ran into his mouth.

And the air round them warped.

The Eye could see it, and so could everyone. Where the two blades crossed above his head the light bent, as if the air there had gone to glass and the glass had been struck; the burning cedars behind the Sin of Wrath went crooked in it, wavering, and the red dome of the oven over them showed for an instant through the place where the swords met as if through water. The ring had gone into it. HIBI's note, the one note, the dead one, was pouring out of Tsugigane into the bind, into the white-hot Byakuya steel, up the Sin of Wrath's arm, through the Hall Irons and the long wire bones of his forearm and his shoulder, along his collarbone, up the back of his neck, to the place where the block had been. Muken could feel it go. He could feel it arrive. A crack in a salt face with the weight piled at its tip. He held the note, in the breath, in through the nose until his ribs ached, and the weight that had gathered in the dead crack for three hundred years did what weight does when it finds the way.

It carried the crack forward.

The Sin of Wrath's head jerked. Muken saw it, a hand's width from his own face across the crossed blades, through the bending air: the head snapping forward on the neck as if somebody had struck him at the nape with the flat of an axe, the living eye going wide. And at the back of his neck, above the black braids, where the vertebrae of a man's spine meet his skull and a headsman's blade falls between them, the crazed glaze of his skin split. It opened in a single straight line from one side of the neck to the other, as clean as a cut, and from it came blood.

It did not hiss. ENSHŌ did not take it. It was not a wound anything had done to him with force, and so there was nothing in it for ENSHŌ to swallow. It was the oldest wound on him opening, and it bled like any man's would, dark red and running, down the back of his neck and into the collar of the black coat.

Amelia went backward.

The wall had taken the weight into the road, and what the road could not take went outward, through the air, and the air was full of people. Muken heard it hit her before he saw it: a long ringing clang off the face of her shield like a bell dropped down a stair, *GWAAANG*, and then the scream of the shield's rim being dragged backward across the frozen road. STONEFAST held her. Her heels did not leave the ground. The ground left with her. She went back across the road on her set heels, the earth plowing up in front of them in two long furrows, the shield up, the axe in her ruined hand, her teeth bared, five paces, eight, and stopped against the fallen trunk of a cedar on the far verge with her whole body still locked into one column from the shield-boss to the heel, and the trunk splintered behind her, and she did not go down. The fur on her mantle was on fire. She did not seem to notice.

Muken stood in the bottom of his crater with the Sin of Wrath's stroke held above his head on a cracked sword in cracked arms, and the blood running into his mouth, and the dead note pouring out of the blade, and he said it. He did not know he was going to say it. It came up out of him in the gallery voice and the king's voice at once, low, slow, the stones going on the cairn one after another, and every burning branch in the wood swung at once, all together, as if a wind had gone through the oven, though no wind could get in.

[young northern Kharven, a cutter's vowels, low and cracked and enormous] "I won't abandon them." *Stone.* "The people I love." *Stone.* "I won't." *Stone.* His forearms creaked under the weight. "I can't do it again. I let her go on a road. I'll not do it again. Not for you. Not for anybody."

The Sin of Wrath grinned at him.

It was a terrible thing to see that close. The long scarred mouth opened, and there was blood in it, running over the bottom lip and down the blade's-spine jaw in a dark sheet, his own blood, coming up from the scalded lung or from somewhere deeper; and the teeth were red with it; and the grin went wide, and wider, and the living eye over it blazed with the ring-lines spinning and spinning and full of red light. He did not let go of the stroke. The weight did not lift. It went on coming down through the Owl's white-hot sword into Tsugigane, a long slow grinding pressure now, testing, the way a man leans on a door to see where it will give. And with the blood at the back of his neck running down into his collar and the dead note in his bones, he laughed. It came up out of him over the furnace roar, a great cracked shout of a laugh, and the blood came out with it in a spray that hissed where it hit Tsugigane's ringing steel.

[a hanging judge who has thrown away the book; the furnace and the iron and the foundry floor all roaring together] "*YES.* Yes! *That!* Show me that, Kōkan! Show me that *anger!* That *wrath!*" The weight came down harder, and the crater under Muken's feet sank another inch, *grrrnk*, and his knee screamed. "Show me who the true king of the north is! Show me what your blood can do! The true testament of your Kōkan power, the red chair, the red line, the eye your fathers read my wife's death with! *Show me!*" He leaned in over the crossed blades until his face was a hand's width from the boy's and the heat off it blistered Muken's lips. "And then I will take it. All of it. Your hope. Your dreams. Every person you love and every person who loves you, one by one, in front of you, the way your house did it to me. I'll take everything you admire in the world and I'll *burn it in front of your face,* boy, while you stand there with your hands open, the way your family took my wife from me all those years ago!" The laugh went to a roar. "*All those years agoooooooo!*"

The roar went out across the oven and the burning cedars bent away from it.

And inside it, Muken heard the other thing. The Eye heard it. The ring in the blade heard it. Under the furnace roar and the laughter and the hate, the dead note at the back of the Sin of Wrath's neck was ringing in time with the shout, sour and wavering and loud, louder with every word, the crack running a hair further with every breath he roared, as if every word of the hate were weight piled on it. And under that, fainter, a thing Muken had never heard in any crack in any salt face in his life: a second note. A living one. Very small. Held down under all the rest like a hand under water.

He felt the rage come up in himself to meet the Sin's.

It came up out of the crack under his own breastbone, the one Faisal had told him about, where his sister had been, the way it had come up in the Foot's yard the day the white hair came down the river: hot and huge and glad of itself, and with it, felt in the long bones and the back of the teeth, the thing under HIBI that he had been forbidden. HIBIWARE. The Crazing. The single crack becoming a web, the blade ringing at the note of everything around him at once, every hairline flaw in everything the rings passed through. It would go through the Sin of Wrath like a hammer through glaze. Faisal had told him what else it would go through. Amelia's shield and her hairline pauldron and her shoulder under it. Lorn's mail and his frozen bones. His aunt's harness, the shard-hole in her cheek. The copper lord's broken rib. Hayato, who had never once been hit hard, and must not be. Every crack in every one of them, the Eye had shown him every one, running all at once.

The Sin of Wrath wanted him to. Muken could see that too, now, in the grin, in the eye. He wanted the boy to do it. He wanted the boy to become the thing that had been done to him.

Behind the mask, Hayato's voice, very quiet, from the edge of the road: "Twenty seconds, sire. Then it falls."

The note held in Tsugigane. The weight held on it. The rage stood up in Muken like a man getting to his feet, and the Eye showed him every crack in the world, and the dead one at the back of the Sin's neck, ringing.

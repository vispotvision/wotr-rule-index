# The Drawing-Off

*A buyer from the rim pays salt to beat the hour;*
*a cooper of the deep hall Wells beneath the stack.*

> CONTRACT · VARRAK-THUR CASK-HALL · ENTERED
> Forty kaas of ollut, drawn off the lees and sealed, to the shaft-lift by the third bell, Guild hour.
> Buyer: Spigot and Daughters, brewers, the rim.
> Pay: twenty copper the shift to the night crew. Hold weight in the Tally, Accord weight on the wire.
> Penalty: one salt bar for each kaas cracked. One shift's pay docked for each quarter-hour past the bell.
> The angles are true. The entry holds.

It was the Drawing-Off, the one week in the year when the deep halls sent up what they had kept, and Kalrin had been asleep on top of the stack since the second bell.

He woke the way he always woke up there, by the cask under his cheek. A working brew talks. New beer in a kaas spits and grumbles like a forge-boy told to sweep, but ollut that has lain forty years in the deep hall only ticks now and then in the stave and lets a long breath out through the bung, and a man who has slept on it for three centuries hears the difference in his sleep. This one had ticked twice. The lamp nearest his head had burned down to a blue bead in the oil. Below him somebody laughed.

"Snorin," said a voice in Common, pleased with itself. "Up you get, Snorin. There's money moving."

The Accord men had made the word out of his name in his first year on the lift, and it had stuck because it was accurate. Kalrin had looked it up once in a Concord grammar at the Seat. It was the noise a sleeper makes. The buyer said it the way Concord men said everything, in no grade at all, every consonant soft as wet bread, so that a Dawi listening could not have told whether the name had been honoured or broken. Voiced. Long ago Kalrin had settled that a word with no grade in it was no insult, and he sat up.

The cask-hall of Varrak-Thur ran two hundred paces into the rock under the Seat, a long vault cut in the old way, the roof left ribbed wherever a seam had run, so that the ceiling was a map of everything the clan had taken out of the mountain. Ironwood racks stood along both walls, black with age and oil. The squat brass frog-lamps hung on their hooks every ten paces and made pools of light that did not meet, and between the pools the staves of the racked casks went from amber to brown to a dark like old lacquer, and the dark held the shape of each cask the way a hand holds a shape it has carried for years. The air was yeast and wet oak and the resin-tar of the pitch-pot, which was spitting on its brazier by the door. Under all of it lay the draw-hum. The Guild main came down the left wall in a sealed conduit as thick as a man's thigh, mortared into the rock through a collar of brass, and the hum in it was too low for the ear. A man felt it in his back teeth. At the far end the shaft-lift waited in its cage. Beside it stood the gauge-housing under the Guild's seal, the needle resting behind the glass. The lintel rune over the stair door had been touched by so many hands on the way in that its edges were worn round at the height of a palm.

The stack stood in the middle of the floor where the night crew had built it: forty kaas of ollut in four tiers of ten, chocked at every end with ironwood wedges, drawn off and waiting to be sealed. Kalrin slid down the end of it the way he had slid down it for three hundred years and landed on his heels.

"Thirty-one to seal," said Valtrin from the croze-bench, without looking up. Valtrin's byname on the lift was Two-Hoops, for the night in his apprenticeship when he had driven both hoops onto the one cask and sworn it was a new style. "Halrin's at the driver. The buyer wants them up by the bell."

The buyer was a long Concord man in a buff coat, who had come down the shaft holding his hat on with both hands and had not yet taken it off. His name was Obadiah Spigot, of Spigot and Daughters, brewers on the rim. Earlier in the night Valtrin had asked him, with a straight face, whether the daughters were also Spigots, and he had said yes, and the crew had needed to go and look at something on the far racks for a while.

Down at the lift end the goblin carter sat on the cage step with his tin crib beside him, unopened. Gorrgorr was all elbows and ears and had loaded every kaas the hall sold up the shaft for eleven years. His name was Old Vaross for *load-load*. The crew had laughed at it for eleven years. He had let them, because he was the best carter on the lift and knew it.

Kalrin took the driver from Halrin and went to work.

You draw off ollut by the bung, once, and you taste it from the thief, the long glass pipe you sink into the cask and stop with a thumb. Kalrin drew a thief's length from the first of the thirty-one and held it to the lamp. Amber gone toward brown, clear as hold water, the lees left behind on the cask floor where they belonged. He tasted it. Deep, with an edge, the edge the clan said came from the rock and the Concord men said came from neglect. Then the head went back on. You set it in the croze, the groove Valtrin had cut at the stave ends, and you seat it with the flat of your hand, and then you drive the hoops. Set the driver on the hoop. Strike. Turn the cask a hand's width. Strike. The hoop walks down the staves a little at every blow, and the staves come together under it, and there is a moment, if the cooper is any good and the angles are true, when the last gap closes and the joints between the staves become lines you could not slide a hair into. Then the cask rings under the knuckle with one note. It is the same note a burin makes when it finishes a rune cut at the true angle, and every Dawi who has ever stood in a forge knows it.

Kalrin knocked the cask and heard it.

"Nikkat on," said Valtrin.

Halrin brought the pitch-pot. The Cask-Oath Pitch went over the chime in a black ribbon from the ladle, and Kalrin sang the oath over it in the fixed metre while it was still soft, as the hold had sung it over every kaas since there had been a hold: *Ollu elaa, kaasme elaa. Ollu kuolee, ei kaasme kuole.* The brew lives, the oath lives. The brew dies, and the oath does not die. The pitch dulled as he sang. By the time he had finished, it had cured.

"I'll take a hundredweight of that up with me," the buyer said. He had come close to watch, and he put a finger to the chime, and it came away clean. "Pitch is pitch. I will not pay hold prices for it on the rim, mind. I will find you a man in the Ring who boils the same for half."

"It cures here," Kalrin said.

"It will cure anywhere, if it's warm enough."

Kalrin set the driver on the next hoop and struck. He did not explain. Pitch that would not cure was a broken oath, and when a batch failed the clerks audited the name-roll before they so much as looked at the barrels, and a Concord man who carried a hundredweight of Varrak-Thur pitch up to the rim would find out on the rim what it was for. Strike. Turn. Strike.

He had sealed nine when the buyer took out his watch and went to stand by the gauge-housing.

The gauge-man was an Accord tradesman named Ned Halfturn, who had been given the lift because he was the one Guild man in the Seat that the Dawi could stand, and Kalrin watched the buyer talk to him with his hat still on. Spigot held up the watch. Halfturn shook his head. Spigot said something more, and two stamped bars went from the pocket of the buff coat into the gauge-man's apron, Nalūn salt with the assay mark on each end, a silver mark apiece. It was about half what the whole night crew would draw for the shift. Halfturn looked at the needle a long time. Then he put his hand on the valve wheel and left it there.

Kalrin was no clerk. He entered it anyway, the way every Dawi enters what he sees: the bars, the hand, the wheel.

Borin and Torin came down the stair together at the eighteenth cask.

Kalrin knew them by the hall before he knew them by the door. The pitch-pot stopped spitting. The lamp flames, which had been leaning a little toward the shaft where the draught ran, came upright in their frogs, and the hall was warmer by a coat. It always went so when Borin came in folded down, the way a forge banked for the night still warms the room. Both touched the lintel rune on the way in, Borin first.

Borin had a new burn across the back of his left hand, glazed and pink. It came from eleven days at the anvil for a customer upstairs who had not yet paid. Otherwise he was what he always was: the beard in its three ringed trunks, the hammer under his arm that he called his walking stick, and the face of a man about to tell a joke worse than the last one.

Torin was the eldest of the three of them, and Kalrin had looked at him for four hundred years the way a cooper looks at any timber, by how it carries. Torin carried like a loaded post. His weight went straight down through his heels and none of it was held in reserve and none of it was wasted. He had the reeve's hands, broad and short, the second finger of the right one stained dark to the knuckle with Tally ink, and Soul-Runes ridged both forearms from wrist to elbow so thickly that a fortune-teller at the Ring gates would have charged double to read him. His beard carried more rings than Borin's and they were plainer, iron to Borin's adamant. The face was Borin's face with the laugh lines left out. He wore the reeve's grey, long and belted, the two-column ledger under one arm and the angle-set hanging from his belt on a thong, and when he stood still nothing on him moved at all.

"Master Spigot," Borin said, in Common, and spread his arms as if the buyer were a cask he meant to lift. "Do you know why the rim sends a spigot to buy our beer?"

The buyer, to his credit, smiled. "I can't say I do."

Borin was already laughing. He laughed through the whole of the next line, so that it came out in pieces. "Because a bung," he said, "would have kept its mouth shut."

Torin did not laugh. Torin had not laughed at one of Borin's jokes in three hundred years. On the end post of the nearest rack, at the height of a cooper's eye, the crew kept a column of short strokes cut with a knife, one for every joke Borin had told in the cask-hall with Torin present, and the column ran from the post's foot to its top and over onto the next post, and nobody had ever needed to cross one out.

Valtrin took out his knife and cut another.

"Kalrin's still on the stack, then," Borin said, to the buyer, with great satisfaction. "My little brother. Three hundred years at Murmuring with a back that would hold up this roof, and he has never once gone near the main. Ask him to put his hand to a current and he says kurlo, every time. Unbegun." He clapped Kalrin on the shoulder, and Kalrin's knees bent with it. "Three hundred and seventy-eight, I am. He could be a hundred by now if he would only get up off the beer."

"Some of us sleep on it," Kalrin said, "so that some of us have something to sell."

The third bell came down the wire.

It came into the gauge-house as a click and a small bell under the glass, Guild hour from Stannvaard, and Ned Halfturn turned the valve wheel through two full turns, which was one more than the plate on the housing allowed. The cage jerked and began to climb with the first nine kaas on it. The draw-hum came up into the hearing for the first time that night, a note like a wet finger on a glass rim, and climbed.

Kalrin was at the lift end of the stack with a chock in his hand.

The brass collar where the main entered the wall began to sing along with the hum, a half-tone above it, then a whole tone. Torin turned his head. Borin stopped smiling. The seal on the collar went with a crack like a stave splitting in hard frost, and something came out of the wall with it that the eye could not keep hold of, a shimmer like heat over a forge-bed, with a grain in it, and it went down into the floor.

The floor took it.

Kalrin knew the stone of the Ring the way he knew his own hands, and he knew what the stone of the Ring did. It grew to meet its load. It was why the galleries above them stood in rock that should have come down six thousand years ago, because the current in it had made the stone stronger than the mountain on its back. Now the current had come out all at once, and the flags under the racks took a load they did not have and grew to meet it anyway. They rose along their joints by the width of a finger. The ironwood feet of the stack went with them, unevenly. The stack leaned.

It leaned toward the lift. Thirty-one kaas still to seal, with nine already gone up in the cage, so there were thirty-one sitting on that floor, and the lean went through all four tiers like a word going down a crew.

Kalrin put his back to the second tier.

There was nothing else in the hall that was not moving except the wall where the main came in, so he put his left palm flat on the stone beside the burst collar, the only fixed thing he could reach. The current was there. He felt it before he understood it.

"Kurme," he said, which was the whole of the oath, and the only word his people had for what he had just done.

It came in through the palm with a direction to it, the way grain runs in oak. It went up the arm into the shoulder and down into the back where the weight was, and wherever it found weight, it set. Kalrin felt his spine stop being a spine. His knees, which had begun to shake, locked one after the other with a sound like a chock driven home. The weight of the kaas came onto him and he did not bend under it, because there was no longer anything in him that could. His palm on the wall grew hot, then still. The skin of it went tight, the way pitch goes tight in the last moment before it cures.

At the far end of the top tier the last kaas jumped its chock.

He saw it go. It rolled the length of the tier, quite slowly, ticking over the joints between its neighbours, and at the end it went off the edge and fell the height of four kaas onto the lift step. Gorrgorr had just opened his crib.

Borin let go of whatever he had been holding folded.

The hall went first. The shimmer across the floor stopped spreading and lay down flat. Every lamp flame stood straight up in its frog and stayed there, held as if a hand had closed on it. The pitch-pot stopped boiling between one bubble and the next. Then the hall went cold, fast, the way the Forge went cold when someone had touched a thing Borin had told them not to touch, and every breath in the vault came out white at once and hung there. After that, the knees. Obadiah Spigot sat down in a spreading pool of ollut without deciding to. Ned Halfturn fell against his gauge and was sick on the glass. The crew, who had grown up under Borin, only sweated, and the sweat froze in their beards.

Borin crossed the hall in four strides and put one hand flat on the stack above Kalrin's head, and the weight went out of Kalrin's back as if someone had lifted the mountain off it.

The current let go of him a moment later. His knees came unlocked and he sat down hard against the second tier. His left hand stayed where it was on the wall until he pulled it away, and when it came the skin came with a sound like a page torn out of a ledger. The shaking arrived then, late, from the heels up, and his teeth went with it.

The line came when the shaking reached his jaw.

[Stage II, Welling. Apprentice.]
[Grade ceiling D. Level ceiling 100.]

It stood behind his breastbone in no voice at all, plain as a rune cut in stone, and it had nothing to say about his hand.

"Snorin's Welled," said Valtrin.

Then the whole crew was saying it, down the racks, one mouth to the next. "Welled." "At the stack." "Three hundred years, and at the stack." "Bare hand on the main." "He said kurme, I heard him say it." "Kalrin." Somebody said his name in the strong grade, the consonant doubled, the way it was said over a completed act, and the sound of it went down the vault and came back off the far end.

It stopped there, because Borin had gone down the hall to the lift step.

He lifted the end kaas off the step with one hand and set it aside on its bilge, gently, as if it were still worth something. The cask had come down chime first. It had staved Gorrgorr's chest the way a dropped driver staves a cask head, the ribs gone in on the left side to the spine, and the ollut from the split staves had run over him and into the gap under the cage, and the blood in it had gone black in the lamplight. His eyes were open. One hand still held the lid of his crib.

Halrin, who had laughed loudest all night, said his name. "Gorrgorr."

Nobody laughed. Somebody further down the racks said it again, quietly, in Old Vaross, the way his mother would have said it. Load-load. Then nobody said anything, and Borin stood over the carter with his breath going up white, and the hall stayed cold.

Kalrin got up. It took two tries. He went down the hall past the leaning stack, past the buyer sitting in the beer and the gauge-man wiping his mouth. He sat down on the lift step on the other side of the crib.

The crib was a pastry case of meat and root in a dented tin, as every carter on every shaft in Stannvaard carried down at the start of a shift. Gorrgorr had pressed his mark into the corner of the crust before it went in the oven, so that he would get his own back at the break: three short strokes side by side, a goblin's tally of one. The crust was whole. The lid had come off in his hand.

Borin came back and crouched in front of Kalrin and took his left hand and turned it to the nearest lamp. The palm had a grain in it now, fine and grey, like hone-stone, and it would not close.

"Welling," Borin said. "Petralon, by the grain. The stone law went into you because you were the thing holding." He pressed his thumb into the centre of the palm, and Kalrin felt it as pressure only, through a thickness that had not been there at the second bell. "Four days. It softens in four days, or it does not soften."

He did not say whose hands he had last said that over. He did not let go of Kalrin's hand for some time, either, and the hall did not get any warmer while he held it.

Behind them the buyer had found his feet. "The contract," he said. His voice shook and he steadied it. "I'm sorry for your man. Truly. But the contract is entered, and there are twelve kaas cracked on that floor, by my count, which is twelve bars against the hold by the penalty, and the quarter-hours besides."

Torin had already opened the ledger on the end of the stack. He did not look up. He entered the cracked kaas against the hold, all twelve, and the quarter-hours, and blotted them. Then he wrote again, and read it aloud as he wrote, as a reeve does when an entry is to be witnessed. "Two salt bars, buyer to gauge-man, at the second bell, for the double draw. To the buyer."

"That was a private arrangement," Spigot said.

"Entered."

The buyer looked at the goblin on the step, and at Borin, and at his own wet coat. "Then let me pay his people," he said. "Whatever is right. Name it. Ten bars."

"A life is not settled by any other life," Torin said. "Still less by salt." He closed the ledger on his finger to keep the place. "Kurlo. Ei kurme."

Unbegun. Not undertaken. It was the answer that ended a negotiation, and it ended this one. The buyer stood in the cold with his hat in his hand at last and did not ask what a life was settled by instead.

Borin opened his mouth, as if something had occurred to him that might be said to make the hall bearable, and for once nothing came. He shut it again.

Kalrin sat on the lift step beside the crib with his set hand open on his knee. The three strokes in the crust had baked a little darker than the rest. Above them the cage hung where it had stopped, with nine kaas in it, halfway up the shaft to the rim.

Later, when the crew had carried the carter up the stair, Torin entered the night in the Tally in the old metre, standing at the end of the stack, with the copula closing every line as the hold had closed its entries for six thousand years:

> Entry: the Drawing-Off, the cask-hall of Varrak-Thur.
> Forty kaas drawn off the lees. Nine to the lift, on.
> Twelve cracked when the main gave. To the hold, on.
> Two bars, buyer to gauge-man, for the double draw. To the buyer, on.
> Gorrgorr of the lift, under the end kaas. By no life settled, on.
> Kalrin of this hall, at the stack, his hand to the main. Welling, on.
> The angles are true. The entry holds.

> CHANGES · KALRIN OF VARRAK-THUR
> Stage I, Murmuring, to Stage II, Welling. Tier of Standing: Apprentice.
> Catalyst: first contact with a Wellspring current under load, the burst main, the third bell.
> Left palm set by the current. Borin's read: four days, or not at all.

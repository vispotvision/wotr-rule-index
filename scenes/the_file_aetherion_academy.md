# The File — Aetherion Academy

*Aetherion Academy. The infirmary, the back theatre. Afternoon.*

Wystan Ashmore had been investigating the dead for a very long time, and he had learned to tell what kind of room he was walking into by its smell before he saw it. This one smelled of carbolic and burned lint and, under them, very faint, of a pond bottom in high summer. He knew that smell. He had first smelled it on a coldhouse table at Greyshaft Nine, nine years ago, coming off a hind that had been dead for four days and was trying to stand up.

He stopped inside the theatre door. Rovhen Talvasciel stopped a pace behind his left shoulder, where a second stands, and Shiori a pace behind his right.

"Three more," said the woman in the oilcloth apron, without looking round from the bucket of snow she was packing. Brenna Hask had red hair gone half to grey and a voice that had ordered men off tables under fire. "Wonderful. Wipe your boots or get off my floor, and if any of you breathes on that man's legs I'll have you carried out on the door he came in on."

Wystan wiped his boots.

The theatre was crowded. The skylight dripped onto an empty tray. On the deal table under it Kwon Mu-jin lay grey and still, his calves open to the air under oiled silk, his chest rising short and wet. His wife sat at his head on a stool too low for her, one long hand on his hair, the four blue lines on her chin very dark against her pale face. She did not look up. On a side tray among the bloodied lint lay a single round lens on a thin chain, unbroken, wiped clean by someone who had not known what else to do with it.

Wystan looked at the lens for a long moment. Then he looked at the room.

He was a tall, narrow man who looked as if he had not eaten a meal in daylight in ten years. His hair was the colour of wet mahogany, red where the lamp caught it, parted off-centre and worn to the waist, clean because a filing officer who looks unkempt gets his findings questioned. He wore black oiled wool closed to the throat, a full cloak with a standing collar, the silver-grey embroidery at his shoulder worn nearly smooth. Everything he owned was black because residue shows on black, and he wanted to see it.

*I should have said it in the arena,* he thought. *The whole of it. I stood in front of the Grand Mage and said "an individual operating under the mantle of The Mother," because that was what the paper allowed me to say.*

The paper. The Night Watch Society did not release an operator's name from a sealed attribution without three signatures and a cause, and the Ferriby file named no operator at all. The Bureau had closed it as a bleed. Wystan had opened that tin on Cutler Row with his own bare hand and lived the last minute of the man who made it, as he lived every operator's last minute when he touched their work. He had seen the hand. He had seen a long scholar's hand with thick knuckles turning the lid down onto the tin, and heard a dry voice say, quite pleasantly, to someone out of sight, *seal it, and watch.* He had known who it was for nine years, and for nine years he had not been allowed to write it down. The release had come up from the Society by courier an hour ago, while the vault was burning. It was in his coat now, against his ribs, three signatures and a cause. It was an hour late.

He had met Malphas five times. Four of them were in his own notebook, dated and timed, in the flat hand he used for interviews. The first was at the Division reading floor, where Malphas had been courteous and dry and very interested in what the Night Register heard after the eleventh division. The second was at Genesio, where he was thinner. The third was at a waystation on the eastern line, where he talked for an hour about retting ponds and never once looked at the man across the table. The fourth was in the back room of a coaching inn at Swale, where he had looked at nothing else. Each time there had been a little less of the man in the chair and a little more of something patient sitting behind his eyes, waiting for the conversation to end so it could go back to work.

The fifth meeting was not in the notebook.

Wystan cleared his throat. It was the only concession he made to the room.

"So," he said. "Malphas has finally shown his hand."

His voice was flat and unhurried and carried exactly as far as it needed to. Rovhen, behind him, had taken out his field book and was holding it closed against his chest, the way a man holds a hat in a widow's parlour.

"I believe he finally has the tools to finish what he has wanted to finish for a very long time." Wystan looked at the man on the table, at the opened calves, at the oiled silk. "I have been on him for nine years. The Ferriby cut on the Swale line, the coldhouse at Greyshaft Nine, the carcasses no bird would touch. His own cell had a name for it, in writing. He insisted on the name. *Project Zombification.*" He said the two words exactly as they had been written in the file, with no inflection at all. "I watched a deer that had been dead four days get up off a butcher's table and walk into a wall until its legs broke, and keep walking. I thought that was the worst of him."

He glanced toward the window, toward the south range and the hole in the charnel yard and the column of steam still rising out of it into the grey.

"I was wrong."

Brenna had stopped packing snow. The round infirmarian had stopped pretending to roll bandages.

"I have done a great deal of investigation." Wystan's hands stayed at his sides. "And all of it points the same way. His wife is dying." He let it sit. "Has been, for some years, by every account I could gather. A wasting illness, in the blood or the marrow, nobody would say which. The men who worked beside him at Genesio say he stopped writing to them about the same time. The ones who did hear from him say his letters changed. Fewer about the work. More about time. One of them burned what Malphas sent him and refused to discuss it. Three others cut him off entirely, and not one of them will tell me why, which tells me why."

He breathed in through his nose. Behind him Shiori shifted her weight, very slightly, and was still again.

"He killed my partner."

It came out in the same flat voice as everything else. Rovhen, who had been taught by a man who could hear grief under any voice, looked up from his closed book.

"Herbert Lyle Greymane. Officer of the Night Register, seventeen years. Third son of a Banner House that never forgave him for taking a desk instead of a sword." Wystan's mouth did something small and quick and stopped. "Herbert went out to Swale alone on a question I should have gone with him to ask. They found him on the bench outside the coaching inn the next morning with his coat folded beside him. He had not been touched. No wound. No working anyone could read. The Bureau wrote heart failure." His eyes went back to the lens on the tray. "At the fifth meeting I asked Malphas whether he had killed Herbert. He said yes. He said it the way a man confirms the hour of an appointment, and then he asked whether Herbert had been married, because if he had, he was sorry for her."

Nobody in the theatre said anything. The skylight dripped onto the tray.

"I believe he wants to stop death because he cannot stop his own grief." Wystan's voice did not rise. "I believed that for years, and it was a small enough thing to hold. A clever man who loves his wife and cannot bear the date. That I understood. That I could have filed." He looked at Mu-jin's calves under the oiled silk, at the salt crusted at the seams of the wounds, at the faint pale pocks where the needles had gone in. "This is much more than that. This is much more than I imagined. He was not practising on deer to save one woman. He was practising on children. And if what the Grand Mage's people are saying in the corridor is true, he was practising on himself."

He turned at last, and looked at the two people in the room he had come to speak to.

"The Society released his name to me an hour ago." He touched his coat once, over the place where the paper lay. "I can give you everything the Night Register holds on him now. Nine years of it." Then his gaze went back to the side tray, to the round lens on its thin chain, and his bare right hand closed slowly at his side. "But first I need to know one thing, and I need to know it before anyone else wipes that clean. Did Malphas hold that lens?"

# The Floor

*The bottom of the trench on the blackened plain, the fourteenth day, the last minutes*

Faisal heard his name, and turned his head toward it in the dirt.

It took a long time. Everything took a long time now. The cord of his neck had gone thin as wet paper, and when he moved it the cracks along his throat widened, *tsssk*, and light ran out of them over his collarbone and pooled in the hollow there like spilled honey. But he got his face turned, and there his brother was, sitting against the trench wall at his shoulder in the drifting gold, close enough to touch, looking up at the red sky so as not to be looked at.

There was a wet black line running from the corner of his brother's left eye down through the blood on his gray cheek.

Faisal looked at it for a long moment. He did not say anything about it. It was the kind of thing that would vanish if it were spoken of, and he had spent eleven thousand years learning which things those were.

On his chest, on the dented plate over the place where the Crystal had been, lay the little laurel cuff, with his ear still in it. He could feel the faint weight of it rise and fall with his breathing. He moved his chin toward it.

[a breath of a laugh, wet] "You brought it back." *Khhh.* "I would have looked for it." His lips pulled into the smile again, red at the edges. "Thank you. You were always the one who found the things I dropped. You just never once gave them back before."

Then the cough came, and took him.

It came up from the bottom of the drowned lung and bent his whole ruined body off the dirt, *KHHHAK, KHHHAK, KHHHHAAAK*, the broken ribs grinding in him like stones in a sack, and blood came up out of his mouth in a thick bright rope, and then a second, and spattered across the cuff and the plate and the black soil beside his brother's hand. There was gold in it now, threads of it, as if the blood had begun to remember what it had been before it was blood. He lay back down when the cough let go of him. His chest heaved. The light poured out of the hole in it, weaker than it had been, the color of a lamp turned low.

He had heard every word of the question. *Why.* The trench had thrown it back off its walls, and he had heard it twice.

[very quietly, with blood, smiling] "I did not do it for them," Faisal said.

He let that stand in the air between them for a moment, the way a man sets down the first stone of a wall and checks that it is level before he lays the next.

"I did it for you."

---

The gold dust came down over both of them, very slowly, the way snow falls on a still night.

"You asked me to look at them," he said. "I did. I have looked at them every day for seven hundred years. You are right about all of it. They are mayflies. They will tell it wrong by the harvest. By their grandchildren I will be a story about a bird." The smile did not move. "They do not need to remember me, brother. I remember them. That was always the arrangement. It was a good one."

*Khhhhh.*

"But it was never them I was kneeling for, first, or at the bottom of it." His bloodshot eyes went to his brother's face, to the black line on the cheek, and stayed there. "When you cracked the Bell, every oath sworn under it for eleven thousand years came loose. All that weight. You know what an oath is, when there is no Bell to hold it. It is a chain. Every promise is a chain on the one who made it, and the heaviest chains of all are on the ones who break them." He coughed, softly, and a little more red came over his lip. "Who had broken more oaths than anyone in creation, brother? Who had laughed at more of them, and torn up more of them, and been glad?"

He watched that land.

"If the weight had come down, it would have come down on you. All of it. Every broken promise from the beginning of the world, in iron, on your wrists and your ankles and your throat. You would never have stood up again. You would never have been free again, not for one more hour, not in Hell, not anywhere." He breathed. "And I knew how much you loved it. Being feared. Being loose. Being the one thing in the whole Continuum that nobody could hold. I watched you love it in the courtyard, before you even knew what you were. I could not bear the thought of you in chains." A pause. "I could not bear it. So I knelt down under the weight and held it up, so that it would not come down on you. That is all it ever was."

He turned his eyes, slowly, toward the plain beyond the trench lip.

"And then I found that if I was holding it anyway, I could hold theirs too. Every oath the mortals swore. All their small true promises, every one of them a weight, and they are so small, brother, and their backs are so short." The light in his chest flickered. "So I took them as well. That is why I take the oaths of the mortals. So they do not have to bear the weight of what they promise, every one of them, alone, every day. So they can promise things, and keep them, and go on living. So that this world can go on." *Khhh.* "It turned out it was the same weight. The floor under you and the floor under them. I only ever had to hold up the one."

---

He looked at the mortals one more time.

He could see them, just, over the lip of the trench, if he let his head fall a little to the side. The bare hills lay gold under the fallen petals of his Domain, and on the nearest of them, small and far off, some shepherds had come out of the brake where they had hidden from the red sky all morning. Four of them, and a dog. They were standing in a little knot at the edge of a petal of written law the size of their whole grazing, staring at it, and one of them, the smallest, had gone right up to the edge of it and put out a hand and touched the gold, very carefully, the way a child touches the surface of still water. Farther off, past the hills, the smoke of the Crownlands went up into the cracked sky, thinner now. People were putting out the fires.

They would go home tonight. They would tell it wrong.

He smiled at them.

[barely above a whisper, entirely certain] "I would do it all again," Faisal said. "Every age of it. The Bell, and the edge, and the kneeling, and this morning. All of it." He turned his face back to his brother. "Even this. Even being defeated by you." The smile widened, through the blood, the way it had widened in the white courtyard every time his brother landed one. "There is no one in all creation I would rather be defeated by, brother. If it had to be someone, I am glad it was you. Nobody else was ever good enough."

He heard the other things, too. *You could have put one through me. You threw away forever. That was ours.* He answered them the way he answered everything, one at a time, in order.

"I did not want to put a hand through you. I wanted to see you stand up." *Khhh.* "And it was never going to be forever. The Bell was cracking a little further every year. You could not feel it, from where you stood. I could. One day I would have broken under it at the edge, alone, with nobody watching, and the weight would have come down on you anyway, and I would not have been there." His eyes were wet now, and the wet was red. "I did not want to end on my knees in the dark. I wanted to end looking at you. That was the selfish thing. You were right. You are always right about me."

He listened to the hole in his brother's chest whistle softly on the in-breath, and to his own lung bubble, *khhhh*, and the two sounds went in and out together for a while, out of time and then, for a few breaths, almost in it.

*I thought I'd enjoy it more.*

"I know," said Faisal gently. "I know you did. I am sorry. That was never something I could give you."

He was quiet for a moment, gathering what was left.

"I hope," he said, and it came out very small, because he could feel the shape of the answer already and did not want to make his brother say it. "I hope you can put your hatred down, after. Some of it. Enough to walk around." He watched his brother's face. "But I know you still blame them. For the Bell. For me. For kneeling. You always thought they took me from you." The smile faltered, for the first time, at one corner. "They did not take me. I went."

The dust came down.

"I do not know why I thought I could change your fate." It was barely a breath now. "I think I always knew I could not. I only thought, if I held the floor up long enough, and you stood on it long enough, one day you might look down and see it." His eyes went to the black line drying on the gray cheek. "And you did. Just now. Did you not?"

He did not wait for an answer. He had never needed his brother to answer him. He had only ever needed him to be there.

---

It started at his feet.

The last of the Essence came off him the way the last leaves come off a tree in a single gust at the end of autumn, all at once, after holding on through every storm: a long slow flaking rush of gold, *shhhhhhhhhhh*, lifting from his boots and his shins and his knees and rising into the air above the trench in a column of soft bright glitter, and under it there was nothing at all, no bone, no flesh, no plate. The greaves fell in on themselves with a small dull clank, *tonk*, empty. Then the knees went, and the thighs, the gold going up off them like sparks off a fire that has been stirred, and the light that had been pouring out of every crack in him all morning went up with it, so that for a moment the bottom of the trench was brighter than noon.

He felt it climb him, and it did not hurt. That surprised him. Nothing hurt any more. The broken ribs, the drowned lung, the split arms, the torn wing: the pain went out of each of them the moment the gold reached it, the way a cramp goes out of a hand when it finally opens. What was left was very light, and very warm, and very clear.

The torn wing went first of the upper body. It lifted off his back in one long sigh of color, every remaining feather of it, rust and indigo and frost-gray and green and copper and the brown of a salt-cutter's hands, and broke up into a thousand small lights that rose and scattered over the plain and over the hills and over the shepherds on their hill, and went out, one by one, softly, each in the direction of the one who had given it. Then the other wing, the gold one. Then the plate over his heart, with the laurel cuff still lying on it, and the cuff slid off through the place where the plate had been and dropped into the black dirt.

*Tink.*

Far above, in the Hall of Equilibria, Marium cried out. Through the crack in the gold floor she saw the light go up out of the trench in a long column, and behind her, on the high mantle, the egg that had hummed all morning took up the note of it, and glowed, and grew warm as a hand, and a single hairline of white ran across its shell from top to bottom, and stopped.

It did not open. Not yet.

The gold had reached his chest. He could feel his heart, *thrum*, very slow, and then the space after it, and then *thrum*, slower. Beside him his brother's heart was beating too. He could hear it, under the whistle of the wound. *THRUM.* Black. *Thrum.* Gold. Answering each other, the way they always had, across every field. He listened to the two of them together, and thought that it was the best sound there had ever been in the world, and that he had not once in eleven thousand years thought to say so.

There was not much of him left. A torso going to glitter. Shoulders. One arm.

His right arm, the clear one, lay in the dirt at his side, and a finger's width from it, palm down, lay his brother's gray hand.

He reached for it.

He had held his hands out for eleven thousand years, to every falling thing in creation, and caught nearly all of them. This was the only time he had ever reached for anything because he wanted it. The clear fingers moved in the dirt, slowly, slowly, dragging a faint furrow through the black soil, across the finger's width toward the back of the gray hand, and as they moved the gold went up off them, from the shoulder down, the elbow, the forearm, the wrist, so that by the time the reaching hand had crossed half the distance there was almost nothing left of it but the shape of a hand drawn in light on the air.

He looked up one last time, past the hand, into his brother's face.

[the last of his breath, smiling] "There you are," Faisal said.

The last of the light went out of the fingertips just as they reached the place where they would have touched.

What was left of them came down onto the back of the gray hand as a fine, warm dust of gold, so light it barely settled; and lay there; and did not go out.

The trench was empty. In the black dirt there was a set of empty greaves, a bent breastplate lying open like a shell, and a little gold cuff of laurel leaves. The light from the crack in the sky went out, one straight rod at a time, from east to west, *tik, tik, tik*, like lamps being put out along a long street, until there was only the red of the low clouds and, very high, a little blue coming through.

Above the plain, the last of the drifting gold went up past the crack in the sky, and was gone.

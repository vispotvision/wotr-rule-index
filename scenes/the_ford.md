# The Ford

*The Tenrai house at Kessen, the hour of the cock; then the garden, the town stair and the Kessen ford in spring flood*

Shōki stepped into the room, and the room began to cook.

He did not hurry. He had walked a long time on roads where nothing waited for him, and he walked the way a furnace door opens, slowly, letting the heat go first. The mats under his boots browned and curled at the edges, *tsssss*, and the smell of scorched rush came up thick and sweet. The lacquered go bowls on the board went soft and began to slump like candle-ends. The burning beam in the east wall cracked and spat and dropped a shower of orange sparks across the floor between them, and the cold morning air coming in through the burst window met the heat of him and roared, *FWOOOOO*, and the paper panes that were left blew in and caught and were gone. Every one of his steps put the room a degree nearer to the color of a kiln's mouth.

The old man did not move from the board.

That was the thing Shōki watched: the feet, more than the hands or the eyes with their slow-turning spokes of light. A Tenrai fights with his feet, the Kingstep of the Winning Thread: the gold line's eye sees where an opponent's next step will fall, and the Tenrai foot is already there when it lands. Every Kurenai child is taught the counter at the same table as the oath, because the Kurenai have stood in the Hall beside the Tenrai for a thousand years and have always expected, one day, to have to kill one. *Stand still. Give him nowhere to step to.*

So Shōki stopped, three paces inside the empty doorframe, and stood still, and let the heat do the walking for him.

*Fwoom.* The floor in front of him went up in a low blue sheet of flame where the oil from the dropped lamp had run into the mats.

Raizan watched it come toward his knees. Sweat ran out of the deep furrows of his brow into his white eyebrows and dripped off them. His blistered fingers lay quite still on his thighs. The singed ends of his beard were curling tighter in the heat, and the hem of his navy-black coat had begun, very gently, to smoke.

[old man, soft and courteous, an old Japanese court accent] "You stand like a Kurenai still," Raizan said. "Jinsuke taught you that. I watched him teach it." He looked at the blue flame creeping across the mats toward him. "It is a good answer to the Kingstep. It is the only one. And in a room this size, with you in the door, it means that whichever of us moves first will be the one who chose to." He breathed out slowly through his nose. "I am seventy-seven years old, and this room is a kiln. You are a patient man. I am a patient man. But I believe you will be patient longer than I will be alive."

Shōki said nothing. He had nothing to say to a chest that read as none.

[the black floor's register, grinding, flat] "Move, then," he said at last. "Or cook. Either is entered."

The old man smiled. It went through the white beard like a crack through ice.

"Then I will move."

He put out his left hand and laid it on the go board, palm down, and swept it across the stones.

---

*Klakklakklakklakklak.*

It was a small, dry, ordinary sound, the sound of a game being cleared away at the end of an evening, and it was the last thing Shōki expected. The black and white stones went off the edge of the board in a little rattling cascade, slate and shell, and the old man's long blistered hand was under them before they reached the mats. He closed it. When it opened again, over the board, it held a palmful of stones; and then, with a flick of the thumb so small that Shōki's one eye did not see it go, one white shell stone went across the room and struck him on the cheekbone, under the dead eye.

*Pk.*

It did not hurt. It was a pebble. ENSHŌ caught the little blow as it caught everything, and the energy of it, all of it, perhaps enough to crack a walnut, stopped in the skin of his cheek as a pinprick of heat. Nothing. A child's flick.

And ONNEN wrote it down, and HAKANASHI took him by the throat.

*He must answer every blow that lands, from anyone, before he does anything else.* That is the law the block burned into him, and it does not weigh the blow. A pebble is a blow. It had landed. Shōki's body turned toward the old man before his will had any say in it, his left arm whipping round in the backhand that had melted the wall, the Hall Iron white on his wrist and the three snapped chain-links flailing out behind it, *VWOOSH*, and the blow went through the place where the old man had been and on into the window frame.

*KRAAAKK.*

The frame exploded outward in a burst of burning splinters. The old man was not there. He had stepped, a breath before the backhand began, to the one place the Tengan said the backhand would not reach: one pace back and one to the right, the Kingstep, his foot landing where nothing was going to be. Because Shōki had moved. Shōki had not chosen to move; the law had moved him; and a man who has been made to move has somewhere to step to, and the Tenrai foot was already there.

*Pk.* A black stone off his temple.

*VWOOSH.* The answer, through empty air, and the corner post of the room went up like a torch.

*Pk.* A white stone off the point of his jaw.

*VWOOSH-KRAKK.* The answer, and the go board itself, slate and kaya wood a hand thick, split in two across the middle and both halves burst into flame.

Shōki understood. It was cold and clear and it went through the heat in him like a draught through a forge. The old man had read him, and the Tenebra veil meant the reading had gone one way only. He had read the card of him, the law of him, the way Raizan had read every lord he ever killed, for a full minute, from a rafter, without blinking. *A fighter who lands something small on him from the side pulls his answer off whoever he was killing, and a crew can drag him by the nose across a field this way while his clock runs.* The Kurenai had known that about him since the black floor. Thirteen of them had tried it, in crews. None had gone home.

This one was alone, and seventy-seven, and he was not trying to drag Shōki across a field.

He was dragging him out of the window.

*Pk.* Off the bridge of the nose. The old man was out on the veranda now, beyond the burning frame, in the cold gray-rose dawn, standing in the garden among the plum trees with the stones in his palm. *VWOOSH.* Shōki went through the wall after him, beside the window. The plaster went soft and ran and the lath behind it caught, and he came out of the house into the garden through a hole the shape of himself with fire all round the edges, *FWOOMPH*, and the plum trees on either side of the path went up one after another like a row of lamps being lit.

---

The garden was cold.

That was the first thing his skin told him. The air of the spring morning was cold, wet with snowmelt, and the gravel was cold, and the stream running down through the raked stones was cold as a knife, and everything in it was colder than he was by a thousand degrees. In the oven of the house the old man had been the one cold shape in a furnace, as easy to follow as a black stone on a white board. Out here everything was cold, and the old man was warm, and so were the others.

The guard. Four of them, Tenrai black and gold, backing away up the garden path with their spears leveled, warm shapes against the cold. The clerk who had dropped his lamp, flat in the gravel by the gate with his arms over his head, warm. Beyond the broken gate, down the long stone stair of the ford town, the whole town was awake: shutters banging open, doors, people running, a hundred warm shapes and then two hundred, pouring out into the steep lanes toward the river in the gray light, shouting, carrying children and lamps and bundles. Their heat came up the hill to him like the heat off a crowd at a festival.

And his eye. He had one, and it was the Reigan's, and he turned it on the town the way a clerk turns his eye on a column, and read the chests.

*None. None. One. None. None. Two. None. None. None.*

They read like the old man. Every one of them. A town of soldiers' widows and carters and fishwives and potters and children, and their chests were nearly empty, as empty as his, as quiet as a pond under fog, and among them, somewhere, walking down the town stair in a navy-black coat with a palmful of go stones, was the one chest in the north he had come for, reading exactly the same as theirs.

The old man had gone into the crowd. Of course he had. *The Blink's counter is the crowd or the dark.* He had put himself where the crowd was his shield and his veil was his face, so that to find him Shōki would have to burn all of them, and look in the ashes for the one that had been lying.

Shōki stood in the burning garden with his fists at his sides and his fever climbing, and the black-violet rim of his aura thickened on the side that faced the town, and he did nothing.

*Pk.*

A black stone. Off the back of his skull. Off the cauterised hole the needle had left, the one place on him still tender, and it hurt. It was the first thing that had hurt him in longer than he could count.

He turned, and the answer went out of him, *VWOOSH*, through empty air.

The old man was standing at the top of the town stair, beside the broken gate, in plain sight. The dawn was behind him. The stones were in his hand.

[courteous, almost gentle] "I am not hiding from you," said Raizan. "I would not insult you. I am only showing you what I could do." He held up a single white stone between two blistered fingers. "You are going north. To the Seat, and the boy in the red chair, and every heir of every line that sat in the Hall that morning. I do not need to read your doom to see that. I only need to read the road." He turned the stone over. "I have seen my own last morning. It is this one. I would like to spend it usefully. I am going to cost you a day."

He flicked the stone.

*Pk.*

And went down the stair, into the town, at a walk, without looking back.

---

The stair of Kessen ford runs down from the Tenrai house to the river in two hundred and eleven steps of gray granite, between houses of red pine with their eaves turned up and their shutters painted green and indigo and saffron, brass draw-lamps hung on chains over every landing, and at the bottom, where the stair meets the water, the long timber bridge of the ford with the Guild main running under its planking in a sleeve of brass and slate. Shōki went down all two hundred and eleven of them behind the old man in less than a minute, and he did not choose a single step.

*Pk.* A white stone, off his shoulder. *VWOOSH.* A shuttered window went up in flames. *Pk.* A black, off the back of his hand. *VWOOSH.* A draw-lamp on its chain burst like a seed-pod, raining burning oil. The old man was always a step ahead and a step aside, on the landing where the Tengan had seen Shōki's answer would not land, never close enough to strike and never far enough to lose, and every stone he flicked pulled the Sin of Wrath one more landing down the stair like a dog on a lead.

And the crowd parted for them. The old man walked through it. He walked through the gaps in the people the way he walked through the gaps in a man's sight, the Blink applied to bodies instead of eyes, so that the running townsfolk were always between him and Shōki and never once in his own way. Shōki walked through it behind him with his heat going out ahead of him like a bow-wave, and the people broke away from him on both sides screaming, a woman dragging two children by the wrists, a carter abandoning his barrow, an old man in a fishwife's apron simply sitting down on a step and covering his head, and the heat of Shōki going by blistered the paint on the shutters and set the hems of their coats smoking.

He did not burn them. He could have. Every step, he could have. He did not, and he did not ask himself why, because the answer was Tsuyu, and he had given Tsuyu to the Plane on the black floor and did not have the right to think her name.

Twenty-nine stones. He counted them, because he could not do otherwise. Twenty-nine blows, every one of them a pinprick, every one of them entered under the same name on the same page. Twenty-nine answers, every one of them through the empty air where an old man had been. His fever was very high now. ENSHŌ had swallowed every one of those twenty-nine little blows and he had spent nothing to vent them; he had answered with his whole arm twenty-nine times and hit nothing, and every answer had been heat poured out of him into air that did not want it and walls that went up in flames. He was hot past what his Tolerance liked. His throat was cracked dry. The ground swam.

At the bottom of the stair the old man walked out onto the stepping stones of the ford.

---

The Kessen was in flood. The spring melt was coming down off the high ground to the east, and the river ran broad and loud and the color of green glass over the ford, chest-deep on a tall man and fast enough to knock a horse off its feet, and so cold that the stones the old man stepped across were furred with a rime of white where the spray froze on them. The draw-lamps along the bridge rail swung in the wind off the water. Under the bridge's planking the Guild main hummed in its brass sleeve, *mmmmmmm*. On the far bank, people who had run across the bridge from the town were packed along the shore, a hundred faces in the gray light, watching.

Raizan stopped on a flat stone in the middle of the ford, with the green water roaring past on both sides of him, and turned.

He was spent. Shōki could see that even with one eye. The old man's coat was black with sweat and scorched brown at the hem and the cuffs. His blistered right hand was held a little away from his side, and the blisters had broken on the stair and were weeping. His breath was going in and out of him in short, careful pulls, the way a man breathes when every breath is being counted. There were three stones left in his palm. His feet were set on the wet rock in the old way, the right heel off the stone.

And Shōki was standing on the last step of the stair, with the river in front of him, and he understood the last thing.

The cold.

*Its counter is the cold: water, ice and saturated cold ground pull the banked heat out of the struck tissue faster than the Trait can hold it.* The old man had read that too. He had not been dragging Shōki to the town. He had been dragging him to the river.

[soft, courteous, out of breath] "I could not hurt you," said Raizan from the middle of the ford. "Not with a needle and not with a stone. Every blow I landed was a coin in your purse." The spokes in his eyes turned. "So I have not been trying to hurt you. I have been trying to make you hot." He held up one of the three stones. "You have answered twenty-nine times. You have vented nothing. You are carrying everything you swallowed on the stair. And the Kessen in spring is two degrees above ice."

He flicked the stone.

*Pk.*

Off Shōki's breastbone, over the Covenant Scar.

And the law took him, and he went into the river.

---

*SHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHH.*

The river did not touch him at first. That was the strangest thing. He went in to the waist, his answer flailing out ahead of him at the old man's stone, *VWOOSH*, through the empty air over the water, and the water came up round him and did not reach his skin. It flashed to steam a finger's breadth from it, a skin of white vapor wrapping his whole lower body, so that he stood in the green flood inside a bag of his own steam like a red-hot iron dropped in a quenching trough, the water dancing and spitting and skating away from him on the cushion of its own boiling, *tsk-tsk-tsk-tssssss*. The smith's word for it is the dance. Water cannot touch iron that hot. It rides on the steam and never lands.

For a moment he thought he would walk through the whole river that way.

Then the current came.

The Kessen in spring is not a trough of still water. It came down on him green and fast and heavy as a running wall, and it tore the bag of steam off his legs the way a gale tears a tent, and for the first time the river *touched* him.

*KSSSSSSHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHOOOOOOOOOOOOOMMMMMM.*

It exploded. The water where it struck his skin went to steam all at once, violently, a white blast that went up out of the river round him twenty feet high and rolled out over the ford in a great boiling cloud, and the cold came in behind the steam and found him, and pulled. He felt it pull. Every degree he had swallowed on the stair, every one of the twenty-nine little blows banked as heat in his tissue, every grain of the fever: the river reached in through his skin and took it, in great greedy handfuls, faster than ENSHŌ could hold it. The banked heat went out of his legs like blood out of a cut. It went out of his belly. The river rose to his chest and it went out of his chest.

And then the quench came, which he should have foreseen, because it was his own art.

*YAKI-IRE.* The swordsmith's word for the moment the blade goes into the water. Steel heated above its change point and then plunged into cold freezes hard and brittle and cracks. SHAKUNETSU had been standing in him since the Hall: his skin crazed like glaze on a fired pot, ember light in every crack. Now the river took the heat out of that glaze all at once, and it did what quenched glaze does.

It shattered.

*Pik. Pik-pik. PIKPIKPIKPIKPIK.*

The skin of his forearms crazed white and then broke, the ember-lit cracks splitting open into real cracks, flakes of black-glazed skin the size of a fingernail springing off him and spinning away in the current, and under them the raw flesh, scarlet, steaming, the blood coming out of it hissing and then, for the first time since the Hall, running. Red, in the green water, in long ribbons downstream. The crazing ran up his chest, across the Covenant Scar, and the spiral law-mark cracked across its middle and wept black. Down his back. Up his neck. His face. The scars across his cheek and brow split open again along their old lines, and the dead eye's ember dimmed to the dull red of a coal at the end of a night.

*GKHHH.*

It was the sound of a man who has not felt cold since the black floor being told, all at once, what it is.

He went down to one knee in the river. The water closed over his shoulders, over his chin, and the steam went up off him in a thick white column into the dawn, and the people packed along the far shore went silent, and the brass draw-lamps on the bridge swung, and under the bridge the Guild main hummed on as if nothing had happened, *mmmmmmm*.

---

When he came up, the river was to his chest, and he was standing, and he was cold, the way a furnace is cold when it is banked down to coals: still hotter than anything in the river by a long way, the water still boiling white where it touched him, but the roar in him gone to a mutter, the black-violet rim of his aura thinned to a line. ONNEN had held, most of it. The bank is not heat in the tissue; it is heat in Vantabriel's keeping, and the river could not reach it there. The old man's page was still written. Thirty-one blows under one name: the needle, twenty-nine stones, and the one on the breastbone. But everything ENSHŌ had been carrying on top of it, everything that made him the burning man in irons, was going downstream in a long red-and-white plume of steam and blood.

The old man was standing on his flat stone, three paces away, with two stones in his blistered palm. He was soaked to the knees with the spray. He was shaking. He had not moved.

[quiet, the voice of a physician at a bedside] "There," said Raizan Tenrai Moto. "Now we are both old men in cold water."

He looked at Shōki for a long moment, the spokes in his eyes turning, slowly, slowly.

"You did not burn them," he said. "On the stair. You could have. Every landing. I watched you not do it two hundred and eleven times." He breathed. "I have known a great many killers. I have not known many who would have walked through that crowd and kept their hands at their sides."

He turned one of the two stones over in his fingers.

"I will tell you something now, because it is my last morning and I do not have to be careful any more. I did not choose the lord at the feast. I was nineteen. I was given a name and a night and a needle. The name was given to me by a Tenrai chair, in a room with a go board in it, by an old man who had watched me for a minute without blinking and decided I would do." The white beard moved. "The chair that sent me is not mine. It never was. And there is a man who sits in it today, and he does not wear a collar, because he has never needed to." The spokes stopped turning. "I can give you his name. Not as a bargain. You are not a man who bargains, and I am not a man who asks for his life. As a fact. Entered."

Shōki stood in the river with the steam going up off him and the blood running out of his cracked skin into the green, and said nothing.

[soft] "Or you can come out of the river," said Raizan, "and we can finish this. I have two stones and no needle, and you have a purse with thirty-one coins in it that are all mine. I think I know how that ends. I have seen it." A ghost of a smile. "I would only like it to end on dry ground. I am tired of being wet."

Downstream, the steam drifted over the town and the bridge and the people on the far shore. Upstream, the Kessen came down green and cold and fast off the high ground, carrying meltwater from mountains that had not heard of either of them.

And under Shōki's feet, at the bottom of the ford, a hundred and fifty meters of river ran in every direction: the whole crossing, the bridge, the near shore and the far, the Guild main in its brass sleeve, and every one of the faces packed along the bank. A Second Release away. *ABI. Without Interval.* One turn, and the edge of it would close round all of that like the lid on a pot, and no heat would cross it, not into the river and not out; the Kessen inside the boundary could take nothing from him any more, and everything he spent would stay, and the water would climb, second by second, from two degrees above ice to boiling, and so would everything standing in it, and on it, and beside it.

He would be warm again in a minute and a half.

The old man was watching him. He knew it too. He had read the card.

The steam went up. The river roared. On the far bank, a child had climbed up onto the bridge rail to see, and her mother had not noticed yet.

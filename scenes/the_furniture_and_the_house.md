# The Furniture and the House

*Aetherion Academy · the Crucible floor, Hiromi Mahuo's bout, from the third crater*

---

"So I moved the room."

Hiromi heard it from the bottom of the third crater, lying on his side in the broken stone with the ward-lines under him still flashing white, and the first thing he felt on hearing it, before the pain had finished arriving, was a great helpless rush of affection for the boy who had said it.

Then the pain finished arriving and he attended to that instead, because it was owed attention and he paid his debts.

He went through himself the way he had been taught. The right side under the ribs, where the knee had gone into him, was a deep sick ache with a sour tide of nausea riding on top of it; the liver, bruised, sulking, no worse than bruised, he thought, because he could still breathe past it. Higher up on the same side, one rib answered every breath with a bright clean stab, and he probed it with two fingers through his coat and felt it shift under them in a way ribs should not. Cracked. One, maybe two. His jaw rang like a struck pot where the palm had come up under it. His back had met the floor for the third time that afternoon and had views on it. There was blood in his mouth and he turned his head and let it out onto the stone, a little, not much.

He had been taken apart with great precision by somebody who understood the motion inside a body better than any physician Hiromi had ever met, and every one of those blows had been beautiful. Every one of them had also come into him lighter than it was thrown. He had felt that at each contact the way he had felt it in the crater before: the clipped coin, the short weight on the scale. And this time he had felt where the missing part went. It had not stayed in the boy.

It had come to him.

He lay there a moment longer and let it keep coming, and while it came, he thought.

---

The boy had answered the Loom. Hiromi gave him that freely and at once, because it was true and because he wanted to understand it all the way down before he took it apart.

Kain had seen that the curved room was not still. It could not be; it was centred on a boy who walked, so with every step Hiromi took, every thread of the room's grain shifted its angle to every other, every straight line through the oval bent to a new straight line, every road a falling thing would take was replaced by another road. Millions of tiny changes, every instant, just to keep the shape wrapped around its moving centre. And the boy was the Paragon of the Archon of Change. He had not tried to escape the changing room. He had joined it. When the room's tides offered his shoulder one road and his hip another, he let them each take their own, and let his spine become the changing thing between them, and changed again before the difference could ever add up to a tear. Then he had taken all the room's loose motion, the dust and the chips and the drifting residue, and added to each piece a small sideways push just as it fell toward Hiromi, so that falling became circling, and the curved well Hiromi had built became a spinning wheel with Hiromi at its hub. He had let the room carry him down its own straight road to Hiromi's body. And he had broken his dragon out of the amber by changing its shape faster than the fire could fix it.

*So I moved the room.*

It had been magnificent. It had also been exactly beside the point.

Hiromi had shown him a room because a room was the easiest thing in the world to show. Threads of light, a lean in the floor, stones sliding toward a pair of boots; it was a picture of the Dominion, the way a crown is a picture of a king. And the boy had looked at the picture and moved it, and believed he had moved the king.

The Dominion was not the room.

The word itself said so, if anyone listened to it. *Dominion*: the right to rule a realm, control over it, the exercise of command across a country. A ruler's dominion is not the shape of his country's hills. Flatten the hills, flood the valleys, turn every river backward in its bed, and the country is still his country and the people in it still his people, because what he holds is not any arrangement of the land. It is the land's belonging to him. Arrangements change every day. Belonging is what stays the same through the change.

Hiromi had read once, by a lamp he should have put out hours earlier, about the strangest branch of geometry there is: the one that does not care about shape at all. To it a cup and a ring are the same thing. You could squeeze the one into the other in clay without tearing it or gluing it. Stretch a thing, bend it, twist it, squash it, change it continuously in any way you like, and certain facts about it never change, because they are not facts about its shape. They are facts about how it holds together. How many holes it has. How many times a loop winds round a post. Those facts are whole numbers. They are one, or two, or none, and no amount of smooth, gradual, patient alteration can ever change a whole number into the next one, because between one and two there is nothing for a smooth change to pass through. You must tear the thing to change them. Nothing less will do.

Rank was a number like that.

Not a measurement, not a quantity that could be shaved a little at a time. A standing: above or below, his or not his, the one who gives the verdict and the one who receives it. The boy's whole art, every move he had made since the bell, had been an art of smooth alteration: a small sideways part, and another, and another, microscopic adjustments accumulating, tiny inequities in a thing's balance added up until it tipped. It was the most refined art of gradual change that Hiromi had ever seen, and it could change any shape in the world. It could not change a standing. A standing is not on the road that gradual change travels. The boy could change how fast his soul moved, how it held itself, what form it took, how it answered the room; he could change all of that endlessly, accelerating, evolving, a new self every instant; and at every one of those instants, at every stop along the way, the new self would find itself exactly where the last one had been in the order of the jar. Under the one whose conviction the jar wore.

That was why moving the room changed nothing. Moving the room was a change of arrangement, and the jar had never been his by arrangement. It was his by belonging. A falling stone turned into an orbiting stone is still a stone in his house. A curved floor made into a spinning floor is still his floor. A dragon that changes its shape faster than amber can fix it is still a soul inside the vessel where Irath's law decides what stands above what. The boy could pour the whole room into a new mould every heartbeat. The whole room, in every mould, was still the realm.

---

And then there was the thing the boy had built around him, which he did not seem to have noticed yet.

Hiromi sat up in the crater, slowly, one arm braced against the cracked ribs, and looked at it with a kind of delight.

The dust was going round him. So were the stone chips, the grit, the drifting motes of Aether residue thrown loose by everything that had happened on this floor today. The boy had taken everything that was falling toward Hiromi and turned it a little sideways, so that it missed and swung round, and missed again, and swung round again, and now the whole debris of the fight was wheeling round the white-haired boy in the crater in great slow rings, faster near him and slower further out, a spinning disc of grey around a hub.

There is a name for a thing that falls toward something and keeps missing it forever. It is called an orbit. A moon is a stone falling toward a world that has been given exactly enough sideways speed never to land, and it is not free. It is the least free thing in the sky. It goes round and round its world for ten thousand years, held, unable to leave without some great new push of its own to climb out of the well, and every astronomer in every age has called it by the same word: captive. *Bound.* The boy had taken the loose motion of the whole Crucible and refused to let it finish arriving, and in refusing he had made all of it a captive of the centre it circled. He had built Hiromi a court. Every grain of the fight now went round him the way the lesser stars go round the King's Heart in the Tyrant's Crown, and the boy had done it with his own hands.

*Only that which moves endures,* Hiromi thought. *Yes. In orbit. Around me.*

He began to laugh, and his rib told him at once to stop, and he stopped, and grinned instead.

---

He thought next about the cost, because the cost was the part the boy could not adapt his way out of. It had been the part from the first exchange.

Change is never free. Every natural philosopher who had ever lived had learned it the hard way, sooner or later: a real change, any real change, leaks. A wheel turns and its bearings warm. A rope is drawn through a pulley and the pulley grows hot. A spring compressed and released does not quite return all it was given; a little of it goes off into the spring's own trembling and into the air as heat, and that little never comes back. Do a thing slowly enough, as gently as a thing can be done, and the leak can be made very small. Do it fast and the leak grows, and grows faster than the speed does. The quicker a change is forced, the more of what drives it is spent in the forcing rather than in the change.

The boy had chosen, as his answer to everything, to change constantly and change fast.

Every adaptation his body made to the room's shifting tides, every redistribution of momentum through spine and hip and heel, every new answer that replaced the last answer the moment the room moved again, every evolution of his soul's own shape, leaked. It leaked Essence. And Essence that leaks from a practitioner does not vanish. It goes into the Aether of the room around him, the way the warmth of a wheel goes into the air.

Into this room. Into the jar. Onto the slope.

GRASP had never stopped. Under the Weight and the Loom and the Lion and all of it, the oldest thing about Hiromi was still true, the thing the adults in the binding circle had felt when he was six: the world lines up toward him before he asks it to. Loose Aether runs downhill to him, the way water finds the low corner of a floor. And there was a great deal of loose Aether in the jar now, a great deal more than there had been at the bell, because a boy who changed as fast as anything Hiromi had ever seen had been spending himself into the room in a fine warm mist with every change.

It came down to him. All of it. Downhill.

That was the leeching, and the boy had not been robbed. Hiromi had taken nothing out of him with any hand. The boy had poured himself out by his own art, as the price of his own art, and the room simply did with it what rooms near Hiromi had always done with anything loose: brought it home. Every attack and every attempt cost him a little more of the Essence that held him up, and every bit of it ran down the slope and into the white-haired boy he was attacking. That was what had come into Hiromi in place of the clipped edge of every blow. That was why a boy with a cracked rib and a bruised liver was sitting up in the bottom of the third crater, and why the grey at the edge of his sight had not come back, and why the heat in his chest, which ought by now to have been white, burned instead at a steady, nourished red, like a hearth with someone feeding it.

The boy was feeding it.

And there was the loop, the one the boy had made around himself without knowing it. The faster he changed, the more he leaked. The more he leaked, the more of him ran down to Hiromi. The more ran down to Hiromi, the more there was to hold the jar with, the deeper and surer Hiromi's will sat in it. Then every new state the boy changed into arrived already underneath it. So the boy changed again, faster, to get out from under, and leaked more, and the loop went round once more. He was trying to make himself more powerful by changing, and every turn of it dragged Hiromi's will upward and drew Hiromi's capture round him one more time. Recapture, and recapture, and recapture. There was no end to it the boy could reach by changing, because changing was the engine that drove it.

It was a loop that wore its runner down. Hiromi had a slate full of the arithmetic of such things, the loops that steersmen and the makers of governors on engines study: a thing that must keep correcting itself against something that keeps moving has to keep spending, and the faster the thing it chases moves, the more it must spend to keep up, and there is no rest in it, not ever, for as long as the chase lasts. The boy was correcting himself against the room, and the room was correcting itself around Hiromi, and only one of them was paying for both.

And there was one thing more, and it was the one Hiromi had been slowest to see, because it was the cruellest, and he did not like to think of himself as cruel.

Irath's law ranked by conviction. Conviction is a thing that settles. It is what remains when the instants are over, the thing a will has been sure of for a long time. Hiromi had been sure of the same thing since he was six. It had had seven years to settle in him like stone into a riverbed. And the boy's answer to the Dominion had been to stop being any one thing for longer than a heartbeat. A new self every instant, each one already obsolete before it was complete. That was Wyther's glory and it was very beautiful. But in Irath's court, a new self is a newcomer. It arrives without standing, because standing is the thing that only time spent being the same thing can give. Every time the boy changed to escape the Dominion, the self that escaped was younger in the court than the one before it, and stood lower. He was not rising out of the hierarchy by changing. He was lowering himself in it, one fresh beginning at a time, down and down, a new arrival at the bottom of the steps with every turn of the loop.

It was no wonder the Archons were what they were to each other. Wyther's storms could go through a country and move every leaf and stone and river in it. They could not make the country theirs. Storms pass through realms. Thrones remain where they stood, and the storm goes on to the next.

---

He stood up.

It took a while and he gave it the while. He came up onto one knee with a hand on the crater's rim, and then onto his feet, with his arm pressed hard against his side to hold the rib still, and the wheeling disc of grey that the boy had built round him came up with him. Every grain of it turned to keep him at its centre as he rose.

Above the floor, in the Soul's reflection, the black dragon still had the haetae in its long tumbling spiral, wings beating through the amber light, dragging the guardian round and round so that it could never settle onto its step before its gate. Hiromi watched it for a moment with real respect. Then he spoke to the Lion, once, without raising his voice.

"The gate's where you are."

A haetae judges whoever comes before it. It does not need the gate to be fixed in any one place to do that; the gate is not a place. It is the house's belonging, carried by the guardian wherever the guardian is, and the step is wherever someone stands before it. In the reflection the Lion stopped trying to sit. It let the dragon tumble it, and as it tumbled it turned its great head and looked, from every new angle the spiral gave it, at the thing that was tumbling it. Every turn of the spiral was a new step. Every new step brought the dragon before the guardian again.

Then Hiromi let go of the Loom.

He opened both his hands. The amber threads he had gathered from every corner of the oval slid out of his fists and went back into the room's grain. The lean went out of the floor; the curve relaxed; the roads through the jar went straight again, everywhere at once. He did not need them. He had only ever drawn them to show the boy a picture, and the boy had taken the picture for the thing, and now it was in the way.

The debris round him did not fly off. That surprised the tiers. It did not surprise Hiromi in the least. Without the curve the grains should have gone shooting away on straight lines like stones from a sling. They went on circling instead, slower now, round and round the white-haired boy, because the curve had never been what held them. GRASP held them. The world lines up toward Hiromi before he asks it to, and the boy had given every grain in the Crucible a reason to keep doing it.

He drew in his breath, carefully, around the rib.

Then he opened GRASP all the way, as wide as it went, and did not hold any of it back.

It showed in the room first. Every lamp flame on every post round the whole oval, which had been burning short and blue, stood up tall and bent toward him, every one, like grass when the wind turns. Every gauge in every wall swung on its pin and pointed at him and trembled there. The ward-lines under the sand took up a low steady drone, the note of a great main drawing hard.

And the mist became visible.

All of it, everything the boy had spent into the jar in leaking change after leaking change, the fine warm Essence of the Paragon of Motion that had been running quietly down the slope to Hiromi since the first exchange, came up into the light under the full weight of his field. Anyone in the stands with eyes for workings could see it now: a faint shining haze lifting off the boy's skin, off his shoulders and arms and the bare soles of his feet, the way steam lifts off a horse after a hard run on a cold morning, and drifting, every wisp of it, across the broken floor and down the invisible slope into the white-haired boy at the centre of the wheeling dust. Hiromi breathed it in. It tasted of nothing and it warmed him to the fingertips.

He let the boy look at that. He thought the boy deserved to see it plainly, after so honest a fight.

Every change from here on would show. Every adaptation of that perfectly adapting body, every evolution of that endlessly evolving soul, every attack and every attempt to escape, would come off him as visible light and run downhill to the boy he was fighting. The jar would not tax him anymore. It did not need to. It would only collect.

Hiromi wiped his mouth on his sleeve and looked across the broken floor at Kain, and grinned the grin that rearranges and does not drop.

"You moved the room!" he shouted, and his voice came back off the barrier at its full, enormous size, cracked rib and all. "You moved ALL of it! Every stone! I watched you! It was wonderful!"

He spread his arms, wincing, and the whole wheeling court of grey turned round him.

"You moved the furniture." He let his arms drop. "It's still my house."

The shining haze drifted off the boy's shoulders and down across the floor toward him, slow and steady, like breath leaving a body on a winter morning.

"Of course."

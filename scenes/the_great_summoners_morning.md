# The Great Summoner's Morning

The morning bell had rung twice already, and the corridors of the Aetherion Academy smelled the way they always did at this hour: chalk dust and lamp oil and the faint copper tang of residual workings that clung to the stone like old sweat. Somewhere below, in the practice halls, an instructor was shouting at a first-year whose summon had gotten loose again. The shrieks carried up through the floor in thin, tinny waves, muffled by three storeys of granite and the constant low hum of the Academy's conduit network thrumming behind the walls.

Hiromi Mahuo walked through the upper gallery as if the building had been raised around him and he was still deciding whether to keep it.

Thirteen years old. White hair that fell to the collar of his coat in loose waves, the colour of forge-ash before it cools, parted roughly down the centre so that two longer strands framed his face like the legs of a compass. The hair came from neither parent, and nobody in the Mahuo lineage had been able to explain it. His eyes were worse. Red irises, pale as diluted wine, set in a face that was sharp where his father's was broad: high cheekbones, a jaw still narrowing toward manhood, skin so pale it caught the blue of the corridor lamplight and held it. Small black earrings hung from both lobes, plain drops on thin chains. His coat was black wool with a high collar that buckled at the throat, cut close through the chest and loose at the hip, the kind of garment that looked like it had been tailored for someone two years older and fit him anyway. He wore it open at the top buckle, because he always did. It showed the black undershirt beneath, and the pale notch of his collarbones.

He was grinning.

Not the uncertain grin of a boy navigating unfamiliar halls. The settled, proprietary grin of someone who had understood his own value before anyone had explained it to him, and had been waiting for the world to catch up.

A knot of female students stood near the pillar junction where the upper gallery opened onto the east stair, four of them, second-years by their sash colour, clustered around a sheaf of notice-papers and pretending to read. Their conversation had dropped to whispers the moment his footsteps reached them. One of them, a broad-shouldered girl with ink-dark braids coiled against her skull, glanced sideways and then looked very deliberately back at the papers.

Hiromi angled toward them. His hands found his pockets. His stride didn't change.

"Hey, ladies."

He said it the way his father said *good morning* to foreign dignitaries: with the absolute certainty that the greeting was a gift, and that they knew it.

The girl with the braids pressed her lips together. The one beside her, shorter and rounder, with freckles across the bridge of a flat nose, made a sound behind her teeth that was trying not to be a laugh. A third turned fully, a tall Eressean girl with copper-green eyes and the kind of studied disinterest that took real effort to maintain.

Hiromi winked.

The freckled girl lost the fight. Her laugh broke out in a sharp burst, and she covered her mouth with both hands, and the tall Eressean turned back to the notice-papers so fast her hair swung. The girl with the braids shook her head once, slowly, without looking at him.

*They're talking about me,* Hiromi thought, and the thought sat in his chest like a warm stone. *Good.*

He passed them without slowing. The corridor ahead stretched toward the eastern lecture halls, lined with tall windows that let in the grey morning light of the Academy grounds. Outside, the practice fields were already occupied. Figures moved in disciplined rows, and above one of the far circles a shape hung in the air, blue-white and trembling, someone's summon held at extension for a duration test. Hiromi watched it for two steps and looked away.

Sloppy binding. The shape's left flank was already fraying. Whoever held it would lose cohesion inside a minute.

*I could hold that shape in my sleep. I could hold three of them. I held three of them when I was nine.*

He put his hands behind his head and walked with his elbows out, taking up more of the corridor than he needed, because the corridor had room and he wanted it.

The voice hit him from behind like a battering ram.

"**WHELP!** Looks like I'm going to be LATE AGAIN, ALL BECAUSE YOU WOULDN'T GET YOUR ASS UP, GETUROOOO!"

He bellowed it without turning, pitching his voice to carry off the stone walls and bounce back doubled. Two passing students flinched. A lamp flickered in its housing. The girl with the braids, still at the pillar junction, closed her eyes with the expression of someone adding a final mark to a long tally.

"Oh, shut it, would you." The voice came from behind, unhurried. "You always make excuses."

Geturo Mahuo emerged from the east stair at a walk, as if the morning bell were a suggestion made to someone else. He was taller than Hiromi by three fingers, broader through the chest and shoulders, built with the dense, settled weight that their father carried and Hiromi had missed entirely. His hair was their mother's: a warm chestnut brown, thick and straight, cut short at the sides and left longer on top where it fell across his forehead in a way that looked careless and was not. His face was rounder than Hiromi's, the jaw heavier, the cheekbones less pronounced. He had Frithia's mouth, wide and set in a line that could pass for patience or disapproval depending on which sibling he was looking at. His coat was the same Academy-issue black, but he wore it buttoned to the collar, cinched at the waist, sleeves rolled to the forearm because his wrists ran hot. His hands were larger than a boy of fourteen ought to have, knuckled and square, and they hung at his sides with the particular stillness of someone who had learned early that his hands attracted attention and had decided not to give them anything to do.

He looked at Hiromi with the flat, unimpressed expression of a man who has seen the same trick performed four hundred times and stopped clapping at twelve.

*He woke up an hour ago,* Geturo thought, watching his twin brother's theatrical indignation. *He's been awake since before dawn. He went to the binding circles. He always goes to the binding circles.*

"You were dressed before I opened my eyes," Geturo said. "You left the room. You came back. You messed up the bed so it looked slept in. And now you're blaming me."

Hiromi spun on his heel, walking backward, arms spread. "Slander. Baseless slander. I am a victim of your sluggish constitution and I won't stand for it."

"You were at the circles."

"I was at the circles."

Geturo sighed through his nose. It was a specific sigh, perfected over thirteen years of proximity, that communicated *I am aware of what you are doing, I have decided not to stop you, and I want you to know that the decision cost me.* He fell into step beside Hiromi, and the two of them moved down the corridor toward the eastern halls, white-haired and brown-haired, one loose-limbed and grinning, the other straight-backed and quiet, taking up the full width of the passage because one of them demanded it and the other permitted it.

They made it eleven steps before the third voice caught them.

"If you two don't stop arguing, I'll destroy this entire hall."

Lily Mahuo stood at the junction ahead, waiting. She had positioned herself exactly in the centre of the corridor, feet planted, arms folded beneath her chest, chin lifted at an angle that suggested the building's structural integrity was, at this moment, a matter of her personal discretion. She was fourteen, a year and two months older than the twins, and she wore that seniority the way some people wore armour: constantly, visibly, and with the clear expectation that it would be respected on sight.

She was taller than both of them, though Geturo was closing the gap. Lean through the waist and long in the limb, with their mother's brown hair falling past her shoulders in a thick curtain, darker than Geturo's, almost the colour of turned earth after rain. Her face was narrow, angular, Mu-jin's bone structure refined into something sharper and less forgiving. She had her father's eyes, dark and deep-set and steady, and she used them the way a bowman used elevation: to look down. Her Academy coat was immaculate, pressed, the collar starched stiff, the sash at her waist tied in the precise knot that the Academy handbook specified and no student in living memory had replicated without being told to try harder. A single silver pin sat on her left lapel, marking her year-rank, and it caught the corridor light like a small, deliberate accusation.

She looked at Hiromi the way a surgeon looks at a limb she has been asked to save and is not optimistic about.

Hiromi stopped. He stuck out his tongue.

"BLAHHHH! I'd like to see you TRY it!"

Lily did not blink.

"You're late," she said. "Both of you. Instructor Kaien has already begun the morning register. If he marks you absent, Father will hear about it before the second bell, because I will tell him."

"You wouldn't," Hiromi said, but his grin had lost half a degree.

"I already have the letter drafted. In my head. It's concise."

Geturo put a hand on Hiromi's shoulder, not roughly. "She means it."

"She always means it. That's the problem." Hiromi shrugged off the hand and started walking again, faster now, his coat swinging at the hip. "Fine. Fine! We go. We sit. We endure the world's most boring roll call. And then I go back to the circles, because Instructor Kaien's lecture on binding theory is six years behind what I figured out before I could tie my own boots."

Lily fell into step on his left. Geturo took the right. The three of them moved down the corridor in formation, a habit so old that none of them noticed it anymore, Hiromi in the centre because he always took the centre, Lily on the side closest to the lecture halls because she was always the one who knew where they were going, Geturo on the outside because he was the one who watched the doors.

"You didn't eat," Lily said, not looking at him.

"Not hungry."

"You're never hungry. You'll collapse in the practice ring again and I'll have to carry you to the infirmary again and the nurse will look at me again as if I'm the one who forgot to feed you."

"That happened once."

"It happened three times."

"Once *this term*."

The corridor opened ahead into the broad atrium where the eastern lecture halls branched off in a spoke pattern, five arched doorways set into walls of grey stone and pale timber. Students moved through the space in clusters, voices overlapping, the shuffle of boots on flagstone mixing with the distant hum of the conduit network. Morning light fell through a high clerestory in slanted bars that caught the dust motes and held them suspended, turning slowly, as if the air itself were thinking about something.

Hiromi slowed. His eyes moved across the atrium the way they always did: counting bodies, reading postures, checking the draw-gauges mounted on the far wall where the conduit pressure was displayed in graduated brass columns. He did it without thinking. He had done it since he was six, since the first morning Mu-jin had brought him to a practice hall and the room had gone quiet and stayed quiet and his father's hand on his back had been the only warm thing in a space full of adults who did not know what to say.

He was the Paragon of Dominion. All things were in his grasp. That was what they said about him, the instructors, the senior students, the men and women who had spent decades building the theory of summoning into a system that made sense and could be taught, and who had watched a six-year-old boy walk into a binding circle and do something that none of them could name.

Hiromi understood what it meant. He understood it the way a river understands a valley: not as a concept, but as the shape of the ground it moved through. Dominion was not control. Dominion was the world knowing what you were before you asked it to.

What his father couldn't understand, what Mu-jin spent years trying to teach him to understand, was that the gift was not the hard part. The hard part was the boy wearing it.

Thirteen years old. Six years past the morning that changed everything. Seven years before the weight of it would start to show.

The second bell rang. The sound moved through the stone like a living thing, deep and sustained, and the students in the atrium quickened their steps.

Hiromi grinned and broke into a run. His coat flared behind him. His white hair caught the morning light, and for a moment he looked like something burning.

"LAST ONE THERE BUYS LUNCH!" he shouted over his shoulder.

Geturo did not run. He watched his brother's back disappear through the third archway, and his expression was the same expression Mu-jin wore when he looked at the boy and could not find the words for what he saw.

Lily looked at Geturo. Geturo looked at Lily.

"He went to the circles again," Lily said. It was not a question.

"Before dawn."

Lily's jaw tightened. She adjusted her lapel pin, an unnecessary gesture, the pin had not moved, and walked toward the third archway at the precise speed that would get her there on time without appearing to hurry.

Geturo followed, hands in his pockets. The corridor emptied behind them, and the dust motes turned in the light, and the hum of the conduit network filled the space the way water fills a vessel: completely, and without asking.

# The Holy Inquisition — Part 1: The Kindling

The chapel smelled of tallow and old stone and something else beneath both, something that sat in the back of the throat and would not move. Burnt myrrh, perhaps, or the chemical residue of whatever the Measurewrights had been doing to the altar before the congregation arrived. The pews had been stripped out weeks ago. The villagers who once sat in them were gone, and where they had gone was not a question anyone in the Ordo asked twice.

Corvinus Meridiael stood in the third rank, between a man whose name he did not know and a woman whose mask was carved from river-clay and had cracked along the left cheek. He could hear her breathing through the crack. Short, shallow pulls. The air was close. Forty-three bodies in a chapel built for seventy, and no window open, because Seraphiel had ordered the shutters nailed before the address began.

The reason was above their heads. Three iron censers hung from the crossbeam on chains that had not been there when the chapel was a chapel, swinging in a motion so slight it could have been the building settling. The smoke they gave off was thin, grey-blue, odorless in the way that meant the smell was already inside you before you noticed it arriving. It pressed against the skin behind Corvinus's ears and sat there.

At the far end, on a step that had once held a basin for ablutions, Seraphiel Aurentius stood with his hands folded inside the sleeves of his vestments. White over red, the inverse of the Pontifex's palette, the hem pooling on stone that was still discolored from something that had been spilled and imperfectly cleaned. His mask caught the candlelight. The ivory had a warmth to it that the rest of him did not.

He did not raise his voice. He had never raised his voice in any address Corvinus had attended, and Corvinus had attended eleven.

"We must remember what brought us here."

The words fell into the chapel the way a stone falls into a well. Not loud. Present. The smoke curled around them as though it had been waiting for the vibration to push against.

"We must remember that the only way to purify this world is through strength, through virtue, and through the holy regimen of the Judger himself."

Corvinus's jaw tightened under his mask. His was wood, ash-wood, sanded smooth and sealed with beeswax, carved by his own hand in the forty-eight hours between the Declaration and the Purification of Calden Reach. He had made the mouth-line too wide. It let in the cold when they marched and the smoke when they stood in rooms like this one.

To his left, the woman with the cracked mask shifted her weight. Her name was Thessa Ignatia. She had been with the Ordo longer than Corvinus by three campaigns, and the thing that distinguished her from the other veterans was that she had stopped flinching at the sound of the censers and had not started flinching at anything else to replace it. A body that has found its equilibrium. Broad through the hips and shoulders in the way of women who carried loads before they carried doctrine, her hands red-knuckled and still, resting on the buckle of a belt that held no weapon. Attestants did not carry weapons. Attestants witnessed.

"Take heed, young acolytes." Seraphiel's voice had not changed. The same register, the same careful placement of each syllable in a mouth that had spoken Common for long enough to lose the Eressean periodicity but not long enough to lose the precision. "Take heed, for you are about to hear something that comes directly from the mouth of our leader."

Corvinus did not look at the man standing in the shadow behind the altar. He did not need to. The room already knew. Every body in it was oriented toward the shadow the way iron filings orient toward a lodestone, and the orientation was not a choice. It was a pressure. Low, constant, directional, and completely indifferent to whether anyone in the room was prepared for it. Cruor's suppression held, had held for the entire address, and the effort of holding it was visible nowhere on the body and legible in the sweat that had started on the back of Corvinus's neck the moment he entered the building.

Something in the corner of Corvinus's vision pulsed. A candle guttering, perhaps, or a trick of the censer-smoke. He did not turn to look.

"We will be marching on a village outside the Holy Sea of Alabaster," Seraphiel continued. The words were measured, each one set down like a tile in a floor that would bear weight. "A village whose way long left them in strife. Incapable of being brought into normal civilization. We are going to purify them. We are going to rid them of all the corruption."

The woman to Corvinus's right, the one he did not know, made a sound. Not a word. A sound, low in the chest, like the sound a dog makes when it agrees with the direction it is being pointed.

From the far left of the assembly, a man Corvinus did know stepped forward. Brother Cassius Pelagiel, who had served as a Measurewright in the mother order for fourteen years before the exile and who now served the Ordo in the capacity that the Ordo needed him most: the man who administered the Kindling.

Cassius was not tall. He was built like a barrel set on legs, with the thick wrists and flattened knuckles of a man who had spent his youth working stone before his hands learned finer work. His face above the mask was seamed and windburnt, the eyebrows gone to grey, the eyes themselves the pale washed-out blue of a man who had spent too long staring at things that produced their own light. His mask was copper, hammered thin, covering the jaw and nothing else. The jaw was the point. The jaw was always the point.

He carried a tray. The tray held forty-three cups.

The cups were ceramic, unglazed, the size of a man's closed fist. In each one, a liquid the color of heated amber, still moving in slow concentric rings though the tray was steady. The motion came from the liquid itself. It was warm. Not heated; warm in the way that living things are warm, as though the substance in the cup had a circulation of its own.

Corvinus had taken the Kindling twice before. Both times his hands had shaken afterward. Both times the shaking had not stopped for two days. Both times nobody had told him that was going to happen, and both times nobody had asked him afterward whether it had.

Seraphiel had not stopped speaking.

"The Scale has spoken. The reading was taken three days ago by Justiciar Orenthal, who walked the village perimeter at dusk and who returned to this chapel before dawn with a number that he recorded and that the Pontifex has weighed." The elf's dark eyes moved across the assembly. They found nothing. They were not looking for anything. They were confirming an inventory that had already been taken. "The village of Thornwell has been measured. It has been found wanting. The correction will follow."

Cassius began at the first rank. He held the tray level, moving with the careful economy of a man who has done this many times and who does not permit his opinion of the task to enter his hands. The first cup was taken by a tall man in a mask of hammered iron. He drank it the way you drink something you know is going to hurt: all at once, chin up, throat working once.

The reaction was not immediate. It never was. The Kindling was not poison and it was not medicine. What it did, as far as Corvinus understood it, was open a door that the body had been keeping shut for a reason. The Aether Shell, the part of the architecture that regulated how fast and how much you could draw, had a width. The Kindling made that width larger. Temporarily. At a cost the Ordo did not discuss and Corvinus had never been told.

The tall man in the iron mask lowered his cup. His hands were steady. His breathing was steady. And then his fingers curled, all ten at once, the tendons in his forearms standing up like cords, and a sound came out of him that was not pain and was not pleasure but was the body reporting that something had changed inside it without asking permission. The veins at his temples darkened. His chest expanded under his cuirass, one sharp inhale, held for three seconds, four, five, and then released in a controlled exhale that fogged in the close air of the chapel even though the room was not cold.

One by one, the first rank drank. One by one, the reactions came.

A man with a wooden mask went to one knee and stayed there for eight seconds, his hands flat on the stone floor, his back rising and falling. A woman whose mask was painted white bit down on a sound and held it until the cords in her neck stood out. A boy who could not have been more than nineteen gripped his own wrist with the opposite hand and squeezed until the knuckles went white, then looked up, then looked around, then looked at his own hand as though he had never seen it before.

Cassius moved to the second rank. The tray was lighter by eleven cups. His expression above the copper mask was the expression of a man carrying a tray.

Thessa Ignatia took her cup when it came. She held it for a moment, looking at the surface, at the slow ambient rotation of the liquid that had nothing to do with her hand or the room. She drank. She set the cup back on the tray, empty, and folded her hands in front of her belt, and stood very still, and the only sign that anything had happened was a single bead of sweat that tracked from her hairline to her jaw and hung there on the clay edge of her cracked mask.

Corvinus's cup arrived.

The ceramic was warm. The liquid inside smelled of copper and something vegetal, something like crushed stems, green and astringent. He could feel the heat of it through the wall of the cup, steady and alive, and for a moment the old thought came back, the one that visited him before every Kindling and that he pushed away each time with the same insufficient answer: *what is it doing to me that nobody will say out loud.*

He drank.

The taste was salt and iron and a sweetness underneath that had no name. It sat on the tongue for one second. Two. Then it was in his chest, behind the sternum, a warmth that expanded outward the way a crack expands through ice, not gradually but all at once along a line that had already been decided. His hands opened. He did not open them. They opened. The fingers spread wide and the palms turned up and the skin across his forearms prickled as though every hair had been individually lifted by a hand too small to see.

The room got louder. Not in volume. In texture. He could hear the breath of the man beside him, and in the breath the rattle of something loose in the left lung, and behind the rattle the heartbeat, thick and steady and faster than it should have been. He could hear the candle flames, each one a small particular sound, like cloth tearing very slowly. He could hear the chains of the censers, each link settling against the next with a frequency he should not have been able to distinguish from silence.

His hands were shaking.

The shaking started in the fingertips and moved inward, through the metacarpals, into the wrists. He closed his fists. The shaking did not stop. It was not fear. It was the body adjusting to a throughput it was not built to carry, the way a pipe shakes when you push too much water through it too fast.

Seraphiel watched them. He had not taken a cup. He never took a cup.

Corvinus did not know what that meant.

From the shadow behind the altar, the Pontifex moved. Cassian Tiberius Cruor stepped into the candlelight, and the room contracted.

He did not walk the way other men walked. He moved the way a debt comes due, with the inevitability of a thing that has been waiting and has decided that it has waited enough. His armor caught the light in segments, dark plate over dark cloth, the golden filigree of the recut root glyphs tracing lines across his chest that Sanctum Lux would have recognized and would not have authorized. His hood was forward. Beneath it, the jaw. The mandible, amber-stained and bare, locked in its permanent rictus. The teeth. The eyes above, deep-set and lucid and the color of heated sap.

He stopped at the front of the assembly. He looked at them. Forty-three cups, empty. Forty-three bodies, changed.

"Thornwell has been weighed," Cruor said. His voice came from a place where lips had been, shaped by a tongue and a palate and the memory of musculature that no longer existed. It was clear. It was steady. It was the voice of a man who had decided that losing his face was a finding and not a wound. "The reading is heavy. The people there have lived outside the Scale for three generations, and the weight of that distance is no longer something that conversation will correct."

He paused. The pause was not dramatic. It was the pause of a man who does not hurry because nothing he is going to say is uncertain.

"We march at dawn. You will carry the Declaration. You will give them forty-eight hours. Some of them will come to us. Some of them will take the mask. Some of them will not." His eyes, amber in the candlelight, moved across the room without settling. "For those who will not, the correction has already been decided. It was decided the moment the reading came back heavy. What happens in Thornwell is not a punishment. It is the Scale returning to level."

The assembly stood. Nobody had ordered them to stand. They were already standing. What changed was the quality of the standing, the way a held breath is different from an unheld one. The Kindling was working in each of them, opening what had been closed, widening what had been narrow, and the cost of the widening was running on a ledger that none of them could read and that Cassius Pelagiel, the man who administered it, had not been permitted to see.

Corvinus's hands had stopped shaking. They had stopped because his body had found the new throughput and accepted it, the way a bone accepts a splint, not because it is comfortable but because it has been given no alternative. He could feel the edges of things he could not normally feel. The stone under his boots had a grain to it, a vibration so low it was more a pressure than a sound, and somewhere in the walls of the chapel the masonry was settling at a rate that he understood, without being able to say how, was wrong.

He did not know what that meant either.

Seraphiel raised one hand. The assembly went quiet, though it had already been quiet. What left the room was not sound but the expectation of sound.

"The Burning Mandate compels," Seraphiel said. The words were not loud. They were not spoken to the room. They were spoken to the smoke and the stone and the forty-three bodies that the smoke and the stone now held. "Purification is mercy delivered early."

The assembly answered. Forty-three voices, muffled by forty-three masks, in a unison that was not practiced because it did not need to be:

"What burns was never meant to stand."

Corvinus said it. His mouth formed the words and his lungs pushed the air and the words came out and he meant them. He meant them the way you mean a prayer you learned before you understood it, the meaning arriving not from the words themselves but from the number of times you have said them and the things that happened while you were saying them.

In the back of the chapel, Cassius Pelagiel collected the empty cups. He stacked them on the tray, forty-three ceramics clicking against each other in the silence, and carried them through a door that led to a room that had once been a vestry and was now a store for things the Ordo did not name in front of its Attestants.

Cruor turned and walked into the shadow behind the altar. The pressure in the room lessened by a degree. Not gone. Lessened. The kind of lessening that reminds you it was there.

Seraphiel remained. His dark eyes found Corvinus across the ranks, or seemed to, or found the space where Corvinus was standing and passed through him and found the wall behind. Corvinus could not tell. He could never tell. That was the thing about the Justiciar of the Quill. You never knew whether you had been seen or whether the seeing had been for something standing in the same place you happened to be standing.

The candles guttered. Corvinus's hands hung at his sides, steady now, the veins in his forearms still visible in a way they had not been an hour ago.

Dawn was six hours away. The road to Thornwell was twelve miles of packed earth through country that had not seen an Accord patrol in two seasons.

Corvinus turned toward the door, and forty-two others turned with him, and none of them spoke, and the censers above swung in their chains in a motion too small to measure and too constant to ignore.

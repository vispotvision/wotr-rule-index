# The Honest Door

Naiser did not hesitate on the way down. He did not feel the slightest desire to.

He came down off the east steps and along the covered walk with the black coat moving round his legs and his hood back, his bare nape open to the cold, and Naevra a half-step behind him at his left with the satchel of letters under her arm. The house was waking around them. Smoke from the kitchen fires. The tap of a broom on the boards of the long hall. A boy of the archive running past with an armful of scrolls, who saw the lord of the house coming at that pace with his hair cut off and flattened himself against a pillar to let him by, eyes wide. Somewhere in the cedars a crow was calling, over and over, one harsh note.

He had been turning the words over since the reading-stone. He had found, by the bottom of the steps, that there was no arrangement of them that would make them softer, and no reason he should want one. The north was going to come back. Whatever the boy at the Foot turned out to be, whatever it cost, the north was going to return to what it had been before the war, and before Gimbzo, and before the long diminishment of the red line. He had seen the shape of it in the threads. He would not stand in front of his guests and pretend that he hadn't.

The guest quarters stood at the south end of the enclave, past the white gravel garden and its three standing stones, where the cedars came closest to the walls. The courtyard in front of them had been swept. The bundles of the Kushara party were stacked by the door, roped and ready. Farther down the path, the two great black panthers lay with their heads up and their saddles on, breathing slow plumes into the cold air, their yellow eyes turning to follow him as he came through the gate.

The Sovereign of Kushara was standing in the courtyard with his arms folded and the white mantle on his shoulders, ready for the road. The Crown Prince stood near him.

Naiser stopped at a courteous distance, on the swept stones, and bowed. Low, and exact, and held for a full breath. Naevra bowed beside him.

"Sovereign," he said. "Prince Dougou." He straightened. "Forgive me for coming on you before you've had a chance to send for me. I'd meant to wait for the farewell at the gate. But news came up the valley this morning, and I promised you at my table that you'd hear things plainly from me, and I'd rather you heard this from me than from a bird on your roof three days down the road."

He did not wait to be invited further. He did not think the Sovereign was a man who needed the ceremony.

"I have news," he said. "From the north. From four directions now, and they agree."

He drew Ulf Stark's letter out of the breast of his coat and held it, folded, in his hand. He did not offer it. He only held it, so they could see the grey wax and the wolf.

"A young man by the name of Muken," Naiser said, "whom the northern traders have taken to calling the Tokenless, led the Stark and Greymane vanguard against Kharven's Foot eleven days ago. He brought the curtain wall down at a place no one else could see a weakness in. He took the fortress in a morning." He paused. "And he met Shigure Tenrai Moto, the captain of the Foot, at the bottom of her own keep stair, and defeated her. She fled south with her arm hanging and her hands cut open, and the Foot is in northern keeping now. The Kessen road is open."

The panthers breathed. The crow in the cedars had stopped calling.

"That's the news," Naiser said. "Now here is what we believe."

He looked at the Sovereign directly.

"We have gathered enough," he said, "from enough mouths, to believe that this Muken is no tokenless at all. We believe he is the true heir of the north." He let it land. "The last of the Kōkan line. The line that sat in the red chair of the Moto for a thousand years, until Gimbzo, the last of it to rule, left the chair to the dust and put most of his own blood in the ground, and the Kurenai closed the book on the rest."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I told you at my table that there were none of them left," he said. "I said it because it was what my house believed, and because every report we had for twenty years confirmed it. I was wrong. My house was wrong. I'd rather say so to your face than have you learn it from someone else and wonder what else I've told you that isn't so."

"And he doesn't know," said Naevra.

Her voice was quiet and level, the archive voice. She had stepped up beside her brother, the satchel under her arm, and her silver-white eyes were steady on the Sovereign.

"That's the part we're surest of," she said. "Surer than of anything else. He doesn't know what he is. As near as we can piece it together, he came into the salt cuttings under Halcrest as a babe in arms, with a woman under no name the clerks would take, and he's been selling his sword since he was fourteen. He's been raised among the Lanternless. A free company out of the chain-cities. Sellswords, the kind who'll take a convoy anywhere for enough salt and bury their own on the side of the road." She paused. "A boy who grew up in a place where there was never anything to do but survive. And the Nalūn seals won't take hold of him, Sovereign. Not one. That's how the traders gave him his name. A seal binds to a house name, the name a man is entered under, and he has never been entered under any. Whatever name he was born to, nobody ever gave it to him."

Naiser let her finish. Then he went on.

"There is more," he said. "And it's the part that touches this house, and so it touches yours, while you're under our roof."

He turned, very slightly, toward the west, toward the far end of the valley where the western road came down out of the hills.

"The Moto council has split," he said. "When the captain of the Foot rode into the Moto hold with her news, Kagenori Tenrai called the seven lines to the Hall of Verdicts. Three of them refused to come. And those three are on the western road now, three days out, riding here." He looked back at the Sovereign. "We believe it's the Fox and the Owl. Tetsugen Akagane, and Kiyome Byakuya, and Hayato Amagiri with them. They're coming, as far as we can tell, to talk of peace. To mend what can be mended. And, I think, to say aloud at last some of what has been festering in the Moto house for years, and that none of them would say while Gimbzo was alive to hear it."

"Twenty men," said Naevra. "No banners. They're coming quietly."

"And the Byakuya," Naiser said, "will want to talk about the boy."

He was silent for a moment. When he spoke again his voice was lower.

"You'll know the Byakuya as the white line," he said. "The near-white chair. Peace and purification. Glass and veils. That's what they've been for a long time now. But in the old days, before the war, before Gimbzo, before any of us were born, they were something else. They were the Kōkan's own guard. The royal guard of the red line. Every Byakuya child for a thousand years has knelt and sworn it: *the red line before the white, until the end of the red line.* They were given dominion over the protection of the king and the king's children." He paused. "And in the war, when the king himself turned on his own blood, they failed to do it. They failed completely. An oath to guard the king and the king's children has nothing to say when the king is the one holding the knife. They stood aside, or were made to stand aside, and the red line went into the dust, and the Byakuya have been carrying that for twenty years."

"Kiyome Byakuya was a child when it happened," Naevra said quietly. "She grew up in the shadow of it. She's the one of them who has received our envoys ever since she was old enough to hold her own hall, Sovereign. Gave them tea. Sent them home alive, when no other Moto lord would let them through the gate." She looked down for a moment, at the swept stones. "I think she's coming here because she's just learned the red line didn't end. And I think she's coming to find out whether there's any part of that oath she can still keep."

Naiser drew a breath.

"So I'll tell you plainly what I mean to do," he said. "And then I'll tell you plainly what I don't mean to ask of you, and you can decide for yourselves what Kushara does with either."

He set Ulf Stark's letter back inside his coat, over his heart.

"I mean to open a door," he said. "Here. In this house. For every part of the Moto family that will not follow the Tenrai any longer. The Byakuya, the Akagane, the Amagiri, and whoever comes after them. I mean to receive them as I received you. With the tea, and the courtesy, and the truth." He held the Sovereign's eyes. "Because the Tenrai have shown, more than once, that they will not hesitate to escalate a thing, and fast. They took the head of the Stark chief on the floor of their own hall for telling them a truth they didn't want to hear. Kagenori means to buy the north out from under the Starks. He means to call the whole council and turn it on that boy. And when he learns that three of his own lines have ridden east to sit at a Yukari table, he'll not stop at words." He paused. "If there is to be any end to it at all, someone has to be the place where the ones who want an end can go. This house has always been a place between houses. I think it's time it did what it was built for."

The panthers had laid their heads down on their paws. One of them yawned, enormously, and showed its long ivory fangs.

"But I'll not ask Kushara to help the Moto," Naiser said.

He said it carefully, and with great clarity, so that there could be no mistaking it.

"I know what's between your house and theirs," he said. "I know it better than most. Forty years. I know what's buried in your soil, and whose weapons put it there. I sat at a table with you a week ago and listened to you speak of the dead your kingdom will be carrying for generations." His voice did not change. "There is bad blood between the Zettari and the Moto, and it will not be washed out in a week, or a year, or by any words of mine. I don't want either of you to leave this courtyard feeling that you owe the Moto anything because the Yukari have chosen to open a door to some of them. You don't. You owe them nothing. Whatever Kushara chooses, the peace between your house and mine stands exactly as it stood when we drank the tea."

"Whether you stay to see them come," said Naevra, "or ride today as you planned. The Sovereign may choose either, and this house will think no less of Kushara for it." She paused. "We'd only ask that, if you go, you go knowing. That's all my brother wanted. That you go knowing."

Naiser nodded.

"As for the boy," he said, and something in his face eased, very slightly, for the first time since he had come into the courtyard. "If Muken is half of what the north is saying he is, then I don't believe I'll need to do anything at all to help him. I think he'll do it himself. I think, given time, he'll put the Moto family back together. All seven lines. Whether they want it or not." He paused. "That's what I've seen. A shape in the snow, standing under one banner, with the north behind him and the Moto beside him. I can't see its face yet. But I think I know its name."

He was quiet for a moment.

"And I'd wager," he said, "that I'm not the only one who knows it. I'd wager the Tenrai have known what he is since the moment their captain saw his eye on that stair. And I'd wager the Starks and the Greymanes have known a good deal longer than that." His mouth tightened. "Old men don't put a sixteen-year-old tokenless at the head of their vanguard because they like his face. They knew exactly what they were sending up that ridge. Even if he didn't."

"Which means," Naevra said, "that everyone with any power in the Inner World knows what that boy is. Except the boy."

She said it very softly. It went into the cold air of the courtyard and stayed there.

Then she turned, slightly, toward the Crown Prince.

"There's one more thing you should hear, Prince Dougou," she said. "Since it touches you, and you'll hear it said in other mouths soon enough, and less kindly." Her silver-white eyes were steady. "Everyone in the west is saying it already. That the Moto began to eat themselves the day Gimbzo died. That the Tenrai and the rest of them have been at each other's throats ever since, and the north has risen, and the old grievances have all come up out of the ground at once." She inclined her head, very slightly. "It's true. It's all true. They were held together by one man's fist for a very long time, and somebody opened it."

She let that sit for a breath.

"If anyone in Kushara ever asks you why the Moto are tearing themselves apart," said Naevra Yukari, "you may tell them the truth. That they were never really whole. They only looked it, because of him. And whatever comes out of the ground now was there all along, waiting." She paused. "None of it is yours to carry."

She stepped back to her brother's side, and folded her hands, and was still.

Naiser looked at the two Zettari standing in the swept courtyard, in the cold morning, with their panthers saddled and their bundles roped by the door and the long road home to Kushara lying ahead of them, and the western road beyond the valley bringing three Moto lords toward the same gate.

"That's everything," he said. "Everything I know, and most of what I suspect. I'll not have you hear any of it from a bird."

He stood with his hands at his sides and his nape bare to the wind, and waited for the Sovereign of Kushara to answer.

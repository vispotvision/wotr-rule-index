# The House of Abscene ( Kujo Arc )

Kujo stood in the corpse of Vaeloris as though the village had been built for the purpose of receiving his footsteps. At ten feet and six inches, he rose above the broken streets with a calm that made the devastation seem ceremonial rather than chaotic, his long black hair stirring faintly in the unnatural current moving through the dead air. Around him the remains of homes, wards, halls, and roads lay buried beneath a silence too complete to be natural, a silence that did not feel like the absence of sound so much as the removal of permission for sound to continue existing there. The shape of his beauty remained intact even in that graveyard of collapse, pale skin untouched, black wings folded behind him like a cathedral’s shadow, but the elegance only made him more terrible. Beauty on Kujo was not grace. It was blasphemy wearing symmetry.

Behind him stood five figures, his Void Generals, each of them cloaked in a deliberate vagueness that seemed less like concealment and more like ontological refusal. Their outlines shifted at the edge of definition, tall and inhuman and made difficult to hold in the eye for more than a moment at a time, as though the world had not yet decided how to interpret them. One seemed too thin and too long, another too broad through the shoulders, another haloed by a dark haze that consumed the line of its own body, but none of them resolved into anything a sane witness could confidently describe afterward. They stood at Kujo’s back in a crescent of stillness, not like attendants, not even like commanders awaiting instruction, but like old disasters given rank and shape.

Kujo looked out over the ruin that had once been Vaeloris and then beyond it, past the forest, past the rivers, past the nations that still imagined themselves secure within the logic of distance. His gaze carried the slow certainty of a being who no longer regarded conquest as an ambition but as an administrative necessity. “The Material Plane,” he said, and his voice moved through the shattered village with the low, ruinous smoothness of something spoken directly into the bones of the world, “shall become the new home of Aelor.” The declaration did not ring like rhetoric. It fell with the cold finality of a filed law. “But Aelor cannot be housed until Aelor is whole. It was broken. Divided. Scattered. Its body cast across the Material World in pieces, hidden beneath kingdoms, buried beneath memory, sealed beneath the arrogance of mortal civilizations that mistook possession for inheritance.” His expression did not change, yet something cruelly reverent passed through the words that followed. “So I will gather the remains. I will return every severed fragment. I will force the world to surrender what it has stolen. And when Aelor is made whole again, this plane will learn what it means to host a god’s return.”

Then the Void answered him.

It did not open all at once. It pulled. The air above Vaeloris bent inward as though reality had been seized by an unseen hand and dragged toward some immeasurable depth beyond itself. The ruined streets trembled. Broken foundations groaned. The sky darkened not by cloud or storm but by subtraction, as though a wound had been opened in the architecture of existence and everything around it had begun leaning toward the loss. The five generals did not move. Kujo merely lifted one hand, and with that motion the Void was forced deeper into the Material Plane, its pressure descending like an invading theorem. Darkness poured without liquid, weight arrived without mass, and the entire valley of Vaeloris began to disappear beneath the emergence of something far larger than any village had the right to contain.

His palace came through the breach.

It did not simply appear. It intruded. Vast black spires dragged themselves into the world through tearing seams in space, their foundations sinking into the corpse of Vaeloris as though the village had always been a placeholder for them. Towers rose so high they vanished into a stratosphere of ash-colored distortion, their silhouettes sharp as spears and broad as mountain ranges, linked by impossible bridges, hanging citadels, descending buttresses, and walls that looked carved from compacted midnight. The palace engulfed the entirety of Vaeloris and went beyond it, swallowing the land with contemptuous scale until the old settlement became nothing more than a forgotten wound beneath its shadow. It stood so obscenely tall that it violated perspective itself; from the Old World, from the New World, even from the distant reaches of the Outer World, watchers would lift their eyes and see the black geometry of its crown interrupting the horizon like the spine of some ascending apocalypse.

Within that palace, at its center, beyond halls lined with voidfire braziers and corridors where the walls seemed to breathe with submerged hatred, Kujo entered the war chamber. The room was colossal, built for beings who did not think in human dimensions, its ceiling lost in shadow and its floor engraved with moving diagrams of continents, currents, ley-routes, Wellspring belts, and sovereign territories, all rendered in dim lines of shifting violet-black light. A circular table of polished dark stone waited at the chamber’s heart, and Kujo took the highest seat without flourish, as though hierarchy itself had simply resumed its proper shape the moment he arrived. Around him the five Void Generals seated themselves in silence, their indistinct forms settling like bad omens around a world already being measured for dismemberment.

There, with the palace anchored over the grave of Vaeloris and its impossible height casting a new axis across the three worlds, they began to plot. They spoke of scattered remains and the hidden sites where Aelor’s fragments had been sealed. They spoke of nations to be studied, infiltrated, or broken. They spoke of the Sancta Lux, of the Old World sovereigns, of the sea routes, the relay failures, the institutions that could be deceived, and the bloodlines that might yet become obstacles if left unattended. Every scheme they laid across that dark table carried the same underlying premise: the war had already begun, and the world was only slowly realizing that it had already lost the comfort of being untouched by it.

And above them all Kujo sat in perfect composure, looking down upon the shifting maps with the stillness of a tyrant who had finally brought his throne into the land he intended to inherit. Vaeloris had not become a battlefield. It had become a capital. And from its desecrated center, beneath the impossible shadow of the palace now visible across the breadth of creation, the work of remaking the Material Plane into Aelor’s future home quietly, methodically began.

Kujo sat at the head of the war table while the map of the Material World burned beneath his hand in dim violet-black lines. The five Void Generals remained around him, their vague silhouettes shifting at the edge of definition as markers moved across kingdoms, mountain ranges, buried ruins, and the scattered remains of Aelor. For several minutes Kujo said nothing. Then his attention passed over one of the symbols representing the search routes dedicated to the Void Paragon, and the entire section of the map went dark beneath his fingers.

“The search for the Void Paragon is halted.”

None of the generals questioned him. Kujo leaned back slowly, pale features composed, as though he had merely corrected an inefficient allocation of resources. “We have been behaving as though the Paragon is the beginning. It is not. A Paragon born into an empty empire is useless to me. Before we concern ourselves with what the Void will produce, we will give it something worth inheriting.”

His eyes moved across the map until they settled upon the mountain territories of the Dawi. Their subterranean cities appeared beneath the surface in layers, forge roads, mineral arteries, deep halls, sealed foundries, ancient industrial chambers, and entire civilizations built inside stone. Kujo studied them with the cold interest of a man examining machinery rather than people.

“First, we build.”

Several mountain regions expanded across the table.

“The Dawi will be the first race we enslave.”

The statement carried no anger. That absence made it worse. Kujo did not hate the Dawi, and he did not need to convince himself they were inferior. He had simply looked at what they could provide and reduced an entire people to utility.

“They understand stone better than most kingdoms understand their own borders. They build beneath pressure, beneath heat, beneath mountains that would crush human cities before the first foundation was finished. Their smiths understand metals the surface races have not learned to name, and their engineers know how to make structures endure where ordinary architecture fails.” Kujo traced one finger along the deepest Dawi settlements. “Aelor will require more than towers. It will require roads beneath continents, forges capable of shaping recovered remains, chambers able to withstand Void pressure, and foundations that will not collapse when the Material Plane resists what we place inside it.”

One of the generals shifted, a broad silhouette turning slightly toward the mountain territories.

Kujo continued. “Do not waste them.”

His pale eyes lifted.

“Break their governments. Seize their foundries intact. Separate the architects from the armies, the master smiths from the clans that would hide them, and the miners from the halls they believe sacred. The useful will work. The defiant will teach the others what refusal costs.”

The Void Generals began altering the map. Dawi trade tunnels were marked first, followed by mountain passes, ancestral vaults, forge complexes, and the deep roads connecting otherwise isolated cities. Kujo watched the campaign take shape without excitement. He did not want a massacre; corpses could not build what he required. The conquest would therefore be slower, more deliberate, and in his mind considerably more valuable.

As the planning continued, Kujo’s enormous black wings began to withdraw. Feather by feather, they folded inward without bending naturally, sinking into his back as though the darkness behind his body were consuming them. Within moments the vast span was gone, leaving only the towering shape of the man himself.

His robe responded next.

Black fabric slid over his shoulders and along the extraordinary length of his body, settling into long, severe folds that concealed much of his frame. At ten feet and six inches he remained impossible to mistake for ordinary, but without the wings his appearance became strangely more restrained, an elongated pale nobleman wrapped in darkness rather than the fallen angel who had stood over Vaeloris.

Kujo rose from his throne.

“The Dawi are not the objective,” he said as the generals looked toward him. “They are infrastructure.”

He placed both hands against the war table, and Aelor’s scattered remains glowed beneath them across the Material World.

“We will build the roads. We will build the forges. We will build the chambers that receive each fragment as it is recovered. We will build an empire capable of surviving Aelor becoming whole.”

His gaze moved from general to general.

“Then, when the world has been shaped into something worthy of the Void Paragon…”

A faint smile touched his mouth.

“…we will resume the search.”

Kujo remained seated at the head of the war table while the Ring burned beneath his hand in layered black-violet lines. Thane-Gorr, the Upper Ring, the Deep Reach, Altherion, the Corridor Holdings, every district of Stannvaard opened beneath his gaze like the internal structure of a machine. Around him, the five Void Generals waited for the command that would turn observation into invasion.

Kujo studied the Forge Guard positions, the Petralon fault-lines, the tunnel systems, and the supply routes feeding the crater cities. Then his expression settled into something almost dismissive.

“A coordinated attack is unnecessary.”

One of the generals shifted slightly, its indistinct silhouette leaning toward the map.

Kujo traced a finger along the Ring’s inner wall. “They have spent centuries preparing for armies. Every tunnel is defensible. Every road narrows where a formation benefits them. Their soldiers anchor themselves into Petralon and their guilds have turned logistics into instinct.” His finger stopped over Thane-Gorr. “Why would I reward that preparation by giving them the war they expect?”

The map changed beneath him. Military routes faded while population centers remained.

“We do not need to kill them,” Kujo said. “In fact, we should avoid it.”

His eyes moved from general to general.

“The more Dawi who survive, the better.”

There was no compassion in the statement. Kujo spoke of survival as another resource calculation.

“A dead smith is metal without hands. A dead tunnel-master is knowledge buried with its owner. A dead inscription worker is decades of discipline wasted because someone lacked patience.” He leaned back slightly, his long black robes falling in severe folds around his body. “I want their forges intact. I want their guild records intact. I want their engineers, miners, assay masters, inscription workers, and array-tenders alive.”

His pale gaze returned to the Ring.

“So we will not begin with conquest.”

The generals remained silent.

“We begin with compromise.”

Kujo raised two fingers, and faint black veins appeared across the projected Dawi population centers. They were small enough to resemble imperfections in the map rather than an attack.

“Void corruption,” he said. “Very slight.”

The words carried more menace because of how casually he delivered them.

“Not enough to transform them. Not enough to trigger Sancta Lux. Not enough for the Ore Council to declare an emergency or for the Forge Guard to mobilize.” His fingers moved slowly across the map. “A trace. Something beneath the threshold of certainty.”

The corruption spread through isolated points rather than entire settlements.

“A miner loses several seconds inside a tunnel and assumes fatigue. An assay master reads the same sample twice and remembers only one result. A lift operator forgets whether a gate was closed and checks it again. A forge-tender feels cold beside a furnace that has never failed to warm him.”

Kujo’s mouth curved faintly.

“Nothing dramatic.”

Another region darkened.

“A guild clerk misfiles an extraction order. A tunnel crew remembers receiving instructions nobody issued. A guard recognizes every face in his formation but feels, for one moment, that one of them does not belong.”

His hand closed slowly.

“Then it passes.”

The Void Generals watched the corruption markers disappear and return elsewhere.

“That is important,” Kujo continued. “Do not let it remain long enough to become measurable. Let them recover. Let physicians find nothing. Let Sancta Lux take readings and discover only people who are tired, frightened, overworked, or uncertain.”

His gaze sharpened.

“Certainty is what gives institutions permission to act.”

The war table shifted, showing the enormous machinery connecting the Ring’s guilds.

“We deny them certainty.”

Kujo pointed toward the Upper Ring extraction cities. “Begin with workers who move between levels. Transport crews. Tunnel inspectors. Assay couriers. People whose absence from one place and presence in another is ordinary enough that small irregularities will not immediately produce suspicion.”

Then he moved toward Altherion.

“Be cautious there. Sancta Lux measures too well.”

The Grand Church compound appeared on the map.

“We do not touch the Church first.”

One of the generals made a low sound that might have been disagreement.

Kujo looked toward it.

“They are useful.”

The general became still.

“Let them investigate.”

Kujo returned his attention to the map. “Every investigation requires a theory. Every theory requires evidence. Give them evidence that almost supports several explanations and completely supports none.”

He allowed the idea to settle.

“They will do the rest themselves.”

The corruption spread again, this time through trade routes and shift patterns rather than territory. One Dawi here. Another several miles away. Then three within the same guild, but separated by enough time that no immediate connection would be made.

“Do not infect families together. Do not concentrate by district. Do not create a pattern obvious enough to be named.”

Kujo’s tone remained quiet.

“Names make resistance easier.”

He glanced toward the Dawi fault-lines glowing beneath the Ring.

“Their Petralon connection is valuable. Their relationship with stone is valuable. Their entire civilization is built around the assumption that measurement produces truth.”

A faint smile appeared.

“So we begin by corrupting the distance between measurement and certainty.”

One of the generals finally spoke, its voice indistinct beneath the chamber’s pressure. “And when they discover it?”

Kujo looked toward Thane-Gorr.

“They will.”

There was no concern in his answer.

“They are Dawi.”

He rested one hand against the table.

“They will inspect. Measure. Compare. Reassay. Quarantine. Argue jurisdiction. The Ore Council will blame labor conditions. The River Crown will demand protections. Sancta Lux will insist upon independent readings. Guild masters will resist interruptions to production.”

His eyes narrowed slightly.

“And while they are proving to one another that they remain rational, we deepen the corruption.”

Not quickly.

That was the point.

“First memory. Then recognition. Then continuity.”

The black marks returned across the Ring.

“But only slightly.”

Kujo’s voice dropped lower.

“I do not want broken Dawi.”

He looked across the five generals.

“I want compromised Dawi who still believe they are themselves.”

The distinction settled heavily over the chamber.

“When the time comes, the Forge Guard should still be able to stand. The miners should still know the tunnels. The smiths should still know their temperatures. The inscription guild should still remember every line it has carved.”

His fingers closed over the projected Ring.

“They simply will no longer know when the command they are obeying became ours.”

For the first time, the map of Stannvaard dimmed almost entirely beneath his hand.

Kujo leaned back in his throne.

“No armies yet.”

His pale eyes remained fixed on the Dawi kingdom.

“No massacres.”

A faint, terrible calm entered his expression.

“We are building Aelor a home.”

He paused.

“We should not destroy the workforce before construction begins.”

The Basilica of the Drowned Meridian had been quiet enough for Lucious to hear the slow pressure of the sea beyond its black stone walls. He stood alone in one of the lower sanctums, the pale fragment of the Judger resting in his palm while its luminous measurement architecture moved faintly beneath the surface. He had been studying the piece for nearly an hour, comparing its reactions against three separate Null Scribe records, when every flame in the chamber bent toward the same point behind him.

Lucious did not turn immediately. That was instinct, not composure. Six hundred and eighty-nine years of survival had taught him what it felt like when another predator entered a room, but this was different; there had been no door, no displacement of air, no warning from the Basilica’s wards, and no pressure from the Void announcing an approach. One moment Lucious had been alone, and the next his body knew with absolute certainty that something vastly more dangerous was standing behind him.

Fear entered him cleanly.

Not panic. Lucious Dreadveil did not possess the luxury of panic anymore. His hand tightened once around the Judger fragment before discipline returned, and when he turned, the High Chancellor found Kujo standing less than three paces away.

At ten feet and six inches, the Void Regent seemed almost absurdly tall beneath the vaulted ceiling. His black wings were absent, retracted somewhere within his back, leaving his long pale body covered by the severe fall of his dark robes. Without the wings he looked less like an angelic catastrophe and more like an impossibly elongated aristocrat, which Lucious found considerably worse because there was nothing theatrical left to warn the eye.

Lucious bowed immediately.

Deeply.

“My Lord,” he said, his normally unshakable voice carrying only the smallest trace of strain. “We were not aware of your arrival.”

Kujo looked down at him for several seconds.

The silence was enough.

“I expect this obedience whenever I arrive.”

Lucious remained bowed for another heartbeat before straightening. “You will have it.”

There was no resentment in the answer. Lucious understood hierarchy too well to confuse submission with humiliation when the hierarchy was real. Kujo was his Regent, and whatever authority Lucious possessed over the Void Vanguard and the Prayers of the Hollow existed beneath a larger architecture that Kujo had just demonstrated could enter his most protected church without disturbing a single ward.

Kujo’s eyes moved toward Lucious’s hand.

“The fragment.”

Lucious opened his fingers.

Kujo took the Judger piece from him without asking permission. The pale angular relic looked almost delicate between the Regent’s long fingers, its luminous geometry shifting immediately as though attempting to measure the thing now holding it and finding no satisfactory frame of reference.

Lucious watched carefully.

He did not attempt to reclaim it.

Kujo turned the fragment once, examining the pale architecture running beneath its surface. “Sancta Lux has spent a great deal of effort searching for this.”

“They have.”

“And Alabaster?”

“Moving cautiously. Their people understand what a missing Judger fragment implies, even if they do not yet understand why we wanted it.”

Kujo’s mouth moved faintly.

“They will eventually.”

Lucious inclined his head. “Yes.”

The Regent lowered the fragment slightly and looked directly at him.

“Have you found Aelor’s Heart?”

That question changed the chamber.

Lucious’s fear did not increase outwardly, but something colder moved through him. Of every artifact, fragment, buried remnant, and ancient Void reference the Church had been instructed to locate, Aelor’s Heart occupied a category of its own. The Prayers of the Hollow had references to it scattered through texts that disagreed on almost everything else, and even those records rarely agreed on whether the Heart was literal anatomy, a metaphysical engine, or the surviving center around which Aelor could eventually be restored.

Lucious chose his answer carefully.

“Not yet, my Lord.”

Kujo’s expression did not shift.

Lucious continued before silence could become accusation. “We have narrowed several false trails. Two sites in the eastern Inner World contained remnants associated with Aelor, but neither demonstrated the structural behavior expected of the Heart. A third beneath the old sea routes was deliberately constructed to resemble a core-site and was almost certainly bait left by a later civilization that understood enough of the mythology to weaponize it.”

Kujo listened without interruption.

Lucious’s voice steadied as he returned to familiar ground. “The surviving references are inconsistent, but one pattern repeats. Wherever the Heart rested, surrounding structures did not merely contain Void influence. They reorganized themselves around it. Distance behaved differently. Recognition became hierarchical. Lesser fragments displayed directional preference.”

Kujo looked again at the Judger piece in his hand.

“So you know what to look for.”

“Yes.”

“But you have not found it.”

“No.”

The answer remained plain.

Lucious had spent centuries learning that excuses only irritated powerful men when facts would have served better.

Kujo stepped closer.

The pressure of his presence moved across Lucious without touching him. For a brief moment the High Vampire felt every instinct inherited through his bloodline demand distance, but he refused the impulse and held his place.

“Then you have been searching incorrectly.”

Lucious’s jaw tightened almost imperceptibly.

“Show me.”

That answer earned Kujo’s attention.

Lucious did not apologize. He did not defend the Church’s methods. He did not explain how many agents had died following false records or how many kingdoms had been infiltrated to trace Aelor’s remains.

He asked to be corrected.

Kujo turned away from him and walked toward the great submerged window at the end of the sanctum. Beyond the thick black glass, deep-sea creatures drifted through darkness while distant bioluminescent shapes moved like stars caught beneath water.

“Aelor was not dismantled randomly,” Kujo said. “Its remains were scattered to prevent reunion. That means the location of each fragment was chosen according to what that fragment could become if left alone.”

Lucious followed at a respectful distance.

“The Heart,” Kujo continued, “would not have been placed where it could be hidden best.”

He raised the Judger fragment.

“It would have been placed where its function could be mistaken for something else.”

Lucious’s eyes narrowed.

A political institution.

A Wellspring.

A natural phenomenon.

A sacred site.

Something ancient enough that civilizations would construct meaning around its effects without realizing what produced them.

Kujo glanced over his shoulder.

Lucious understood.

“You believe an existing civilization has built itself around the Heart.”

“I know it has.”

The High Chancellor went still.

That was considerably worse than having a location.

“If the civilization depends upon its effects,” Lucious said, “removing it may destabilize the entire state.”

Kujo faced the window again.

“Yes.”

Lucious considered that for several seconds.

“Do you want the state preserved?”

Kujo’s pale reflection appeared faintly in the black glass.

“I want the Heart.”

There was the answer.

Lucious inclined his head.

“Then I will search for dependency rather than concealment.”

“Good.”

Kujo looked down at the Judger fragment one final time before closing his fingers around it.

“And Lucious.”

The High Chancellor straightened.

“My Lord.”

“When I ask whether you have found something, do not tell me what your people have attempted.”

Kujo’s voice remained calm.

“Tell me what remains between you and the answer.”

Lucious held his gaze.

“Understood.”

Kujo stepped away from the window, robes moving silently around his long frame.

“The search for the Void Paragon is suspended. Aelor comes first. The Heart comes before the Paragon, and the Material Plane will be prepared before either is allowed to become useful.”

Lucious absorbed every word.

“And the Dawi?” he asked.

Kujo’s expression became faintly colder.

“Already beginning.”

The implication required no explanation.

Lucious bowed again.

This time, when he lowered his head, there was no surprise in the movement.

Only obedience.

“Then I will find the Heart.”

Kujo looked down at him.

“You will.”

And Lucious understood that it had not been encouragement.

Noricc entered Stannvaard without an army.

That was the first intelligence of the invasion. The second was that no one who saw him understood that an invasion had begun. He came through one of the eastern Ring approaches beneath a weather-dark cloak, broad in the shoulders and low through the center of gravity in the unmistakable manner of the Dawi, carrying a travel pack that had been repaired more times than replaced. His beard was iron-grey and divided into three heavy braids bound with plain metal rings, his hands thick with old forge scars, and his boots carried enough ore dust that a gate inspector could have guessed three different mining roads from the color alone.

He looked Dawi because he was Dawi.

Or he had been.

Noricc had been born beneath stone long before several of the younger guild halls had their current names. He remembered forge bells at shift-change, the taste of sulphur collecting at the back of the throat, the way Dawi children learned to distinguish good stone from stressed stone by sound before anyone bothered teaching them formal Petralon theory. He remembered the Assay marks his father used, the names of tunnel saints nobody prayed to anymore, and the old insult for a smith who heated metal twice because he had failed to think properly the first time.

Then he had been banished.

The Void had received him where the Dawi had refused him, and whatever had happened across the years afterward had not erased his body so much as emptied its answer to the world. He still possessed weight. His boots still struck stone, his lungs still drew air, and his blood still moved beneath skin that remained unmistakably Dawi. Yet anyone spiritually perceptive enough to look beyond the obvious found something horrifying: Noricc felt like absolutely nothing.

Not concealed.

Not suppressed.

Nothing.

Petralon did not reject him, because there was apparently nothing to reject. Resonant senses passed across his body and returned without the usual density of soul, Essence, history, or Fate. Even his ancestry seemed to terminate at the surface, as though a Dawi-shaped absence had learned how to walk, speak, remember old songs, and complain about the quality of a rivet.

That was precisely why Kujo had chosen him.

An army would make Stannvaard become itself. The gates would close, the Forge Guard would anchor into Petralon, the Ore Council would compress extraction into wartime schedules, and every Dawi from tunnel-master to apprentice would become one more piece of a civilization specifically designed to survive pressure. Noricc knew this because that was the civilization that had expelled him.

So he would not give them pressure.

He would give them familiarity.

At the first checkpoint he argued over an inspection stamp for twelve minutes because an innocent Dawi returning home after years abroad would never accept an incorrectly applied customs mark merely to appear agreeable. He criticized the inspector’s hammer, corrected the young clerk’s Dawi Common twice, and demanded that the weight of his tool chest be entered again because the scale had obviously been calibrated by “some surface-born bastard who thinks a quarter-grain is philosophy.” By the time they allowed him through, the guards disliked him thoroughly.

Noricc considered that excellent.

Trust among the Dawi was rarely built through charm. Charm was what merchants used immediately before asking for a worse price. Competence, irritation, specificity, and the willingness to argue about details nobody else cared about, that was closer to home.

He spent the first three days doing almost nothing useful to Kujo.

He drank in workers’ halls. He listened more than he spoke. He found an old extraction crew whose foreman remembered the district in which Noricc claimed to have apprenticed and allowed himself to be questioned until every answer had acquired the dull, inconvenient texture of truth. When somebody asked why he had been gone so long, Noricc did not invent tragedy.

“I was banished,” he said.

The table became quieter.

Noricc drank.

“For what?”

“Being right badly.”

That earned the first laugh.

He never explained further.

The absence of explanation traveled faster than any lie could have. Within days there were six versions of his exile moving through the Ring: murder, guild theft, forbidden inscription work, refusal of an Ore Council decree, an affair with somebody important, and one particularly imaginative account involving a collapsed forge and three naked magistrates. Noricc corrected none of them. A man hiding a secret drew suspicion; a man apparently resigned to everyone inventing a more entertaining secret became local.

Only then did he begin looking toward the Queen.

Noricc understood immediately that approaching her directly would be stupid. A banished Dawi who returned after years of silence and somehow obtained royal attention within a week was not mysterious; he was an intelligence failure waiting to be corrected. Worse, Noricc needed more than an audience. Kujo had not sent him to stand before the Queen and make threats.

He needed favor.

Favor was slower.

So Noricc solved problems.

A supply lift in an older mining tier had been grinding its third chain against the housing for six months. The maintenance guild had replaced the chain twice and blamed the shaft alignment. Noricc listened to it once, put his hand against the support stone, and told them the problem was not the chain but the anchor plate vibrating against a Petralon reinforcement seam three levels below.

They laughed at him.

Then they checked.

He was right.

A week later he identified a flaw in a forge-array that would have cracked an entire heating channel by winter. After that he noticed that three assay irregularities from separate guild shipments shared the same mathematical error and proved the fault belonged to a measuring instrument rather than the ore. He asked for no payment beyond food, access to a better sleeping room, and occasionally information offered so casually that the person giving it forgot it had been information at all.

His name began climbing.

Not quickly.

Correctly.

That was the distinction Noricc cared about.

He allowed minor officials to discover him before major ones did. Then guild factors. Then a warden. Then someone attached to the Queen’s household who had been tasked with understanding why reports from three separate guilds contained variations of the phrase the returned exile noticed it. Noricc never asked to meet the Queen.

He made it increasingly inconvenient that she had not met him.

All the while, the Void entered with him in amounts so small they could not reasonably be called corruption.

A thumb against a railing.

A hand resting on a ledger.

His palm against the shoulder of a miner laughing too loudly after shift.

Nothing visible remained afterward. There were no black veins, no altered eyes, no voices in the dark. Noricc had listened carefully when Kujo gave the order.

Slight.

So slight that the Dawi would diagnose themselves before they diagnosed an enemy.

A tunnel-master forgot whether he had completed an inspection and performed it twice. An assay clerk became absolutely certain she had entered a figure that remained absent from the record. A Forge Guard sergeant passed one of his soldiers in a corridor, greeted him, walked six paces, and suddenly could not remember whether anyone had been standing there at all.

Then the symptoms vanished.

By the time concern became organized, every affected Dawi was functioning normally again.

Noricc made certain he was among the concerned.

He was very good at that part.

“This is not contamination,” he told a guild physician after examining one supposedly affected miner. Noricc kept his brow furrowed and his voice irritated. “If it were contamination, it would accumulate. Whatever this is, it releases.”

The physician stared at him.

“You sound certain.”

“I sound old.”

Noricc looked back toward the miner.

“There is a difference.”

That statement reached the Queen.

So did another two days later, when Noricc publicly argued against quarantining a group of workers because the evidence did not establish transmission. He made himself inconveniently humane. He reminded the authorities that frightened Dawi pulled from their guild shifts would begin lying about symptoms simply to avoid being removed from work, which would corrupt the data they were attempting to gather.

Again, he was right.

Again, his name traveled upward.

Noricc knew the Queen would eventually hear the pattern she was meant to hear: a banished Dawi had returned with no political faction, no guild position, no wealth, and apparently no ambition except an almost offensive desire to point out mistakes before they became expensive. More importantly, he appeared to be one of the few people discussing the strange recognition failures without hysteria.

The Void General smiled only once when he learned that her household had requested a formal report from him.

He was alone when it happened.

For an instant the Dawi face remained, beard and scars and weathered stone-colored skin, but the thing beneath it seemed to recede even further until the room around Noricc felt occupied by an empty chair.

“Good,” he murmured.

He had not conquered one tunnel.

He had killed no Forge Guard.

He had broken no gate.

Yet somewhere above him, the Dawi Queen was beginning to believe that a returned exile might be useful.

Noricc adjusted the metal rings in his beard, collected the report he had prepared with several carefully placed conclusions that were entirely true, and started toward the upper levels.

Kujo wanted the Dawi compromised without wasting them.

Noricc intended to do better.

He would make them grateful while it happened.

It took Noricc six weeks to reach the Queen, and for the first five he behaved as though reaching her had never crossed his mind.

He did not petition the palace. He did not ask guildmasters for introductions, did not leave flattering reports where royal secretaries would conveniently discover them, and never once mentioned the crown while speaking in public. Instead, he made himself useful at precisely the level beneath royal attention, where usefulness could accumulate without looking like ambition. A disputed extraction quota between two guilds ended after Noricc found that both calculations were based on the same outdated tunnel-yield estimate. A Transport Guild argument over delayed ore shipments ended when he pointed to a pressure failure three stations down the line before the engineers responsible for the station had even realized they possessed one.

Each time, Noricc refused the larger portion of credit. That was deliberate. Men who wanted advancement collected accomplishments around themselves until ambition became visible from the other side of the room; Noricc scattered his accomplishments among other people. A junior assessor received praise for finding a flaw Noricc had quietly led him toward. A guild secretary presented one of Noricc’s recommendations as her own and discovered that he did not correct her. When a senior tunnel-master publicly thanked him for averting a collapse, Noricc answered only, “You listened when somebody beneath your station told you the stone sounded wrong. That saved the tunnel. I merely had ears.”

The story that began traveling through Stannvaard was therefore not that Noricc was brilliant. Brilliant men were common enough around the Ring to be irritating. The story was that Noricc saw things before they became expensive, had no visible faction, and appeared strangely uninterested in taking what his usefulness could buy him.

That made politicians curious.

By the third week, Noricc was being invited into rooms that technically had nothing to do with him. He sat at the end of guild dinners and listened while men with fifty years of administrative experience complained about Ore Council quotas. He attended a Transport Guild review as an “informal technical witness” and spoke only twice, both times after everyone else had exhausted themselves. He learned who despised whom, which marriages had become political treaties, which guildmasters feared retirement, which councilors believed Altherion received too much attention, and which Dawi resented that Sancta Lux could sit inside their kingdom and measure them without asking permission.

Most importantly, he learned how people spoke about the Queen when they believed the conversation harmless.

Nobody insulted her.

Noricc would almost have preferred that.

They praised her judgment. Her steadiness. Her symbolic importance to Dawi unity. Her ability to represent Stannvaard before foreign courts without allowing the guild-state’s internal complexity to become visible. They spoke of her with genuine respect, and then proceeded to discuss every important decision as though her role began after the decision had already been made.

Noricc recognized the wound immediately.

The Queen was respected enough to be protected from irrelevance and constrained enough to feel it.

He did nothing with that knowledge for another eleven days.

That patience was what finally brought him to her.

A disagreement concerning the Upper Ring reached the royal household after three guilds produced mutually incompatible recommendations. Noricc had already examined the underlying figures and knew which one was correct, but rather than submit his own finding he sent three questions through an intermediary. The questions exposed that one report had ignored labor attrition, another had treated temporary output as sustainable production, and the third had quietly assumed an expansion authorization that had never been granted.

The Queen asked who had written the questions.

For the first time, Noricc’s name entered her chambers without anyone else putting it there for him.

He still did not request an audience.

Four days later she requested him.

The dinner was deliberately small. No court spectacle, no musicians, no long ceremonial table engineered to remind everyone where authority sat. The dining chamber had been carved into old Ring stone and warmed from beneath by a narrow geothermal channel, and the table was broad enough for conversation without forcing intimacy. The Queen sat opposite Noricc rather than at its head.

Noricc noticed that immediately.

He noticed everything.

He wore formal Dawi clothing that evening, but not court clothing. Dark worked wool, iron fastenings, a high collar, and the same plain rings in his beard that he had worn when entering Stannvaard weeks earlier. Nothing about him suggested he had dressed to impress her. He looked like a Dawi who had been told dinner required cleanliness and had reluctantly complied.

What he felt like was another matter.

Nothing…

The Queen had been warned about that peculiarity. Two separate attendants had mentioned that spiritual perception behaved strangely around him, and one palace practitioner had described the experience as “finding a chair where a person is clearly sitting.” Noricc made no attempt to hide it. Concealing the absence would have made the absence incriminating.

The Queen studied him over the first course. “You have spent six weeks making yourself difficult to ignore.”

Noricc broke a piece of dark bread with thick fingers. “That would imply a plan.”

“You expect me to believe there was none?”

“No.”

That made her pause.

Noricc dipped the bread into the broth before continuing. “I expect you to believe that if my plan had been to meet you, Your Majesty, I would have done something considerably more embarrassing than correcting extraction arithmetic.”

The Queen’s mouth moved slightly. “Most men who sit at this table begin by telling me how honored they are.”

“Most men who sit at this table wanted to sit here.”

“And you did not?”

Noricc looked at her for just long enough.

“I wanted to become worth inviting.”

That was the first hook.

Not flattery.

Distinction.

The Queen leaned back slightly, and Noricc knew from the change in her posture that he had been correct about at least one thing: she was accustomed to people wanting proximity to her station. She was less accustomed to someone presenting proximity as something that ought to be earned from the other direction.

Dinner continued.

Noricc did not rush toward politics. He spoke about old forge methods, complained sincerely about modern chain-lift design, and made the Queen laugh once by describing an Upper Ring administrator as “a man who has mistaken owning six ledgers for producing ore.” When the conversation eventually turned toward governance, it was the Queen who brought it there.

“You have been asked for advice by three guilds now.”

“Four.”

Her brow lifted.

“The fourth asked me not to tell anyone.”

“Which one?”

“I was also asked not to tell anyone that.”

That earned another small smile.

Then she asked, “What do you think of the Ore Council?”

Noricc could have attacked it.

He did not.

“Excellent.”

The answer visibly disappointed her.

That was useful.

He continued eating for another moment before adding, “At the work it believes belongs to it.”

The Queen’s eyes returned to him.

Noricc set his fork down. “Twelve guildmasters can tell you how many tons of nickel leave the Ring this quarter. They can tell you the furnace losses, road attrition, labor rotation, and whether a three-percent reduction in output will affect the southern armories before winter. That is extraordinary institutional competence.”

He let the praise stand on its own.

“What they cannot do,” Noricc continued, “is decide what Stannvaard means.”

The Queen became very still.

Noricc appeared not to notice.

“They can administer the kingdom until the last forge goes cold. They can keep every tunnel open and every shipment measured. But administration answers how. It becomes dangerous when people begin allowing it to answer why simply because it answers how efficiently.”

The Queen’s fingers tightened fractionally around her cup.

There.

Not jealousy.

Not hunger for power exactly.

Something more vulnerable.

She feared being unnecessary.

Noricc lowered his voice slightly. “Forgive me.”

“For what?”

“I am discussing your own court as though you are not sitting in it.”

“No. Continue.”

Noricc waited half a breath longer than necessary.

“They respect you.”

The Queen’s expression hardened a little. “That sounded almost unfortunate.”

“Sometimes respect is the most elegant method of exclusion ever invented.”

She said nothing.

Noricc did not look away.

“A fool is ignored openly. A dangerous ruler is opposed. A respected ruler can be protected.” He spoke the final word carefully, giving it no theatrical emphasis. “Protected from unfinished reports. Protected from guild disagreements beneath the dignity of the crown. Protected from decisions someone else has already concluded are technical rather than sovereign.”

The Queen’s eyes narrowed.

“You have been here six weeks.”

“Yes.”

“And you believe you understand what reaches this table?”

“No.”

That answer stopped her again.

Noricc folded his hands before him. “I believe I understand what does not.”

Silence settled between them.

The Void General felt no triumph. Triumph made men careless. He watched instead as the Queen looked briefly toward the door, toward the attendants positioned far enough away not to hear ordinary conversation.

Noricc knew better than to tell her that her advisers diminished her. That would insult her intelligence and expose his purpose. He gave her something far more useful: permission to articulate what she had already suspected.

“They give me summaries,” she said eventually.

Noricc nodded once.

“They are supposed to.”

“Yes.”

“Sometimes after the Council has already reached a working position.”

Another nod.

“They are supposed to do that too.”

The Queen stared at him. “You disagree?”

“I think everyone involved can be behaving correctly and still produce a result nobody intended.”

That landed deeper than accusation could have.

Noricc continued quietly. “A guildmaster thinks, Why burden the Queen with a technical dispute before we understand it? Another thinks, Why give her contradictory findings when we can resolve them first? A secretary thinks, Why waste royal time with an issue likely to disappear by morning? Every choice is reasonable.”

He leaned back.

“And after twenty reasonable choices, Your Majesty learns about a crisis twenty minutes before everyone asks what you intend to do about it.”

The Queen stopped touching her cup.

There it was.

Noricc had never been told that happened.

He did not need to be.

Institutions behaved similarly everywhere. Kujo understood metaphysical standing; Noricc understood bureaucratic standing. People rarely stripped authority from rulers maliciously. More often, they made themselves useful until the ruler could no longer identify where advice ended and permission began.

“You think they do not trust me,” she said.

Noricc shook his head.

“No.”

The answer came immediately.

“I think they trust you so completely as the final answer that they have stopped permitting you to participate in the question.”

That hurt her.

He saw it.

He also saw that she liked him for saying it.

Noricc hated neither fact.

The Queen looked down at the table. “And what would you suggest?”

There.

Not what do you think.

What would you suggest.

The first surrender was always grammatical.

Noricc did not smile.

“Nothing dramatic.”

He reached for his cup.

“If you begin demanding every raw report tomorrow, the Council will know somebody has placed a thought in your ear, and they will begin managing around the thought rather than changing the habit. You will receive more paper and less truth.”

The Queen watched him carefully.

“Then what?”

“Ask earlier questions.”

Noricc placed the cup down.

“Not more questions. Earlier ones. Request the unresolved version of three matters each week. No recommendation attached. No Council position. No summary. Let them send you the disagreement before they have made it respectable.”

Her gaze sharpened.

“And then?”

“Nothing.”

The Queen frowned.

Noricc’s expression remained neutral. “Do not interfere unless interference is necessary. If every unfinished matter becomes a royal intervention, they will stop showing you unfinished matters.”

That was the second hook.

He was not feeding her desire for control.

He was feeding the deeper insecurity beneath it: the fear that others did not believe she could see uncertainty without needing to dominate it.

“You are telling me to ask for information and then deliberately not use it.”

“I am telling you to demonstrate that your knowledge is not a threat to everyone else’s competence.”

The Queen sat back.

For the first time that evening, she looked at Noricc not as an interesting returned exile but as someone whose presence might solve a problem she had never been able to name without sounding insecure.

Noricc saw the moment.

He moved away from it immediately.

“You should also dismiss me after dinner.”

That startled her.

“Why?”

“Because if I leave tonight with an appointment, half the Ring will conclude I spent six weeks engineering this meeting.”

“Did you?”

Noricc looked almost offended.

“Obviously.”

The Queen laughed.

It came unexpectedly, deeper than the earlier amusement, and Noricc allowed himself the smallest movement at the corner of his mouth.

“But,” he continued, “they do not need to know that.”

She shook her head. “You are remarkably comfortable admitting manipulation.”

“Only after it has worked.”

The humor remained for another moment before Noricc let his expression sober.

“Your Majesty, I do not need an office.”

That was a lie, technically.

He needed something better than an office.

“I need access enough to tell you when everyone around you is being reasonable in the same direction.”

The Queen studied him.

“And what do you get?”

Noricc answered carefully.

“To be useful to a country that once decided it had no use for me.”

That one contained enough truth to become almost beautiful.

His exile was real. His resentment was real. His memories of the Dawi were real. Even the ache beneath the statement belonged to him, though the Void had hollowed most ordinary feeling into something distant and cold.

The Queen recognized the truth and mistook it for loyalty.

That was precisely what Noricc needed.

She looked at him for a long while, then said, “You will dine here again next week.”

Noricc lowered his head.

“If invited.”

“You were just invited.”

“Then I will dine here next week.”

Nothing more was granted.

Nothing more was needed.

When Noricc left the palace later that evening, he carried no title, no seal, no appointment, and no written authority. Yet one of the Queen’s personal secretaries walked him to the outer gallery instead of an ordinary attendant, and before he descended the first stair she asked where correspondence could reliably reach him.

Six weeks.

No armies.

No blood.

No broken gates.

Noricc stepped into the warm mineral air of the Ring and looked down across the lights of Stannvaard burning along the crater walls.

The Queen had not given him power.

She had given him something considerably better.

A second dinner.

And by the time she realized she had begun wanting his opinion before hearing everyone else’s, Noricc intended to be the voice she mistook for her own caution.

Noricc waited until the palace had gone quiet before he wrote to Kujo. He did not use the Queen’s paper, her seal, or anything that could be traced back to the dinner; the letter was written on a strip of blackened forge-cloth in an old Dawi hand, the characters small and exact enough that any ordinary reader would have mistaken it for a maintenance notation. Only when the final line was finished did Noricc press two fingers against the cloth and allow the faintest thread of Void to enter it.

The writing disappeared.

What remained looked like nothing more than folded black fabric.

Noricc held it between two fingers for a moment, his expression unreadable. Six weeks had purchased what an army would have destroyed in a day: access, curiosity, and the beginning of dependence. The Queen had invited him back twice since their first dinner, then asked that several unresolved guild matters be routed through him before they reached her chambers. She was not compromised yet. That would have been crude.

She trusted him.

That was better.

Noricc spoke quietly to the folded cloth before releasing it. “Our progression will soon be rewarded, my Regent. The Queen has begun listening before she realizes she is listening. She believes I am giving language to doubts that were already hers, which is exactly where she needs to remain.”

The Void beneath his palm opened like a slit.

“The next stage requires you.”

Noricc’s eyes hardened.

“I will introduce her to the Void gradually. Not as worship. Not as power. As an answer to the limitations she already resents. Give me enough time and she will approach it believing the decision was her own.”

There was a brief pause before he added the part that mattered most.

“Once she opens herself willingly, her mind will be within reach. You may shape what follows.”

Noricc released the letter.

The folded cloth fell downward and vanished before reaching the stone floor.

Far away, within the palace that had swallowed Vaeloris, a narrow tear opened above Kujo’s war table. The black cloth slipped from it and landed soundlessly beside the maps of the Material World. Kujo reached for it without looking surprised.

The five Void Generals were gathered around him, though Noricc’s seat remained empty.

Kujo touched the cloth.

The hidden script returned.

He read the message slowly, one hand resting beneath his chin while the shifting map of Stannvaard burned below him. Thane-Gorr glowed within the Ring. Altherion sat along the northern rim, Sancta Lux marked within it like a pale wound surrounded by black geography.

When Kujo reached the final line, he smiled.

It was small.

That made it worse.

“Noricc remembers what conquest is for.”

One of the generals turned its indistinct head toward him.

Kujo folded the letter once and placed it beside the map. “The Dawi have prepared themselves for siege, famine, military pressure, and economic coercion. They have not prepared themselves for their Queen to decide that surrender is policy.”

The broadest of the generals leaned forward. Its outline shifted beneath armor that never seemed to remain the same shape twice.

“Will you enter her mind yourself?”

“When Noricc has opened the door.”

Kujo’s pale eyes settled on the Ring.

“I will not break her.”

His fingers moved across the map until they reached the Queen’s seat.

“I will allow her fears to become reasonable.”

The distinction drew silence from the chamber.

“A ruler who is forced knows she has been defeated,” Kujo continued. “A ruler who is persuaded believes she has discovered necessity. The second is considerably more useful.”

He turned away from Stannvaard.

The war table changed.

The Ring diminished, and the wider Material World returned beneath them. The Old World, New World, and Outer World stretched across the black stone, threaded with faint marks representing Aelor’s scattered remains, interrupted communication routes, old Wellspring concentrations, and places where the Void had begun touching the world without yet announcing itself.

Kujo looked toward one of the remaining generals.

“You.”

The figure straightened.

“Paragon watch.”

The chamber seemed to contract around the words.

“The search remains secondary. Do not commit forces to it. Observe births, anomalies, spontaneous Void phenomena, impossible continuity failures, and any individual whose existence begins reorganizing the Void around itself.”

Kujo’s expression remained calm.

“If the Void Paragon surfaces, I want to know before the churches invent a prophecy explaining why they noticed second.”

The general lowered its head.

Kujo then looked toward the other three.

“You wait.”

None reacted.

“No independent campaigns. No demonstrations. No kingdoms touched because you have grown impatient.”

His voice never rose.

“Noricc is building our first acquisition. I will not have the rest of you frighten the Material Plane into cooperation before we understand which parts can be taken without war.”

One general shifted, its outline briefly suggesting horns before the shape disappeared again.

“And Aelor’s remains?”

“Still priority.”

Kujo placed one long finger over several scattered marks.

“Watch them. Trace who guards them. Learn which nations depend upon them without knowing what they are. But do not move until I give instruction.”

His gaze passed over all four generals.

“We are not raiders.”

The black palace groaned around them as another portion of its architecture settled deeper into the Material Plane.

“We are building permanence.”

Kujo picked up Noricc’s letter again and looked once more toward the mark representing Stannvaard.

The smile returned.

“Let Noricc have his Queen.”

His fingers closed around the cloth.

“Then I will teach her what she has been missing.”

Noricc never introduced the Queen to the Void as power. Power would have made her cautious, and caution would have brought Sancta Lux, the Ore Council, and every old Dawi instinct against corruption into the room at once. Instead, he introduced it as refinement. The first sample was no larger than a grain of blackened metal resting inside a shallow Petralon dish, spiritually empty enough that every instrument placed near it reported almost nothing. When she asked whether it was Void, Noricc answered simply that it was a trace, enough to observe but not enough to obey. Then he placed it beside a minor inscription array and allowed the smallest corruption to remove a redundant relational thread. The array did not collapse. It became cleaner, quieter, more efficient. To the Queen, it looked less like corruption and more like unnecessary complexity being stripped away.

She asked him to repeat the demonstration, and Noricc obliged only after making her wait. That reluctance was part of the design. If he offered the Void too eagerly, she would recognize recruitment; if he appeared cautious, every step closer to it became her own decision. The second sample came days later, hidden beneath a table while she reviewed three versions of the same Ore Council report. The Void introduced tiny discontinuities in the associative weight of the annotations, making repeated conclusions feel less authoritative simply because others had agreed with them. Suddenly she noticed how many recommendations reached her already softened, filtered, and polished into decisions she was expected merely to approve. Noricc never told her what to think. He only let her experience the feeling that the Void removed other people from the space between herself and the truth.

That was where her old insecurity began to sharpen. She had always feared that Stannvaard respected her so completely that it had learned to function without needing her. Every report already resolved before reaching her, every guild master who said they had spared her unnecessary detail, every unanimous recommendation began to sound like another version of exclusion. Noricc fed the wound carefully, never by flattering her and never by declaring the councils disloyal. He asked questions instead: how often did the final authority receive the problem only after everyone else had decided what the solution should be, and at what point did protection from unnecessary burden become protection from relevance? The Queen began requesting unresolved reports, attending preliminary sessions unannounced, and demanding to see the questions before her ministers could turn them into answers.

The Void samples remained small enough that none of this looked unnatural. A shard beside her during a difficult meeting made the room seem quieter. A trace incorporated into a Petralon exercise made competing pressures easier to separate. Noricc taught her to place a hand against stone, feel every load acting through it, and imagine removing one influence at a time until only what was necessary remained. For a Dawi ruler, the exercise felt intuitive. Soon she was applying the same thinking to government. Committees became redundant loads, duplicated reviews became wasted weight, and independent offices began looking like unnecessary stress points in a structure that would function better if authority traveled through fewer hands.

At first, the reforms were reasonable. A duplicated review board disappeared. A transport committee was absorbed into a larger office. Several reports that once took days to reach the throne arrived directly within hours. The state did move faster, and Noricc made sure to say so without praising her. That restraint only deepened her trust in him. When she accused him once of believing she was taking too much authority, he answered that perhaps she was only discovering how much had never truly been hers. The sentence remained with her long after the conversation ended.

Control slowly stopped being a political concern and became emotional relief. The Queen began sleeping less, keeping ledgers beside her bed, reading mining projections at midnight and troop rotations before breakfast. Every unresolved matter irritated her because unresolved now meant uncontrolled, and every decision made without her knowledge felt increasingly personal. The more authority she gathered, the more intolerable the remaining uncertainty became. Noricc recognized the transition before she did, because he had been waiting for the moment when desire stopped being ambition and became fixation.

It happened over a map of the Ring. A transport master had altered a shipment schedule within his lawful authority, and the Queen stared at the route longer than the issue deserved. When Noricc reminded her that the man had not required royal permission, her fingers tightened against the table and a dark crimson pressure appeared around her hand. It was faint at first, barely more than a distortion, but it curled around her wrist when she said that he should have needed permission. Noricc lowered his gaze before she could see the satisfaction in it. The Obsession Force had manifested exactly where he expected it to: not from an artificial implant, but from her own fixation on control.

When she noticed the crimson-black strands and asked whether they were Void, Noricc told her no. That answer mattered. He explained that the Force was hers, born from fixation, while the Void had merely removed enough internal noise for the pressure to become visible. She asked what the fixation was centered upon, though by then both of them already knew. Noricc looked toward the map rather than answering immediately, and the Queen understood. Control had stopped being something she wanted because rulers required it. It had become the thing she used to silence the fear that she was unnecessary.

Noricc then did what she did not expect: he told her to stop using the samples. The fear that crossed her face was brief but unmistakable, and it was not fear of corruption. It was fear of losing access to the clarity they gave her. She argued that the Obsession Force was hers, demanded to know why she should stop, and Noricc answered that something belonging to her could still be changed by something that did not. He recommended a week without exposure. She demanded three days, then one, and finally ordered him to give her the shard immediately. Noricc resisted just long enough for the command to feel like hers before bowing and obeying.

After that, the corruption advanced in increments too small to resemble a transformation. The first samples had removed noise; now they began altering what she considered noise. Dissent felt inefficient, uncertainty felt insulting, and independent authority began to resemble structural disorder. Each time she centralized an office, the pressure behind her eyes eased. Each time a minister obeyed without delay, the Obsession Force settled. The Void was teaching her nervous system a simple relationship without ever speaking a word into her mind: control ended discomfort, therefore discomfort meant insufficient control.

Noricc was careful not to let the process become obvious. Some days he removed every sample and argued against her decisions so she could reassure herself that she remained capable of disagreement. Other days he encouraged her to reverse an order, because corruption that survived reversal was stronger than corruption sustained by momentum alone. The Queen increasingly trusted him precisely because he remained the one man willing to tell her no. She never realized that his resistance was part of the architecture.

By the seventh week, she no longer asked whether the Void was dangerous. She asked whether it could be made precise. Standing beside a private forge, with a small black crystal suspended inside a Petralon ring, she asked whether it could distinguish what belonged from what did not, whether it could remove interference without damaging the structure, and whether someone who understood the structure well enough could control that process deliberately. Noricc let the questions hang between them before telling her that such a person would no longer be using the Void merely as a sample.

When she asked what she would be using instead, Noricc gave her the answer he had been guiding her toward from the beginning: authority. The Obsession Force tightened around her wrist, crimson strands darkening as a thread of black entered them so subtly that the Queen never noticed the difference. Noricc did. Kujo had ordered him not to break her, and he had followed that command perfectly. She was still intelligent, still capable, still herself, but one desire had been made louder than all the others. Her fear of becoming ceremonial had become a need to decide, that need had become a need to control, and now the need for control had become an opening the Void could enter without ever needing to force the door.

Noricc introduced the idea carefully, long after the Queen had begun trusting his judgment enough that advice no longer felt like intrusion. He waited until the end of an exhausting Ore Council session, when three guild masters had spent two hours arguing over succession privileges, trade authority, and whether the Crown possessed sufficient independent standing to renegotiate a northern corridor compact. Only after the chamber emptied did he mention Rainmere, almost as though it were merely another line in the ledger. “There is another solution to the question of standing,” he said. “Not administrative. Dynastic.” The Queen looked at him immediately, suspicion sharpening her expression, and Noricc continued before she could reject the thought. “A political marriage would give the Crown an external pillar that does not belong to the Ore Council, Sancta Lux, or any institution already capable of filtering your authority.”

She disliked the suggestion on instinct. Marriage meant another person close enough to become a competing center of influence, and the obsession that had begun wrapping itself around control recoiled at the idea before reason could examine it. Noricc anticipated that reaction perfectly. He did not tell her she needed a husband, nor did he appeal to romance. He framed the arrangement as structural independence: a consort without hereditary claim over Stannvaard, backed by foreign wealth and diplomatic recognition, could expand the throne’s reach while remaining legally subordinate to her sovereignty. “You would not be marrying upward,” Noricc told her. “You would be importing leverage.”

That phrasing changed the conversation.

The name he eventually offered was **Ser Luthor of Rainmere**, an aristocrat whose reputation had been curated with extraordinary care. Rainmere was distant enough not to threaten Dawi territorial identity but sufficiently connected to merchant houses, river courts, and frontier nobility that Luthor’s presence could open doors Stannvaard currently approached through guild intermediaries. He possessed wealth without an army large enough to alarm the Ironfold states, lineage without a crown of his own, and enough political polish to survive a Dawi court without appearing desperate for one. Most importantly, the records surrounding him were flawless because Kujo had not invented Ser Luthor overnight. He had constructed the identity backward through recognition, creating enough social residue that investigators would discover old invitations, estate documents, correspondence, witnesses, and memories rather than an obvious blank where a man should have been.

Noricc made certain the Queen investigated him before agreeing to a meeting.

Every inquiry returned something reassuring.

Rainmere merchants remembered him. A minor noblewoman recalled dancing with him years earlier. Customs ledgers showed his household crossing regional boundaries. Old financial disputes existed with exactly enough imperfection to make the identity feel real. Ser Luthor had friends, enemies, debts, preferences, and an uncle whose handwriting appeared in three unrelated archives. Nothing about him looked manufactured because Kujo understood that perfect history was suspicious. Real people accumulated contradiction.

When Luthor finally arrived in Stannvaard, he came without spectacle.

No enormous procession announced him. No jeweled carriage attempted to overwhelm Dawi austerity. He entered with a compact household, six guards, two secretaries, a physician, and several wagons carrying Rainmere steelwork, books, preserved wine, and trade gifts selected for guild relevance rather than royal vanity. Even his clothing was measured carefully: dark charcoal formalwear cut in long continental lines, subdued silver embroidery, gloves removed before entering the Queen’s audience chamber so that nothing stood between his hand and Dawi custom. Kujo had compressed his impossible height into a more plausible human frame for the disguise, reshaped the pallor of his skin into a warmer complexion, and softened the supernatural precision of his features just enough to become handsome rather than alarming.

Noricc watched from beside the throne as Ser Luthor bowed.

“My Queen.”

The voice was nothing like Kujo’s usual register. Warmer. Easier. Educated without sounding rehearsed. It possessed the confidence of a man accustomed to courts but none of the oily deference that made powerful rulers suspect they were being handled.

The Queen studied him. “You traveled a long way for a conversation.”

Luthor smiled. “I was told Stannvaard dislikes men who arrive assuming conversation is already agreement.”

Something almost amused entered her eyes. “Who told you that?”

“Three people. All of them contradicted one another about everything else.”

That earned the first real smile.

Noricc lowered his gaze.

Kujo was better than he expected.

The first audience lasted forty-three minutes longer than scheduled. Luthor never tried to dominate it. He asked about the Ring, listened while the Queen explained Petralon engineering, and displayed just enough technical understanding to ask intelligent questions without pretending mastery of Dawi disciplines he had supposedly encountered only through trade. When guild relations came up, he did not immediately agree with her complaints. That was the first point at which Noricc understood exactly how Kujo intended to capture her attention.

“You think they diminish me,” the Queen said after describing the way recommendations reached the throne already resolved.

Luthor considered the statement instead of rushing to validate it. “I think institutions become efficient by reducing the number of decisions that require exceptional people.”

Her expression cooled slightly.

I came because you possess an elegant mechanism serving an obsolete philosophy.” The bladed arm rose while the other hand remained ready, the hand meant not to strike, but to enter. “I am going to end you, take the Crevice from your heart, and give collapse a purpose larger than the one you have spent epochs defending.”

Kujo’s tendrils spread until the darkness behind him resembled a second abyss.

“Defend it well.”

His smile returned, thin and terrible.

“I require it intact.”

The first collision erased distance.

Valmir came forward with the weight of an ending already decided, one colossal arm sweeping through the Crevice as the Law of Rupture gathered around the motion. Space compressed before the blow landed. Crystal terraces folded inward, molten rivers changed direction, and gravity became a weapon in its own right, dragging everything toward the path of the Titan’s fist. Kujo met it head-on. His bladed arm came across in a black-red arc while a dozen tendrils anchored behind him, and when the two powers struck, the Crevice did not explode so much as lose agreement with its own shape. Whole shelves of stone vanished into dust, reappeared several miles away, and broke again before they finished arriving.

Kujo was driven backward through three suspended crystal formations, each one collapsing around his body in luminous fragments. Valmir followed without hesitation. The Titan’s footsteps did not simply shake the ground; they instructed the surrounding strata to begin failing, every impact sending golden fractures racing outward through stone, Essence, and local continuity. Kujo tore himself free before the next blow arrived, one enormous tendril wrapping around Valmir’s forearm while another pierced into the Crevice wall and pulled. The Void Regent twisted with brutal leverage, redirecting the Titan’s own momentum and driving his shoulder into the canyon face hard enough to split a mountain-sized section from the wall.

Valmir answered by opening the fracture.

The crack Kujo had created spread through the Titan’s shoulder, down his chest, and across his opposite hip in one brilliant golden line. For an instant it looked as though Kujo had broken him. Then Valmir allowed the fracture to continue beyond himself. The break passed out of his body and into the surrounding world, transferring the failure into the terrain. Stone miles away tore apart while Valmir’s body sealed behind it, whole again.

Kujo laughed.

“Still outsourcing the bill.”

Valmir struck him across the torso.

The blow launched Kujo through the air with enough force to turn the atmosphere around him incandescent. He disappeared into the far wall and emerged through the other side beneath a storm of pulverized crystal, crimson channels across his true form flickering where Valmir’s rupture had entered deeper than the surface. One of Kujo’s tendrils hung severed. Another had split lengthwise. Black matter crawled along both injuries and began reconstructing them, though not with Valmir’s clean cycle of collapse and renewal. Kujo’s body simply refused to treat absence as final.

“You confuse preservation with debt,” Valmir said, the words rolling through the Crevice like an earthquake. “Everything returned sustains what follows.”

Kujo stepped from the crater.

“And you confuse recycling with virtue.”

He moved.

The difference in scale ceased mattering.

Kujo crossed the distance in a single collapse of absence, appearing beneath Valmir’s guard with the bladed arm already rising. It cut through the Titan’s lower ribs and opened a seam wide enough to reveal molten gold and trapped stars within. Before the wound could close, Kujo drove three tendrils into it and pulled in opposite directions.

Valmir roared.

The sound passed through every slab in the Crevice at once.

Then his body broke deliberately.

The entire right side of the Titan shattered into thousands of burning crystal plates, escaping Kujo’s grip by becoming debris. The fragments circled once around the chamber, gathered behind Kujo, and reconstructed into Valmir’s arm with the fist already descending.

Kujo barely turned in time.

The impact buried him.

A crater opened through several layers of the Crevice, each floor collapsing into the next until the descent became a shaft of molten rock and broken crystal. Valmir stood over it, golden seams blazing, one hand still extended into the wound in the earth. The Law of Rupture followed Kujo downward, forcing each successive layer to fail around him.

Then darkness climbed back up the hole.

Not shadow.

Void.

Kujo erupted from it with both arms spread and his tendrils behind him like a black storm. The next exchange became too fast and too violent for the Crevice to preserve cleanly. Valmir fractured Kujo’s shoulder. Kujo severed a section of Valmir’s thigh. Valmir transferred the damage into a distant stone formation. Kujo removed the space between two of the Titan’s joints and forced them into collision. Valmir released the entire limb, broke it apart, and rebuilt it before the imbalance could travel into his stance.

Neither gained ground.

They fought across the Crevice for what could have been minutes or hours. The domain had ceased presenting time honestly.

Valmir called mountains downward and made their failure arrive early. Kujo stepped through absences between falling mass and emerged where no path should have existed. Kujo spread Void through the Titan’s fractures and tried to prevent their reconstruction. Valmir answered by completing the collapse himself, discarding entire sections of his body before the corruption could settle and rebuilding from remembered structure.

At one point Valmir caught Kujo by the throat.

The Titan’s hand closed around the Void Regent and the surrounding space compressed with it. Golden light poured through Valmir’s arm while the Law of Rupture entered Kujo’s body directly, searching for the oldest structural contradiction it could force open.

For the first time, Kujo’s true form convulsed.

Cracks ripped across his torso.

One of the crimson channels beneath his skin went dark.

Valmir tightened his grip.

“All things reach completion.”

Kujo’s claws closed around one of Valmir’s fingers.

“So do mistakes.”

He tore it off.

The severed piece disintegrated into molten crystal before it struck the ground, but Valmir’s grip loosened just enough. Kujo vanished from the Titan’s palm and appeared above his shoulder, driving the bladed arm down through the base of Valmir’s neck.

The Crevice flashed white-gold.

Valmir threw him away.

Both recovered.

Both stood.

For the first time since the battle began, neither immediately attacked.

Kujo’s true form had been damaged in a dozen places. Several tendrils were still reforming. Crimson light leaked intermittently through fractures in his torso, and his bladed arm had lost part of its edge beneath repeated contact with Valmir’s law. The Titan was no better. Golden seams crossed nearly his entire body now, some repairing slowly where Kujo’s Void had contaminated the normal cycle, while sections of his chest remained partially transparent from repeated forced reconstruction.

They had reached the same conclusion.

At this depth, they were equal enough to continue indefinitely.

Valmir saw it as proof.

Kujo saw it as inconvenience.

“You expected superiority,” Valmir said.

Kujo looked at his damaged hand and flexed the fingers once.

“No.”

He glanced upward into the blackness above the Crevice.

“I expected this layer to be insufficient.”

Valmir became still.

That was the first moment the Titan understood Kujo had not yet brought everything with him.

The darkness above them changed.

The Void Kujo had used until then had possessed recognizable behaviors: absence, severance, collapsed continuity, spaces through which he traveled and conditions he imposed upon matter. Terrible, but legible. Valmir had fought it before. He understood how to redirect some of it, how to release contaminated structure, how to allow collapse before Kujo’s negation could become permanent.

What descended now was different.

It came from deeper.

A higher layer of the Void did not announce itself by consuming light. Light continued to exist. It simply stopped being sufficient evidence that something was there.

Valmir’s golden fractures dimmed.

The Archive of Dust faltered.

Not failed.

Faltered.

That alone had not happened since the Second Epoch.

Kujo lifted his head as the deeper stratum entered him.

His true form changed subtly rather than dramatically. The crimson channels beneath his black flesh darkened toward a color the eye could not hold. His tendrils lost their visible edges. Portions of his body seemed to occupy several incompatible distances at once, near enough to touch and impossibly far away in the same instant.

Valmir shifted his stance. The Crevice responded late.

Kujo smiled.

“There.”

Valmir raised one hand and the Law of Rupture descended upon him.

Nothing happened.

Not because Kujo resisted it.

Because for one impossible instant the law could not establish whether he had entered the state that was supposed to rupture.

Valmir’s head moved slightly.

Kujo noticed.

“You require a sequence.”

The Void Regent took one step forward.

“Structure.”

Another.

“Recognition.”

The higher Void moved around him like an absence of premise.

“And, most importantly, a transition between what something is and what it becomes when it falls.”

Valmir’s golden light intensified.

Kujo kept walking.

“This layer has very little respect for transitions.”

Valmir struck.

The Titan’s fist crossed the Crevice with enough force to split several floating formations behind Kujo before the blow even arrived. Kujo did not evade.

The fist passed through the space containing him.

For a heartbeat Valmir’s hand existed on both sides of Kujo without ever having crossed his body.

Then Kujo grabbed the wrist.

This time Valmir could not release the damage fast enough.

Kujo’s bladed arm descended and carved through the Titan’s forearm. The fracture tried to transfer into the Crevice.

The higher Void intercepted the relationship.

The wound remained.

Valmir looked at it.

For the first time in the battle, something he had lost did not immediately return.

Kujo’s smile widened.

“That is the difference.”

Valmir tore his arm backward and drove his other hand into Kujo’s chest, releasing enough entropic authority to collapse every structure within the impact radius. Kujo was blasted across the Crevice, his torso splitting apart beneath the hit.

But he came back faster.

The wound in his body closed through Voidic refusal.

The wound in Valmir’s arm did not.

The standstill broke.

Valmir attacked with greater force now. Crystal continents came apart around them. Gravity rotated through impossible axes. Golden fissures opened across the floor and sent waves of collapse upward, each one capable of dismantling whole civilizations if released beyond the Crevice.

Kujo began passing through them. Not all of them. Enough. And each time he returned, another piece of Valmir failed to reconstruct.

A section of shoulder. A line through the ribs. Two fingers. A fracture beneath the knee. Small losses at first. Then accumulated ones.

Valmir understood the danger and changed tactics, collapsing whole regions of his own body before Kujo could contaminate them, rebuilding farther from contact. Kujo anticipated the adaptation. Higher Void spread along the transition itself, interfering not with destruction or reconstruction but with the relationship between the two.

Valmir staggered.

The Crevice screamed through stone.   Kujo appeared directly before him.

“You were right about one thing.”

His monstrous hand drove into the Titan’s chest.

Valmir caught his wrist before it could penetrate fully.

The force of the stop shattered the ground beneath both of them.

“All things must fall to reveal their shape,” Kujo said.

Valmir pushed against him.

Kujo pushed back.

For several seconds the two stood locked together, Titan against Void Regent, golden entropy against depthless absence, neither moving more than inches while the entire Crevice deformed around their struggle.

Then the higher Void descended another degree.

Valmir’s knee touched the ground.

Only one.

Only for a moment.

But it happened.

Kujo looked down at it.

Valmir looked up at him.

The balance had changed.

Kujo’s voice became almost quiet.

“And now…” His fingers pressed deeper into the golden fracture beneath Valmir’s sternum. “…I can finally see yours.”

Kujo kept his hand buried in the fracture beneath Valmir’s sternum, but he did not pull.

That was the first difference between extraction and destruction.

The higher layer of the Void remained wrapped around his arm, not devouring what it touched, but separating relationships with impossible precision. Valmir’s body convulsed around him as golden fractures raced across the Titan’s chest, searching for some route by which the damage could be transferred into the Crevice. Ordinarily, the Titan would have released the failing structure, allowed the wound to become terrain, and reconstructed himself from the remembered pattern. Kujo denied him that option without denying the pattern itself. The Void formed a perfect interval around the thing buried inside Valmir, isolating the Crevice from flesh, Wellspring, Archive, and renewal one connection at a time.

Valmir’s voice came through the stone beneath them, weaker now but no less immense. “You do not know what you are separating.”

Kujo’s fingers tightened around something that did not possess an ordinary shape. “I know exactly enough.”

The Crevice inside Valmir was not an organ. It was a junction: a concentrated fold where the Titan’s body met the Wellspring of Collapse, where the Veil Threshold passed through him, where broken Matter surrendered its burden and where Urionic memory prevented endings from becoming meaningless waste. To remove it carelessly would have torn the surrounding substrata open with him. Kujo had no intention of damaging what he had come to steal.

So he used the Void as a surgeon would use space between tissues.

Thin black boundaries appeared inside Valmir’s chest. One passed between the Crevice and the Archive of Dust. Another separated it from the Interval Between. A third slid through the Aegoric structure by which Valmir’s body carried lawful rupture without collapsing into uncontrolled ruin. Each separation should have triggered catastrophic recoil. Instead, Kujo’s higher Void created temporary absence between cause and consequence, giving each severed system nowhere to discharge its failure.

Valmir understood what was happening before Kujo finished.

“You are removing the return.”

“Yes.”

The Titan’s remaining hand closed around Kujo’s shoulder, but there was little force behind it now. Kujo barely acknowledged the grip. His attention remained within the golden cavity in Valmir’s chest as the Crevice began revealing itself, first as a line of black-gold light, then as something deeper, a narrow vertical fracture hanging inside the Titan without touching surrounding flesh. Looking into it produced the sensation of falling toward every ending that had ever become another beginning.

Kujo smiled.

“There you are.”

The higher Void closed around the fracture.

For one moment the entire Crevice resisted him.

Every suspended slab shattered simultaneously. Molten rivers surged upward. Gravity folded toward Valmir. The Archive of Dust screamed in voices too old to belong to language, and the golden seams throughout the Titan’s body became blinding as the world attempted to preserve the office that had regulated collapse since the Second Epoch. Kujo planted his feet and allowed the force to gather around him.

Then he removed the relationship that said the Crevice still belonged inside Valmir.

The resistance stopped. Kujo drew his hand backward. The Crevice came with it.

There was no spray of blood, no violent tearing of an organ from flesh. The extraction was almost obscenely clean. The vertical fracture slid from Valmir’s chest enclosed within a cocoon of higher Void, perhaps three feet long in Kujo’s grasp and yet containing depth that had no meaningful measurement. Black crystal edges surrounded molten golden fissures, while tiny fragments of creation appeared within its interior, collapsing into dust and becoming light again before the eye could follow the cycle.

Valmir staggered. The cavity in his chest did not bleed. It simply had nothing left to organize around.

Golden seams spread outward from the emptiness, but this time they did not close. The Titan attempted reconstruction and found no central law telling the returning pieces what completion meant. One shoulder fractured and remained fractured. A section of his chest became glass dust and failed to return. The stars within him flickered unevenly.

Kujo looked down at the Crevice in his hand. It pulsed. Once.

The force that entered him nearly made even his true form move.

Entropy poured through his arm in a tremendous surge, not as destruction but as comprehension. He felt the lawful exhaustion of stars, the quiet surrender of mountains, the decomposition of bodies into soil, the moment exhausted Essence ceased clinging to one shape and became available to another. Entire cycles of collapse opened inside his perception. For an instant Kujo understood why Valmir had called the function necessary.

He found the conclusion tedious.

“So much power,” Kujo murmured, studying the fracture, “spent making death charitable.”

Valmir remained standing behind him.

Barely.

“You cannot replace me.”

Kujo turned.

“I do not intend to.”

The Titan’s body continued failing in stages. Without the Crevice, his Aegoric function still attempted to rupture stagnant structure, Bairosian timing still searched for the correct moment of completion, and Urionic memory continued trying to preserve what was lost. But there was no longer a central junction binding those functions into Valmir. He had become several divine laws attempting to inhabit the memory of one Titan.

Kujo regarded him without triumph.

“You fulfilled your purpose very well,” he said. “That is precisely why I needed what was inside you.”

Valmir raised his head.

The stars within his fractures were going dark.

Kujo extended his free hand.

The Void answered.

Not the shallow absence he had used to travel. Not the deeper layer that had disrupted Valmir’s reconstruction. Something colder moved through the Titan now, entering the spaces between his remaining functions and expanding them until relationship itself became impossible. The Law of Rupture could no longer reach renewal. The Archive could no longer reach what had fallen. The Interval could no longer measure the passage between ending and return.

Valmir began to come apart.

Slowly.

The Titan’s immense arm separated along its golden seams and dissolved into crystal dust. His chest followed, great plates of volcanic glass loosening and drifting away as though gravity had forgotten its claim. The molten light behind them darkened from gold to red, then from red to nothing. There was no cycle this time.

No reconstruction.

No return.

Kujo had not destroyed Valmir with entropy.

He had killed him with absence between the parts that made entropy meaningful.

Valmir’s final voice arrived through the ground as a fading vibration.

“All things… must fall.”

Kujo watched the Titan’s head fracture.

“Yes.”

The last golden seam extinguished.

“But not everything rises afterward.”

Valmir collapsed.

The sound traveled through the Crevice for miles, though almost none of the Titan actually struck the ground. Most of him disintegrated before impact, enormous pieces becoming dust, then smaller dust, then a faint descending shimmer that the Archive could no longer collect. What remained of the Riftlord settled across his own dominion like the ruins of a mountain whose geological purpose had abruptly ended.

Kujo stood alone among it.

The Crevice continued pulsing in his hand.

Its power had begun entering him more deeply now. Crimson channels throughout his true form flashed gold-black, and for several seconds his tendrils spread involuntarily across the dead Titan’s domain. Every pulse showed him another structure preparing eventually to fail. Every crack became legible. Every aging material revealed the direction of its deterioration.

Kujo closed his fingers around the fracture.

“Beautiful.”

The word was almost affectionate.

He wrapped the Crevice in successive layers of Void until its lawful entropy could no longer interact freely with the surrounding world. Not containment in the ordinary sense. Isolation. The artifact remained fully alive with its own cosmic function, but Kujo placed enough absence around that function that nothing outside the shell could accidentally inherit it.

Only then did his body begin to diminish.

The grotesque architecture of his true form folded inward. Tendrils withdrew into his back one after another, bladed anatomy narrowing, crimson channels disappearing beneath pale flesh as his immense silhouette compressed toward the hauntingly beautiful humanoid shape he preferred within the Material World. The Crevice remained suspended beside him, wrapped in black layers that made its golden interior appear impossibly distant.

Kujo looked once at what remained of Valmir. No bow. No farewell.

No acknowledgment beyond the fact that the Titan had been sufficiently difficult to make the journey worthwhile.

A vertical absence opened before him.

Through it waited the Material World.

Kujo stepped toward the threshold, carrying in one hand the mechanism that had once ensured collapse remained part of renewal.

Before passing through, he glanced back at the dead Crevice and the dust of its former master.

“You were correct about one thing, Valmir.”

His pale eyes lowered toward the fracture he had stolen.

“Everything reveals itself when it falls.”

Then Kujo crossed the threshold.

The opening closed behind him.

And for the first time since the Second Epoch, the Material World continued turning without Valmir beneath it.

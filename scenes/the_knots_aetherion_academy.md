# The Knots — Aetherion Academy

Niran let the joke go past him the way he let wind go past a ward, without bracing against it. Two of the strongest men alive. He was not sure he was one of them, and he was certain it would not matter to whatever had tied the road. But Xhem had always made a joke when the room got heavy, the way some men touch the hilt of a knife they have no plan to draw, and Niran let him have it.

The question was the part that mattered. He answered it in order.

"Both." He kept his hands loosely clasped in front of him. "The old knots were travellers. The new ones are the road."

He looked down at the floor between them for a moment, at the grain of the boards and the ash fallen out of the tray, and laid it out the way he would have laid out a failing wall for an apprentice: what broke first, what broke after, and which crack was carrying the weight.

"Knots on that road usually mean a failure to bring someone back. There have always been some. A grief-rite in a hill village. A hedge-necromancer with a book he shouldn't have. A mother who will not let go of her son. Things that die were never meant to come back cleanly, and the dead know it even when the living don't. The soul starts home, someone on this side pulls, it tears loose going the wrong way, and the road keeps the tear. Those knots are coarse. You can feel the panic in them." His voice stayed quiet. "They've thinned out these last months. I took that for good news."

He paused there. The pause had a shape to it, a man looking again at something he had already filed.

"The ones I found this week are different. Small. Fine. All of them the same size, and each one a little tighter than the last." He turned one hand over, palm up, as if there were thread in it. "That's a seam under practice. The failures thinned out because whoever is doing this stopped failing the coarse way, the way an apprentice stops tearing the paper once he learns how hard to press. Now they fail small, correct, and try again."

The thread-marks along his forearms brightened a shade under his sleeves and settled.

"Behind those small ones there's a paradox. The road holds her as dead, and your infirmary holds her as alive, and both records are true. The world does not keep two true records of one soul for long." He met Xhem's eyes. "I'm no alchemist, so take this as a ward-carver's guess. I think someone is forging a chemical abstraction to bring the dead to life. A chemical formula built to stand where a soul should stand, close enough that a body accepts it and keeps walking. The architecture your victims are losing is the material. You don't hollow out a living Crystal for nothing. You hollow it because you need what was in it for something you're building."

He was quiet a moment. The Lattice under the floor held its attention on the tower and did not reach for him, and he was grateful for the courtesy.

"Your student." He said it gently, which on him meant more slowly. "I'm afraid she may not be alive, not in the traditional sense. She may be a student who died here, in some unfortunate way nobody has written down yet, and was brought back to life by someone on your staff. Someone who knew where she slept and when nobody would be sitting with her." His eyes went to the window, to the long quad and the grey shapes crossing it. "Her name doesn't turn her head. A name answers from wherever the soul is standing. If hers is out on that road, facing west with my dead, then what's eating soup in your infirmary isn't wearing it. I can't be a hundred percent on that. Not until I've analysed what's left of her soul."

He did not say the rest aloud.

He had come up the stairs with a name in his chest, held back behind his teeth, and it did not fit any more. Cozbi took things apart. Every thread Rengai had ever traced back to that man ran one way, toward the unmaking, and he had never once given anything back. Men who raise the dead do it because they miss someone, or pity someone, or want to be the one who could, and Cozbi had never carried any of the three. He would not have brought back a dead girl, having no use for one.

Whoever this was wanted the dead back. A great many of them. Nobody practises a stitch that many times for one seam. A legion of souls, pulled up the road one by one and held in bodies that had stopped belonging to them. And all of it to mend something, Niran thought, that was never meant to be mended. He did not know what it was yet. He knew the shape of a man who thought he could fix what the world had already closed. He had seen it in a mirror once.

"Earlier I said I could tell you whose hand this was." He said it plainly. A man who carries what his reads cost does not leave a wrong one standing. "I was wrong. The man I meant unmakes. This one is trying to put back."

A knock came at the door before anyone could answer him. It was quick and apologetic, the knock of someone who had been told not to.

Hollis Grebe put his head round the frame first, collar starched, ink to the knuckle, his face the colour of a man about to interrupt the Grand Mage and wishing he were an inkwell. Behind him stood a boy of perhaps sixteen in the infirmary's undyed linen, sleeves rolled to the elbow and a carbolic smell coming off him strong enough to cut the smoke. He was all wrists and neck. A shock of straw hair had been wetted and flattened and had stood back up anyway. His apron was clean except for one brown thumbprint at the hem, and he held a folded chit in both hands the way a boy holds something he has been told is important.

"Beg pardon, Grand Mage. Masters." Grebe looked at the floor, then at the chit, then at nobody. "Matron's orderly. She said it was to come up at once, and I said you'd someone with you, and she said..." He stopped. "He'll say what she said."

The orderly swallowed. "Wat Pellow, sirs. Orderly." His voice cracked on the second word and he pushed through it. "Matron says to tell the Grand Mage the girl's hands have gone cold. Cellar cold. And she's still talking, sirs, she asked for more broth and said thank you." He held out the chit, which nobody had yet taken. "And Matron says the girl asked what day it was. Matron told her. And the girl said that can't be right, because yesterday was the bell."

He looked from face to face, and his ears went red.

*I said it wrong. I've said it wrong in front of the Grand Mage.*

"That's all, sirs. Matron's written it down proper." He held the chit out a little further. "She don't trust my mouth."

Niran had gone very still at the word bell. The chapel had rung itself three nights running at the hour after midnight, and a sexton who drank and did not lie had sworn he tied the rope off. He felt the timing settle against the girl's words the way a lock's pins settle under a key that fits.

He did not take the chit, which was not addressed to him, and looked back at Xhem instead.

"Your anchors first. Agreed. I won't cross without them, and I won't take you across without a clean record either. If the road's been worked from this side, I want proof before anyone touches it, same as you." He tilted his head the last degree, weight forward. "And I won't touch her architecture. You have my word on that, and I keep it."

He drew one breath, and the crimson in his eyes slowed its turning.

"I'm asking to read the bed she's lying in, the room around it, and the chapel rope. Surfaces only. Anything touched in the last three days keeps its thread. Who stood there, what was worked, and when. Three minutes, eight at most, and nobody's soul gets handled but mine." His voice dropped to its quietest. "If she died in that bed, the bed will say so. And it will tell me who was standing over her when she sat back up."

# The Last Combustion

*Midreach, the Hall of Equilibria, under a sky of beaten gold; then the Material World, the fourteenth day*

Faisal could not mistake it any longer.

It came up through the floor of the Hall of Equilibria the way a deep bell's note comes up through a man's boots before it reaches his ears, and every scale in the Hall felt it at once. There were ten thousand of them, hung from the vaulted gold by chains so fine they could only be seen when the light moved, every pan holding an oath somebody had sworn and kept, and in the same instant all ten thousand dipped a hair's breadth toward the floor and stayed there, as if something below Midreach had grown heavier than it had any right to be. The Tally-keepers along the colonnade stopped mid-word. Down the long gold nave, past the windows where Utopia ran out to the edge of sight in terraces of light the color of honey held up to the sun, the far bells of Highspire began, one after another, to ring without being struck.

His brother had made his return to the physical world.

[softly] Faisal stood at the great west window with his hands clasped behind his back and felt the threads of the Material move beneath him, every one of them, the gold and the silver and the black, the whole loom of it pulling sideways toward a wound that had not been there an hour ago. He had felt that pull once before, seven hundred years ago, in the eleven minutes it took to crack the Lawbell. He had felt it every time since that the cracked Bell's weight had shifted on his shoulders at the edge of creation, when something came to the edge to try to break him. He knew the hand behind it the way a man knows his own name said in another room. The separation between the Material and the place below it had held for longer than the Host had kept records, and it had not been broken. It had been worn through, from beneath, patiently, with two years of war for a rasp, until the last thread holding it gave.

The seven deadly sins would ravage the world again, as they had in the times of old. He had stood in the old times. He remembered what they had left.

"My lord."

Marium had come up the nave behind him without her bells, which was how he knew she had come to say something he would not want to hear. She was small for a Celestial, the height of a tall mortal woman, and her wings were the soft deep pink of the inside of a shell, folded close against her back with their long primaries trailing the gold floor like the hem of a gown. Her hair was dark and cropped short as a novice's, and her face was round and earnest and had never learned to hide anything, which in the Midreach was not a virtue so much as a condition everybody shared; Vow-Cant has no grammar for lying, and those who speak it grow faces to match. She had kept the Tally at his right hand for two hundred years, since before the Bell's weight had begun to wear him thin, and she had watched it wear.

"My lord, are you sure it is wise to prepare for battle?" She looked at his wings. She could not help it. The left one, which Muken Moto's rage had torn in the white space by the Foot, had mended under the threads to its first joint and no further, and the feathers past the joint were still gray ash where they should have been gold. "You may not be fully recovered from your previous fight. You may not be recovered at all."

Faisal looked down at his hands. Then he looked out at the golden expanse of Utopia, at the terraces running down and down in their honey light, at the far towers of Highspire with their bells still ringing on their own, at a realm so bright that a mortal set down in it would have to learn to see again. He had kept oaths in that light for eleven thousand years.

[slowly, gently] "Fear not, Marium," he said. "My power will not be the one to cleanse the world of this profane evil."

"Then why are you putting on the plate?"

"Because destiny has other plans, and fate has already been decided." He turned his right hand over, and in the palm, faint as a watermark, lines of gold Vow-Cant rose and sank and rose again, every oath he carried trying to be read at once. "I have heard them. Every oath sworn on honor in the Material for seven hundred years, and lately three of them louder than all the rest. A king swearing by a woodpile. A Yukari lord swearing peace he means to keep. A Zettari prince swearing to fight the king when the snow goes, and both of them meaning every word." His voice did not rise. Faisal's voice never rose. "The body will overcome the greed of corruption, Marium, and rise above it in true mortal fashion, by their hands. That is the shape of it. I can see the shape of it from here."

He said it slowly, as if he were reading it off something only he could see, and in a sense he was: Midreach Celestials do not prophesy, but a being who has heard eleven thousand years of oaths kept and broken learns which kind of oath bends the world and which kind breaks under it, and Faisal had been listening to those three for a long time.

Marium's wings came half open, the pink catching the honey light. "But you will surely die."

"Death is nothing but a passage into the greater expanse of reality." He said it the way he said everything, finished and true, and the Vow-Light round him did not tighten, because it was true; it would have been effortful to say if it were not. "Fear not. My power is a flickering flame now. You have watched it flicker. It will go out in one last combustion, and the light of it will be seen a long way."

She was crying. Celestials cry the way mortals do, which is one of the things the Host was not Sung to do and learned anyway, and the tears on her round face caught the gold light and ran it down her cheeks in two thin bright lines. Faisal lifted his hand and laid it against her cheek, and with his thumb he rubbed the tears from under her eyes, carefully, the way he had once watched a mortal surgeon close a wound.

"Another will rise," he said. "It is the destiny of us angels to die in servitude to a greater cause. You have known that since you were Sung. So have I."

Marium knew it to be true. That was the trouble with the Midreach: she could not tell herself it was not. She loved him. It had come up in her slowly over two hundred years at his right hand, the way a blossom comes up through the cold ground at the end of winter, and it was in full bloom now, at the worst possible hour, and she had never once said it in Vow-Cant because in Vow-Cant every completed sentence binds. Faisal loved her too. He had known it for longer than she had. And he knew, with the same certainty with which he knew the weight on his shoulders, that what he felt could not buy his way out of what he owed, and that he would not have spent it so even if it could.

He did not say any of that. He took his hand from her face and let it fall to his side.

The plate came to him out of the air. It was not carried; in the Hall of Equilibria nothing that has been sworn to a purpose needs carrying. The pieces rose off their stand by the window one after another and closed round him, pale silver gone dull with ages of use, never once polished: greaves, cuisses, the long fauld, the breastplate with its line of gold running down from the throat, the high standing collar edged in gold that closed under his jaw with a sound like a door shutting in a quiet house. Then he drew his wings in. They folded down along his back and went on folding, the great gold pinions and the ash-gray ruin of the left one together, the light of them sinking into his shoulders the way the sun sinks into the sea, until there was nothing on his back but plate and the long fall of his white hair. What stood in the window was a tall, long-boned man with dark skin and a still face and eyes like burning gold, a gold cuff of laurel leaves on the rim of one ear and a chain of gold leaves hanging from it. A shape a mortal could stand to look at.

Marium's pink wings curved down at the tips, slowly, the way a flower closes at evening.

"I will leave the sins to Muken," Faisal said, half to her and half to the window, "and Dougou, and Naiser." He breathed out. "After all, this is where they become what the world will remember."

He put out his hand to the air in front of the window and closed it, and the air tore along a line that had always been there, the way a page parts along its fold, and through the gap came the cold, and the dark, and a smell Utopia had not held in seven hundred years: rain on stone, woodsmoke, iron, living earth.

He stepped through and fell.

---

He fell for a long time. Between Midreach and the Material there is a distance that cannot be measured in miles, only in how much of what a thing is it has to set down to cross, and Faisal set down a great deal of it. He came through the last of the Veil into the Material sky like a star, a long streak of white gold scored across the dark, so bright the clouds he passed through lit from inside and went on glowing for a minute after he was gone, so fast the air in front of him packed into a white shock and roared. Below him the night went on in every direction, enormous and black and spattered with the small warm lights of a world that did not yet know what had opened under it.

He had no wings out. He did not need them. A Celestial's Aether is Vow-Light, and Vow-Light is weight that has been promised somewhere to go; he gave it somewhere, and shaped it round himself in long curved planes like the inside of a sail, and pushed against the air with it, and the fall bent, and slowed, and became a descent. He came down out of the clouds over a long valley in the hour before dawn, upright, his white hair streaming above him, the plate gone rose and gold where the first light from the east found it, and the frost on every slope below him flashed as if a thousand mirrors had turned at once to look.

The valley was wrong. He had known it would be. Down its middle, where a river should have run, a seam had opened in the Material, a long ragged line of the black-red that Utopia's oldest records name and do not describe, and the ground on either side of it had begun to change. Grass had gone the color of old blood and curled in on itself. Stones sweated. The air above the seam shimmered with a heat that was not heat, and in it, if a man had been there to listen, there was a sound like many people a long way off all saying the same thing they did not mean.

Faisal set his feet down on the bank above the seam, and where his boots touched, the ground remembered what it was.

That is the only way to say it. Corruption in the Material is a lie the ground has been made to tell about itself, that it is hungry, that it is cruel, that it belongs to something below it, and Vow-Light makes lying cost. Gold went out from his feet into the soil in fine veins like roots, like the thread-marks under a Yukari's skin, and where it went the blood-colored grass shuddered and straightened and greened, the sweating stones dried, and the shimmer above the seam flinched back from the bank as a hand flinches from a stove. The dread came up to meet him all the same. Pain, and sin, and the long hunger of the place below, all of it bubbling up through the seam toward the first clean thing it had found, and every bubble of it, as it touched the edge of his light, had to say what it was, out loud, and could not bear to.

Faisal stood on the bank and let it come, and did not kneel. For the first time in seven hundred years, he did not kneel. The Bell's weight sat on his shoulders all the same, every oath sworn under it for eleven thousand years, riding him down into the Material as it had ridden him at the edge, and without the edge to brace against it pressed on him like a hand on the back of the neck. He could feel the cost already, a slow bright burning at the core of him, oil drawn up a wick.

He would wait for his brother. He knew his brother would come. His brother had never once in all the ages been able to leave alone a thing that would not break.

In the meantime, he would see the world he had knelt for. He had heard it for seven hundred years. He had never once looked at it.

---

Two hundred miles north, at the top of the east tower of Kharven-Seat, Muken Moto woke with his hands already on the bedframe and felt the oak begin to split under them, and for once it was not the dream.

He let go. He sat up in the gray before the first bell with the dragon kimono half off one shoulder and his black hair in his eyes, and he did not know what had woken him until he looked for it, and then he could not stop seeing it.

The threads had twisted.

He had seen them once with his own eyes, in the place where Faisal knelt: gold and silver and black, fine as hair, strung across the white in their millions like the aurora woven into cloth. He could not see them here. But since the Plane of Fate he could feel them the way he could feel the grain in a beam he laid his palm on, and something had taken the whole of the cloth and wrung it. Somewhere far to the south and below, a long way below, the weave had been pulled out of true, and the strain of it ran up through everything like a crack runs up through ice, and at the end of every strain-line, faint and sour, was a note he had never heard in anything made by human hands. The Eye read it for him whether he wanted it or not, the way it read the bedframes: where the weight was gathering. Where it would go next. It came back with one word he had no business knowing, and knew anyway, from the place under his breastbone where the Work of Ruins lived. *Malformation.* Such corruption, such hate, so much wrongness folded into one place, could only be demonic.

And closer than that, much closer, on the north road, something was walking toward him through the dark with heat coming off it like a forge door left open, and every few miles it stopped.

He did not know where it was. He knew it was coming.

---

The Tenrai house at Kessen stood at the top of the ford town where the old road climbed out of the river valley, a long two-storey hall of red pine lacquered black, its eaves turned up at the corners and hung with brass draw-lamps on the Guild main, its gate painted the Tenrai gold. Since the spring the gold had been chipped off the gate's lower half by boys from the town who had heard about the Seat, and nobody in the house had dared to send anyone to paint it back. In the garden behind it the plum trees were in flower, a froth of white and deep pink against the black wood, and a stream ran down through raked gravel into a stone basin where three fat red carp moved slowly in the cold.

The gate came off its hinges at the hour of the cock.

It went soft before it broke. The lacquer blistered and ran in long black tears down the gold, the pine underneath smoked and then caught, and the whole gate leaned inward on its hinges as the iron straps glowed dull red and bent like wet leather, and then a fist went through the middle of it and it came apart in a spray of burning splinters that set the gravel smoking where they fell. The stream hissed where they landed in it. The carp went to the bottom.

The house woke screaming. A Tenrai clerk came out onto the veranda in his sleeping robe with a lamp and saw what was walking up the garden path, and dropped the lamp, and ran; a maid ran the other way, toward the kitchens, dragging two children by the wrists; somebody inside was shouting for the guard, and the guard, four men in Tenrai black and gold with spears, came round the corner of the hall at a run and stopped as if they had run into a wall. The air at the end of the path was shimmering. Frost was going off the ground in front of the thing in a long dark wedge, the gravel steaming, and a plum tree beside the path had begun, very gently, to drop its blossoms, which browned and curled before they reached the ground.

Shōki did not look at the guard. His one living eye went over them once, as a clerk's eye goes over a column of figures, and the Reigan's ring-lines turned in it, and he read their chests. *Two. None. One. None.* Nothing worth the heat. He walked past them, and they let him, and one of them, the youngest, sat down on the gravel with his spear across his knees and was sick from the heat of him going by.

He went up the three steps onto the veranda, and the boards smoked under his boots, and he put his hand flat on the sliding door at the end of the hall, and the paper in its lattice turned brown and then black and then fell away in flakes, and he stepped through the empty frame into a plain room.

The room had a window, and a go board, and an old man.

The old man was sitting at the board on a cushion with his back to the window and the dawn coming in gray and rose behind him, and he was in the middle of a game against no one. The stones lay across the board in a long struggle for the lower right corner, slate-black and shell-white, and his long thin hand was poised over it with a white stone between two fingers. He wore a high-collared coat of deep navy-black, banded at the collar in dull gold, closed at the throat with a clasp of northern knotwork. He was bald on top, the crown brown and shining in the lamplight; the hair at his sides and back was white and long and blown back from his temples as if he had been sitting in a wind, and his beard was white and soft and cut square at the chest.

He set the white stone down. Click. Then he took a thin paper cigarette from inside his coat, and looked at it, and held it up toward the door, and the heat coming off the man in the doorway reached across the room and lit the end of it for him. A curl of smoke went up. He put it between his lips, drew on it, and rubbed his beard with the back of his free hand, and blew the smoke out slowly toward the doorway, where it went white and thin and vanished in the shimmer.

Raizan Tenrai Moto looked at the man in the door, and the Tengan opened in his pale gray-blue eyes, fine spokes of light in the irises like the spokes of a wheel. He looked at the black hair and the braids, the long scarred face, the dead eye with the ember behind it, the bells. Then he looked lower, at the wrists, at the edge of black iron showing under each sleeve with the verdict script still on it, and something in the deep folds of his old face moved, and settled again.

Ah. Yes. He remembered him.

How long it had been. He had been new to the needle then, a boy of the war-schools with one doom broken and a year of his own futures gone into the gap, and the name the Doom Breaker only beginning to be said in the corridors where such things were said. He had stood at the back of the Hall of Verdicts in a servant's gray that morning and watched the headsman refuse the blade.

[old man, soft and courteous, an old Japanese court accent] "It is you," Raizan said. "Shōki of the Kurenai." He drew on the cigarette. "Who would have thought you would come back a demon."

The heat in the room climbed a degree. Two degrees. Sweat came out on Raizan's brow, beading the furrows, and the lacquer on the go bowls began to soften and smell.

"I suppose you are here for your revenge," Raizan said. He tipped ash into a little bronze dish beside the board. "For what I did to your wife. I could tell you it was the line's order, and it was. I was nineteen, and the Tenrai wanted a lord dead at a feast without a mark on him, and a needle in the nape leaves no mark a physician will find. His heart stopped at the table. The Hall went looking for a hand that could stop a heart, and the Akagane have one." He looked at the board, at the white group in the corner, and moved nothing. "It would be pointless to tell you I was following orders. That has never once made a man less dead. I have told it to a great many men, and not one of them got up."

Shōki said nothing. He had stopped in the doorway with the burning frame round him and his arms at his sides, and his living eye was fixed on the old man's breastbone, and the ring-lines in it were turning, turning, and finding nothing.

The confession told him nothing he had not known since the black floor: somebody's hand had stopped that lord's heart, and every year since he had counted every chest in the north looking for the weight of it. What stopped him in the doorway was that he could not count this one. The Reigan went to Raizan's chest the way it went to every chest, by itself, faster than choosing, and where a man's record should have been, the scar-on-scar of every killing laid down in the Attraction Layer like the dose in fired clay, there was a gray quiet, smooth as a pond under fog. Raizan's Tenebra lay over it. The HUSH that kept dogs from barking at him kept the Sin of Wrath from reading him; it was shaped against the angles he expected, and he had been expecting this one for a long time.

[low, flat, a judge reading sentence] "You are veiled," Shōki said. His voice was a grinding thing, iron dragged over a foundry floor. "Your chest reads nothing. A Tenrai of the Hall, with a count of none." The air round him rippled and the blossoms outside the window began to fall. "That is the first lie I have read since the Hall."

"A veil is good manners, in a house with guests." Raizan smoked. "I am supposed to be retired. I have a window, and a board, and a game I have been losing to myself since the autumn." He looked up, and the spokes in his eyes turned once. "But if I have to kill you like a dog, then I suppose I will permit one more confrontation."

Shōki did not move.

He wanted to. Every grain of him wanted to. ONNEN had nothing to bank yet, but the heat that lived in him had found its author at last, and it pushed at his ribs like a crowd at a door, and HAKANASHI had not yet been struck and so had not yet taken his choices away from him. He held. He had been the best stroke in the Hall for twenty-two years, and a headsman learns to read a room before he walks the condemned down the black floor, and he read this one. The board. The window. The old man's left sleeve, a little heavier than his right. The way the old man's feet were set under him on the cushion, not in a sitter's fold but with the right heel off the floor. The Tengan in his eyes.

He knew the Tengan. Every Kurenai child knew it, as they knew the Akagane's copper and the Byakuya's oath. The gold line's eye saw the likeliest way a body would move before it moved, and the Doom Breaker's was fifty years sharper than any other living Tenrai's; it would see Shōki's rush begin before his weight had left his heels, and the old man would not be there when it arrived, and something thin and gray would be in the back of Shōki's neck instead. Rush in at Raizan and he would be cut into a thousand pieces before he had the chance to fight properly, and each of those pieces would be placed exactly where the Tengan had seen it would go. That was the true power of the Tengan: a man who saw where you were going to be never had to be fast.

So he stood in the burning doorway and let the room heat.

The old man smoked. The old man was watching him. Shōki understood that at the same moment he understood why the cigarette: it was a clock. A thin paper cigarette burns down in a minute, a little more, and the Doom Breaker would not break a doom he had not watched for a full minute, close enough to read, without blinking. Raizan had not blinked since the door came down.

The Tengan was reading the knot of all the ways the next hour could go for the man in the doorway, drawn tight round the likeliest strand, looking for the one where he died and the breath when every other strand would thin at once. And Raizan, who had looked into more dooms than any man alive, looked into this one and saw something he had never seen.

It was held.

The knot of Shōki's futures did not hang loose the way a man's should, waiting to be read and cut. It was gripped. Something had its whole weight wrapped round it, a hand the black-red of an old wound, so vast that the Tengan could only see the fingers, and the grip ran down through the Sin's soul crystal, Core and Layer and Shell, and on down past the edge of the eye's reach into somewhere very hot and very deep. A stronger fate-sight held this doom. Raizan could not break what was not loose. No strand would thin for him. The futures of the man in the doorway did not belong to him to break.

And behind it, the way a reflection stands behind a pane of glass, the Tengan showed him his own last morning, the one he had watched creep closer for fifty years every time he broke someone else's. It was very clear now. There was a window in it, and a go board, and the dawn coming gray and rose, and plum blossom falling outside.

[very softly] "Ah," said Raizan Tenrai Moto. "This morning."

The cigarette had burned down to his fingers. He set it in the bronze dish.

He stopped breathing.

One heartbeat. Shōki heard it stop the way he heard everything in a room, through the heat: a man's breath is warm, and when it goes out of the air of a room the room changes. Two heartbeats. The old man was still on the cushion with his hands on his knees. Three.

Shōki blinked.

He had one eye. A man with two eyes blinks and the brain paints over the dark with what was there before, and he never notices; a man with one eye and a dead ember where the other was has twice the gaps on the dead side and paints over all of them. The Blink lived in those gaps. Raizan rose from the cushion in the dark of Shōki's blink and crossed the room in the dark of the next, and the white stone he had left balanced on the edge of the board fell and clicked and rolled, and he was seen at the board and then seen nowhere, and never seen walking between.

But Shōki did not need to see him. The room was an oven. Every surface in it was warmer than blood: the walls, the mats, the lacquered bowls, the air itself shimmering at the height of a man's face. And there was one shape in it that was colder than everything else, a man-shaped hollow of mere body heat moving through the furnace like a fish through hot water, and Shōki's skin read it the way a blind man's hand reads a face. It was behind him. It was at his left shoulder, on the dead side, low, where the base of the skull met the first bone of the neck, where the cord of iron beads and small bones lay against the skin and left a finger's width of nape bare.

The Last Thread came out of Raizan's left sleeve into his fingers already moving: a needle-blade of gray Tenrai steel the length of a hand, its point ground so fine that the pressure at its tip would go through mail rings and leather and bone as through cloth. Almost none of the force behind the thrust was force at all. It was all spent keeping the point true. Up, into the gap, toward the brainstem, where breathing and the heart are kept.

Shōki turned his head a finger's breadth.

The point met bone instead of the gap: the hard ridge at the back of the skull, a hair above the soft place. It should have skidded and opened a long shallow furrow along the bone. It did not get the chance. The air at the nape of Shōki's neck was at the heat of a forge, and the skin there glowed a dull cherry under the black hair, and the Last Thread had been passing through that heat for the whole length of the thrust; a needle that fine holds almost nothing, and in the last finger's width of its travel the steel went past the color where its temper lived and became, all at once, something softer. It went in a thumb's depth into the bone, and it bent. It curled against the skull like a hot wire against an anvil.

Blood came out of the hole and did not run. It hissed. ENSHŌ caught the thrust as it caught everything, the whole of the energy of it stopping in the struck place as heat, the bone flushing red-hot round the wound and cauterising it closed around the bent steel. And ONNEN, which forgets nothing, opened its ledger, and tagged the heat with the soul that had dealt it, and wrote the old man's name at the top of a new page.

*Entered.*

Raizan's fingers were already off the needle. He knew to the hair what the heat would do to his hand if he held on, because the Tengan had shown it to him, and it had been likely, and he had paid the likely price: the tips of his thumb and two fingers were white and blistered where the hilt had cooked in them, and the hem of his coat sleeve was smoking. He left the Last Thread standing in the back of the Sin's skull, curled like a question, and stepped back.

And HAKANASHI took Shōki's choices away from him.

He had been struck. He must answer every blow that lands, from anyone, before he does anything else; that is the law the block burned into him, and it does not ask. He turned on his heel with his whole body behind the turn and swung his left arm backhand through the place the old man had been, the Hall Iron on his wrist gone white-hot, the three snapped chain-links on it whipping out behind like a flail. The air screamed. The blow met nothing. Raizan had stepped, a moment before it began, to the place the Tengan had seen Shōki would not reach: one pace back and one to the right, the Kingstep of the Winning Thread, his foot coming down where nothing was going to be.

The backhand went on past him into the wall.

The plaster melted. A stripe of the wall the width of a man's forearm went from white to orange to a running glassy black in the time it takes to say it, and the beam behind it caught, and the window frame beside it caught, and the paper panes of the window burst outward one after another with small soft pops, and the cold morning air came rushing into the room through them and met the heat and roared. Outside, the plum tree nearest the window went up like a torch, white and pink blossom and all, a great soft explosion of flame and sparks against the gray and rose of the dawn, and the falling petals burned in the air like a slow snow of fire.

Raizan stood by the go board with his blistered hand held a little away from his side and his white beard singed brown at the ends, breathing again, slowly, through his nose. The sweat was running off him now. He was seventy-seven years old and the room was killing him by inches, and the spokes in his eyes were turning as steadily as a clock.

He looked at the curled gray steel standing out of the back of the Sin's skull.

"It has been a long time," he said, "since one of those came back crooked."

Shōki reached up behind his own head, took hold of the Last Thread by its hot hilt, and drew it out of the bone. It came with a sound like a nail coming out of a board. He looked at it, the needle that had made the lord's heart stop at the feast, that had made the Hall look for a hand, that had put Tsuyu on her knees on the black floor; then he closed his fist round it, and the gray Tenrai steel ran between his fingers like wax and dripped on the smoking mats.

The black-violet rim of his aura had thickened toward the old man, all down the side of him that faced the board. One blow banked. One name on the page. And every blow the Doom Breaker landed on him from here would go onto the same page, and the whole of that account was spendable against its author, and against no one else.

Raizan read that, too. He did not need the Tengan for it. He had watched the needle bend.

"So," he said. "If I strike you, I am only paying into your purse." He looked at the board, at the white stone on its side on the mat. "Then I had better not strike you until I mean it to be the last time."

[the black floor's register, grinding] "Entered," said Shōki Kurenai Moto, and stepped into the room.

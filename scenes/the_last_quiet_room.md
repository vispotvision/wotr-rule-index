# The Last Quiet Room

*Aetherion Academy · the preparation rooms under the Crucible, before Hiromi's bout*

---

The preparation room was a stone cell the width of two arm-spans, and Hiromi had been sitting in it without a sound for the better part of an hour.

That was what frightened Geturo. The fight did not; his brother had been walking into fights since he was six, grinning, shouting, at the centre of every room because the centre was where he lived. The quiet did. Hiromi sat on the bench against the back wall with his forearms on his knees and his hands hanging loose between them, and he did not talk. He did not tap his heel. He did not tell anyone what he was going to do to whoever came through the far gate, or how many people would be talking about it by supper. He sat with his white hair falling forward around his face. His pale red eyes were fixed on the floor between his boots, as if the flagstones had a fault in them only he could see and he was going to find it before the bell.

Geturo had never once seen him this focused: not at the binding circles before dawn, or at the bench, or in the orchard at Sum-gol with the practice staff knocking at his rings, or even on the Arena sand six days ago, flat on his back with blood coming out of both nostrils, saying *no* to the word concede.

The room did not help. It had one door, iron-banded, and one window, a slit high in the wall that let in a blade of afternoon light and the noise. The noise was everything. Above them and to the north the Crucible floor was full, and the crowd came down through the stone in a roar that never quite stopped, rising and falling with whatever was happening on the sand, so that the whole room seemed to breathe with it. Every few breaths something out there struck hard enough to come up through the floor: a deep blunt shudder in the soles of the feet, a sifting of grit from the ceiling joints, the water in the jug on the shelf shivering into rings. Somebody out there was fighting with something very large. Geturo did not let himself think about who. The draw-gauge bolted beside the door, brass face and black needle, the Academy's seal stamped on its rim, had been trembling toward the high end of its dial since they came in.

It leaned toward Hiromi now. Geturo watched it do it, a hair's width off true, the black needle drifting the way it always drifted when his brother was in a room, as if the brass knew what Hiromi was before Hiromi said a word. The lamp in its iron cage over the door did the same thing. Its flame stood short and bent, its tip leaning down and across the room toward the bench, every wisp of its smoke drawn the same way. Their father had a word for it. GRASP, he called it, on the card he kept for each of them: the world lining up toward Hiromi before he asked it to, the way iron filings line up toward a magnet. It had been doing that since Hiromi was born.

Today it was doing it harder.

And there was the other thing. Geturo could hear it if he listened under the roar. A thin high hum in the stones, so faint it was almost a pressure in the ears, rising and settling and rising again a little higher. He knew that sound the way he knew his brother's step in a corridor. It was Hiromi's summons, uncalled and unnamed, ringing up on their own in the place where Hiromi kept them, the way they did when he was wound too tight to keep his own voice off them. ECHO, their father called that one: the family art, a Mahuo command growing louder every time it was given. When Hiromi was calm it waited for him. When he was not, it started without him, and every time it rang it rang a little higher.

It was ringing now.

Lily sat beside him on the bench. She had been there since before Geturo arrived, upright, knees together, coat pressed, sash tied in the knot the handbook demanded and nobody but her had ever managed. A cup of tea sat on the bench between her and Hiromi. She had poured it for him a quarter of an hour ago. It had stopped steaming almost at once, the way every hot drink stopped steaming in a room Hiromi was sitting in, and now it sat there going cold under a thin wrinkled skin, untouched. Beside it, on a square of waxed paper, lay a barley cake with one bite out of it. The bite was Lily's. She had taken it herself, to show him it was safe to eat, the way she had been doing since she was eight.

She had not stopped watching him. Her pen was in her lap and for once it was not moving.

"You shouldn't go into the fight angry," she said.

She said it to the side of his face, in the clipped level voice she used for facts, as if it were a rule copied out of the handbook and she was only reading it to him. But her thumb was working at the edge of her lapel pin, and the pin had not moved.

Hiromi lifted his head. He looked at her first, a long, steady look, nothing in it of the grin that usually went everywhere ahead of him. Then he turned and looked at Geturo, standing by the door with his back to the cold iron and his big square hands hanging still at his sides.

"I'm not angry."

It came out quiet. That was the second thing that frightened Geturo. His brother had one register and it was loud, and he used it for everything, for jokes, for boasts, for the word *lunch*. When the volume went out of him it meant something had gone through the noise and hit the place underneath. Geturo had heard him this quiet perhaps four times in their lives.

"I'm just realising," Hiromi said, "how many people underestimate me. My ability. My talent." He stopped. The lamp flame over the door leaned a little further. "I joke too much. I know I do. I do it on purpose. I make the noise so nobody looks at the work." He looked down at his own hands, turned them over, looked at the palms. "And then they believe the noise. All of them. Every single one."

The hum climbed.

"Do I look like a joke to you, brother?"

Geturo did not know how to answer that.

He stood with it for a moment, because the true answer was long and none of it would fit in a room this small. He thought of the orchard at Sum-gol, a whole summer of mornings with the practice staff, knocking at his brother's binding rings until they broke and making him hold them again and again until they did not. He thought of the bed pulled apart every morning so it would look slept in. Of the circles before dawn, never mentioned, never missed. Of a six-year-old in a practice hall full of grown summoners who went quiet and stayed quiet. Of the boy six days ago on his back on the Arena sand, emptied, bleeding from the nose, telling the whole Academy *no*.

He thought, too, of the corridor that morning. Hiromi taking the centre. Hiromi shouting about lunch.

"No," Geturo said. He pushed himself off the door. "You're the strongest of us. You always have been." He let that stand, and then let his mouth go wry, because if he left it there his brother would hear exactly how much he meant it, and neither of them would survive that in a room this size. "Even if you are a foolish idiot sometimes."

Something went across Hiromi's face, briefly, in the place where the grin usually lived.

Geturo crossed the room and stood over him. He did not sit; he was too broad for the bench with both of them on it. He looked down at the white crown of his brother's head and chose the next words more carefully than he had chosen anything in a while.

"You've been beaten down," he said. "The Arena. The imprint gone. And now this." He did not say *this* any more precisely, and he did not need to. All three of them could feel the shape of it in the room: their father's empty chair at the end of the Class X rail this morning, and the infirmary door on the east stair with the warden standing outside it. "I hope this isn't about Father."

He said Father. Here it was only the three of them and the stone, and there was nobody to call him Teacher for.

"You aren't the reason that happened to him."

Hiromi shook his head slowly, the way someone shakes it at a sum he has already checked and found wrong.

"Sure feels like it." He was looking at the floor again. "It isn't like him. You know it isn't. He doesn't rush into a fight. He doesn't rush into anything. He makes you sit there for an hour while he reads the gauges and tells you what the fault in your circle is before you've even drawn it." The hum in the walls dipped, then caught and climbed again. "And then he goes down to that room under the south range by himself. Without telling anybody. With nobody watching his back." His hands closed slowly between his knees. "That's not him. That's someone with his head somewhere else. And I know where his head's been all week, because I'm the one who put it there. The duel. The nosebleeds. The cake on the infirmary step. Every night this week he's been sitting up worrying about me. I'm the thing he was worrying about when he went down there."

"Hiromi."

"I'm not saying I made it happen." He looked up. His eyes were very bright. "I'm saying I was the noise in his head. I'm always the noise."

Lily's pen hand had gone white at the knuckles.

The room shuddered again. Out on the Crucible floor something struck the ground, and the floor of the cell answered with a long low tremor and a hiss of grit from the joints overhead, and the crowd went up in a roar that seemed to press on the ceiling. Hiromi did not even look up. The draw-gauge needle by the door swung, wavered, and drifted back toward him.

"So I'm going to give everything I've got," Hiromi said, quieter still. "All of it. Everything I keep under the noise. I'm not going to hold back any longer. I'm done being the one they laugh at on the way past." A pause. "We're done."

*We.* Geturo heard it. It was the old family plural, the one Hiromi had used for himself when he was very small and very frightened, slipping out of him without his noticing. In the walls the summons rang one step higher.

Lily heard it too. She put her pen away.

She did not argue. She never argued with him when he was like this; she had told Geturo once, at nine, that arguing with Hiromi only gave him a wall to push against, and that the thing to do was give him nothing to push. Instead she picked up the barley cake on its square of paper and held it out, at the exact height of his hands.

"Then eat," she said. "You can't give everything with nothing in you. That's arithmetic."

Hiromi looked at the cake. For a moment Geturo thought he was going to say *not hungry*, the way he always said it. Then he took it, and bit into it, and chewed, slowly, looking at nothing. Lily watched him swallow. Only then did her thumb leave the edge of her pin.

Geturo sighed through his nose.

It was the old sigh, the one he had been perfecting since they were small. It said that he saw exactly what his brother was doing, that he had decided not to stop him, and that the deciding had cost him. Hiromi heard it, and the corner of his mouth moved, and that was something.

Then Geturo put his hand on his brother's head.

He did not pat. He spread his big square hand flat over the white hair and scrubbed, hard, the way he had done when Hiromi was four and crying about a broken summoning top, until the neat waves were wrecked into a white fuzz standing up in every direction. His wrists ran hot, the way they always did; he felt the heat go into his brother's scalp and saw Hiromi feel it. Somewhere at the back of his tongue the faint metal taste came up that always came up when he was close to anything he cared about and did not trust his hands with. He left his palm there a moment longer than the scrubbing needed.

"Be careful out there," Geturo said. "You're still my little brother."

Hiromi made a noise under the hand that might have been a laugh.

"I can't have Father and you both laid up at the same time." Geturo let go and stepped back. "Who would I complain about?"

Hiromi looked up at him through the wreckage of his hair. For a moment the six-year-old in the binding circle was in his face, small and very quiet. Then it went, and the grin came back, partway, further than it had come all hour.

"You'd find someone," he said. "You always do."

---

The bell went.

It came down the corridor from the arena mouth, three hard strokes on iron, and on the third the board on the corridor wall clacked over. Geturo heard the brass plates turn: a dry run of clicks like a rosary being counted, and then silence. He did not need to go out and read it. The next name up was his brother's.

Hiromi stood.

He set the last of the barley cake down on its waxed paper, folded the paper once over it, and put it on the shelf beside the jug, meaning to come back for it. He picked his coat up off the end of the bench. It was the long black one, wool, high in the collar, cut long enough to swing below his knees, the one their mother said made him look like a gallows-bird and he said made him look like the greatest summoner who ever lived. He shrugged into it. He buckled the throat, then unbuckled the top buckle and left it open the way he always did. He ran both hands back through the wreck Geturo had made of his hair, did nothing whatever to improve it, and let it fall.

Then he looked at them both.

He did not say anything. That was strangest of all. He only looked, a long look at Lily, sitting very straight on the bench with her hands flat on her knees, and a long look at Geturo, standing by the door with his hands at his sides. Then he nodded once, to both of them, and opened the door, and went out.

The roar came in through the open door like weather.

Geturo stood in the doorway and watched him go.

The corridor ran north toward the arena mouth, long and stone-vaulted, lit by caged lamps set into the walls at every twenty paces. As Hiromi walked down it, the lamps went the way the lamps always went for him. Each flame leaned toward him as he came level with it, bending on its wick, and stood upright again behind him as he passed, so that a slow wave of leaning light went down the corridor at his pace, one lamp after another, like a crowd turning its head. A draught off the arena mouth came back up the corridor to meet him, warm and gritty, smelling of hot sand and the burnt-metal tang of a working thrown somewhere out there, and it lifted his white hair off his collar and blew it back behind him in a long pale stream. The coat swung at his calves.

Halfway down he stopped, and fixed the coat.

He did it with both hands, without hurry, the way their mother did up her sash before she went into a training hall. He pulled the front straight. He settled it on his shoulders. He did up the top buckle at his throat, the one he never did up, and drew the collar close. Then he dropped his hands and kept walking.

Under the roar, under the stone, the hum of his summons went quiet and held. It settled down to one steady note and kept there, the way a hand holds a bowstring at full draw and does not shake.

Behind Geturo, Lily had come to stand at his shoulder. She did not say anything either. She took her pen out of her pocket, looked at it, and put it back.

At the end of the corridor the arena mouth was a rectangle of white light and noise, and Hiromi went into it without breaking stride, and the white light took him.

He was going to take this one seriously.

Geturo stood in the doorway until the last lamp in the corridor had stopped leaning.

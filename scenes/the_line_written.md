# The Line Written

---

## I · The Outcomes

*The ridge above the Aerne mouth, the Old World; the same evening, the sun touching the sea, the clouds over the hills turning*

Fear was not what filled Naiser Yukari.

It was there. He would not have lied to himself about that, and the Lambert half of him would not have let him. It sat in the cold slick of sweat along his spine and in the slow roll of his stomach and in the hum of the stone under his sandals, and it had been sitting there since he came out of the Soul Plane onto the ridge. But it did not fill him. It took up the room a lamp takes up in a hall, and the hall was full of something else.

The outcomes.

They came up in him the way the record comes up when the archive is opened for a hearing, shelf after shelf, every page lit at once. The Lambert count laid them out in columns and the Yukari sight laid them out in threads, and between the two of them he could see the whole field of what might happen on this ridge in the next minute, spread out across the inside of his skull like a map of a country he had walked all his life. If he opened the Corridor, here, and here, and here, and every one of those threads ran out within twenty paces into the place on the cloth where nothing was tied. If he struck with Kage no Keifu, the shadow-current would go looking for something unresolved and find nothing to hold, and come back to him with nothing in its hands. If he stood and talked, the sun would go into the sea, and nothing would come of that either, and he would be standing on a cold ridge in the dark with half a Crystal and a daughter watching from the deck of a ship. If he turned and went back down the line of the Soul Plane, he would never know.

He looked at every outcome, the way his father had taught him to look at every page of a case before he said a word in the hall, and he set them down one after another, and none of them was what he wanted.

There was one left.

His right hand went to the hilt at his hip.

---

## II · The Blade

He drew.

It came out of its scabbard the way it always came out, without hurry, because the Breath in his blood did not hurry and never had. He set his left thumb against the guard and eased it a finger's width forward, *chiki*, the small dry click of the collar leaving the mouth of the scabbard, and then his right hand drew the length of it out in one long, level stroke, the back of the blade riding against the scabbard's lip so that the edge never touched the wood. The scabbard itself was plain black lacquer worn brown at the mouth by twenty years of his hand, with no fittings on it worth stealing. Thieves had looked at it in three cities and walked on.

The blade did not look like much at first glance. It was a katana of the old Japonic sort the Yukari smiths had been making in the cedar valley since before the Moto had a red chair, long and narrow and gently curved, a little narrower than most, so that men who knew swords looked at it and thought it was a court piece, a thing for ceremonies. The steel was plain gray in the low light, with a pale, quiet temper line along the edge that rose and fell like a range of low hills seen from very far off, and no engraving, no gold, no name chiseled into the tang. The guard was a round of black iron the size of a palm, pierced once, near the rim, with a single crow's-eye hole. The grip was wound with gray silk cord gone soft and dark with sweat. Assayers on three continents had looked at it and laughed and called it Rare at a stretch, and then they had seen it used, and stopped laughing, and some of them had stopped talking about it altogether.

The air paused around it.

He felt it happen as the last of the blade cleared the scabbard. The wind that had been snapping his mantle and tearing his smoke away down the ridge went on blowing everywhere else. Along the length of the steel, in a sheath of air no thicker than a finger, it stopped. Dust that had been blowing across the stone hung in that sheath, motionless, a fine line of rust-red grains suspended along the edge from guard to point as if they had been stitched there. The smoke from the leaf in his mouth drifted into it and stood still.

It was the sight that did it. Whatever the Whole Light had left behind in his eyes was still in them, and his eyes were on the blade, and a thing looked at closely enough, without any gap in the looking, does not get to change. The air touching the steel was looked at. It could not move. So the blade sat in a pocket of air that was not going anywhere, as if the edge itself had told the world to wait. A sword cutting through still air meets no drag at all. It loses nothing to the wind it parts. He had never asked his eyes to do that. He let them.

He stood on the warm stone with the sword in his right hand, point low, and the black orb hanging behind his left shoulder, and the gray skein of his own sentence still standing in the air in front of his mouth where the wind could not take it. *No reason to hold back.* It had set. He could feel it there, a weight on the front of his chest like a hand.

Naiser did not fight with anger. He never had. Hell, he did not even fight with grief, and he had more grief to fight with than most men; the house had a whole wing of the record for it. Anger made a man's two notes ring out of true. Grief drove the clocks apart. He had learned that on the slope above the river in the war's last winter, with his father four winters gone into the seam, watching good men of both houses die because they had come up the hill angry. He fought the way he did everything. He counted.

He was calculating now, and he was calculating harder than he had ever done anything in his life.

## III · The Talent

He spoke while the sum ran. A man who is counting talks the way a man who is walking breathes: without deciding to.

"I am no Senri." He drew on the leaf. The coal at its tip glowed violet-black. "And I won't stand here and pretend I am even nearly as strong as he was. I am not."

He let the smoke out. It went into the still sheath of air along the blade and lay there.

"He had a talent I was never given. He could not stop caring. He could not stop protecting things. He would walk a day out of his way to sit with a widow in the next valley because her husband's name was in the record and nobody else had come. He would stand in a door so nobody else had to." A pause. "He went into the seam in Ashura alone, on a frost morning, with the pack already on his back, because a door like that only takes one. And he did not look back at the gate, because if he had looked back he would have seen us, and he would have had to care about that too, and then he could not have gone."

The wind moaned across the ridge. Below, very small, the river-water ran orange into the blue.

"I am not that. I care for my people. My sister. My daughter. A boy with a Founding in him who asks the cook how many arms a squid has. My valley, and the names in my record. That is the whole of it. I want peace because I am tired of war, and because war takes the people I care for and puts them in the record with a date beside them." He turned the sword a little in his hand, and the still air turned with it. "I do not want to use what I have. I never have. My house says I am one of the most skilled men of my generation, and I have spent every year since the slope trying to make sure I never have to show anyone how skilled."

He was being brutally honest. He knew it, and he could feel why. The Entered Word ran in him whether he wanted it to or not, and near him a man stated his case more plainly than he meant to. That was true of his own case too. Every soft word he might have used to dress it up fell away before it reached the air.

"You sent your Sins to find out what I would do when there was nothing left to bargain with. You could have saved yourself the trouble. This is it."

---

## IV · Mimamori

He lifted his eyes.

He did not look straight at it. He had learned that much. But he let the sight come all the way up, everything that was left of the Whole Light, and he let it go out over the ridge and the coast and the cloth of the world, and he did not hold anything with it. He watched.

"Mi..."

He had no name for this. The house had seven Regards and none of them was this. It was the oldest thing a parent does and the oldest thing the Yukari did, before there were Regards, before there was a record, when the first household stood in the cedar valley at night and looked out at the dark over the sleeping houses: they watched over. In the house's old Japonic the word for that was the same word a grandmother uses for sitting up with a sick child.

"...Mamori."

Mimamori. Watching over.

The charge came first, and there was no one on the ridge to see it but the thing he was not looking at. His breath and his heartbeat fell into one count, as they always did, and the two notes of his aura, drifted apart since the ship, strained toward each other and very nearly met, a hair's width of quarrel left between them like a crack in a bell. The thread-marks at his temples lit. They lit silver and then went violet, and the red skin over the backs of his hands smoked again in the wind.

The pull was harder this time. The Aether of the Old World was rich, and it came up into him through the soles of his sandals from the warm red stone, but the Crystal it went into was half empty and quarreling, and the refining did not run as clean as it had at the bow. More of it came off him as heat. He felt the waste go out of him in a long hot breath along the thread-marks, more than a tenth, nearer to two parts in ten, and he felt the Crystal, low already, go lower, toward the line where a man's hands begin to tremble.

The aura came up over him like black silk, and it was thinner than it had been on the river. Gray, with the faded violet living in it, and the black-violet laid over his shoulders in a mantle that he could see through in places, the way you can see through a coat worn thin at the elbows. At its seams the silver-violet flared, and guttered, and flared again.

And he saw.

He saw everything as it was and as it is. Anamnesis gave him the first. Every stone on the ridge carried the history of its own making, the way fired clay keeps the dose of its kiln, and under his eyes the ridge laid its history open: the old mountain range that had been the roof of the world, ground down over ages into hills; the seam of sulfur in the rock that had been a vent once, breathing; the wild thyme that had seeded itself in a crack a hundred springs ago. The Null-Light gave him the second, the null reading, the whole of a thing known from the place where light does not come back. And laid over both, the cloth of the spiritual world, every current of Aether across the Old World, every ley junction standing up in its slow whirl of color, every soul's thread, his daughter's among them on the deck of a ship far below, burning brighter to him than the rest.

As it was. As it is. Both at once, and both held still under his watching, so that for the length of the look the ridge had a past and a present laid exactly one over the other, like the two pages of the Twin Mantle held up to the light.

And in that light, at last, he could see the shape of the thing nothing was tied to.

Its place on the ridge his sight still slid off like a foot off ice, and where it would stand in a breath no sight in the world could have told him, because there was no reading where chaos would go next. That was the whole nature of chaos, and the Measurewrights had a plain account of it that every trained eye in the Dark Ages knew. In a chaotic thing, the smallest difference now grows into a large difference later, doubling and doubling, so that no reading is ever fine enough to tell you where it will be: a feather's weight more or less at the start, and an hour later you are wrong by a mile. That is why nobody can say what the weather will do on a given day three weeks from now.

But anybody can say it will be winter.

That was the other half of the account, and it was the half Naiser cared about. A chaotic thing never goes where you predict, and it never goes everywhere either. Weather never repeats a day, and still it keeps inside its seasons. Watch a chaotic thing long enough, and its path, however wild, keeps winding back through the same region, round and round, never the same way twice and never once leaving it. The Measurewrights called that region the thing's keeping-place. A practitioner would have called it where its Essence held together. It was the one part of a chaotic thing that did not move, because it was the shape of all its moving.

Mimamori had watched long enough.

---

## V · Sengaki

He aimed past where the thing stood, and past where it would stand.

Reading chaos as if it were a man, guessing its next step the way the Lambert count guessed a shoulder's commitment against ten thousand others, would have been a fool's gamble. The count was made for men. It had always been made for men. Naiser Yukari was a great many things, and he had been called most of them in the course of a forty-year war, and he was not a fool.

He aimed at the keeping-place. At the tide of that Essence where it held together, the one region it wound back through forever, where something with no ties to anything in the world still, somehow, had to be itself.

He set his feet. His right foot slid back a half-pace on the warm stone and his left turned out, the weight sinking into the back knee, and his hips came round a quarter-turn to the right, winding, the way a door is drawn back before it is slammed. His shoulders followed his hips. The sword came up and back over his right shoulder in both hands, point to the sky, the still air along its edge coming with it, and the line of fine red dust stitched along that edge rose with the blade and did not fall.

The orb moved.

It came off its place behind his left shoulder for the first time since the Soul Plane and swung round him in a slow curve, the violet-black coal of it trailing a thread of dim light, and it went to the blade. It began to circle the steel. It went round the sword once, slowly, and then faster, until the ball had stretched into a ring, a band of violet-black light wound round the blade in a tight spiral from the guard to the point, round and round, the way the thread is wound on a spindle. He felt the heat of it come into the steel. The banked waste of the Whole Light, everything his Crystal had thrown off and Fixatio had cured into a coal, went back into the work. The blade drank it. Its gray went dark, and then darker, until the whole length of it was the violet of a deep bruise, and the temper line along its edge lit silver from the guard upward like a fuse catching.

Nothing a man spends is spent clean. But a man who keeps a record can make his own waste pay part of the bill. The coal paid for some of what came next, and his Crystal paid the rest, and still it took him down. He felt the reserve cross the line he had been watching all evening: a fine tremor came into his hands on the silk of the grip, and the gray of his aura went thin as smoke and pulled in toward his chest.

The two notes of his aura came together. For one beat, against everything the quarrel in his bloods wanted, the Yukari reach and the Lambert count swung as one clock on one beam, and the sound of it went out across the ridge, one note, very low, with a long hall's echo under it, *ooooooooooonnnnnnn*, and every ley junction he could see in the cloth below him shivered in its slow whirl of color.

"Sengaki."

The Line Written.

He cut.

His back foot drove into the stone and the stone took it, the force of the drive running down through the ball of his foot into the ridge and the ridge giving nothing back. His hips unwound, a quarter-turn to the left, faster than the eye; his shoulders came round behind his hips, a beat late, the way the end of a whip comes round behind the handle, so that every part of him arrived at the end of the stroke one after another, each faster than the last, and all of it went down his arms into the blade. The sword came down out of the sky on the diagonal, from over his right shoulder toward his left hip, a long clean descending cut of the kind every child in the Yukari yard learns on a stick of green bamboo, and the air in front of him was the least of what it cut. It cut the record.

That was how a Yukari would have said it. The blade moved through the world, through the still pocket of air it carried with it, and met no drag at all, and in the same instant, in the place where the threads run, it drew a line. One line, the way a clerk draws one line across a page of the record to close an account, through the keeping-place of a soul that nothing in the world was tied to, the region it wound back through forever. The violet-black spiral round the steel came off the edge as the blade passed the bottom of its arc and went out ahead of it in a thin crescent, almost black, edged with silver, *shiiiiiiin*, and the sound of it was the sound of a brush drawn once, fast, across a sheet of good paper.

It went past where the thing stood, and past where it would stand, to the one place the thing had to come back to.

The stroke finished low by his left hip with the point a hand above the stone, and he held it there, his weight down in the front knee, his breath gone out of him all at once. The tremor was in his hands. The thread-marks smoked. The red dust that had been stitched along the edge in the still air finally fell, all at once, in a thin rust-colored line across the stone in front of his feet, exactly the length of the blade.

The crescent was still going.

He watched it go, and did not look at what it was going toward, and did not breathe, and the skein of smoke in front of his mouth, holding the sentence he could not take back, stood perfectly still in the wind.

# The Medlar Tree — Aetherion Academy

Malphas looked out into the distance as he walked into the woods, alone, in the rain.

From the ridge above the vinegar works the valley lay open beneath him, grey laid over grey. The town's stacks smoked low, the smoke beaten flat onto the slates before it could climb. Beyond them the district main ran east on its iron trestles toward the gauge-housing at Hallow Cross, a black line ruled across the stubble fields, and at that distance its draw-hum reached him less as a sound than as a faint pressure behind the hinge of his jaw. A lamp burned in the housing's window at noon. Past it the low hills stood one behind another into the weather, each paler than the last, until the last was only a darker place in the cloud. He looked at all of it for a long while. He looked at it the way a brewer looks down a cellar of casks he has already filled and sealed, with nothing left to do but wait.

Then he turned in under the beeches.

He was alone by any count a stranger could have made. The Remnant came a half-pace behind him on the path, small and gold and hunched, the second skull at its collar swinging on its chain though no air moved under the trees, and it was no company, being the rest of him. Rain ran down the bone of his face from the crown of spurs and gathered at the hinge of the jaw and dropped. Wet, the bone had gone the colour of strong tea. The robe had drunk all it could hold and hung from him in sodden black ropes, the rags at the hem dragging through the leaf-litter and coming up with beech mast caught in them. No breath stood in front of his face. None had for a long time. The small gold crosses at his throat knocked together as he walked, with the sound a purse makes.

The path was his own. He had walked it every day since the summer, and it showed: a track a foot wide and a mile long, up from the back gate of the works through the beech and the birch, where nothing grew any more. The bracken either side stood waist-high and copper with the season. On the track the moss had gone to black slime and the leaf-litter lay grey and soft and shining like wet paper, and every root that crossed it had furred white. He held himself down when he walked. He had held himself down all his life, in rooms full of instruments and in rooms full of men who would have hanged him for what the instruments missed, and the long habit of it kept almost all of him in. Almost was a foot wide. Every morning he left a little more of the wood behind him rotting, and every morning he came back by the same foot of ground so as not to widen it.

He went to the garden his wife had kept when she was not sick.

It lay in a clearing at the top of the wood where the charcoal-burners had once had their hearths, and two centuries of their ash had left the soil black and sweet. Maren had found it the spring after they came to the works. She had paced it out in her good boots with her skirt pinned up and a ball of twine in her apron pocket, and come down at dusk with her hands black to the wrist, and told him over supper that she had found the only honest ground in the parish. A hazel hurdle stood round it. She had woven it herself in her second year, badly, and again in her third, well. Inside the hurdle the beds ran in the order she had set them. Leeks earthed up in a double row. Cabbages for the crock, blue-grey and beaded with rain. Sorrel and lovage, and a great sprawl of horseradish she had never been able to get rid of and had stopped trying. Beans on hazel poles, the pods gone black and rattling, left for seed because she always left the last of them for seed. Marigolds down the path, the only bright thing in the whole grey wood. In the far corner stood the medlar she had planted the year they married, a crabbed little tree no taller than a man, its leaves gone russet, its brown fruit hanging from the twig-ends like small closed fists.

In the low corner under the medlar, where the ground fell away and the rain pooled first, lay her bed.

He did not look at it first. He never did.

It had been a bed of sorrel once. He had dug it out in the week after she died, he and Nuvilak, down through the black topsoil to the yellow clay beneath, and they had puddled the clay with a rammer and their own feet until it would hold water like a crock. They had filled it with moss cut on the Uplands, three cartloads of sphagnum still dripping when the carter tipped it, and Nuvilak had laid her in it, because Nuvilak let nobody else handle a body she had prepared. Then the moss had gone over her. Then the rain had come, and the moss had begun to grow.

He knew what it was doing to her. He had built it to do it. Sphagnum is a sour moss. It takes the lime out of any water that touches it and pays back acid in its place, so that a bog sours itself as it grows, season on season, until nothing that rots can live in it. It makes a sugar of its own in its cell walls that binds the stuff flesh is built from and tans skin the way an oak-bark pit tans hide. The water standing in it holds no air. The peat-dead came out of the Uplands cuttings after two thousand years with their eyelashes on, and the whorls on their fingertips, and the stubble on their chins. A raised bog drinks nothing but rain. Ground water carries lime up out of the rock, and lime would sweeten her, and sweetened she would rot; so he had raised her bed a hand above the clearing on its clay and given it no water but what fell out of the sky.

It had rained on her every day for a week.

She had loved rain on the shutters. She had asked at the end whether it was raining, and a kind woman had told her yes, and it had not been. He thought of it every day it rained, and every day it did not.

He began to work on it in the rain. Daily maintenance had become his religion, and like any religion it had an order, and he kept the order.

The birch first. Birch comes first to any bog that is drying. A bog with birch on it is a bog that is dying, because the trees drink it from below and drop their leaves on it from above, and the leaf-mould sweetens the moss. He found twelve seedlings that morning. None stood taller than the last bone of his finger, and their first two leaves were still folded together like hands at prayer. He drew each one out with its white thread of root whole, slowly, so as to leave nothing broken in the moss to sprout again, and laid them on the hurdle in a row. Then the water. A peeled hazel stake stood at the head of her bed, notched with a knife at the height of the moss, and he crouched and read it the way he had read gauges all his life, with the bone of his face a hand from the mark. The water stood a thumb below the notch, and rain dimpled the dark pools between the moss-heads. At the foot of the bed the overflow ran out through a lip he had cut in the clay. A wad of beech leaves had blocked it. He cleared it with two fingers and watched the brown water start, and run, and darken the path below.

Only then did he look at the moss over her.

It had grown. It grew a finger's breadth a year if the rain held, and she lay a forearm deep, and he had not seen her face since the day it closed. He did not need to. He could feel her the way he felt the seeded at the ends of their threads, faintly, a cold place inside the cold, his own culture still at work in her. Green at the tips of the moss, red where the summer had caught it, gold-brown below, and below that, dark.

Then her rows.

The hoe leaned where she had always leaned it, against the hurdle by the gate. Its ash handle was worn pale in two places by her hands, and the finger-bones of his did not fill the hollows her fingers had worn, and he used it anyway. He worked down the double row of leeks drawing the black soil up round their white shanks, and between the cabbages, and along the marigolds, and the whole time he held the rot in. In here he held all of it. Let one breath of it out and the leeks would slump to slime in their rows and the marigolds would brown and the black soil would heave and stink, and her garden would be his. So he kept her garden hers. Of all the disciplines he had ever kept, it was the hardest, and he kept it every day.

It was the only remnant of the man he used to be.

He knew where it came from. His father had kept the retting ponds by the same rule, every day, in every weather: the stones lifted and set back, a stalk drawn and snapped across to see if the fibre slipped from the core, the water read by its smell, the hour kept. The man in the woods with the hoe was the retter's son still. Nothing else of that boy had come through. The rest had gone with the body, or long before it, and the truth was that under the order of the rows only chaos had been left behind. He had stopped lying to himself about that. What chaos had left him in exchange was the truth about the fundamental aspects of the world, which he had been taught as a child as four words. Life. Death. Good. Evil. A parish taught them the way it taught the four quarters, as though a man could stand in one and point across at the others. The retting pond had taught him otherwise before he could read. In the pond the flax died, and the linen was born of its dying, and the same water that freed the fibre killed every fish in the stream below the dam. The parish wore the linen to church. Life and death were one process in one pond. Good and evil were the names the parish gave that process, depending on which side of the dam a family lived.

*Upstream they called my father honest. Downstream they called him a poisoner. He was the same man at the same pond, keeping the same hour.*

*And who was I,* he thought, *in the years I lived and wore a coat and signed my name, to stand at the top of a stair and tell other people where it went?*

He had done it. He had done it for years, and in print. *If you hold this without clearance, read it whole, and then give it up to the nearest archivist.* He had written a complete architecture for calling the dead and then struck the steps from it with his own pen. *I set down no glyph of it here, since a chain written once is a chain copied.* He had sat across the reading floor of the Division with open books between him and every student who came to him burning, and told them kindly, over the tops of the pages, where the edge was and how far back from it to stand. He had told Mu-jin. He had told himself. *Do not animate one for the mourner. Do not animate one in which you hold a stake.* He had said it to a hundred hungry young people who had found the door and wanted only to go through it, and every one of them had been right and he had been the coward at the top of the stair, wearing a warden's face over a hunger worse than theirs.

Magic was a door. He understood that now with the whole of what was left of him. The Archons had left it open, and not by accident. They were crowned ideas, functions that had condensed out of the first tension of the world and taken thrones, and to work their magic was to invoke a function, to call upon the labour of a crowned idea and ask it to work through you for a while. Invocation was the gateway, and the gateway was a gift. It had been given so that a soul could grow, Stage on Stage, Threshold on Threshold, until what it was could no longer be told apart from what it did, and it stood in the world as law. Every Domain any practitioner had ever opened was that: a soul shown as law, for a little while, over a little ground. He had stopped working alone at Invocation and been relieved past words that something answered. He knew now what had been on the far side of the door. It had been himself, further on, waiting.

He had been tangled in sorrow at first. He did not pretend otherwise. He had gone down the stair from the room where she lay with his voice thinning out on the last word, and the weeks after had been the worst of his life, and he had lived through Pyraeon. The crown of spurs had gone on growing through his scalp. The Crystal in his chest had stood Overgrown past the point any living Crystal survives it, full to the seams of the Teacher's fire and his own backlog, pressing out against him from inside like a cask whose wine has kept working after the bung went in. He had not slept. He had sat at her bedside, and then at the edge of this bed in the clay, and let it press. He had been pushed to the limit and held there, and held there, by something that had waited the whole span since Invocation for him to run out of body.

And then he had run out of body, and he had not died.

He had transcended death. He had broken the limits of the body. The overload he had carried since the day he named it a strength had found somewhere permanent to go: one seat of him in the chest of the thing that wore his name, the rest housed in the gold at his heel. He did not breathe. He did not sleep, or hunger, or feel the rain go through the robe to the bone. The need for the physical had fallen from him like a coat from a hook. The Archive of Names had nothing of his to file, because his Echo had never left for the Soul Plane and his residue had never gone home to its Wellspring and the three things that part at every death in the world had not parted in him. He had shed the lowly title of mortal the way a snake sheds a skin it has outgrown, and left it lying, and gone on. He stood at Zenith now, the fourteenth Stage, where the oldest names in the histories had stood. He had become more. More powerful, by any measure the Accord had ever drawn up, and he had read every one of them.

He would free this world. He would guide the next generation with his merciless pursuit, the pursuit that had cost him a wife and a school and a body and the good opinion of everyone he had ever respected, and he would show it no mercy at all, because mercy was a hand to hold on the way down, and he was finished with the way down.

He heard Nuvilak before he saw her, coming up the black path through the bracken, her boots taking the slime with the short flat tread of a woman who has crossed sea-ice.

She came in at the hurdle gate and stopped inside it. She was hooded against the rain in oiled sealskin, and the hood had slipped back enough to show one ear, pale and small and cold-pinked at the rim, and a dark plait wet against her neck. Her sleeves were pushed past the elbow as they always were, as if every room were a room with a carcass in it, and her forearms were roped and freckled and wet to the bone. She did not look at the bed under the medlar. She had laid the woman in it, and she did not look. Her apron was dark with rain, and one hand rested flat on its pocket, where paper made a square shape under the cloth.

She wiped her hands on the apron, front and back, slowly, before she spoke.

"Lord Malphas..." She stopped, and began again, level. "They have sent word. They are happy about your developments."

Malphas did not say anything.

He stood up from the leeks with the hoe in his hand. The robe came up off the soil black to the knees, and the mud hung on it in clots, and the rain went on taking it off again in long brown runnels. The bone of his hands was mud to the wrist. Mud packed the joints of his fingers. He stood in the middle of her garden in the rain, between the cabbages and the marigolds, and let the word sit in the clearing.

*Happy.* Draycott's hand, then, under the lord's seal. The lord had never been happy in his life and would not have known the word if it were spelled out for him in his own blood. Draycott would have written *gratified*. Nuvilak had turned it into something a person could carry up a hill.

Then he looked back to her.

"Then I assume that Project Aegon is complete."

Nuvilak nodded her head.

Once. Her hand stayed where it was, flat on the apron pocket, over the square of paper.

Project Aegon was complete.

The name was not his. It was the only one of his works he had never named himself, and the cult had noticed, and nobody in the cult had asked.

The man who goes by a title had chosen it, in the winter of 694, in Malphas's rooms at Genesio, with the passes shut and the reading floor cold enough to show breath. He had sat down without being asked, beside Gisli Draycott, and told Malphas what they wanted written, and he had already named it. Every lowland schoolroom learns the herdsman's song he took the name from. A herdsman called Aegon has gone away to the games and left his cattle in a boy's keeping, and the cattle stand at the fence and will not eat, and go thin waiting for him; and the boy's friend, leaning on the fence, gives the line the whole song is remembered for, that hope is for the living and the dead have none. The lord's wife had been a scholar of the dead. She had died of the lung-sickness two years before he climbed the stair, and she had left him a sealed paper and the instruction to open the room. He named the work for the herdsman whose cattle waited. In the song, the herdsman comes home.

Aegon had been a commission for one woman.

It had made the Necrocursica. It had made the Category Three completions Strom ran at scale in the hall at Genesio, until the floor went pale and the bark of the trees outside went pale and the gauge at the gate below the Archivum fell from twenty-six to twenty and stayed there. It had made Madeleine Ault, poured into a vessel graded and priced like any other Draft, and given what life the vessel would carry. The likeness had lasted eleven days. It had known the house. It had known the names. On the last day it took her husband by the hand to the sealed paper, and when the sentence on the paper was finished it ended in one breath, and was spent, and the lord had stood in the room afterward with the hand that had held hers still out in front of him.

Malphas had written the reason into the margin of his own manuscript, and Mu-jin had copied it into the sealed edition and called it the most honest page in the book. The likeness takes its identity from its maker's own Core. Whoever draws on Mortalis to rebuild what has come apart summons himself, arranged in their image. Madeleine Ault had spoken eleven days in the lord's house with Draycott's patience in her voice and Strom's habit of counting, and the one thing in her that had been hers was the decision she had died holding, and it had been delivered and spent. *Never claim it is the soul,* he had written. *Never give it the name.*

The commission had closed in 702. Its file lay sealed below the gate at Genesio, under his own warning. Any Register that went looking for a word called Aegon would find a dead lord's grief and a dead lady's short return and a margin note telling them it could not be done, and close the file again. A word already filed with the dead was the safest word in the world. That was the reason he had given the cultures for keeping it, and it was true.

The other reason was the song. He had never told anyone the other reason.

"And her," Nuvilak said.

She did not look at the bed when she said it, either.

"First."

"Before his lordship's lady."

"Before everyone." He set the blade of the hoe in the soil and rested both hands on the butt of the handle. "She is the best of them. Whole. A season gone. Her Echo is the freshest thing she has left on the Soul Plane, and her residue went home to its Wellspring in nine days like anyone's and is lying there yet, near the top, where it settled. Her Name is filed. Nothing in her has had time to blur."

Nuvilak wiped her hands. "Where I was raised," she said, "the sky finishes what the ground starts."

"Then the sky has had long enough."

The rain came down between them on the cabbages.

It had taken him the whole span from the Sour House to the hoe to see what Genesio had missed, and when he saw it he had laughed, alone, at her bedside in the clay, for the first time since the second bell. It was a thing he had shown Maren at the supper table in their first winter, with a jar of cane syrup and a crank he had made from a bobbin and a pencil. Two glass cylinders, one inside the other, and syrup in the gap between. A single drop of ink set into the syrup with a quill. Ten slow turns of the crank, and the drop drew out into a thread, and the thread into a sheet, and the sheet into a grey haze spread so thin through the syrup that the eye could find nothing of it at all. Gone, by any test the eye could make. Then ten turns back. And out of the haze the sheet came, and out of the sheet the thread, and on the tenth turn the drop sat in the syrup where it had been put, round and dark and whole. Maren had laughed and made him do it again. She had done it herself the third time, with her tongue between her teeth, and the drop had come back for her too.

The ink had never been destroyed. It had been carried, every part of it along its own path at its own rate, and every path could be run backward, because the syrup was too thick to let any part of it wander off its line.

Death was the first ten turns.

The Crystal did not leave the body whole when the spending stopped. It came apart into three things under three laws and went three ways: the Echo to the Soul Plane within hours, the Temperance residue home to its Wellspring over nine days, the Name-record filed in the Archive of Names, which received it and never let it go. The body went last and slowest, into the ground and up into the grass and out through the beasts that ate the grass. Every part of a person went along its own path at its own pace, and none of it was destroyed. The Archive held every Name ever entered. Residue thinned on its schedule toward a floor and never passed below the floor. The Soul Plane held memory, which was the whole reason it existed. Every grave in the world still held its bones, and every Crystal-bearer's bones still held their Trait, mineral laid into mineral. Every person who had ever lived was still in the world, drawn out so thin through four jars that no instrument in the Accord could find them, and the Accord called that dead, and wrote laws about it.

Genesio had tried to put a drop back together by pouring fresh ink into the syrup in the drop's shape. That was a completion. It was the maker's own ink. It lasted until it ran out.

Aegon would turn the crank back.

What turns a crank that runs through a whole world was the work of all the months since the summer. A crank needs a hand on it in every jar at once. He had spent the cultures to put one there.

They were in the mains. The Mother's cells sat at forty-one gauge-housings across the four quarters, on capped legs and dead branches and cut districts where nobody drew. Each had laid a culture of his into the conduit. The conduit ran to the Wellsprings, because that was the whole purpose of a main. The Guild had spent four centuries building the finest set of veins any body had ever owned, from every Wellspring core in the world to every town that could pay, and sealed every joint of it with lead and a proof-mark. Down those veins the rot would go home to the sixty, into the residue of every practitioner who had ever died and paid back his Temperance there.

They were in the graves. Rot goes into the ground. That was the oldest fact he knew. Every churchyard, every plague-pit, every barrow and battlefield and cairn on every hill: the culture would find the bones there, as a vinegar mother finds the skin of a cask, and grow on them. First the pale skin of cellulose the mother makes, a lattice laid down cell by cell into the shape the bones gave and the ground's old Imprint remembered. Then mineral, laid into the lattice as the culture laid it down in his own wounds. Then flesh into the mineral. A body grown as a mother grows on wine, on the old bones, in the old grave, out of the earth it had gone into.

And they were in the living. The Walking Dose had no thread back to his hand; it went from body to body like any fever, with nothing to cut. And every living person who had ever loved someone and lost them carried, in his own Crystal, the living end of a thread that ran to the dead. The trade called it the Bond Trace. It attenuated slowly and never severed. A mourner's grief was the living end of it, and it was the thread every commission at Genesio had pulled. Seeded, every widow in every parish became a hand on the crank, and every orphan, and every old man at a graveside on a rest day, pulling his own dead back up out of the dark toward him without knowing he pulled.

The Name was the index. The Archive kept every one. It would tell the crank which haze was which drop.

And he was the turn. Mortalis was a gate, and a gate runs both ways. A living practitioner who harmonised with it borrowed a current that ran past him one way, toward the stop. He did not borrow it. He stood in it. He was the one dead thing in the world that had stayed itself, the proof standing up in a garden in the rain that the three products could be held together after the spending stopped and remain who they had been. He knew what held them. He was what held them. He had spent his whole life working Nihiloth, putting one wave out of step with another until the two cancelled into silence, and Aegon was that same sum run backward, every drifted phase in the world brought back into step at once, until the silence spoke.

He had done the arithmetic. He could not stop himself; it was still what his mind did when it was left alone in a room.

A body is not cheap. A grown man holds the burnable worth of a cartload of good firewood in his fat and his meat, and to grow one through a culture costs ten times what it holds, because fermentation throws most of what it is given away as heat. Seven thousand EU a body, near enough. Alive, at Greyshaft, with his whole reserve at the full, he could have raised about eleven thousand of them, and then lain down on the floor of the coldhouse and starved. For a million he would need ninety of himself.

So the world would pay.

Everything that lives has the sun's work stored in it, and rot gives the stored work back. A compost heap scalds the hand. A standing forest is a reserve that has been banking sunlight, ring on ring, for two hundred years. Every million of the dead would cost him five square miles of forest gone to black slime, the work of two centuries of sun spent in a season and banked in him and spent again into lattice and bone. Then fields. Then herds. And the cities had more stored in them than any forest, in their larders and their beasts and their people.

That was the first cost. The second was the record.

Every record in the ground spends as it is read; he had written that too, on the second page of the second chapter, and it had been true at Genesio, where the floor of the hall and the bark of the trees had gone pale and nothing in them could be woken again. To turn the crank he would read every record the world held. Every Imprint a family had laid into its walls by living against them. Every Echo resting on the Soul Plane. Every thread of grief in every Crystal alive, pulled taut and emptied as it pulled. The ghosts would go out of the old houses. The old fields would fall quiet. Nobody living would be haunted by anything, ever again, and no mourner would ever again wake at the hour the dead one used to wake, because the thread that woke him would have been spent bringing the dead one home.

A clean slate. A world with nothing written on it.

Nuvilak had come a step further in from the gate. Rain dripped off the edge of her hood. She was looking at him now, at the bone of his face and the mud on his hands, the way she looked at a carcass on the rail when the instruments said one thing and her nose said another.

"The ink," she said. "In your jar. Did it all come back?"

He had told her about the jar once, at Greyshaft, over the gauge bench. He had not known she was listening.

"The drop came back," he said.

"All of it."

He did not answer her. The rain answered for him, drumming on the cabbages, pattering through the medlar into the moss. *The drop came back,* he thought, *and its edge had gone soft.* That was the other half of the trick, and Maren had found it on the fourth go, when she let the jar stand an hour on the windowsill before she cranked it back. What runs along its line can be run back along its line; what wanders off it in its own small heat cannot, and given an hour, some of every drop wanders. Her drop had come back round and dark with a grey bloom round the rim of it, like a bruise. She had laughed at that too.

A season was nothing. Maren had lain a season in the moss, and her drop would come back with an edge as fine as a hair. The lord's lady had lain twenty years, and would come back a little soft at the rim. And behind them, generation on generation, the dead of the plague years and the war years and the years before anyone wrote years down, each of them a longer hour on the windowsill, each a wider bloom. The oldest would come back mostly bloom. A bloom has to be filled with something, or the drop will not hold its shape, and the only ink in the world near enough to fill it would be his. The lord's lady would speak with a little of his patience. The oldest dead would speak with a great deal of it. The first generations, raised out of barrows whose names nobody remembered, would stand up out of the ground wearing their own faces, and look out of them with his eyes.

And every one of them, grown on his culture, would carry a thread to him as the seeded did, and on each thread a share of what he took in and could not turn. His Conversion had not moved since the day he stopped working alone, and it never would now, whatever he became. A Cauldron with no bottom does not care, until it does. A million vats would. Ten million would make it a sea.

He would guide the next generation. That was what guidance meant.

He looked at Nuvilak across her mistress's garden, and she looked back, and neither of them said it.

She wiped her hands on her apron, front and back. One of them went back to the pocket afterward and lay flat over the square of paper there, and stayed.

*The sky finishes what the ground starts,* she thought. *Here he means to finish it with the ground, and keep the sky for himself.*

"I will tell them," she said, "the day is coming."

"Tell them it will be the same day everywhere."

She went. He listened to her go, her flat sea-ice tread on the black path down through the bracken, until the rain covered it.

Project Aegon was complete. He was going to spread the rot throughout the world, down the mains and into the graves and through every grieving body that walked it, and bring the dead back. Every dead. From every generation the Archive held a Name for, back to the first Name it ever filed, he would give them life anew, grown on their own bones out of the earth that had taken them. He would give this world a clean slate.

He went back to the bed under the medlar.

He knelt in the wet at the edge of the clay, the robe settling round him into the mud, and the Remnant came and stood at his shoulder with its small gold skull tipped as if it listened too. The rain fell on the moss over her. It ran off the medlar's russet leaves and off the brown fists of the fruit, and dimpled the dark water between the moss-heads, and found its way down through the red and the gold-brown and the dark to where she lay a forearm deep, keeping. It soured as it went. It would go on souring for as long as it fell.

The medlars would be ready soon. They were no good off the tree. A medlar has to be picked hard and laid on straw in a cold room and left until it goes soft and brown and half rotten through, and only then is it sweet; the old country word for it is bletting, and Maren had loved them bletted, and had eaten them with a spoon out of the skin at the kitchen table in the first frosts, every year, and called him a heathen for not liking them.

He reached up with a hand that was mud to the wrist and took one from the nearest twig. It came away hard and cold. He held it in the bone of his palm and looked at it for a while, the way he had once looked at a bundle of flax in the far corner of the last pond.

"It's raining, Maren," he told her.

This time it was true.

**The mechanism (Phenomenon line, R13 / Thirteen §2).**
- **Real phenomenon:** reversible laminar mixing. Ink drawn out by a turning cylinder in a thick syrup comes back whole when the crank is reversed, because viscous flow at low Reynolds number runs backward exactly. True molecular diffusion cannot be reversed, and it grows with the time left before the reversal. The best-known demonstration is G. I. Taylor's in the 1960s; the apparatus is a Couette cell (1890), so it stays inside the 1800 to 1900 ceiling. The same idea underlies the spin echo (Hahn, 1950), which is named here only, not on the page.

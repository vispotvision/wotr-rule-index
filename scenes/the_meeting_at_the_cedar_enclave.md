# The Meeting at the Cedar Enclave

Naiser and Naevra walked together, brother and sister, the way they always had.

The path up to the cedar enclave climbed through old forest in long switchbacks, and the trees on either side were so tall and so close that the morning came down between them in thin bars of green-gold, full of drifting motes, and the ground under the needles was soft enough to swallow the sound of their feet. It had rained in the night, and every fern along the path held a bead of it that caught the light and burned there, small and white. Moss lay over the old roots in cushions so green they looked lit from inside, and the trunks of the cedars were a deep red-brown, ridged and stringy, going almost purple where the wet had soaked them. The air smelled of wet bark and limestone and the faint sweet resin that bled from the cedars where the house had cut its marks into them, generations ago, at the height of a man's eyes: a crow's foot, a circle, a single line. Somewhere above them a stream was running, and somewhere below them the riders who had brought them up from the valley were watering the horses and talking low. Otherwise the forest was quiet in the way only Yukari ground was quiet, as if the trees themselves were listening for something.

They walked in step without thinking about it. They always had. Their father used to say, half laughing, that he had never quite believed they were born in different years; that some clerk of fate must have made an error in the register, and they were twins, and had simply arrived a year apart because Naevra had refused to be hurried. *The same coin,* he would say, putting a hand on each of their heads, *struck twice. Shaped differently. Worth the same.*

Naevra glanced sideways at him.

"Did you really need to bring your katana," she said, "for a meeting?"

It rode at his left hip under the edge of the black founder's coat: long, narrow and severe, in a plain black scabbard with no fittings worth stealing, the grip bound in faded cord. He wore it the way some men wore a signet. He had cleaned it in the night, after the funerals, sitting cross-legged on the floor of his room with the oil and the cloth and the soft paper, three times over, until there was nothing left on the steel but his own face.

Naiser looked at her.

"I can never be certain," he said, "that they aren't here to harm you."

It was true, as far as it went. It did not go all the way. The rest of the truth was that he did not like this meeting, had not liked it since the runner opened his mouth in the courtyard, and liked it less with every switchback of the path. The Zettari had fought them for four years, the last four of the forty and the worst. Half the blood in his own body answered to that house's name whether he wished it to or not. He could feel that blood now, the dense, heavy, patient part of him, stirring as they climbed toward the enclave, the way a dog's ears come up when it hears a voice it knows on the far side of a door. He did not trust it. He did not trust anything that made him want to be calm.

Naevra smiled. It was a small smile, and fond, and she looked away into the trees as she gave it to him. Her brother being protective. Her brother being her brother. He saw her decide to let him have it, and he let her let him, and they went on climbing.

---

The enclave sat in a clearing at the top of the hill where the cedars drew back in a rough ring, as if they had agreed among themselves long ago to leave this one place open to the sky. It was very old and very plain, and beautiful in the way plain things are when they have been kept by careful hands across many lifetimes: a long low hall of cedar timber weathered to the color of old copper, on a footing of pale limestone, a covered walk running round it, a garden of raked white gravel on the south side with three standing stones set in it like islands, each one shawled in moss and freckled with orange lichen. A stream came down out of the trees on the north side, clear as glass over green stones, and ran under the walk in a stone channel and out again, and the sound of it filled the clearing. Under the eaves of the walk hung a row of the house's worked lamps in cedar frames, unlit in the daylight, their slate sockets dark. Paper screens stood open along the hall's south face. Beyond them, in the long room, a single low table had been set for four.

At the edge of the clearing, Naiser stopped.

He felt the wards before he saw the boundary stones. They came up out of the ground around the enclave in a ring the width of the clearing, and he walked into them like a man wading into cold water: a tightening of the skin, a held breath, a pressure behind the eyes that searched. They were the house's oldest wards, older than the hall, laid down by hands that had been dust for longer than anyone could count, and they did one thing and did it perfectly. They weighed intent. Whoever crossed them was read, gently, completely, the way a bell is read by striking it, and the reading was carried to whoever in the house was listening.

He was listening. He let the wards read him, and felt them find him and know him and settle, warm as a hand laid on his shoulder. Beside him he felt them find Naevra and settle around her too, softer, like silk.

And past them, far down the other side of the hill, on the southern road where it came up out of the valley through the cedars, he felt the wards touch someone else. Several someones. A cluster of them, moving up the road at a walking pace. Heavy. Dense in a way that pressed on the wards like a foot pressing on a floorboard, so that the whole ring of them seemed to lean very slightly south. He knew that weight. He carried half of it.

The Zettari were on the road.

He held still and listened to the wards weigh them. What came back was hostility, some, and over it a great deal of caution, packed down hard and kept in order, the way a soldier keeps his kit. Under that, something he could not read at all, and did not like.

"They're coming," Naevra said quietly. She did not need the wards to know. She never had.

"I know."

Naiser crossed the gravel and went up the two steps onto the walk and into the long room, and sat down at the table, on the north side of it, with his back to the open screens and the stream. He set the katana on the floor at his right hand, sheathed, the hilt toward him and the edge toward himself, the way a guest set his blade down in the old manner to show he did not mean to draw it. Naevra came in after him and knelt at his left. She arranged her skirts and folded her hands in her lap and became, all at once, perfectly still, the stillness of someone always listening to something no one else could hear.

A maid came in from the back of the hall on bare feet. She was an older woman of the enclave, gray-haired, with the house's long fingers and its quiet way of moving, and she set out four small cups of unglazed clay and a heavy iron pot, and poured. The tea came out pale green-gold and steaming. It smelled of the hill: of cedar tips and mountain sage and the bitter little white flowers that grew only on Yukari ground, above the frost line, and that every child of the house was given in a cup when it had a fever, and that no one outside the house had ever been able to make grow. It was the custom. Any guest who came to Yukari land to talk was given it first, before a word was said, so that whatever passed between them afterward would pass with the taste of the house still in their mouths.

The maid bowed and went out. The screens whispered as she slid one closed behind her.

Naevra lifted her cup in both hands and breathed in the steam.

"I'm sure," she said, "you'll want to raise what the Tenrai are doing."

Naiser looked at her over the rim of his own cup.

"They've been converging with the other families," she went on, in the same light, even voice, the voice she used for reading accounts in the archive. "The Moto lines. All of them, or near enough. Drawing in together under one hand to keep order over their people. Except their people are fighting back." She turned the cup a quarter-turn in her fingers. "I hear there's a radical movement. Real, organised, growing fast in the north. And all of it is led by the Starks."

He thought about that. He thought about it for some time, drinking the tea, letting the bitterness of the little white flowers sit on his tongue.

It made sense. The war had broken every house in the world in one way or another, and the Moto had come out of it holding the threads of fate in a grip that had tightened through forty years of fighting and closed hardest in the last four. A grip like that did not loosen after a war. It closed. And when it closed hard enough on people who had already lost everything they had to lose, someone always stood up. That it should be the Starks did not surprise him either. He had met a Stark once, on the northern road when he was barely grown and the war was already older than he was, a big silent rust-bearded man who had studied him from under his brows a while and then held out his hand without a word. Naiser had never forgotten the hand.

And the Zettari, who kept the mandates of law, would care very much about a house of fate tightening its grip on the world while a rebellion rose against it. They would want to know where the Yukari stood on that. They might want it more than they wanted a marriage.

"Yes," he said. "We'll talk about that as well."

He set his cup down. Then he looked at her properly, for the first time since they had left the valley.

She had bound her white hair up off her neck in the old style, the heavy braid coiled and pinned at the back of her head with two long pins of black lacquer that had been their mother's. She was wearing the formal robe of the first-born line, which he had not seen her wear since before their father went into the seam: layered gray silk over white, so pale it was nearly the color of her skin, with a sheen of lavender in the gray where the light moved on it, like the throat of a dove, and the house's crow worked small in silver thread at the collar. Someone had put a little color on her mouth. She had done something to her eyes, too; he could not have said what. She looked like the portraits of their grandmother in the long gallery at home. She looked tired under it, and very young, and very beautiful, and frightened, and determined not to show any of it.

"I see," he said, "you've tried to make yourself look presentable."

Color came up into her face at once, in the pale cheeks and right down her neck into the gray silk at the collar, and she looked away from him at the screens and the stream and the white gravel garden, anywhere at all.

"You shouldn't say such things," she said. Her voice was not quite as level as it had been. "I just wanted to make a good impression."

He nearly smiled. He very nearly did. It was the closest he had come to it in four days.

Outside, the wards went quiet.

---

Shisui Yukari had been standing at the enclave's southern entrance for two hours, and in all that time nobody had seen him.

That was not a figure of speech. He stood in the shadow of the gate post where the southern road came up out of the cedars and onto the white gravel, in a plain dark kimono and hakama, with two swords through his sash and his hands loose at his sides, and the riders who had brought the Lord Naiser up from the valley had walked their horses past him an hour ago at a distance of six feet and talked about their wives and never once turned their heads. A squirrel had come down the gate post and sat on the stone by his foot, cleaning its whiskers. The wards themselves, the house's oldest and most perfect wards, had passed over him as they passed over the gate post: wood, stone, cedar shadow, nothing.

He was sixteen. Some days he was willing to admit to seventeen. Either way he was the youngest man in the house who could do what he did, which was to take the whole of his essence, every thread and current and flicker of it, and lay it down into the world around him so evenly that there was nothing left anywhere to show where he ended and the cedars began. The elders had a name for it. He did not like the name. It made it sound like a technique, and to him it had never felt like one. It felt like holding his breath, if holding his breath had been the most natural thing in the world, and breathing out the strange part.

He heard the Zettari before he felt them. The scuff of good boots on the last of the road. The creak of a leather strap. Then the pressure of them came up the hill ahead of their bodies like the warm air ahead of a forge door, dense and heavy and slow, and the squirrel by his foot went up the gate post in a single scrabbling rush.

He let them reach the gate.

Then he let go of the breath.

He came out of the shadow by the post the way a figure comes out of the grain of a wood when a lamp is moved: there one moment where nothing had been, close enough to touch, standing very correctly in the middle of the path with the white gravel behind him. He bowed. It was a good bow, deep and exact, from the hips, eyes down, held for a full breath and released. He had practiced it in front of the mirror in the archive until his grandmother told him to stop because he was frightening the copyists.

"My name is Shisui Yukari," he said. "I will be guiding you to meet Lord Naiser."

He straightened. His voice was quiet and even and very polite, and he had worked hard on that as well.

"Before we go in, I must ask you, please, to refrain from drawing any weapon while you are inside the enclave." He said it with a small apologetic tilt of the head, as if it pained him to mention it. "My men will automatically take it to mean that you are trying to harm me."

He did not say how many men. He had been told, very clearly and by the Lord Naiser himself, not to say. He did not glance at the cedars. He did not look at the ridge to the east, or the stream bed to the north, or the long shadow of the hall's south walk, or any of the hundred other places where the house's men were standing at that moment as he stood at the gate post, breathing out the strange part, laid down into the bark and the stone and the shadow so perfectly that the wards themselves could not have found them. There were hundreds. He knew every one of their positions and he did not look at any of them.

He waited a moment to see if the guests wished to say anything. Then he turned and fell in a half-step ahead of them on the gravel path, to the left, where a guide walked, and led them toward the hall.

The silence of the walk seemed to want filling. He had been told, by his grandmother, that it was courteous to fill it.

"I will say," Shisui said, "it is good to make your acquaintance, Lord Zettari." He glanced back over his shoulder and inclined his head, a little lower, to the younger of them. "And yours, young prince."

He thought it came out rather well. He kept walking.

The path went round the white gravel garden and its three standing stones and up two steps onto the covered walk, and along the walk past the open paper screens toward the long room. The stream ran loud under the boards. The smell of the house's tea came out through the screens to meet them, cedar and sage and the bitter white flowers. Ahead, through the last of the open screens, the low table was set for four, and the Lord Naiser sat behind it in his black coat with his back to the stream and his katana on the floor at his right hand, and the Lady Naevra knelt at his left in gray silk and white, perfectly still, and both of them were looking at the door.

Shisui stopped a pace short of the threshold and turned to face the guests once more.

"Again," he said gently, "I will remind you, please, to refrain from drawing any weapon while you are in the enclave. Not only for the protection of our lord. It is the custom of our people. These are sacred lands, and we ask that you respect them."

He bowed one last time, as deep and as exact as the first.

"May Ashura bless your spirit," he said, "with many blessings."

Then he stepped back and to one side, off the walk and into the long shadow of the eaves, and breathed out, and was gone. The squirrel, which had followed them all the way along the walk from the gate post at a careful distance, sat up on the rail where he had been and looked about for him, puzzled, and did not find him either.

In the long room, the tea was still steaming in its four clay cups. Naiser did not rise. He sat with his hands resting on his knees and his face composed into the house's stillness, and looked at the two Zettari standing in his doorway, and waited.

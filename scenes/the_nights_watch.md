# The Night's Watch

## THE THING WITH EDGES

The main under Cutler Row had been off since the eleventh division, and the silence it left had a shape to it. Eleven years in and Wystan still heard the absence as a pressure behind the ears, the way a man who works beside a mill hears the mill stop. Rain on the shutter. A dray somewhere north, iron tyres on wet stone, the driver swearing at a horse that had opinions. Lamp oil, damp wool drying badly on the back of the chair, and under both the sharp vinegar reek from the wash-tray where the evidence tins sat draining.

The insert had come up from the Society's bulletin office in a bundle of nine and it was the only one he had read twice.

*Filed 14th, Tally, N., Timberline chapter, North Ferriby walk.* The Night Watch Society printed its inserts on the cheapest rag it could buy and the ink bled into the fibre, so a man reading one at the third hour by a single lamp did it with his nose eight inches off the page. Wystan had a scholar's slouch and no scholar's title, whatever the Division put on his door.

Tally walked forty miles of timber every week and counted deer for a living. His insert said that on the morning of the ninth he had come down the Ferriby cut and found eleven hinds and a stag inside a quarter mile, all of them dead where they stood rather than where they fell, and all of them carrying.

That was the word he used. *Carrying.* Not rot, not swelling, not any of the things a forester has words for. Tally described a pale grey-green mass emerging through the hide along the spine and the flanks, fibrous at the base and soft at the crown, and he had measured it, because he was the sort of man who measures. Nine inches at the tallest. He had cut a section with his knife. He reported that the tissue under the mass was not decayed at all.

Wystan had read that line four times.

He set his chin on his knuckles and let the room be quiet around him.

The Lattice Classification Bureau had already had it. Edwyn Brack, whose signature was on half the classifications out of the northern office and who had never in Wystan's experience been wrong in a way that mattered, had returned it inside two days as **Wellspring bleed, Verdantia, tertiary expression, no operator indicated.** Two of the day-desk had agreed before they had finished reading. The Green Pulse regenerates rather than resists. Its practitioners heal by growing. Turn that loose on ground with nobody holding the other end of it and you get exactly what Tally had described, growth arriving in a body that had no further use for it. Brack's note said the vein had probably surfaced through a fault in the drift geology and would exhaust itself by autumn. Brack's note recommended no further action and no file.

It was a good answer. It fitted the residue, it fitted the tissue, and it had the great institutional virtue of costing nobody anything.

Wystan turned the insert over and read Tally's last paragraph again, the one the bulletin office had nearly cut for length.

*Ravens would not take them. Counted four in the birch across the cut, sat the whole time I worked, none came down. On the tenth I walked the line north to the Ferriby stones and found the ground clean, no carcasses, no mass, nothing sick. Went back and found the last one. Twenty-two paces short of the parish boundary marker. Clean ground on the far side of it. I checked the far side twice.*

That was the thing. That was the whole thing, and Brack had not mentioned it, and neither had the day-desk.

A bleed has no edge. A bleed is a vein surfacing under pressure and it goes where the rock lets it, which is to say raggedly, in fingers, thinning at the margins until nobody can say where it stopped. It does not stop twenty-two paces short of a parish boundary marker. It does not check the far side.

A boundary means somebody chose one, and a choice means a hand, and a hand means Article XV and a purse behind it.

He wrote *edges* in the margin, and under it, *ask Tally who set the marker*, and then sat with the pen off the paper because the second half of the arithmetic did not want to go where he was pushing it.

Four counties. That was the part the day-desk kept saying as though it settled the matter rather than opened it. Ferriby in the north, the Swale walks, two chapters out of the eastern timber towns, and one filing from a Society man in the Marches who had sent a sketch instead of a count and whose sketch Wystan had pinned to the boards above the desk for a week before taking it down again. No two of the sites were within eighty miles. The Grand Mage's clerks had acknowledged receipt of the summary and returned the standard form, which was the form they returned for weather.

And underneath all of it, the thing every historian in the four quarters had been failing to answer for four hundred years, which was why a Wellspring does anything at all. They surface. They wander. They go quiet for a century and come back forty miles east of where anybody left them, and the scholarly houses had produced eleven competing accounts of the mechanism, and the Accord enforced the one that suited its supply contracts, and the honest position was the one Lu Sanjing had written in his studio at the end of a long and distinguished career: *we do not know, and the instrument does not care that we do not know.* Vaeloris had reformed after the void took it. Nobody had made that happen. It had simply occurred, and the Curia had called it a miracle, and Wystan had read the Palatine's own return and noticed that what the Palatine had actually described was a subtraction with a hard boundary at a tannery wall.

Boundaries again. He did not like how often the word kept arriving.

*I am the least able man in this Society and I am the only one looking at the edge of the thing.* He had held the title Warden of Duty for four years and had never once used it to get a document, a reading, or an hour of a scholar's time. It sat on his brass like a rank in an army he had left.

The knock came at the third hour and a half, which meant the outer stair had let somebody through, which meant somebody with a chit.

"Master Wystan."

She came in on the second word rather than waiting for the first, and he had her inventoried before she had the door shut, because that was what eleven years did to a man.

Twenty-three or twenty-four. Short, and built solid through the shoulders in a way the current cut of coat was designed to hide and did not. Black hair oiled and pinned up off the neck in the double coil the Measurewright lineages had worn since before anyone could remember why, with the pins plain iron rather than silver, which said apprentice or said thrift. A round face, broad at the cheek, with the skin across the nose and forehead marked by the fine dark speckling that came off a forge bench when you leaned too close too often. Heavy brows. A mouth that had been set hard before she knocked and was working at staying that way. Rain had got into the grey wool coat at the shoulders and turned it near black, the coat itself cut long and narrow to the knee in the fashion of the season, with a gap at the wrist because the sleeve had been tailored for it.

The bracelet sat in that gap. Three brass bands, wound with a fine grey wire that carried its own faint sheen under the lamp, seated against the inside of the left wrist where the coil could hold contact with the pulse. Guild proof-marks on the outer band, two of them, one struck badly. A regulation device, and not a cheap one.

Bureau, then. They had sent somebody to sit on him about Ferriby.

*He is thinner than the drawing. Nobody said he would be this thin.*

"Master Wystan," she said again. "Qiu Yinzhi. I carry coils for the Timberline chapter and I am here on the Society's warrant, not the Bureau's."

"I did not say Bureau."

"You looked at my wrist."

He let that sit where she put it and did not pick it up. "You are wet through. There is a stool."

She did not take the stool. She took three steps into the room, which put her at the edge of the lamp, and she looked at the boards above the desk where the Marches sketch had hung and where the four pins were still in the plaster.

"I am guessing you have read the news of the phenomena."

"I have read nine inserts and one classification." He turned the page over so the margin faced away from her, an old habit and a rude one. "The classification says Verdantia bleed, no operator. Two of my colleagues agree with it. My colleagues are better practitioners than I am."

"Do you agree with it."

"I have not finished."

"That is not the same as no."

"It is the only answer I am permitted to sign." He looked up properly then. "You did not come out in this to hear a classification read back to you. What do you have."

She was quiet for long enough that he heard the dray come back south, slower, the horse's shoes off-rhythm on the wet.

"Nol Tally has not walked his line since the eleventh," she said. "He sent his insert in on the fourteenth from the bulletin office in person, which he has never done, and he asked the clerk what happens to a filing after the Bureau closes it. The clerk told him it goes to the Night Register. He asked whether the Night Register was a person." Her jaw moved. "The clerk said it was."

"And you are here because."

"Because I sealed his sample tin on the ninth, in the cut, standing in it, and I assayed the residue at the chapter bench on the tenth with a coil I had calibrated that morning." She brought her right hand out of the coat pocket at last, and there was a tin in it, tarnished, Fixatio-anchored, the seal unbroken and the chapter's wax across the join. "And it came back clean."

He did not move.

"No signature," she said. "Not a faint one. Not a degraded one. Not Verdantia, not anything. I ran it four times and I burned a coil doing it, and I have been carrying that around for five days telling myself I am not good enough to have read it, because I am not, because I am twenty-three and I have been capped for eight months and the man who trained me has been dead for two years." Her thumb was moving on the rim of the tin, back and forth, wearing at nothing. "And then I heard a Warden of Duty was sitting nights on Cutler Row reading inserts nobody sent him, and I thought, that is either the stupidest man in the Accord or the only one left who would look."

Something in the room had gone very still, and it was not the Domain, because he had not seated it.

Nine inches of grown mass on eleven hinds and a stag, undecayed tissue beneath, a boundary marker twenty-two paces off, ravens that would not come down, and a residue that carried no author at all. Residue always carries its author. That is the first line of the first lecture of the first week, the thing the instrument is for, the reason the Division has a creditor schedule and a courtroom and a man on nights.

He had no idea what a clean tin off a live scene meant. None. He turned the thought over twice and it gave him nothing either time.

Wystan reached across the desk and took the lamp by its base and set it between them where the light would fall on her hands and not on his. Then he opened the drawer, took out the gloves he always forgot, and put them on.

"Break the wax," he said.

## WHAT DOES NOT SMELL DEAD

The main under Greyshaft Nine had been capped four years and the building still held its cold the way a cellar holds it, in the slate and the brass and the iron of the standpipe housings nobody had bothered to strip. Water came off the ceiling in the far corner at a rate Vesk had counted once and never stopped hearing. Twenty-two drops a minute. The ice press turned over in the next room with the long dragging complaint of a machine built for a purpose it no longer served, and under all of it, in the place where a man who had grown up beside a working main expected a hum, there was nothing at all.

Nine hinds hung on the rail. They had been on the rail eleven days.

There was no smell.

That was the part Vesk still went and stood in front of, on the nights when the work was slow. A coldhouse with nine carcasses in it eleven days on should have had its own weather. The ammonia off the plant, yes, and the wet-slate smell, and the tallow off the lamps. Past that, nothing. A man could walk in blindfolded and be told he stood in an empty room and he would believe it.

He came up the length of the rail with the flask held out in front of him in both hands, the way a server carries wine, because his hands did not close properly any more and he had learned what they would and would not do.

"Experimentations were exactly as you said, Lord Malphas."

Malphas was sitting on the gauge bench with his coat folded beside him and his forearms on his knees, looking at the floor between his boots. He had been sitting that way for the better part of an hour.

"We were capable of intruding a process of rot that presents as a Wellspring process," Vesk said, "by hiding it under the layers of careful synthesis. Nobody has to believe it. The instrument says it. You are truly a genius."

His fingers tapped the glass. They were long and they had been longer before the second graft, and the nails on the two that took had come in ridged and yellow and had kept growing at a rate that embarrassed him. Inside the flask the formulation sat green and faintly cloudy, with the fine suspended matter turning over in it whenever he moved, the whole volume catching the lamp and throwing it back up the wall in a colour that belonged to a pond in July.

Malphas did not answer for a while. That was usual and Vesk had stopped minding it.

"Ilmar," Malphas said. "Tell him why it reads clean."

Ilmar Foss did not look up from the bench. He had a coil in pieces in front of him and a jeweller's glass screwed into his eye, and he had been struck from the Hexagonal Oath eleven years ago for a thing he still described, when drunk, as a rounding convention. His hands were the steadiest in the building.

"He thinks it reads clean because the synthesis is pure," Foss said. "It is not pure. It is filthy. There are four intermediates in that flask I could name and one I could not."

"Then why."

"Because nothing in this room ever couples." Foss set the glass down and turned around on the stool. "Every attribution the Register has ever signed works off one fact. When a practitioner draws ambient Aether he couples it to his Crystal, and coupled Aether keeps the signature of the thing that coupled it, and what he leaves behind in the room is signed. That is the whole science. That is why a Warden can stand in a burnt shop and tell you the name of the man who burned it."

"I know all this," Vesk said.

"You know it and you do not use it." Foss nodded up the rail at the nine hinds. "We draw nothing. We couple nothing. We put a formulation into an animal and the animal's own Lattice runs the working, because a deer has a Lattice, a poor one, sufficient for this. The residue in that cut is signed. It is signed by the deer. So the coil reads a working with an operator that cannot be charged, filed, invoiced or hanged, and the Bureau writes *bleed, no operator indicated*, and closes it in two days, and they are not being stupid. They are being correct. The signature is genuinely not ours because it was genuinely never ours."

*He explains it like a man who still expects an appeal hearing.* Vesk turned the flask a half-turn in his palms and said nothing, because he had been about to say that the purity was the elegant part and it now seemed better not to.

Malphas lifted his head.

"Nuvalik. The bodies."

She was down at the tail of the rail with a knife and her sleeves pushed past the elbow, and she had been working through the fourth hind since the tenth hour without once asking for a lamp brought closer. Far-Northern, and carrying a name that had belonged to three people before her, which she had explained exactly once to Vesk and never again. She wiped the blade on her apron before she spoke, which was the only thing about her he found frightening.

"The matrix is gone and the meat is not," she said. "That is what you asked for and that is what you have. The ferment eats the scaffold. Collagen, the connective net, the tissue that holds an animal in the shape of an animal. It leaves the cells. So you get a hind that is structurally liquid and cellularly whole, and the hide holds her together because there is nothing left inside doing it, and she stands where she died until something touches her."

"And the smell."

"There is none because there is nothing making one. Ordinary rot is bacterial and the bacteria make the stink. Our ferment takes the same intermediates first and makes nothing out of them a nose can find." She looked at him flatly. "Which is a fault and I have said so twice."

"Say it a third time."

"A carcass no bird will touch is a carcass that stays where it fell." Nuvalik went back to the hind's shoulder and opened it in one draw. "Four ravens sat over the Ferriby cut for a full morning and not one came down, because nothing down there was telling them food. Where I was raised, the sky finishes what the ground starts, and when the sky refuses, people walk out to see why. You did not make a quiet thing. You made a thing that sits in a field for eleven days holding still and waiting to be counted."

*Three names for this body and I have spent all three of them on this.*

The ice press turned over. Somewhere past the wall a dog that belonged to nobody had been barking at intervals since dusk.

"The growth," Malphas said.

Vesk got in ahead of Foss this time, because the growth was his. "Verdantia formulation, third dilution, introduced live and carried on the animal's own circulation. The Green Pulse does not repair, it grows, and that is exactly why it is the correct Wellspring for this and why nobody has used it this way. Put growth into a matrix with the scaffold taken out and it builds a fresh scaffold to a plan of its own." He set the flask down on the bench with both hands, carefully. "The mass comes up along the spine and the flanks because the dose travelled the vasculature and the vasculature quit last at the trunk. That is why the pattern looks deliberate. It is only perfusion."

"Height."

"Nine inches at the crown on the best of them. It will go further. It does not know it has finished."

Xu Deming had been standing by the door for a quarter of an hour with his hat in his hands and rain still on his shoulders, and he chose this moment, which was the wrong one and which he knew was the wrong one.

"May I put a number in the room."

Malphas turned his head.

"Third dilution against nine hinds and a stag is two myriads and a fraction, and I cannot buy two myriads." Deming had the careful diction of a man who had been capped young into a trading lineage and had taken a courtesy name he did not much like. "I diverted it. It came off a legitimate supply contract on the Swale line, drawn down as wastage, and wastage is a column, and a column is a line in a Logistics return, and the Logistics Division publishes. In eleven weeks somebody in a room in the capital will look at the Swale line and see that a pumping array with four years of flat wastage suddenly had a bad spring."

"Then they will have a bad spring in a ledger."

"They will have a purse." Deming's thumb went round the brim of his hat and round it again. "The Arbitration Division does not chase hands. It cannot use a hand. Article Fifteen says the liability follows the payer and every officer in that building has it framed over his desk. They will not come looking for whoever made the flask. They will come looking for whoever bought it, and I am the man who bought it, and I would like it noted that I said this before it happened rather than afterward."

*I have thirty-one days of usefulness left and he has already counted them.*

Malphas stood up.

He was not a large man. Vesk had met three people in his life who filled a room by arriving and Malphas was not one of them, and Vesk had never once been able to say what Stage he was, or whether he was seated in anything at all, or why the cold of the coldhouse never seemed to reach his hands. Eleven days Vesk had watched for it. Eleven days and the man's fingers had stayed the colour of a living man's fingers in a room at four degrees, and Vesk did not know what that meant, and had not asked.

"The objection is the same objection," Malphas said, "and both of you have arrived at it from different ends, which is why it is correct. Nuvalik says the carcass stays where it fell. Deming says the ledger stays where it was written. What they are both saying is that the work has an edge."

He walked up the rail without looking at the hinds.

"A bleed has no edge. That is the only reason the Bureau's classification is safe. A vein comes up through a fault and it goes where the rock will let it and it thins out raggedly at the margins and no man alive can tell you where it stopped. What we have is a dose. A dose stops where the dosing stopped. Twenty-two paces short of a parish marker, because that is where the carrier turned around and walked home."

"Nobody has looked," Foss said.

"A man named Tally has looked."

Nothing moved in the room except the water in the corner.

"North Ferriby walk, Timberline chapter of the Night Watch Society. Forty miles of timber a week and he counts deer, and on the ninth he found eleven hinds and a stag inside a quarter mile and measured the mass with a rule, and on the tenth he walked north to the stones and walked the far side of the marker twice to be certain it was clean." Malphas said it the way a man reads a bill of lading. "Then he carried the insert to the bulletin office himself, which he has never done, and asked the clerk what happens to a filing after the Bureau closes it. There was also a girl. Chapter coil-carrier, an artificer's apprentice out of a Measurewright line, and she sealed a sample tin standing in the cut on the ninth and assayed it the following morning."

"Then she got what we wanted her to get," Vesk said.

"She got a clean return, yes. She has been carrying it for five days and she has not filed it." Malphas stopped at the ninth hind and finally looked at one of them. "A man who checks the far side of a boundary twice is a man who has understood that the boundary is the evidence. I do not need to know how clever he is. I only need to know he wrote it down."

Foss took the glass out of his eye. "So we stop."

"We stop having edges." Malphas put two fingers against the hind's flank, above the grey-green crown of the growth, and pressed, and the hide moved under the pressure like the skin on cooling milk. "We have been practising rot synthesis and the slow breakdown of biological matrices, and we have proved it out, and the proof has a border drawn round it in a forester's notebook. The next work removes the border by making the dose walk."

Nuvalik straightened up with the knife still in her hand.

"You want them to get up."

"I want them to leave." Malphas took his fingers off the hind. "A carcass that stays in a field is a fact with a location and a date and a boundary marker twenty-two paces to the north. A carcass that walks eleven miles and lies down somewhere else is a bleed, because a bleed has no edge, and by the time anybody has a map of where the sick ground is, the sick ground is fifty miles wide with nothing in the middle of it and no line anywhere on it that a man could stand and check the far side of."

"With what," Foss said. "There is no Crystal in there. There is no self. You will not get a deer. You will get meat with a new scaffold in it and no reason to do anything."

"I do not need a reason. I need a gradient."

Vesk felt the whole thing arrive at once and had to put his hands flat on the bench.

"The new matrix is contractile," Malphas said. "Nuvalik has already got contraction out of the fourth hind's shoulder twice and did not think it worth mentioning, which I will discuss with her separately. Contractile tissue in a saturation gradient does what every growing thing in the world does, which is lean. It does not decide. It leans. Put a sink at one end of a valley and everything in that valley with our matrix in it comes down the gradient toward the sink at whatever speed the tissue allows, and it will keep coming until the substrate is spent, and the substrate is the animal."

"Days," Foss said.

"Four to six. Without an anchor it cannot persist and I am not putting an anchor in it, because an anchor is a binding, and a binding is a name, and a name is the only thing in this world that the Accord can actually prosecute." Malphas wiped his fingers on a cloth and folded the cloth and put it in his pocket. "Four to six days is eleven miles and eleven miles is enough. I will call the task Project Zombification."

"You will call it nothing," Deming said, with more voice than he had used all night. "A named project is a filed project. That is how they found the Hollow Shaft people. They found a name in a private correspondence and they hung everything off it."

"Then it is named and you will all use the name, and you will use it in writing, and you will use it in the correspondence Deming is worried about." Malphas looked mildly around the room. "We are going to lose somebody eventually and the Register will build its file off whatever is written down. I would prefer the file be built off a word I chose."

*He is doing something with the name and it is not about the deer.* Vesk had the thought and lost it immediately, the way he lost most things now, and what stayed was the pleasure. The dead getting up. He had been a boy in a village that put stones on its graves and he had asked why, and had been told, and had thought even then that it seemed a poor way to settle a question.

"Nuvalik. The ninth hind is the freshest and she is the one I want. Bring her down and have her on the table by the second division."

Nuvalik stood at the tail of the rail with the knife hanging and the fourth hind open to the shoulder behind her, and she did not move for long enough that Vesk started to count the drops in the corner.

She broke the wax with her thumbnail and did it badly, in four pieces, and two of them went on the floor.

"Leave them," Wystan said. "They are chapter wax and I will want them later. The back room."

The back room at Cutler Row had been a scullery before the Division took the building and it still had the drain in the floor and the long stone sink under the window, which was the only reason the Register had a bench worth anything. Vinegar first. The wash-tray reek came up as soon as he lifted the tins out of it and laid them along the sill to drain, and under the vinegar was cold brass and the iron smell the rain brought through the frame, and under that the mustard-and-lamp-oil staleness of eleven years of a man eating at his desk.

He lit the second lamp. Then the third, which he did perhaps six times a year.

"Ambient," he said.

Yinzhi already had her own coil out of the coat and was walking it slowly along the wall at chest height, watching the wire rather than the room. "Low. Very low. Your main has been off since when."

"The eleventh division. It goes off at the eleventh and comes back at the sixth and that is half the reason this desk exists." He pulled the stool round and sat, which put his eyes at the height of the bench. "Number, please, not an adjective."

She gave him the number. It was low enough that he did the arithmetic twice out of habit and got the same answer, which was that anything he found in the next hour would be sitting on a floor so quiet that a candle would have looked like a bonfire.

*Somebody spent years teaching her to read a wall and then died before she could pay it back.*

"Your assay," he said. "Walk it to me in sequence, and leave the result until the end."

"Coil calibrated at the chapter bench on the tenth, against the standard, at the eighth hour." She set her hands flat on the bench either side of the tin, and they were steady, which he noted. "Ambient taken before and after. Sample loaded dry, no wash, because I did not want to lose anything soluble. Four passes. Signature sweep, Family sweep, Category alignment, then a full sweep with the gate open because the first three gave me nothing."

"And the fourth burned the coil."

"The fourth burned the coil."

"Because you had the gate open on a null field." He turned the tin round with one gloved finger. "That is not a mistake, it is what the instrument is for, and whoever told you it was a mistake was protecting a budget. Say the result."

"Clean." Her jaw did the thing it had done in the other room. "No signature at all. Nothing faint, nothing degraded. Nothing."

"Then we are going to do it wrong."

He took the lid off.

The sample was a wedge of the crown mass, cut with a knife eleven days ago and sealed inside the hour, a little under two inches at the base. Grey through the body of it and greening toward the surface, fibrous at the cut where it had been part of something larger, and soft on the outer face and still damp. It had a smell. Faint, and green, and entirely wrong for a thing that had come out of an animal.

Under the third lamp he could see that it had grown since she cut it.

A quarter inch, perhaps, out along the crown face, in a fresh pale band that stood clear of the older tissue. Eleven days inside a Fixatio-anchored tin with the chapter's wax across the join. Fixatio holds what it is given. That is the entire reason the Division buys the tins, and it is why an evidence seal is admissible, and there was a quarter inch of new growth sitting inside one with the seal unbroken.

He looked at it for a long moment and then he wrote *crown +¼ in sealed, 11d* in the margin of the day book and moved on, because he had no explanation and the work does not wait for one.

"Mistress Qiu. Everybody who has looked at this has asked the instrument the same question and you asked it four times. The question is *who signed this*, and the answer is nobody, and four people have now written *bleed* on the strength of it." He set the wedge on the glass with the forceps and squared it to the bench. "I am going to ask a different one."

"Which."

"What is carrying the signature, instead of who signed it."

She did not say anything. He glanced up and found her watching the wedge with her arms folded and one thumb working at the brass of the bracelet, round and round the outer band where the proof-mark was struck badly.

"Residue is Aether that has been coupled," he said. "That is the whole of the science and it is four sentences long. A practitioner draws ambient Aether, couples it into his Crystal, spends it, and what he leaves in the room is coupled Aether carrying the signature of the thing that coupled it. The signature is the coupling. It is not a mark he puts on it, it is the shape his Lattice bent it into, and that is why he cannot wash it off and why I have eleven years of convictions." He adjusted the lamp a half inch. "So a null return means one of two things. Either there is no residue in this tin, which there plainly is, since there is a working still running in it eleven days on. Or there is residue, and it is coupled, and it is signed, and the signature belongs to something your coil is not built to look for."

"The coil looks for practitioners."

"The coil looks for Crystals above the Bureau's floor, because the Bureau pays for it and the Bureau prosecutes people." He held out his hand. "I need your gate open and I need you to sweep below the floor, down to the bottom of the range. You will read noise for a minute and a half and I want you to keep going through the noise."

"That burns coils."

"Yes."

She took the spare out of her coat, which told him she had come out in the rain with two, and she seated it and opened the gate and put the head down flat on the glass beside the wedge.

The noise came up first, a dirty crawling nothing that turns a good instrument into a wet fire. She held it. Her hand did not move. At about a minute she shifted her weight once and he saw her breathe out through her nose and hold the head steady, and at a minute and forty the wire went over.

It went over hard.

"There," she said, and her voice had gone flat, the body understanding ahead of the mouth. "There is a signature. It is enormous. It has been sitting under the floor the whole time."

"Read it."

"It is not a person." She had her face down to the coil. "Crystal, yes, developmental, the bottom of the tier table, barely opened. Harmonic profile is wide and shallow and there is no Path in it at all. There is no doctrine in it. There is no Domain seed." She looked up. "It is an animal. The residue is signed by the hind."

The room was very quiet. Out on Cutler Row the rain had settled into the steady kind that goes on until morning, and the gutter above the scullery window had a fault in it that Wystan had reported twice, and it ran down the glass in a sheet and made the lamplight swim.

"Say what that means," he said. "Out loud. I want to hear somebody else say it first."

"It means the deer performed the working." She said it slowly. "The deer is the operator. It coupled the Aether. Its own Lattice ran it."

"Which is why the tin reads clean at every level the Bureau cares about, and why Edwyn Brack is not a fool and is not corrupt and wrote the correct classification off a true measurement. There is no operator indicated because the operator has four legs and cannot be indicted." He was already writing. His hand had gone unsteady and he wrote through it, which produced the sloped illegible script the day clerks complained about. "And a hind does not wake up on a Tuesday and route a Verdantia expression through a Lattice that cannot hold one. Somebody put the working inside the animal and let the animal carry it."

"How."

"I have no idea. That is the part I cannot do and you can." He pushed the day book at her. "You are an artificer out of a Measurewright line and I am a filing officer who reads. Tell me what a formulation would have to be to survive introduction into a living body, run off that body's Lattice, and not couple to the hand that made it at any point in the process."

*He is not asking to be kind. He is asking because he has hit the edge of himself and he does not mind saying so.*

"It would have to never be in contact with the hand that made it while it was live," she said. "Inert in the flask. Live in the animal. You would need the synthesis to hold at some threshold and only go over it in the presence of something the body supplies and the bench does not." She had picked the pen up without noticing. "Heat. Or pH. Or the blood. Blood is the obvious one."

"Cost."

"Enormous. Verdantia at any working dilution is metered draw and metered draw is a supply contract." She stopped with the pen down on the paper, and he watched her arrive at the second half of it, and he let her arrive. "Which is a purse."

"Which is mine." He took the book back. "That is Article Fifteen, that is the only jurisdiction this desk has, and it is the first honest reason I have had in four years to be awake at this hour."

He put the forceps down and started on the left glove.

"Master Wystan."

"I am aware of what I am doing."

"You are not aware that I know what it is." She had come round the corner of the bench without his hearing her, which said something about his Perception at four in the morning. "Anamnetic contact off a live residue. My master did it. Off a coil housing out of a foundry fire, against advice, and he was sixty-one and his Anchoring was a C and he spent the last eleven months of his life correcting people who called him by the wrong name and being right about half the time."

"My Anchoring is not a C."

"You have not told me what it is and you are not going to, because you have your whole Stage under suppression and you have been holding it since before I came up the stairs, and I can tell because my bracelet has been pulling toward you since the door." She said it without heat. "I am telling you that I have watched this. That is all. I am not going to stand in front of the bench."

"Good, because I would move you." He got the second glove off and laid them both across the day book, squared, because he liked things squared. "Nine hinds and a stag are dead in a cut in the north and four counties have the same thing and the Grand Mage's office has sent back the weather form. This is the only piece of it I can touch with my hands."

"Then count out loud," she said. "Afterward. Names, if you have them. My master's people made him do that."

He put his bare fingers on the wedge.

It arrived without warning and from the wrong direction, already in progress, like a door opening onto a room where people have been talking for an hour.

Cold in the feet first. Wet ground, and the particular ache of standing in a cut in the ninth hour of a northern morning, and the whole enormous architecture of a body he did not have, four-legged and wrong-jointed and heavy at the front. The light was different. It came in flat and wide and there was too much of it at the edges. He was aware of the others without looking at them, seven or eight, spread along the birch, and the awareness had a texture he had no word for and would not get one.

And underneath all of it, in the gut and the spine and running out along the ribs, something enormously good was happening.

That was the part.

It did not hurt. There was no alarm in it anywhere. Whatever had been introduced into her was making her feel the way an animal feels in the second week of spring, warm through the trunk, heavy and slow and entirely unafraid, and she had stopped walking twenty minutes ago because standing still was better and there had been no reason in the world to do anything else. Her legs had gone at some point. She had not noticed. She was standing because the hide held her up and she did not know the difference and she was, in the last measurable moment of her existence, content.

The ravens came into the birch. She heard them arrive.

They did not come down.

Wystan took his hand off the wedge and made it to the stone sink and was sick until there was nothing left, and then twice more after that, which was the part that hurt.

He stayed bent over the sink with both hands on the cold edge of it and the rain running down the glass eight inches from his face and waited for the grey to come back off the top of his vision. Yinzhi did not touch him and did not speak. The gutter ran. Somewhere off toward the river a bell went for the fourth division, and he counted it, and got five, and let it go.

"Forty-two," he said, when he could.

"What."

"You asked me to count out loud." He straightened up by degrees. His mouth tasted of vinegar and bile and, faintly and impossibly, of green. "I carry forty-one minutes belonging to other people. Now it is forty-two. Thirty-nine of them are men and women whose names are in my files and I could give you every one in order. One of them is my senior officer and he is alive. One of them I have never identified." He wiped his mouth on the back of his wrist. "And one of them is a hind in the North Ferriby cut who was not frightened, and did not suffer, and stood there being warm while her legs came apart under her, and I will have that for the rest of my life and it is the worst thing in the tin."

Yinzhi had gone the colour of the wall.

"It is a kindness," she said. "Whoever did it made it a kindness."

"It is a design." He came back to the bench, unsteady, and sat, and looked at the wedge. "Pain makes an animal run. A hind that runs dies a mile away in a thicket where nobody counts her. He needed them to stay where they were put, so he built it so that stopping felt good, and that is a man who thought about the problem for a long time before he mixed anything."

He wrote it down. The writing took a while.

"There is no hand in this," he said, mostly to the page. "There never was. He does not go near them. He cannot, because the moment his Lattice touches the live formulation it couples and it signs and this whole apparatus falls over, so whatever he is doing he is doing at a distance from the animal and probably at a distance from the ground. He is not walking that line. He has never walked that line." He underscored it twice. "Which means the answer is not in the cut. It is in the water, or the licks, or whatever those animals all put their mouths on, and then it is in the ledger of whoever paid for two myriads of Verdantia."

"And Tally."

"Tally counts deer and has no signature on anything and is of no interest to a man who has arranged never to be within a mile of his own work." He capped the ink. "Write to him tomorrow and tell him to keep walking his line and to measure anything he finds. I want a second dataset from the same observer."

Yinzhi looked at him for a moment longer than the sentence needed. Then she reached past him, took the lid, and seated it back on the tin.

"Wrong tin," Wystan said. "Fresh tin, and I want the wax struck with my brass, not the chapter's, because from tonight this is a Register exhibit and not a Society filing." He found the drawer with the blank forms in it and pulled one out and turned it face up on the bench in front of her. "And I need your full name for the head of the docket. Lineage, generation character and courtesy, in the hand you were taught, because if this ends in a hearing you are the assay and they will come for you first.

She took the pen off the bench and wrote it out.

# The Regent — Aetherion Academy

*Aetherion Academy. The broken cold room under the south range. Continuous.*

Velder Crace was on his knees by the pier with both hands pressed under his ribs on the right side, where a bullet that should have been in Mu-jin's skull had gone into him instead. Blood came out between his fingers dark and slow. It was liver blood, the kind that does not spurt and does not stop. What was left of his pocket revolver lay in two pieces at his knees, the receiver slumped and still glowing where the cut had gone through it. The point of a blade rested a finger's breadth from the side of his neck. He was retching. Nothing came up but yellow spit, and his stomach went on heaving at nothing, and he did not dare move his head.

Malphas stood up.

He should not have been able to. The flesh was coming off him. The blue fire had blistered him from scalp to hip, and Lapis had split every blister in a heartbeat, and the skin hung from his forearms and his jaw in wet grey sheets that slid and peeled and dropped to the black floor with soft, heavy sounds, like wet paper coming off a wall. Underneath was red, raw meat, weeping. He got one foot under him, then the other. The crown of spurs rose out of the ruin of his scalp, white and steady.

And under the falling skin, something else was already growing.

Mu-jin saw it with the lens off and did not understand it for a full second, and then understood it all at once, the way he understood a failing glyph chain.

It was pale. It came up over the raw red of Malphas's arms in a thin, wet, translucent film, faintly milky, like the skin that forms on cooling milk, and then thickened as he watched into something rubbery and layered. Every vinegar-maker has seen it. Leave a crock of wine open long enough and a living mat grows across the top, a slab of pale jelly the brewers call the mother. It is made of the fibre that vinegar bacteria spin around themselves, sheet on sheet, until it holds together like wet leather. Malphas was growing it. He was fermenting himself a new skin.

And it was growing fast. Far too fast. A vinegar mother takes weeks to thicken on a crock. This one was taking seconds, because every reaction within fifteen metres of Kwon Mu-jin ran a billion times faster than the world allowed. The bacteria spun their cellulose at the speed Lapis gave them, and the sheets went down over Malphas in layers, one on another on another, too many. They swelled into ridges along his arms and lumps along his jaw. The growth ran past the edges of the wounds onto healthy skin and kept going, the way a thing grows when nothing tells it to stop.

*I am healing him.* The thought arrived late, like everything. *The stone does not ask whose body a reaction belongs to. It never has.*

Malphas flexed his cooked left hand. The new pale skin stretched over the charred lattice and held. He looked at it, and then at Mu-jin, and something in the ruin of his face that might have been a smile moved under the jelly.

---

Up at the rim of the hole, Agnes Tull was the first to see her.

The students had finally gone back. Agnes had stood at the broken low wall with her arms out long enough, and the wardsmith had shouted something about his gauge that made the boldest of them go pale, and they had backed off toward the infirmary steps in a loose, frightened crowd, forty pairs of eyes on the column of steam. Agnes turned to make sure the last of them was moving. When she turned back, there was a woman on the rubble where nobody had been a moment before.

She was tall. Layered robes: a dark plum overrobe with sleeves wide enough to hide a hand, a maroon tabard fringed with pale fur and marked with a silver crescent. Long pale-blond hair hung loose under a mask-helm of shingled green scales that covered her brow and one eye, set between two dark crescent horns, with cords and tassels swinging from it. Her face was painted white. Her mouth was dark. At her raised right hand, not quite touching her fingers, a sphere of glass the size of a skull turned slowly in the hail, pale blue, with something moving inside it, a thick slow swirl that pulsed as though it were breathing. A ribbon of violet-white light trailed off it and coiled back around her.

Agnes's keys stopped jangling.

She did not know who the woman was. She knew a great deal about faces, after thirty years of doors, and she knew this one did not belong to the Academy, and that it was not afraid of anything, and that it was looking down the hole the way a woman looks into a pot to see if it is done.

"You're not faculty," Agnes said. Her voice came out smaller than she meant it to. She raised it. "You're not faculty, and you're not to be on that rubble, missus, so I'll ask you to step back."

The woman did not look at her.

Behind Agnes, on the infirmary steps, a first-year began to cry, a thin, high, hiccupping sound, and the sphere at the woman's hand pulsed once, brighter, as if it had heard. The wardsmith at the rim looked down at his gauge, and his face went grey. The needle had swung over hard and was holding there, trembling, and the reading was not coming from the hole. It was coming from the glass.

Every frightened breath in the yard was feeding it.

---

The sphere tipped.

It did not fall. It leaned in the air over the hole the way a jug leans over a cup, and out of it, from somewhere inside that slow pulsing swirl, came rot. It poured down through the hole in a thick, glistening rope and struck the floor of the vault at Malphas's feet, and where it struck, it spread.

It was purple.

Mu-jin knew that colour. Every salt-pan worker knew it, and every marsh-cutter. Where the brine grows thick enough on a drying pan, the water turns pink, then rose, then deep purple, as salt-loving creatures too small to see bloom across it in their billions. And where a pond goes foul and black and stinking of sulphur, the same thing happens at its edges: a purple bloom spreads over the black, living creatures that drink the very gas that kills everything else. They eat sulphide. They turn the poison into their colour.

That was what came out of the glass. A purple bloom that went across the Black Ground like dye spreading through wet cloth and drank the last of Malphas's miasma out of the air as it went. The rotten-egg stink thinned. A smell of salt came up in its place, sharp and clean as a tide flat, and the black floor around Malphas went violet in a widening ring, and the ring rose.

It came up over his boots, over his shins, over his new pale skin. It came up over the overturned cots and the dead girl's outflung hand, and dried them as it passed, so that a crust of white salt bloomed on her fingertips. Lapis caught it. Mu-jin felt his own field take hold of the bloom without asking him, as it took hold of everything, and the purple doubled and doubled again in the time it took to see it, every one of those tiny living things dividing a billion times faster than it should.

*She knew,* Mu-jin understood, very late. *Whoever she is, she knew what my field would do with it. She is using the stone to carry him out.*

The bloom reached Malphas's waist, his chest, his shoulders. It masked him. Only his face was still clear above it, ruined and jellied and crowned with spurs.

He began to laugh.

It started as a low chuckle in his chest, wet with blood, and grew. He laughed the way a man laughs at the end of a long argument when he realises both sides were right. He laughed with his head tipped back toward the hole and the hail and the sphere turning far above, and the laughter came out of a throat with new skin growing over the inside of it.

"Ah," he said at last, softly. "Look at the time." His eyes found Mu-jin's through the steam, and they were almost fond. "It seems our battle will have to be suspended for now, Teacher. We'll call it a lesson unfinished. You hate those." His gaze went past Mu-jin, briefly, to the man on his knees by the pier with a blade at his throat and his liver bleeding into his hands. "Velder. You have been a fine student. I'm sorry. A student can't come where I'm going."

Velder Crace looked up at his lord. Whatever had been in his face until that moment went out of it.

Malphas looked down at the lens lying on the black floor where he had dropped it, unbroken, staring up at the hole.

"Keep it," he said to Mu-jin. "You'll want to see me clearly next time, and I would hate for you to squint."

The purple closed over his face.

And then he was not standing in it. He was part of it. His body went into the bloom the way sugar goes into hot water, all at once and without a sound, and the shape of him held for half a heartbeat in violet and then flowed. The whole purple ring drew inward on itself, and up, into the rope of rot that ran from the floor to the glass. It went up the rope in waves. Each wave squeezed the next along, the way a gut moves its food, the way a peristaltic pump in a Draw Age waterworks pushes water uphill by pinching its pipe in rings. One wave. Another. A long throb of purple climbing out of the vault through the hole, past the broken ribs, up into the grey light and the hail.

The rope ran back into the sphere, and the sphere drank it, and pulsed once, deep and slow and satisfied, and something new turned in the swirl inside the glass.

The floor of the cold room was black again where the bloom had been. There was salt on everything. The dead girl's hand lay crusted white. Of Malphas there was nothing left at all but the lens, and the smell of a tide flat, and the empty place where he had stood.

---

The woman at the rim lowered her hand, and the sphere drifted down to hang at her hip, turning gently. She had not moved her feet once.

Then she looked down the hole, past the broken ribs, past the steam, to the figure standing over Velder Crace with a blade.

Zeraphine Drowl had a voice like a channel-keeper calling the order of the beds, level, unhurried, pitched to carry exactly as far as it needed to and no further. She used it now.

"You're quick," she said to Haruki. "I saw what you did with the bullet. Quick ends wars early." The sphere at her hip pulsed, and on the infirmary steps the first-year's crying hitched and grew louder. "We can't afford an early end. So I'll take him, and I'll go, and you will let me, because the only thing between you and this glass is forty children on those steps, and every one of them is afraid."

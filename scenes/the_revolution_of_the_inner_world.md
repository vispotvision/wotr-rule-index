# The Revolution Of The Inner World

dHe had lost his daughter and there was no arrangement of the world in which that was survivable, and he survived it, and both of those were true at the same time for eleven months and are true still.

Sodoku Moto did not grieve in any manner the Seat could recognise. There was no shutting of a door and no drinking and no long silence in a cold room, and there was nothing anybody could point at afterward and say that was the week he broke, and the absence of a week like that was what frightened the people around him. He got up. He ate what was put in front of him. He sat the councils and read the returns and asked the correct questions about grain and about the standpipes and about which valleys had lost their ward-keepers, and he did all of it with a competence nobody had seen out of him before the ninth hour, and the competence was the symptom.

What he had instead of grief was a passion, and it had one object, and the object was that the thing which had happened in that cistern doorway would not happen again to anybody's daughter in any valley under the sky, and that this required the four quarters of the north to stop being four quarters.

He was in solace. That is the word Yoko used for it and she used it once and never again. Peace was no part of it and neither was resignation. Solace: the state of a man who has been given the only consolation available to him and has found that it is enough to stand on and not enough to sleep on. He worked eighteen hours and slept five and was courteous to everyone and buried nobody twice, and Emira, who was eighteen and had been watching men lie to themselves since she was four, told Yoko in the third month that she had stopped being able to find the place where her brother ended and the work began, and that she had gone looking for it eleven times and had not found it, and that she was not going to look again because looking was beginning to feel like an accusation.

The south went first because the south was always going to go first.

Edward Lambert did the honours for the dead and there were three thousand and eleven of them in the southern registers alone, and he did every one himself and would not delegate a name, and the reason was the reason it always is with that man, which is that a name delegated is a name approximated and an approximated name is a small lie told about a person who is no longer in a position to correct it. He sat in the Seat's lower hall for six weeks with a clerk on either side and read out the returns from the reach, valley by valley, and had the parish rolls fetched when a return gave a byname instead of an entered one, and where no roll existed he sent riders, and where the riders came back empty he entered the person by what they were called in their own village and marked the entry as unconfirmed and put his initial beside it so the responsibility was legible.

Three thousand and eleven. Fifty-three years old, grey at the temple and nowhere else, collar done up to the throat, gloves on indoors, and a wax tablet he no longer wrote on because there was nothing left worth noting.

He also, in the same six weeks, orchestrated the destruction of the orc host in the southern reach, and did it without ever once being present at an engagement.

That is the thing they will remember about the southern campaign and it is the thing the Bench will spend forty years arguing about, because there is no battle in it with his name on it and there is no order in the file that anyone can point to and call a masterpiece. What there is instead is a supply schedule. Eleven weeks of it, in a dry hand, itemised, and any officer who reads it for the first time comes away thinking he has read an administrative document, and any officer who reads it twice comes away cold.

He starved them out of a country they thought they had taken.

The orc advance out of the Ashenmoor had gone eleven miles past the wall in the first month and had held, and holding is expensive, and Lambert had done nothing about the holding at all. What he did was buy grain. He bought it in Nalūn through four factors who did not know about each other and he bought it at prices that made the Vaultmere houses laugh at him, and he shipped it north, and he did not distribute one bushel of it to any settlement in the southern reach. He let those settlements empty. He let them walk north with what they could carry, on roads he had ordered cleared and picketed six weeks before there was any visible reason to clear them, and he received them at Ashwell and at the Stormfold crossings and fed them out of grain nobody could work out why he had bought.

And he left the orc host in possession of eleven miles of empty country with the winter coming and eleven thousand mouths and no standing crop, and every well salted by the people who had dug them, and every root cache emptied by the families who had cut them, and the whole of it done voluntarily and in advance and without a single order being issued that could be entered as scorched-earth, because Lambert had never issued one. He had simply moved the people and their food out from under an army and left the army standing on ground that had nothing in it.

They ate their draught animals in the second month. They ate the sick in the third and there is a deposition about that which Lambert read once and had entered and never referred to again.

When Bram Greymane came south in the fourth month with eleven hundred men, what he found was not an army. He said so in his report, in four words, and the four words are quoted in every staff college in the Inner World.

*"There was nothing to fight."*

And the thing that will be argued about for forty years is not whether it worked. It is a question Tabitha Hallenfeld asked in open council in the seventh month, in front of the Queen's chair and the whole assembly, and which nobody answered then and nobody has answered since.

*"Why did you buy the grain in the first week?"*

Because the advance came in the fourth. Because on the day Edward Lambert placed his first order with a factor in Vaultmere, no orc had crossed the wall, and no council had been called, and no threat assessment existed in any file in the Seat that would have justified the expenditure, and he spent eleven thousand in Kharven's coin on a contingency that had not yet occurred and told nobody what it was for.

He never answered her. He put his hands behind his back and waited, and the council moved on because councils do, and Tabitha Hallenfeld went home and wrote the question down and dated it, and has asked it twice since in private and been refused twice.

The Great Battle of Stormfold was fought over four days in the fifth month at the crossings above the Stormfold hall, and it is the largest set engagement in the recorded history of the Inner World, and it did not need to happen.

That is the part nobody says out loud in the halls. The host was already broken. The starving was already done. What came over the Stormfold crossings in the fifth month was a thing that had stopped being an army and had become eleven thousand desperate creatures moving north because north was where the food had gone, and Bram Greymane put himself across their line with eleven hundred men and four hundred Ashwell tenants and did not let one of them through.

He held the crossings for four days.

The first day was a fight. The second day was a fight and the fighting was worse because the ground had gone to mud and mud is the great leveller of formations, and on the third day it stopped being a fight in any sense a manual would recognise and became a slaughter conducted uphill in freezing rain by men who had been awake for sixty hours against creatures who had been starving for ninety days, and on the fourth day the Stormfold hall's own bell rang the whole afternoon because the woman ringing it had gone into the kind of state where a body does one thing and cannot be persuaded to stop.

Bram Greymane lost the last three fingers of his left hand on the second day, taken off at the second joint by a falchion he never saw, and he wrapped the hand in a strip of his own surcoat and went on holding a shield with a forearm strap and a stump for the rest of the engagement, and when they finally got the wrapping off in the Stormfold hall on the fifth morning the surgeon said out loud, in front of him, that he had never seen a man do that to himself on purpose.

He has the scars from the second day across the whole left side of his jaw and down the neck. He has a bad hip from the third and walks with the left foot turned out. He has a mark across the back of the skull that his hair no longer covers, and the shield that came off the ridge with him, the same one, the one he threw down once and picked back up, went into the Stormfold hall with a boss cracked through in two directions and has never been rebound and hangs there still.

He lived. That is the whole of what the campaign got him and it took him eleven months to work out whether it was a gain.

Because he learned about his brother in the eighth month.

It came in the ordinary way, which is the cruelty of it. A courier out of the north with a bundle of returns, and among the returns a Reckoner's entry from the Ashgate road with a list of the dead on it, and Wren Greymane's name in the middle of the second column with a note beside it that read *ditch bank, entered by field observation, body not recovered*.

And underneath that, in the same bundle, because Lambert believed that a man learning a thing should learn all of it at once, sixteen depositions from men who had marched sixteen months in a Tenrai column under a Greymane banner.

Bram Greymane read them in a room in the Stormfold hall with the door shut and did not come out for a day and a half.

Sixteen months. Six hundred men chosen for faith and never for skill. Eleven of them with wives who had stopped writing. Cass Holloway burying his mother in the autumn and not being permitted to attend and saying *aye* and going back to the horses. Three killed by their own kin, entered in a book. Two hundred and ten feet at the narrows, counted in summer under a false name with a cart of salt fish, drawn on the back of a psalter.

The smile across two hundred paces of dead men on the ridge.

And nowhere in sixteen depositions, in any hand, from any man, one line explaining why nobody had been told, because none of the sixteen had known either.

He came out of that room on the second morning and gave one order, and the order is entered in the Stormfold book in his own hand with the fingers he had left, and it is four lines long and it is the only thing he ever said about it in public.

*"This campaign is dedicated to Wren Greymane, who was called traitor in every village between here and the Stormfold for sixteen months, by me among others. Any man who says the word in my hearing from this day will answer to me personally. Enter the dedication in the roll. Enter it before my own name."*

Then he went back to the crossings and finished it.

What was left of the Tenrai after Kujo's army came apart was very little and it came north in pieces over the sixth and seventh months, and Kharven took them in as refugees, which is the single most contested decision of the entire post-war and which Sodoku Moto made alone and against the advice of every officer in his service.

They came in wagons and on foot and eleven of them came in a boat that should not have floated. They came having eaten what the orc host had left, which was nothing, and having been the enemy for eleven years, and having in a great many cases personally burned villages that were now expected to feed them. There were four thousand two hundred at the first count and three thousand nine hundred at the second, and the difference is entered as *attrition on the road* and everybody in the Seat knew what the difference actually was and nobody made a record of it.

Lorn Stark would have refused them. Lorn Stark is dead.

Osric would have refused them and would have said so at volume and would have been right on every count he raised. Osric is dead.

Hild would have taken them and would have been called soft for it for the rest of her life and would have carried that, and Hild is dead, and that is the reason her father took them, and he has never once said so.

And Miku Tenrai Moto came in with the eighth column.

They had him in the middle of a wedge of eleven men when the Kharven riders came up on them at the Brine crossing, and the eleven had been with him since the collapse and had walked him four hundred miles and had eaten less than he had, and they closed around him and went to a knee with their weapons out and their faces up, which is the Tenrai posture for a household guard that intends to die where it is standing and wishes the fact entered.

Sodoku Moto rode into the middle of that on Tōga with the frost coming off the animal in a front and eleven hundred men behind him.

He asked once.

He asked in form, in the old register, which is a courtesy the Tenrai had not extended to a Moto in eleven years, and he asked them to stand aside and put the weapons in the mud and be taken in as the others had been taken in, and he gave his word on the boy's life in terms that any Reckoner in four quarters would have entered as binding.

They refused.

They refused because they had been told for eleven years, by men who believed it, that the Kinslayer King had opened his own family's throats in a settlement in the south and that a Moto in his hands is a Moto in a box, and because that was true, and because there was nothing in that man's history that a frightened household guard could weigh against it. Their captain said so out loud. He was perhaps forty and had a face like a gate that had been kicked in and he said, in Common so the boy would understand, that the Tenrai household did not hand its heir to the man who had done the Tenrai settlement, and that they would be obliged if he would get on with it.

Sodoku Moto got down off the Droval.

He killed all eleven and it took him under a minute and he did it with Kurosetsu and he did not use the cold, and Yoko, who was there, entered afterward in her own hand that the refusal to use the cold was the worst thing about it, because the cold would have been faster and the cold was available and a stilling would have put the whole eleven down inside a second and a half without one of them feeling the finish.

He cut them apart in the mud in front of the child instead, one at a time, in the order they came at him, because the boy had been told what he was and there was no version of that afternoon in which the boy did not find out whether it was true, and Sodoku Moto had decided somewhere on the ride down that the finding out would be honest.

The captain went last and went badly. He got a hand on the harness and got the point of a knife into the mail at the hip and it stopped there, and Sodoku broke his arm at the elbow with the flat and then opened him from the collarbone down through the sternum with a cut that came up instead of down, and stood over him for the three or four seconds it took, and did not look away, and did not permit the man the courtesy of being finished quickly, because a household guard who has said what he said in front of his charge has earned the entire truth of the position he took.

Then he cleaned the blade on his own sleeve, which is a thing no king does and which every man in that column saw.

And he turned round and there was a nine-year-old boy standing in eleven men's blood in the mud at the Brine crossing, trembling so hard he could not keep his feet under him, and Sodoku Moto walked over and went down onto one knee in front of him and put his hand on the boy's head.

"You don't have to tremble."

Miku Tenrai Moto could not answer. His whole frame was going and his teeth were going and he had wet himself and had not noticed and would notice later and would remember the noticing longer than he remembered the killing.

"I am not going to hurt you. I promise."

He said it in Common. He said everything in Common that afternoon and he had thought about it on the ride down and had decided that the boy had been given enough things he could not follow.

"Your family has been telling you a lot of things. Corrupting your mind. Making you think that you are destined to continue their hatred." His hand did not move off the boy's head. "But you see it all, don't you."

And Miku closed his eyes and the tears came down his face, and he did not answer because the answer was so far past anything a nine-year-old has words for that the crying was the whole of it.

The Tenrai were gifted with the temporal, which is the thing every hall in the Inner World knows about them and which nobody outside the line has ever properly understood. It is not prophecy and it is not a vision and it does not arrive when it is called. It is the sight of the road forward, given without warning and without mercy, at whatever length the Plane has decided the bearer can carry.

He had watched all of it. Every man who had ever fed him or carried him or wrapped his hands in winter. He had watched them die at the ages he would be when it happened, one at a time, over four years, from the age of five, and he had told an adult about it exactly once and had been beaten for it and had never told anyone again.

He had watched this. The mud, and the eleven, and the captain, and the man on one knee with a hand on his head.

And he had come to the Brine crossing anyway, because there is no version of the temporal that lets a child do anything about it, and because a nine-year-old who knows what is coming still cries when it arrives, and being unable to stop yourself from being vulnerable while watching yourself be vulnerable in advance is a specific torture that the Tenrai have never once written down.

Sodoku moved his hand and put it flat on the boy's back.

"It isn't your fault." He kept it there. "You were destined to carry the hatred of your line. But there is no such thing as lines. We are all one family, Miku. We are all Moto. We are all descendants of Bara. Do you understand?"

The boy did not understand. He was nine.

But he understood the weight. That was the thing about Miku Tenrai Moto and it had been the thing about him since he was five, that he had spent his entire short life watching adults change how they stood when he came into a room, watching them lower their voices and raise their chins and hand him things, and he had worked out very early and entirely alone that the weight was not about him and that it was about something he was carrying, and that everybody could see it except him.

He understood that the man in front of him was asking him to put it down.

Sodoku pulled back and sat on his heels in the mud so their eyes were level.

"We have to do better. We have to be better. For the sake of these lands." He let it sit. "With you. With your support, we can end this endless fight."

And the boy said nothing for a long time, and then said the thing that ended eleven years of war, in a small flat voice, with his hands at his sides.

"I will forfeit. And tell all of my men to pull away. And order whoever is fighting against you to stop."

Sodoku Moto did not thank him.

That is entered in three separate depositions and every one of them notices it. He did not thank the child and he did not smile and he did not tell him he had done well, and Yoko has said since that it was the single most correct thing she has ever watched him do, because a man who thanks a nine-year-old for surrendering an empire has told the nine-year-old that a transaction occurred.

What he said instead was the offer, and the offer was already made before he came down the road, and the boy's answer had changed nothing about it, and that was the point.

"You don't need to worry. Your family will be treated as royalty." He got up out of the mud with the old sound his knees made. "In fact all those, all those of the Moto lines, will take part in becoming the Royal Family of the new empire. The one my father always wanted to build. A union. A body of peace."

Miku Tenrai Moto looked up at him.

And whatever he saw, standing in eleven men's blood at the Brine crossing at nine years old with the temporal running behind his eyes, he has never told anybody, and he is fourteen now and holds a seat at the table and has been asked eleven times, and the answer he gives is always the same and it is always four words.

*"I saw him mean it."*

Seiji Tenrai Moto was taken alive in the ninth month at a hunting lodge four miles inside the Amagiri line by men who had been his own household guard eleven weeks earlier, and there is no deposition anywhere that describes how he was found, because the men who found him were paid and dismissed and none of them has ever been asked.

What is entered is what happened afterward, and it was entered by a Reckoner who was made to stand and watch it and who has since retired and does not speak about it.

He crawled.

That is the first line of the entry and the Reckoner wrote it plainly because it was the plain fact of the matter. Twenty-nine years old, the architect of thirteen years of a design that had cost the north perhaps two hundred thousand lives, and the smile that reached his eyes because he had trained it to, and at the end of it he crawled backward across a lodge floor on his elbows with his heels pushing and his hands up.

"Please spare... me my lord..."

His father's face had been a wall. Say nothing. Be overlooked. Seiji's face had been a door, and the door had worked for thirteen years on everybody it was pointed at, and at the very end he tried it one more time on a man who had spent a lifetime learning what a face is for, and it did not open, and he had nothing behind it.

The chains came up around his legs.

They came out of the floor and out of the air and out of nothing at all, and the Reckoner's entry describes them accurately and at length and the entry is unpleasant to read, because what they did to the man's legs before they reached his waist was structural. The bonds of a body are not metaphor. A body is a set of agreements between parts about staying joined, and Calamity is the argument against every one of those agreements simultaneously, and what it does first is win the small ones.

*"Spare you."*

*"Where was that mercy when you killed my brothers?"*

*"Where was that when we asked you to stop?"*

There is a pause entered between the third and the fourth, and the Reckoner has marked the length of it, and the length is eleven seconds.

*"Seiji. I revoke the name Moto from you."*

*"You're no Moto. You're just a snake dressed up as a Moto. Gifted blood you cannot live up to."*

*"Enjoy this torment."*

And it was slow, and it was meant to be slow, and it took the better part of an hour.

Calamity does not burn and does not cut. What it does is remove the permission by which a structure continues, one structure at a time, from the smallest upward, and a human body has a very great many of those and the small ones are the vessels. Every blood vessel in Seiji Tenrai's body came apart, from the capillary beds outward, over fifty-one minutes, and the man was conscious for forty-eight of them because the brain is well supplied and goes last.

He stopped being able to scream at around the thirtieth minute. He did not stop trying.

There is one further line in the Reckoner's entry and it is the last one and it is the reason the entry is sealed.

*The revocation was performed in form. Full register, correct contours, before a witness. It is valid. The name Moto has been taken off him and cannot be given back by anybody, and he died without one, and I have entered him as elar-ama, contingent, held by nobody, and there is no other form available to me.*

*I have been a Reckoner for twenty-two years and this is the first time I have had to enter a man as having no name while he was still audible in the next room.*

Edward Lambert went to the graves on the morning of the first frost with his hands behind his back and stood in front of three stones in the upper burying ground at Kharven-Seat and did not kneel and did not remove anything.

Hild Ice. Twelve years old. Lorn Stark, who had made an arrangement about that girl twelve years ago and had kept every part of it. Osric of Hallenfeld, who had put himself in a doorway.

They had been cut and set in the same week by the same mason, and the mason had matched the depth of the lettering across all three at Lambert's own instruction, because a difference in depth between stones cut in one week says something about relative importance and Edward Lambert had spent fifty-three years refusing to let a document say anything he had not intended.

Brida came up the path behind him.

She should not have been walking. She had taken it across the abdomen at the sally gate in the ninth hour and had been eleven days in the altar-room with the night-stone ticking as it cooled beside her, and the wound had been what she herself called the third bowl, the one where they stop asking for water because they have forgotten they were thirsty, and she had gone into it and come back out of it, which almost nobody does. She was walking with a stick and would be for a year. Her colour was wrong and would be wrong for longer than that.

She had been on that path forty minutes because it is four hundred yards.

She stood beside him and did not say anything for a while and then said the true thing, which is what she does, which is why the Seat sends her to the ones who will not talk.

"It must be hard to be the oldest," she said. "And be the only one alive."

Edward Lambert did not answer immediately.

Then he looked down at the fourth stone, which was set apart from the other three by the width of a path, and which was smaller, and which had less on it, because there had been less to put on it: a name, and an office, and no dates, because the parish book at Vaeloris that held his brother's dates no longer contained them and nobody in four worlds could say why.

Charles Lambert. Archbishop. Nothing else.

There is no record of what happened to the man. There is a hole where the record should be. The ordination roll has a gap, the vestry minutes for nineteen years are missing a hand that everyone remembers reading, the gate-roll for the morning has no entry, and the Alabaster correspondence file has a nine-year run in a hand nobody can now attribute. Edward had ridden south himself in the second month and had spent six weeks in three archives and had come back with nothing at all, and had never told anybody what he had been doing, and everybody had known.

"If only I was stronger."

He said it to the small stone.

"But only vengeance." His hands stayed behind his back. "Only rage."

Brida put her hand on his arm and left it there and did not tell him it was not his fault, because she had been eleven days in the third bowl and had come out of it with no patience left for the sentences people say at gravesides.

They stood there a while.

And that is where Robin Ice found him, seventeen years old, having ridden through the night as he does, with blood on his left ear that was not his as usual and a courier's coat two sizes too big cinched round twice.

"Edward."

He had stopped saying *my lord* in the fourth month and had never been corrected.

"We have come to report. We hanged all the members of the Goblin Senate." He got his breath. "The Hobgoblins want to speak about peace offerings."

Edward Lambert turned round.

Robin Ice had ridden a hundred and eleven miles in eleven hours and had seen the man's face in every condition it comes in, at the wall and in the hall and on the road, and he said afterward, to a woman in a tavern in the Cooper's quarter who did not believe him, that the face which turned round in the upper burying ground that morning was one he had never seen before and has not seen since, and that there was nothing in it at all.

"Kill all of the hostages."

Robin Ice did not move.

"They are to be examples why they should never raise a hand at the Kingdom of Kharven."

There were four hundred and six hostages. They were the surety for a truce negotiated in the sixth month by Lambert's own office, under his own seal, and eleven of them were children under twelve, and the seal on the instrument was his, and the instrument is in the file with his initial beside the clause on their treatment.

Robin Ice stood on that path for what he later put at four seconds.

Then he said "Aye," and turned his horse, and rode, and did it, and has never once said in any hall that he questioned the order, and has told two people privately that he did not, and both of them believe him, and the reason they believe him is that Robin Ice is a runner and a runner who begins weighing the traffic stops being one.

Brida Ashwell watched the boy go down the path.

"Edward."

"No."

"I have not said anything."

"You have been standing beside me for a quarter of an hour and I have heard all of it."

She let a moment go past.

"Your brother would have."

"My brother," said Edward Lambert, "is a hole in an ordination roll." He was still looking down the path. "And he did whatever it was he did, and nobody knows what it was, and there is no version of the man left that anybody can appeal to. Not by me, not by you, and not by the four hundred and six."

"And if there were."

"Then I would still have given the order," he said, "and I would have had to look at him afterward, and I have been spared that, and I would like it noted that I am aware of what I just said."

He put his gloves on properly and went down to the hall and sat the afternoon council and asked the correct questions about grain.

Bram Greymane stood over the commander of the Third Ashfold in the lower yard of the Stormfold hall on the eleventh day of the tenth month with a cup of something hot in his good hand.

Vresk Dokkan of Kaadre-Six had been Vorruk-Kaan for eleven years. In Old Vaross a name is a service record: given name, patronymic, district, and the rank compound, and only the compound changes in a lifetime, and it changes at every promotion and every demotion, so that any hobgoblin in the Expanse hearing a man's name hears exactly what the institution currently thinks of him. *Vorruk*, ground that tried to move and was stopped. *Kaan*, the hand set to a thing.

The officer whose function is holding ground. Eleven years, and never once given a compound that meant taking any, and he had been sent north to take some, and he had broken forty feet of the Kharven wall in four nights of patient work under a shed roof of his own making, and had then met three hundred men in a gap twenty-two feet wide.

Bram crouched so their faces were level, which took him a while now because of the hip.

"Remember when your men took my fingers."

He held up the left hand. Three stumps at the second joint, healed hard and shiny, the skin over them still bright pink at eleven months.

"You might be asking why I've taken my time to mutilate every bit of you."

He had. He had taken eleven days. It had begun with a fingertip and had gone on from there in a specific order, and the order had a document behind it, and the document was a roll.

"I've taken a fingertip, then all of your toes, for each of my comrades who died during this war."

There were more names on the roll than there were extremities on a hobgoblin, and Bram Greymane had known that on the first morning and had gone ahead anyway, and had moved to other things when he ran out, and had entered each one against a name, and the roll is in the Stormfold book with a mark beside every entry in a hand with three fingers missing from it.

Egil Vald is on it. Cass Holloway is on it, from the depositions, entered by a man who never met him.

Wren Greymane is not on it, and the omission is deliberate, and Bram has never explained it.

"I don't want to kill you."

He said it without heat. That is the thing everyone who was in that yard has said since: there was no heat in any part of it, at any point, on any of the eleven days.

"I am going to return you back to the Hobgoblin Imperials, so they'll see what I've made of their commander."

And that is the cruelty, and it is not the mutilation, and Bram Greymane knew exactly what he was doing because Lambert had briefed him on Old Vaross naming in the fourth month and Bram had listened.

A hobgoblin's name is his record. Vresk Dokkan was going back alive, and the Keth-Gorrum would have to look at what came through the gate, and would have to give him a compound, because a returning officer is renamed and there is no provision anywhere for declining to.

And there is no compound in four hundred years of Old Vaross for what he now was.

It could not be *vorruk*, because he had not held, and it could not be any of the taking compounds, because he had not taken. He was not dead and could not be entered as dead. He was not a captive, because he had been returned. Whatever the Keth-Gorrum wrote beside his name would be a new word, coined for him, and every hobgoblin in the Expanse would hear it and would know it was new, and would know why, for as long as the man lived and afterward.

Bram Greymane had not maimed a commander. He had made a nation invent a word.

"I want you to remember the name of the person who took your virginity."

He put his hand on the man's chin, which had very little left on it, and moved the head so the eyes came round, and smacked it twice, lightly, the way you rouse a drunk.

"Don't die now."

And then he stood up, on the hip, with the cup still in his good hand, and gave the order to load him into the cart, and went inside out of the rain, and drank the rest of it sitting by the fire in the Stormfold hall under a shield with a boss cracked in two directions, and did not say another word for the rest of the evening to anybody.

And in the spring, under a sky that four countries had agreed to describe with the same word, the instrument was drawn.

Kharven, and the Holy Sea of Alabaster, and the Kingdom of Nalūn, and the stretches from the Brine crossing east to the marches of Sum-gol, under one seal and one body of law, with the Moto lines seated as the royal house and a fourteen-year-old boy with the temporal running behind his eyes holding a chair at the table by right of a forfeiture he made at nine years old in eleven men's blood.

The first Accord circle. The thing Muken Moto had wanted and had died four years short of asking for.

Sodoku Moto put his hand to it last.

And Yoko Mishiro, who had been chained once and had been unchained by that man and had spent nine years finding out what he was, wrote in her own record that night, in the plain form she uses for everything, four lines that the Bench has never been permitted to see.

*He built it for her. Every clause. The refugee clause, the royal house clause, the one about children of the defeated lines. All of it is a twelve-year-old girl in a doorway and none of it says so and none of it ever will.*

*He will not say it because saying it would make her the reason, and he does not want her to be a reason. He wants her to be a person who was killed.*

*I have been beside this man for nine years and this is the first thing he has done that I do not think I could have done.*

*And it is going to hold. That is the part nobody has understood yet. It is going to hold, because a thing built out of grief that refuses to name the grief has no argument in it that anybody can get a grip on, and there is nothing in that instrument for a future council to repeal.*

The forges went out for the announcement and that was the part the Dawi never forgave.

The annexation they had costed and absorbed. The banners were new and nobody had been consulted about them, and the two men on the assay platform above the third furnace floor stood with the whole of the Ore Council ranged behind them in the posture of witnesses at a hanging where the question of who is being hanged remains open. All of that a Dawi files and carries. The forges were something else. Thane-Gorr's furnaces had burned continuously for four hundred and ten years, fed by array-work that holds a temperature no fuel reaches and holds it for days, and the guild masters had banked them at the fourth hour of the morning so that twelve thousand people could hear a speech.

Borin Ironheart stood in the second rank of the Forge Guild's delegation with his cap in his fist and smelled the crater cooling.

That was the thing nobody outside the Ring understood about a banked furnace. A banked furnace has its own smell, sulphide going over from hot to warm, sweeter and worse than the working stink, and under it the quench oil in the trough behind him had stopped moving its skin and had gone over to the flat rancid stink of oil that has sat, and under that, faintly, from the fourth level down, the cold mineral breath of the seam itself with no fire between it and the air.

The silence was the expensive part. Fourteen hours of banked furnace across seven levels is a number the Assay Guild could put to the kes, and did, and submitted, and had returned to it unmarked.

*They stopped the fires to be heard. There is not one man up there who knows that the fires are the hearing.*

Sodoku Moto came to the rail of the platform.

He had been in the crater since the second day and had said perhaps two hundred words in that time and every one of them had been a question about throughput. He was in the coat and not the armour. His hair was tied back and the forge-glow from below put a colour in it that white hair only takes near a working furnace, and the furnaces were out, so the colour came off the banked coals and was duller, and the men watching noticed that too because Dawi notice light.

"I have freed you of the corrupt government that was taking control over you," he said, "but I was simply repaying an old friend."

It carried. He did not raise his voice to make it carry and that was noted also.

"You may think that because I have taken over you that I want to control your creativity, but that isn't why I came to Stannvaard. I want to make sure the guild seats understand their importance to the younger generations."

Nobody moved. Twelve thousand Dawi standing in a cold crater do not move and do not murmur, and any commander who has fought them will tell you that the stillness is not respect and is not fear and should not be read as either.

Sonzai stepped forward and continued his brother's speech.

"You are the mecca of creativity. The Dawi are the most important race in the Inner World. You'll be the reason we push forward into a technological revolution. However, do not think we will allow you to create weapons of mass destruction. We have shown you that we won't let one person's agenda control the trade through the Inner World. So I've decided, as The Hand to the Empire of Whitemere, to take Stannvaard under my control, with a representative of dwarven men and women."

And the Dawi came out.

They came up the stair from the Council floor in a line of six, the way a guild delegation walks to an arbitration, and Borin Ironheart was the third of them and had known for nine days and had not slept for four of them. Twelve guild masters watched six of their own cross a platform to stand under another people's banner, and the Council Chair's face did what a Dawi face does when the thing being done is legal and is also theft.

What the Ore Council understood, and what took the empire another two years to understand, was that six representatives on an imperial body is not the same object as twelve guild masters on the Council, and that the extraction schedule had not been mentioned once.

The extraction schedule is the kingdom's sovereign instrument. Everything else is administration. Nobody on that platform said the words *extraction schedule* all morning, and the Ore Council went back down to the Council floor at the ninth hour and set the next quarter's quotas at the number they had already agreed on in the fourth month, and the empire's first act of governance in Stannvaard was to receive a schedule it had not written and file it.

Vesh Ondal of Shaft-Eleven watched the whole thing from the gallery rail with her arms folded on it and her chin on her arms.

She was a Tinker Goblin, four feet and a thumb of her, grey-green as wet slate with the copper cast across the cheekbones that comes up in her people after enough years near hot metal, and she had a scalp shaved to stubble on the left side where a burn had taken the follicles in her nineteenth year and hair to the shoulder on the right, dull black, tied through with a length of gauge-wire because she had never in her life carried a ribbon. Her hands were the tell. Every knuckle thickened, the right index shorter than the left by most of a joint, three fingers permanently curled toward a tool that was not there, and both palms the specific slick shine of skin that has been burned and regrown often enough to stop having a print.

She wore a Transport Guild under-coat with the Transport Guild badge cut off it, which is a thing you are allowed to do and which everyone reads correctly.

The Tinker Goblin quarter of Thane-Gorr had been petitioning the Ore Council for a guild seat for two hundred and six years. They maintained every shaft-lift, every chain-bridge winch, every pump-array gasket and every ventilation fan in the crater. They were not a chartered guild, so they were not represented, so their petitions went to the Council as correspondence and never as motions, and correspondence is read at the end of a session by a clerk, if there is time.

*Six seats. Six seats to a people who stopped the fires for a speech, and not one of them handed down the stair to the level where the fans are.*

She went back to work before the platform had cleared. There was a bearing on the number four lift that had been singing for a week.

The empire of Whitemere was born that morning and was named eleven days earlier in a room at Kharven-Seat by four people who could not agree, and the argument is entered.

Sodoku Moto did not want a name. His position, stated twice and then not again, was that the instrument was already signed and that a name would make it a thing that could be praised, and a thing that can be praised can be sold. Edward Lambert's position was that an unnamed sovereign cannot be filed, and that a state which cannot be filed cannot issue a bill, garrison a road, prosecute a theft, or be sued, and that the four hundred and eleven administrative functions of a government all rest on a header.

They took it from the water.

There is a mere on the north side of Kharven-Seat, under the shoulder of the ridge, that the Kharven had called *the white* for longer than anyone had written anything down. It ices at the first hard frost and stays iced until the grass comes, and the ice goes white and opaque by the second week and you cannot see anything through it at all. Underneath, it does not freeze through. Something keeps the water moving down there, and nobody in four quarters has ever explained what, and the Kharven have never been interested in the question. What they use it for is a saying. A man who has gone quiet and is not finished is *white-mere*. A grudge held past the point where anybody remembers the offence is *white-mere*. You cannot see it, it is not dead, and it will be there when the ice goes.

Emira suggested it. She was twenty-four and had been in the room to fetch her brother and had stayed, and she said the word once and did not argue for it, and Lambert wrote it down in Common with the two halves closed up into one because the Concord closes up everything it files.

Whitemere.

Sodoku Moto looked at the word on the tablet for some time and then said that it would do, and the Kharven have never once used it among themselves, and call the empire what they have always called the thing that comes down from the Seat, which is *the Stack*, after the woodpile, because everything that arrives from the Seat arrives as an addition to a count that somebody has to keep.

Nalūn took six years and was never fought.

The problem was stated in the eleventh week by Edward Lambert to a room of four, over a map of the northern shield with the basin inked in a colour the cartographer had chosen for salt and which was a poor choice, and he stated it in the tone of a man reading a valuation.

"Nalūn," he said. "You're a tough cookie to crack. You hold the ports of the Inner World so you could cut off the people of the North. However, we could simply buy up the land."

Bram Greymane was standing because sitting had become a thing he did rarely and got out of badly. He had walked in from the Stormfold road in the fifth month with the hip and the hand and had gone straight through the hall without stopping to be greeted, which is the Greymane manner and which people had begun to find restful.

He looked at Edward.

"That would take a majority of our reserves. Sonzai asked us to come up with an idea that doesn't require buying our enemies."

Edward Lambert rubbed his cheek.

He was fifty-three that year and had begun to do that, the two fingers at the cheekbone, in the six weeks he had spent reading three thousand and eleven names aloud, and the gesture had followed him out of that hall and stayed.

"The Holy Sea of Alabaster just agreed to a treaty," he said, "and Sodoku named the Lamberts the representatives of The Holy Sea. However, my nephew is still a child. I have to teach him. So we should end this quickly."

It did not end quickly. It ended in the sixth year, and the reason it took six is that every serious military assessment of the chain-cities for two hundred years has arrived at the same paragraph, and Lambert had read nine of them.

Take the tunnels and you hold rock. The value is in the seals, and the seals answer to the Wellsprings, and the Wellsprings do not recognise occupation.

A Parun-bound instrument enforces itself. That is the whole of Nalūn and it is the reason a bill drawn at Ironlink Hold is honoured in the Ring smelters and in the western ports of the Old World by people who have no opinion at all about the Kingdom of Nalūn and no ability to compel anyone. An unarmed man walks three jurisdictions with a piece of salt-marked paper and is paid, because the paper does not need a court. Metal does not have to travel if claims on metal can.

An army cannot touch that. Neither can a purchase. If Whitemere had spent its entire reserve buying the basin floor it would have owned two hundred fathoms of halite and a drainage system requiring three centuries of institutional knowledge, and would have flooded the lower workings inside a season, and the fourteen factor houses would have moved their sealing to Deepvein, which is eight hundred feet under the flood, and gone on issuing.

So Lambert did not attack the seals. He attacked the distance.

Every one of Nalūn's instruments is a solution to one problem, which is that a man in Rimward cannot know what a man in the Old World did four months ago. The chain-cities had solved it by making the promise enforce itself, and had charged a premium on the solution for two centuries, and the premium was invisible because there was nothing to compare it to.

In the first year Whitemere surveyed a rail line from the Ring to the coast.

It did not follow the valleys. That is the thing that every account of the Inner World's first line notices and that the Old World engineers who came north to laugh at it could not get past. A road follows the flattest ground. The line followed the shallowest current, which put it over two ridges that a competent surveyor would have gone around and through four villages that had no reason to exist and past three cities that did, and the cities complained, and the complaint is in the file, and the answer in the margin is in a Measurewright's hand and is four words: *the draw is there*.

An engine is a conduit retuned to a current, and a current runs where it runs. So the line is crooked, and it is crooked for a reason nobody on board can see, and the first thing a passenger out of the Old World says on the Inner World line is that it is crooked.

There are dead miles. Eleven of them on the first line, in three stretches, where the current runs thin under the roadbed and the engine takes what it can hold at the last housing and coasts through on it. The hum drops away as you come into a dead mile. It goes from the low continuous note that a district makes when it is drinking, the note you stop hearing by the second day, to nothing at all, and the carriage goes silent enough that you can hear the rail joints, and the conductors learned in the first month to walk the aisle and tell people to sit down before it happens, because a hum that stops is a thing everyone in the Inner World has been taught to be frightened of.

A district's draw being cut is a political act. Eleven miles of track being quiet is not. It took three years for the second sentence to be as widely believed as the first, and in the interval two hundred people jumped from moving carriages.

The wire came the year after and the wire is what broke Nalūn.

It goes on poles beside the line, and it is not a pipe and does not carry anything, and the men who put it up called it a wire because it is a wire. What it does is hold two ends in the same condition. A keyer at Ashwell puts a hand on it and a keyer at Rimward, who has been sitting with a hand on the other end for four hours, knows it. Beyond that nothing is published, and the Guild of Measurewrights has never explained it, and by the third year the question had stopped being asked because a thing that works stops being interesting to everyone except the people it puts out of work.

What the wire did to the chain-cities was not dramatic and there is no day on which it happened.

A factor at Bridgemarch quoted a bill against an Old World cargo at a rate that included four months of not knowing. In the fourth year the same cargo could be confirmed by wire at the port, in the afternoon, for the price of a keyer's time, and the merchant did not need the bill to enforce itself because he could simply look. The seals still held. They hold today. Nothing in the basin failed and no Wellspring wavered and no instrument went inert that had not been breached. The premium collapsed, because a promise you can verify is worth less than a promise you cannot, and the chain-cities had been selling the difference since before there was a Concord.

Adelin Roke, Chainwarden of the Eastern Reach, worked the arithmetic in the second month of the fourth year and sent it to the Council of Links in nine lines with no recommendation attached, which is the most aggressive thing a Chainwarden can do.

The Council of Links sat on it for a year and a half.

They sat on it because the fourteen factor houses were still profitable, and because three of the fourteen had already bought into the Ashwell line through intermediaries and did not wish to be asked about it, and because the High Chainlord, Sarran Vell, had come out of the Trial of Ten Deals thirty years earlier as the man who could resolve any commercial problem without creating a new liability, and a man trained never to create a liability will not act first.

In the sixth year the Old World did the rest, and that part was Lambert's and he has never put his name on it.

The western houses that carried Nalūn paper stopped carrying it, over four quarters, by no decree, with no instrument anywhere that a Bench could point at. Four of the largest wrote that they were reviewing their northern exposure, which is a sentence that means nothing and cost the basin eleven percent of its sealing volume in a quarter. Then two Old World ports began requiring wire confirmation on top of a Nalūn seal, which is a small, reasonable, prudent administrative requirement, and which says out loud that the seal is no longer sufficient, and once a thing has been said out loud it cannot be taken back into the place where it was not said.

Nobody ordered it. Sixteen people were persuaded of it over four years by a man who never travelled to any of them, and every one of the sixteen believed he had reasoned his way to it alone, and one of them still says so in print.

Sarran Vell came north in the sixth year with eleven of the fourteen factor houses behind him and sealed at Kharven-Seat, and the instrument he sealed preserved the Council of Links, the Trial of Ten Deals, the fourteen houses, Nalūn's internal law, its judicial autonomy over sealed disputes, and its title to every foot of the basin. It conceded one thing. The Iron-to-Salt corridor, its way-stations, its licensing, and the ground the rail ran on.

He was seventy-one and had been the second-best negotiator of his generation for fifty years, and he had done the correct thing, and the correct thing had been available to him for two years.

*The instrument is sound. Every term is sound. In nine years a boy in Rimward will ask his father what the salt was for, and his father will not be able to answer him in a way that makes the boy understand, and this room is the reason.*

Torven Greis lost eleven way-stations in a morning and was paid for every one of them.

The Greis Trading House had held the Iron-to-Salt corridor for two generations on the strength of knowing which fords go out in the melt and which ward-posts have a tender who drinks, and the empire bought the stations at a valuation Lambert set himself, above market, in a single instrument, and turned nine of them into rail halts and two into nothing at all. Greis walked away richer than his father had died and with no trade left in his hands, and he took the money and went into the thing the wire had made instead of the thing it had killed, and the Greis name is on forty percent of the northern line's freight contracts today, and he has never once described the transaction as a purchase.

He calls it the morning he was paid to stop existing.

Bram Greymane stopped the line at the Ashenmoor in the fourth year and did it in a way nobody could appeal.

The survey wanted to run the southern spur across eleven miles of flat empty country between the wall and the moor, and the eleven miles were flat and empty because an orc host of eleven thousand had starved to death on them in a winter six years earlier, and there is no stone on that ground and no register entry for most of them and no burial of any kind, because the people who came back in the spring had no intention of digging for them.

The Engineering Office's position was that the ground was clear, which it was.

Bram Greymane rode down with four men and a chain and had the chain laid along the survey line from the wall to the moor and left it there, which is not a legal act and has no standing in any body of law in the four quarters, and then he sat down on a rock at the southern end of it with the bad hip out straight in front of him and told the survey party that the line would go round.

They asked him under whose authority.

"Mine," he said, and that was the whole of the exchange, and it is entered.

It cost four months and a hundred and sixty thousand and the spur goes round the eastern edge of the moor on a curve that adds nine miles, and every driver on the southern line knows why, and the curve is called the Long Way and is marked on no map by that name.

*They will ask again in twenty years when I am not here. And they will be right, and the ground will still be flat, and there will be nobody left who remembers that it was flat because of what we did to them and not because of what the ice did.*

He never said any of that to the survey party. He said one word and sat on a rock for an afternoon in the rain.

Robin Ice was twenty-three when the wire reached Ashwell and understood inside a week that his trade had been killed.

He had ridden a hundred and eleven miles in eleven hours more than once. He had come up the path in the upper burying ground with blood on his left ear that was not his and told Edward Lambert that the Goblin Senate was hanged, and had been given an order on that path, and had said *aye*, and had ridden, and had done it. Four hundred and six, of whom eleven were children under twelve. He does not discuss it. Two people have asked him privately whether he questioned it and he has told both of them that he did not, and both of them believe him, because a runner who begins weighing the traffic stops being one.

The wire did in a quarter of an hour what he did in eleven hours, and did it while he was asleep, and did it for the price of a keyer's time.

He went to Lambert in the second year and asked for a place on the line.

"No," Lambert said.

He was at a desk with four files on it and he did not look up until he had finished the sentence he was reading, and then he did look up, and he put his pen down, which he did not always do.

"The wire is a wire. Anyone with a hand on it between here and the coast has what is on it, and we will catch most of them, and most is not all." He let that sit for a moment. "There are things this government will need to say that cannot be on it. There will be more of those every year, not fewer, because the more a state can send openly the more particular it becomes about the rest."

Robin Ice stood there.

"You will carry those."

And that is the office Robin Ice has held for four years, and it has no name in any establishment list, and he is paid out of the Seat's household account as a courier, and he is the only courier in the northern empire who is never given a written thing to carry.

*He has made me the part of it that does not get written down. And he did it on purpose, and he did it because he knows what I am, and the thing he knows about me is the thing I found out on that path, and neither of us has ever said it.*

The technological revolution arrived in the Inner World in six years and the histories give it to the rail and the wire, and both of those are downstream of a decision the empire made in its first year about who is allowed to sink a well.

Nobody built an engine. The whole of it is hydrology. A site is where the current pools, and a seat is built on a site, and the street plan of every old city in the north is dictated by where the draw runs shallowest, which is why the rich in Kharven-Seat live on the low ground under the ridge and the poor live up the eastern slope where output is thin and the lamps gutter through the Thin Weeks. That geography is four hundred years old and nobody arranged it.

What Whitemere did was licence new sites, and licence them fast, and licence them to chartered companies instead of to seats.

The Ore Council had been sinking at the pace of a body that intends to be there in three hundred years. The empire sank eleven new sites in four years, and the fourth of them, at Vault Hollow, came in at a density that put a thousand-house district on metered housings within eighteen months, and the Measurewrights' survey of that site has a line in it that the Engineering Office has never published and that four people have read.

The apparatus does what the current does, and inherits every way the current fails.

A Fulguria lamp dies in fog. Every lamp in Ashwell's lower quarter goes out together on a bad river morning and the quarter has built its whole social calendar around that fact, and there is a man who sells tallow on fog mornings and on no other morning and is wealthy. A Materia lift will not work over open water and will not work on a moving platform, which is why every crane on the Brine wharf is still a hand-crane and why the dock companies hate the age they are living in. A Fluxia pump is worthless in arid country and the whole southern reclamation is built on that sentence and has failed twice because two engineers in two different decades did not believe it.

Nobody explains any of this. The Guild of Measurewrights wrote the addresses down and maintains the housings and issues the certificates, and the standing instruction to a Measurewright asked why a thing failed is to state the condition and stop. They know where. They have never claimed to know why, and the Hexagonal Oath has a clause about it, and the clause is the reason the order has lasted.

The cost of the age is in the bodies of the people who tend it, and the empire legislated for that six years late.

Stannvaard had the Tender's Obligation before Whitemere existed. A guild that runs forge-arrays supports for life any tender who develops the tender's shake, the fine persistent palsy that arrives at about eight years of array service and does not leave. The Dawi did not do this out of tenderness. They did it because the alternative was a shortage of tenders.

The wire produced its own and nobody had a word for it for three years.

A keyer sits with a hand on the end of a coupling for a shift. In about the fourth year of service the ticking stops being only in the hand. It is in the ear afterward, at night, in a quiet room, at the rate of the line he works, and it does not stop when he leaves the office and does not stop when he leaves the trade. The keyers call it the tick. The Physicians' College has a longer name and no treatment. The first generation of keyers in Whitemere were recruited at fifteen and sixteen because the empire wanted the line open and young hands learn a key in a third of the time, and the second generation was recruited on a four-year rotation with a pension attached, and the reason the second generation got a pension is that the first generation was unfit to key by the time the rotation was drafted.

Vesh Ondal of Shaft-Eleven got a seat on the Ore Council in the fifth year and it ruined her.

The empire granted it. That is the whole of the problem and she saw it in the first hour. Two hundred and six years of petitions, forty of them in her own hand, every one of them read out by a clerk at the end of a session if there was time, and the thing arrived in an afternoon as a decree from a platform, over the objection of the twelve, signed by a man who had never been below the fourth level.

She took it. She was not going to refuse two hundred and six years of her own people's paper because the hand that finally moved was the wrong hand.

The Council seated her and was correct with her and voted her down eleven times in her first year, and the Tinker Goblin quarter, which had wanted this for six generations, discovered within a season that a seat granted by an empire is a seat the other eleven can discount at no cost to themselves, and that a thing you are given is not the same object as a thing you take.

She has the number four lift bearing in a drawer in her office. It sang for a week and then it went, and eleven people went down with the platform, and that was the eighth month of the fifth year, and the requisition she had filed for it is dated fourteen months earlier and had been read out at the end of a session by a clerk.

*I have the seat now. I have had it for a year. If I had had it then I would have filed the same requisition and eleven people would still be dead, because the Council would have voted it down eleven to one instead of not voting on it at all, and I do not know how to say that to anyone in my quarter and I am not going to try.*

The press came last and Lambert authorised it and everyone who has looked at the sequence closely has concluded that he knew exactly what he was doing and did it anyway, and the four people who have said so to his face have received the same answer, which is no answer.

A wire that puts the same sentence in nine towns on the same morning is a government's instrument. A press that prints it is a government's instrument. The two together are the reason the Inner World has a public, and the Inner World's public existed for eleven months before it did anything the Seat disliked.

In the third month of the sixth year, the Ashwell Register printed a question.

It ran in the fourth column of a Thursday sheet under no headline at all, four lines, unsigned, with a note that it had been put to the council of the seventh month six years earlier and had not been answered then. Then it gave the question in the words the woman had used.

*Why did you buy the grain in the first week?*

Tabitha Hallenfeld did not give it to them. She has said so, and the editor has said so, and the editor is the only man in the Inner World who can prove it and has declined on the grounds that proving it would require naming who did.

The thing about the question is that it has a sum in it, and the sum is what carries.

The advance came in the fourth month. On the day Edward Lambert placed his first order with a factor at Vaultmere, no orc had crossed the wall and no council had been called and no threat assessment existed in any file in the Seat, and he spent eleven thousand in Kharven's coin on a contingency that had not occurred, through four factors who did not know about each other, and told nobody what it was for, and then the thing he had bought grain against happened, and the grain was in the right place.

Sixteen thousand people in the southern reach are alive because of it.

Three thousand and eleven names were read aloud by the man who bought it, one at a time, over six weeks, with a clerk on either side.

Both of those are true and the second one is the reason the question is unbearable, because a man who knew would have said so and been thanked, and a man who guessed would have said that too, and the only version of Edward Lambert that fits the file is a version that has never been named out loud in any hall in the north.

He has never answered it. He put his hands behind his back in the seventh month six years ago and waited for the council to move on, and councils do, and six years later a press he licensed put it in front of forty thousand people on a Thursday, and he read the sheet at breakfast, and he folded it in quarters the way he folds everything, and he asked for the returns.

Tabitha Hallenfeld is fifty-one now and sits the Bench and has asked it twice in private and been refused twice, and has begun, in the last two years, to think about a third possibility that she has written down once and burned.

Rikudoku was entered on the Seat's roll in the second week of the Thin Weeks in the fourth year, in the corridor off the altar-room with the night-stone in the room behind ticking as it cooled, and there was a woodpile outside the window that had been counted on the ninth of every month since the Seat had stood and was counted that morning with the rest.

He was ten that winter. He had been born in the Scourge years, when his father was twenty-two and his parents had not yet named what they were to each other. In the north a child is entered on the roll where he is born, and he had not been born anywhere that kept one.

The Reckoner who had come up to enter him was a man of sixty out of the Seat's own registry who had entered every Kharven birth on the ridge for thirty-one years, and he stood with the roll open and the pen in his hand and the plain problem in front of him, and the problem was that the form has one slot for a bloodline.

He could enter the boy as Moto of the Kōkan Line and the entry would be true and would be the entry a Reckoner in any of four quarters would make, and the second half of the boy would be nowhere in any book, and in ninety years a clerk reading the roll would find a Moto heir and no fox at all.

"There is one slot, my lord."

"Then the form is wrong." He did not raise his voice and he did not look up from the boy. "Enter both. Put your initial beside it so the responsibility is legible, and send the form to Lambert with a note that it is insufficient, and he will have a new one drawn inside a month, and every Reckoner in the north will be using it inside a year."

That is exactly what happened, and the current birth-form used across the northern empire has two bloodline fields and a third line beneath them for a note, and the third line is there because a Reckoner at Kharven-Seat asked a question in a corridor in the fourth year, and there are perhaps nine people alive who know why the form carries it.

Yoko Mishiro's own record for that week is four lines and she has never shown it to him.

*His ears came up at the word slot and I watched the room. Eleven people. Four of them were glad, five of them did not know what their faces were doing, and two of them looked at me instead of at the boy, which is the one I will remember.*

*He did not look at the room at all. He was looking at the ears, and he was smiling, and I have seen that man smile four times in ten years and I could have told you the other three from memory before tonight.*

*My son is the heir of the oldest bloodline in the world and half of him is the thing that world puts in a pen. Both of those went into the roll on the same night. They are going to stay in the same room for the whole of his life and neither one is going to leave.*

*I am not afraid of what they will do to him. I am afraid of the year he works out that he can use it.*

The unification of the lines took the whole six years and was the only thing in them that Sodoku Moto argued for in person.

He sent Lambert to Nalūn. He sent Bram to the wall and Robin to the roads and six representatives to Stannvaard and surveyors to eleven new sites. He went himself to the Moto, every time, in the coat and not the armour, and sat in seven halls across six years and made the same argument to six lines who had each been declining it for longer than the Concord has existed.

The argument is four sentences and he has never varied it.

There are seven lines and one blood. The Works are not seven properties, they are one Working that seven households have been holding pieces of since Bara. A house that has spent four thousand years refusing to submit its Work to a single sovereign judgment has spent four thousand years arranging for its own children to be killed by its cousins. We are all Moto, and there is no such thing as lines, and the proof is that the last war was fought between people with the same hair.

The Byakuya came in first and came in cheap, and the Kurenai came in second and were bought, and the Akagane took four years and came in because the Akagane hold the spirit-registers and the registers had begun to be kept on the wire and the wire was the empire's.

The Amagiri came in because of a road.

They are route-readers and pursuit-breakers, and their whole political instinct for two thousand years has been to be somewhere else by the time the question is asked, and the rail took that from them in four years without ever intending to. An Amagiri hall's value to the Inner World had always been that it knew which way a thing had gone. In the fifth year a freight clerk at Ashwell with a wire and a manifest knew which way everything had gone, all of it, on a Tuesday, for the price of a keyer's time, and the Amagiri came to Kharven-Seat and sealed and asked for the Archive portfolio, and got it, and hold it now, and are the most dangerous household in the empire for reasons that will take another twenty years to become anybody's problem.

The Tenrai came in on the first day and had come in six years before that, in eleven men's blood at the Brine crossing, when a nine-year-old said *I will forfeit* in a small flat voice with his hands at his sides.

Miku Tenrai Moto is twenty now.

He is not tall. He came up narrow through the shoulder and stayed there, and the Tenrai gold is in his hair, the storm-grey end of it, worn short because he has never once in his life enjoyed being looked at. He has his father's jaw, which is a thing three people in the empire are impolite enough to mention, and he holds his hands behind his back in the exact manner of Edward Lambert, which he picked up at fifteen without noticing and has been unable to put down.

He sits the table. He has sat it since he was fourteen. He is asked, eleven times a year by somebody new, what he saw at the Brine crossing, and the answer is always four words.

*I saw him mean it.*

What he has never told anybody is that the temporal has not given him anything since the crossing. Eleven years of it, from the age of five, watching every man who fed him die at the age he would be when it happened, and then a wet afternoon in the mud with a hand on his head, and then a closed door. It did not thin and it did not weaken. It stopped, whole, in one afternoon, and has given him nothing in eleven years, in a line whose entire function is convergence and whose seat at the imperial table exists because of what he is.

He runs the War Court's forward planning and he does it on maps and reports and counting, as any other officer does, and he is good at it, and every officer under him believes he is being given the road ahead, and he has never corrected one of them.

*The day they find out, the Tenrai have a boy at the table and nothing in his head, and the six lines will not have unified around a convergence-reader. They will have unified around a story. I do not know which of those the empire is actually standing on and I have stopped being able to tell whether that matters.*

The Shirogane refused, and are refusing, and the refusal is the best-argued document produced in the Inner World in a generation.

Sayo Shirogane was forty-four in the sixth year and had held the silver line for eleven of them. She is small and square-built and carries none of the Moto bearing, which is deliberate and which took work, and her hair is the silver-white with the faint blue that the Shirogane throw more often than the other six, and she wears it in a single flat coil at the nape with a steel pin through it that is not ornamental and has been used once.

She has the Meigan. Her line's Work is knowledge sharpened by Ruins, which in practice means the Shirogane have spent four thousand years being the household that can tell you what a thing is actually made of and what it will do when it comes apart, and they have never once in their history held a throne and have never wanted one, and every other line has learned across four millennia that the Shirogane are not to be lied to in writing.

She came to Kharven-Seat twice. She sat at the table both times, ate what was put in front of her, asked about the Vault Hollow density figures, was correct with everyone, and left without sealing.

In the fourth month of the sixth year she published.

It runs to thirty-one pages and it is titled *On the Question of a Single Judgment*, and the north calls it the Silver Objection, and it was set and printed on a press licensed under the empire's own charter by a printer in Altherion who lost three contracts for it and has never regretted a thing in his life.

It makes four arguments and every one of them is the strong version.

The first is the constitutional one and it is the one the lawyers have spent six years on. The Kōkan hold the only Work that contains the other six. To ask the six to submit is to ask them to agree that a Kōkan judgment is the final reading of a Shirogane finding, and there is then no reading left anywhere in the world that can correct the crown, and a state with no instrument that can correct it has not been unified, it has been closed.

The second is the count. She lists the Tenrai settlement. She lists it in full, without adjectives, with the count, and states that the man asking the six lines to place their Works under one sovereign judgment is the man who conducted the most complete act of judgment in the bloodline's recorded history against his own family, and that he was correct to, by every reading available at the time, and that this is the argument against him rather than the argument for him, because a correct judgment of that magnitude is precisely the thing that a household which has already made one is most likely to make again.

The third is the succession and it is the ugly one, and she writes it in the coldest register in thirty-one pages, and she is careful in a way that has made it impossible to answer.

She does not say that the child is unfit. She says that the empire has seated as heir to the oldest bloodline in the world a child whose second inheritance the empire's own law still classifies under a schedule written for property, that the schedule has not been repealed, that the crown has had six years and has repealed a great deal else, and that until it is repealed the heir of Whitemere is, by the empire's own instruments, simultaneously the sovereign's son and an item in a register. She invites the crown to fix the schedule. She observes that fixing it would cost the crown Sātūlagi and four factor houses and half the Old World treaties.

She is right about the schedule. It is still there in the sixth year. It is in the fourth title, eleventh article.

The fourth argument is one line and it is at the end of page thirty-one with nothing after it.

*The instrument was not built for the north. It was built for a girl, and every clause of it is her, and a body of law that is a grief with the grief taken out of it cannot be amended, because there is nothing in it anybody can get a grip on.*

She had never read Yoko Mishiro's record. Nobody has. Four people in the Inner World knew that sentence was true, and one of them wrote it in her own hand on the night of the signing and has never shown it to a living soul, and a woman in a hall four hundred miles away with the Eye of Revelation and thirty-one pages of public documents arrived at it from the outside in six years.

The Objection reached Kharven-Seat on a Tuesday in the fourth month by rail to Ashwell and by the last forty miles on a cart, because the Seat is on the wrong side of the ridge and the line does not come up.

Sodoku Moto read it through in the map room with the door open.

He read it twice. The second reading took longer than the first, which is the thing the two clerks in the room both noticed and both entered afterward in their own accounts without knowing the other had. He did not put a mark on it. He did not send for Lambert, who was at Vaeloris, and did not send for Miku, who was in the next room, and did not send for his wife.

At the end of the second reading he squared the pages, put them face down on the map of the northern shield, and set the inkstand on the corner so the draught from the door would not take them.

Then he went out to the woodpile.

It was the Thin Weeks. The light was already going at the fourth hour and the wind was coming down off the ridge the way it does, over the mere and up the eastern slope and into the yard, and the mere had been white for six weeks and would be white for eleven more, and under it the water was doing whatever it does under there.

The stack was low.

It had been counted on the ninth as it is counted every month, and the count was in a book, and the count was four ricks short of where the household should have been in the second week of the Thin Weeks, because two of the outer holdings had sent their cut down to the eastern slope instead where the lamps gutter and where the people are.

The block was frozen into the ground and he had to break it out with the heel of his boot before he could stand a round on it.

He took the coat off and hung it on the fence. He got the maul and worked the head into the grain the way you do when the wood is frozen, three short taps to set it before the swing, and the first round went in one and came apart clean along a fault it had been carrying since the tree, and both halves went off the block into the snow.

He picked them up and stood them and split them again.

The household knew better than to come out. Emira watched from the kitchen door for a while with her arms folded. Yoko came down at the sixth hour with the boy beside her in four layers of wool with only the ears out, and stood on the step, and did not say anything, and the ears turned toward the sound of the maul a half-second before the boy's face did, every time, for the whole of it.

He did not stop until it was properly dark.

There were nine ricks on the ground when he finished and the stack was still short, and he put the maul back in the shed with the head down, which is how it is kept, and stood in the yard a moment, and looked at the white flat of the mere with nothing showing on it at all.

Then he went in and asked the household what had come in on the wire.

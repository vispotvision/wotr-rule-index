# The Seam — Aetherion Academy

*Aetherion Academy. The broken cold room under the south range. Continuous.*

Malphas went down badly.

The yoke of light between his ankles caught his heel as the kick drove him back, and he sat down hard on one hip in the yellow layer with his wrists drawn together in front of him and his chin hauled up by the collar at his throat, so that he stared straight up at the hole in the vault. The bindings sang. Mu-jin could hear them under the storm, a thin bright note like a wet finger run round the rim of a glass, and with the lens off he could see them for what they were. Gas torn open until it could no longer hold its own charges, the nuclei left bare and the electrons running loose along every cuff in a hot blue-white skin, the whole of it held in the shape of a shackle by a will that was not his and that he did not try to read.

The wound in the man's back smoked where the blade had gone in. It had entered between the ribs a hand to the left of the spine, high, below the shoulder blade, and come out of the angle between lung and backbone without touching either, and a blade that hot did not leave a man anything to bleed from. The edges were cooked shut. The smell crossed the room to Mu-jin through the clean air he had made: burnt wool from the coat, and under it the smell of pork left too long on a griddle, sweet and black.

For a heartbeat Malphas did nothing at all. He sat in the gas with his hands yoked and breathed through his nose, and the girl and the carter stood where he had left them with the cold threads running from their breastbones to the lattice on his left hand, and did not move to help him, because nothing in them had ever been made to want to.

Then the skin under the cuffs began to darken.

It began at the wrists. Where the blue-white burned against him, the flesh went the grey of a bruise four days old, and then the brown of a pear left in a bowl, and then the black of the ground at the far end of the vault, and the black spread up into the light instead of back from it. Mu-jin watched the colour of the cuff change. Blue-white went to violet. Violet went to the dull red of iron left in a forge after the bellows stop. The note it sang dropped by a tone, and then by another, the way the Star's hum had dropped when it swallowed Raga's bolt.

He knew what he was looking at a moment before he wanted to.

He had found the hole in Malphas with an oxidiser. The aqua fortis had wanted electrons, and it had torn them out of the rot and left the rot burning, because a ferment cannot digest the fire that eats what it makes. He had said it to himself in exactly those words. And what had just been wrapped round the man's wrists and ankles and throat was the other half of the same sentence. A plasma was nothing but loose electrons and the cores that had lost them. It was the richest gift of charge a working could hand a body. Ferment did not have to fight it. Ferment had only to open its mouth.

*She has fed him,* Mu-jin thought, and the thought came late, the Arrears dragging it behind him like a cart with a broken wheel. *Every cuff of it. She has given him supper.*

The rot drank. He could see the electrons going down into the black flesh the way rain goes into dry soil, and he could see what they did once they were there. They went into the fermenting tissue and pushed every reaction in it uphill, the reducing side of the ledger, the side that builds. The rot in that man had always been a cellar full of fuel. Now someone had poured a cartload of new fuel in at the door, and the rot was spending it on the only thing a ferment ever spends anything on. It made more of itself. It made it in the shape of the man who carried it.

The collar went first, being thinnest. It dimmed to an ember round his throat, then to a ring of ash-coloured light, and then it flaked into pale scales that drifted down into the yellow layer and went out like sparks falling into snow. Malphas's chin came down. He swallowed, and the swallow was audible across the room, a dry click in a dry throat.

The wrist cuffs went next, both at once, with a sound like a lamp wick pinched between wet fingers. The ankle yoke lasted three breaths longer and then went the way of the others.

Malphas turned his hands over and looked at them. The black had climbed past both wrists into the forearms and stopped there, a hard edge, the way the rust on the cot frame had stopped when the sulphur reached Mu-jin's fifteen metres. As Mu-jin watched, it began to recede. It went back down into the skin and the skin above it came up pink, and then pale, and then simply a man's skin, a living man's colour in a cold room.

And the wound in his back closed.

Mu-jin saw it because the coat had burned away round it. A film grew across the cooked edges, pale grey and faintly translucent, rubbery, the thickness of a fingernail, the exact stuff that forms on the top of a vinegar cask left alone in a cellar and that every brewer in the Accord calls by the same name. The mother. It spread from the rim of the wound inward in a slow wrinkling sheet, and where it met itself in the middle it fused, and under it something moved, a working of flesh being laid down by a thing that had never once in its life been taught what a man was supposed to look like inside and was guessing well.

The Mother, Mu-jin thought. The cult. The cells that are cultures. He had read the Register's sheet on it and thought the name was a piece of theatre.

Malphas stood up.

He did it slowly and without using his hands. He came up out of the gas onto his feet with one shoulder a little lower than the other, where the blade had passed, and he rolled that shoulder once and the lowness went out of it.

"Plasma," he said.

His voice had been burned at the edges by the collar and it came out rough, a rasp under the old mildness. He did not look at Mu-jin when he said it. He looked across the room, at the woman whose light had done it, and he spoke to her with the courtesy of a reader returning a borrowed book.

"Gas pulled apart until it cannot keep its own electrons. You wrapped me in them." He flexed his fingers, and the knuckles cracked one by one down the hand. "Every binding you make has something to give. You gave all of it, generously, at the wrists and the ankles and the throat. I have been fed worse suppers by people who wished me well."

Beside him Zeraphine had not moved her feet at all.

She stood where she had stood since she came into the cold room, one boot planted in the yellow layer and the other on a dry flag, the dark plum of her overrobe hanging straight in air that could not decide which way to move. Her face had changed since Mu-jin first saw it. The white paint on her cheek and brow had cracked in the heat the other woman had raised, a web of fine lines like old glaze on a cup, and under the cracks her own skin showed pink and sore. She did not seem to have noticed. The glass sphere turned at her raised hand, pale blue, the slow swirl in it moving no faster than it ever had, and the ribbon of violet-white light that trailed from it lay coiled round her arm like a cat that has decided to stay.

She had heard the question put to her. She took her time with it.

"Who am I to decide how the battle is won." Zeraphine said it back flatly, word for word. Her voice was low, and it did not carry, and still it arrived in every corner of the vault at once, as though four other mouths in four other rooms had said it alongside her. "Nobody. I agree with you. I never decide how it is won." She tilted her head a fraction, and the green scales of her mask-helm caught the light. "I decide what it costs, and who is left to pay. Different trade. I am very good at it."

She looked at the bindings' ash still drifting down round Malphas's boots.

"You told us to leave," she said. "Clearly. It came through the bones, and four generals in four other rooms felt it come through theirs." She lifted the sphere a finger's breadth higher. "So we are leaving. You may write it down as obedience, if that helps you sleep. A complicated woman should know the cheapest move on any board is the one that takes your piece off it before the other side has finished speaking."

The swirl inside the glass quickened.

Mu-jin read it through the migraine and through the long grey lag of the Arrears, and the read came to him in pieces, a page at a time. Transference. Two bodies ringing at the same pitch, so that energy passes from one to the other with nothing between them to carry it, the way a struck tuning fork will set its twin humming across a room. The sphere at her hand was one of a pair. He was certain of that before he could have said how. Somewhere a second glass was turning in the same slow swirl, and the dead suspended in it were ringing in time with the dead suspended in this one, and when two vessels rang that closely together the distance between them was a thing a strong enough will could choose to ignore.

She chose.

The sphere did not grow. The dark inside it came out of it, the way ink comes out of a broken pen, and spread on the air in front of her in a flat disc the height of a man. Its rim was only the place where the cold-room light stopped being able to reach, a soft boundary that shivered when the hail struck near it. The draw-hum in the walls went out of tune. Mu-jin heard it bend, the long low note of the south range main sliding half a tone sour, and somewhere above them in the Academy a lamp on a conduit flickered and a porter swore.

Air came through the disc. It smelled of brine and cold iron and vinegar, the sharp sour of a cask room, and it was colder than the cold room, colder than anything in the Academy, a cellar cold that had never once been warm.

Beyond the dark there was a lamp. A single blubber-yellow flame burned behind horn on a stone sill. Below it a second glass the size of a man's skull turned on a stand. At the stand, waiting with the patience of someone who had been told the hour and come early, stood a figure in a long dark coat with the hood thrown back. The lamp lit a jaw. A pale ear. One hand was already lifted toward the seam, palm up, fingers open, the way a man holds out his hand to steady someone stepping down from a cart.

Nuvilak.

Zeraphine's ribbon of light uncoiled from her arm. It went across the gap to Malphas without hurrying and wrapped once round his left wrist, over the lattice, where the cold threads to the girl and the carter met in his palm.

Then it reached past him.

It went toward the floor. Toward the man lying face-down in the yellow layer with three fingers gone and his own blood dried brown on his cuff, the scar from his left ear to his jaw turned up to the vault where the other woman's heat had dropped him. Velder Crace had not moved since he fell. His back rose, barely, and fell. The skin of his cheek had cracked like the paint on Zeraphine's, dried out in the heat that had been laid on him, and the cracks wept clear.

"No," Malphas said. "Leave him."

The ribbon stopped a foot above Velder's head and hung there.

"He will talk," Zeraphine said, without argument, the way she might have stated the width of a river her column had to cross.

"He knows the name of a train," Malphas said, "and how I take my coffee." He looked down at his student for exactly as long as it took to say it, and his face did nothing. "Let them have both. They will learn a great deal about coffee."

The ribbon drew back.

Malphas turned then, at the lip of the dark, with Nuvilak's open hand a pace behind him and the cellar cold pouring round his legs, and for the first time since the light had come into the room he looked at Mu-jin.

He looked at him the way he had looked at him across the reading floor of the Division, a hundred afternoons, over the tops of open books. Kindly. Steadily. As a man looks at a colleague who has done good work and is about to be wrong about something.

Mu-jin was swaying. He knew it only because the lamp on the far wall was moving and he knew the lamp was fixed.

"Teacher," Malphas said, and there was something almost tender in it. "Get your face up."

He stepped back into the dark.

Nuvilak's hand closed on his collar and drew him through. The ribbon went taut and pulled Zeraphine after him, taken the way the sphere was taken, her boots leaving the flags with the yellow layer clinging to the hem of her skirt in long slow strands, and the last of her to go was the glass, turning, blue, calm, before the dark folded in round it.

The seam shut with a sound like a cork drawn from a bottle, flat and small and wet.

The cold threads to the girl and the carter had followed their master to the edge. They had run from the two still bodies at the foot of the cots straight into the dark, drawn out thin as fishing line, and when the dark closed they were on the wrong side of it. They parted with a high faint ping like a lute string breaking in a far room. The carter fell first, straight forward, stiff, the way a plank falls. The girl folded where she stood and went down in a heap of village smock, her cheek on the wet stone a yard from the brine where the black lightning had grounded. Her eyes stayed open.

Mu-jin watched her ribs. He could not make himself look anywhere else. They moved. Once, shallow. A long time after, once again.

The draw-hum in the walls slid back into tune.

And the bill came in.

The Arrears had been holding it for him all night, the way a patient creditor holds a note until the debtor is on the stair. The blood he had been losing since the first exchange arrived all at once, every drop of it a debt his body had not yet been told about, and his body was told now. The cold came first. It came into his hands, and then his feet, and then crept up his shins as if he had walked out into a river in winter. His coat was heavy on one side and he could not remember why. His mouth filled with water, thin and tasting of copper and old pennies, and he swallowed it and it came back.

The edges of the room went grey and then turned to a fine white snow, the way the edges of a page go when the lamp is turned too low to read by, and the snow came in from all four sides toward the middle where the girl lay breathing.

Sound went tin. Hail on the flags was rice spilled on a tray. The storm over the hole had become someone else's storm, a mile off, over another city, in a year he had never lived.

Raga's slow tick in his chest went on, patient, unbothered, waiting for a name.

There were boots on the stair.

He heard them late, of course. Hobnails on stone, coming down fast and heavy, one man and then a lighter step behind, and a voice ahead of them both swearing steadily in the flat vowels of the south range, the voice of a man who has carried the same ring of keys up and down the same stairs for forty years.

"Hells' teeth. Hells' teeth and their gums. Who's opened the cold room? Who's had the draw off this whole range and told nobody? I've got a lamp out on the Founders' Walk and a dean asking me why and I'm meant to say what?"

Wat Hobday came round the last turn of the stair holding his lantern high. He was a small man gone wide, with a white moustache stained yellow at the ends from his pipe, a porter's brown coat buttoned wrong, and the great ring of the south range keys on his belt pulling his trousers down on one side. He had a Guild key among them, brass, with the seal on its bow, the only key in the building that could shut the main. His other hand was on it already. Whatever was in this room, he was going to cut the draw on it first and ask its name after, because if it went up he was the one who would be standing in front of the bursar in the morning.

Behind him came a woman in a proctor's grey with a sealed draw-lamp in one hand and a brass whistle on a cord round her neck. Annis Coole. Mu-jin knew her from the faculty table: thin, tidy, a career built a rung at a time, never once seen in a hall without her collar pinned. She took in the room in one look, the black lightning scars and the hole in the vault and the two bodies at the cots and the man face-down in the gas, and her face went smooth.

"Nobody touches anything," she said. "The Register has been sent for."

Her thumb was on the whistle she had not blown. Mu-jin saw the thumb and saw the whistle and could not, with everything he had, make the two things mean anything to each other.

He saw the lantern instead.

Hobday was coming down the last three steps with it held out in front of him, a plain porter's lantern, horn windows and a tin chimney and an oil flame burning naked behind the horn. There was no gauze on it. There was no reason there should be. It was a lantern for walking corridors, and the corridors of the Academy had never had anything in them that wanted to burn.

The yellow layer lay at knee height round the foot of the stair. In the old coffin niches along the low walls, Mu-jin had read it himself, it was past four in a hundred and climbing.

He opened his mouth to say *the lamp.*

The floor came up to meet him before the words did. It came slowly, and from the side, and it was cold and wet and smelled of sulphur and brine, and it was much softer than he had expected, and the last thing he saw through the white snow at the edge of everything was the little yellow flame swinging lower behind its horn, down the last step, into the gas.

He heard his own voice arrive a long time after the dark.

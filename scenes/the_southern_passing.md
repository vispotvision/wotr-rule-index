# The Southern Passing

*Vaelmarr. The Grand Principality of Eresse, early. The eleventh year of the Landward Suppression.*
*POV: Ithuran Melloran, Marshal of the Landward Wall.*

---

## I. THE FOURTH BELL

The council room smelled of hot iron and wet wool and, under both of those, the sea.

It came up through the floor. Vaelmarr is built on chalk and the chalk breathes, and every room on the seaward side of the keep carries a cold mineral edge in the mornings that the braziers never quite burn off. Ithuran Melloran had smelled it for two hundred and forty years and had stopped noticing it somewhere around the first century, and had started noticing it again the winter his knees went, because the damp found the joint before anything else in his body knew the weather had turned.

He stood at the long table with his weight on the left leg and both palms flat on the chart, and the chart was the landward approach, and it was four days out of date, and he was going to say so.

The room was too warm. Someone had built the braziers up for the Prince, who did not feel cold, and the six of them who did not have the Prince's constitution were standing about in wool in a room got up for a man who could have sat through the session in his shirt. Velenn Ithalan had her sleeves pushed past the elbow and her forearms shone. Corvienne Sorreth had her Reckoner's collar undone at the throat, which in forty years Ithuran had never once seen her do, and she had not remarked on it, and nobody had remarked on it to her.

Outside, gulls. Not many. Fewer than there should have been at the fourth bell with the fleet in.

*The stacks are empty.*

He had come up the western stair and the three chalk stacks off the point had been bare white, the whole of them, top to waterline, and there are four thousand birds on those stacks in an ordinary season and there had been none. No carcasses on the shingle either. No smell of a die-off. He had stood there with his hand on the rail for a moment and had not been able to make it mean anything, and had come inside and had not mentioned it, because a marshal who reports birds gets asked about birds for a year.

The tide was running against the causeway arches. He could hear it through two feet of stone, the particular hollow knock the water makes when it comes up under the third arch and finds the undercut, and he did not need a glass or a tide-board.

*Two hours early.*

That was the read and it was a fact and he let it be a fact.

---

Ruvaen Aloreth came in last and sat down before anyone could stand.

He did that. It had taken the council a year to stop trying and another year to stop resenting that they had been made to stop, and now it simply happened, and six people who had been raised in a court where the standing is half the meaning sat in their chairs while the first prince of Eresse took his.

Nine hundred years of Sunroot ceremonial and the man walked into it like a farrier coming into a stall.

He was in the half-harness again. Cuirass and pauldrons and nothing below the hip but wool and boots, which is the kit of a man who expects to be on a wall inside the hour and does not expect to be on a horse, and the plate was the pale Vaelmarr steel and the plate was clean. That was the thing Ithuran had never got used to. The Prince's armour was always clean. Whatever he did out there, he came back to the sea gate with the harness stripped and scrubbed on the causeway before he climbed, and he did it himself, and Ithuran had watched him do it once by lamplight with the tide coming in over his boots and had never asked and had never been told.

Hair the flat unlit white of the northern lines, worn long and pushed back off the temples, and a single fighting braid down the left side behind the ear, the way the Landward companies wear it, which is not how a prince wears it. Grey eyes set deep under a heavy brow. A jaw you could have quarried. He was six feet one and built through the chest and the shoulders like a man who had spent a century pulling a war bow, and the forearms below the vambrace were thick and corded and marked all over with the small silver seams that come off a blade edge glancing on mail.

And that was the impossible part, and every jurist in Velthaeir had an opinion about it, and none of the opinions agreed.

An Eressean breaks cleanly. That is the whole of the lineage, written into the bone: light frame, long life, the finest Gnosis in four quarters and a body that snaps where another would bend. Ithuran Melloran was two hundred and eighty-nine years old and had put four fingers of steel through more Under-House infantry than he could count and he weighed a hundred and fifty pounds in his shirt and always had.

Ruvaen Aloreth had the Gnosis. Ruvaen Aloreth also had two hundred and twenty pounds on a frame that should not have carried it, and did not tire, and had once picked a fallen horse off a wounded boy at the Hollow Gate in front of eleven witnesses who were all still alive and all still disagreed about what they had seen.

*Nobody can tell me where it comes from. Not the Court, not the archive, not the physicians, not the man.*

*And I have stopped asking, because the last time I asked he answered me, and the answer was that he did not know either, and he said it the way you would tell a quartermaster the count on a barrel.*

"Marshal." Ruvaen did not look up from the chart. "You have that face."

"The chart is four days old, my lord. Ollavaeth Ridge is on it as held and Ollavaeth Ridge fell on the second night. I have been asking the draughtsmen for a re-cut since the ninth bell yesterday and I have been told the draughtsmen are copying the grain tallies."

"Are they."

"They are."

"Velenn."

Velenn Ithalan did not stop writing. "Mine. Grain tallies go to the Court by the fifteenth or the Court holds the winter charter, and if the Court holds the charter I cannot buy off the Vessel factors, and if I cannot buy off the Vessel factors we eat the garrison stores in the eighth week instead of the fourteenth." She turned the page over. "You may have the draughtsmen the moment the tallies are sealed. Not one hour before."

*He will not overrule her. He never overrules her. She is the only person in this room who talks to him as though the war were an expense and he likes it, and I have never understood why a man like that likes it.*

"Fourteenth week," said Ruvaen.

"Fourteenth."

"Seal them tonight."

"Then I need lamps for eleven clerks and the lamps come out of the same oil as the wall braziers."

"Take the braziers on the south curtain. The south curtain has had no traffic in a month and the men on it are sleeping standing up and they can do that in the dark as well as in the light."

Corvienne Sorreth wrote it down without being asked, which is the office.

---

## II. THE CHILD WITH THE SLATE

The door at the small end opened and Saenna came in sideways with the slate held against her chest in both arms.

She was twelve. She had the Court's page livery, which is cut for adults and taken in by whoever has the needle that week, and hers had been taken in badly at the shoulder so that the left seam stood off her collarbone, and she had a wool underlayer showing at the cuff because the livery was summer weight and it was not summer. Sandy hair, unusual in the Sunroot lines, cut at the jaw and pinned back off the right side with a plain brass pin. Ink to the second knuckle of the right hand. Chalk dust on the left.

Ithuran had opposed the appointment and had said so in this room.

A page at a landward war council is a mouth that carries what it hears, and a twelve year old is a mouth that has not learned yet what it costs to be interesting at a supper table, and he had put all of that to the Prince in plain Common and the Prince had heard him out and had done it anyway. *A house that will not train the next one is a house that has decided how long it intends to last.* That was the whole of the answer. Ithuran had thought it a sentiment and had gone away irritated, and eleven months later he had come to think the Prince had meant it as a schedule.

She stopped two paces short and did the reverence badly, which she always did, and nobody in the room had ever corrected her, which Ithuran had also noticed.

"S-sir." She swallowed and started again. "Sir. There is reports from the Cliff Watch. Towards the southern. The southern water." The slate came up an inch and went back down. "A passing. Of d-dragons."

The room did nothing at all.

*There it is. She has said it and now she is going to stand there while six adults decide what it costs her to have said it, and that is the whole of an apprenticeship and it is a rotten way to spend a morning when you are twelve.*

Ruvaen Aloreth put his hand out and set it on the top of her head and left it there.

"Good work."

Two words. He did not ask her how many. He did not ask her whose glass. He did not ask her the bearing or the hour or the sea state, and Ithuran Melloran, who had been giving and receiving reports since before the Vessel Coast had a charter, sat with his hands on a four day old chart and felt the floor of the morning move under him.

*He is not concerned.*

*He has had a Cliff Watch report of a dragon passing off his southern water in the eleventh year of a suppression and he has taken it the way a man takes the weather, and either he knows something the Watch does not or he has decided the thing is beneath the council, and knowing him it is the second.*

*Which is the first mistake I have watched him make.*

The Prince took his hand off the child's head.

"Saenna. The slate."

She gave it to him. He read it at arm's length the way the long-sighted do, and turned it over, and read the back, and gave it back.

"Ashvenn's hand?"

"Yes, sir."

"Then go and stand at his shoulder until the watch turns and copy everything he writes, including the things he crosses out. Especially the things he crosses out." He was already looking at the chart again. "Bring me the crossings-out separately."

"Yes, sir."

"Saenna."

She stopped at the door.

"You said it badly," Ruvaen said. "Say it worse next time rather than not saying it. Go on."

The door shut. Velenn Ithalan's pen had not stopped once.

---

## III. THE MARSHAL'S OBJECTION

"My lord." Ithuran straightened and his knee complained about it. "The southern water."

"Say it."

"I have four hundred spears on the Landward Wall and a hundred and ten of them are boys who have held a spear for a season. The Under-Houses have been coming up the ridge line for eleven years and I know what that costs and I know to the bushel what it costs to keep the wall fed. I do not know what a dragon costs. Nobody in this room does. There has not been a passing on this coast in nine hundred years and there is no line in any ledger at Vaelmarr that tells me what to spend against one."

"No."

"Then I would like a position from you. In form, if you will give it to me, because if I am to strip the south curtain of braziers I would prefer the entry to say who ordered it."

Corvienne Sorreth looked up at that. So did Velenn.

*I have just accused him of something in front of a Reckoner and I did not mean to and I am not going to take it back.*

Ruvaen Aloreth sat back in the chair and the chair took it.

"You will have it in form when I have something to put in it," he said. "Here is what I have. Ashvenn Lorreth has counted nine over six days, all southbound, all high, all in the hour before dawn. Not one of them has come within four leagues of the stacks. They are going somewhere and they are going there in a straight line and they are not stopping, and a thing that does not stop is not, this week, my problem."

"And when it stops."

"Then it will be my problem and I will be on the wall for it like everything else." He tapped the chart twice with two fingers, on the ridge, on Ollavaeth. "Ilthára Korvaeth has put eleven waves against that ridge in eleven years and has taken it eleven times and has never once held it past the thaw, and she has done it at a cost that would have broken any house in the New World that was not sitting on the deep roads. Ask yourself what she does in the twelfth year."

"She comes again."

"She comes again. She has nothing else. Her doctrine will not let her hold and her people will not let her stop, and so she comes again, and my four hundred spears and your hundred and ten boys stand on the wall and she does not break us, and the whole of the northern argument grinds itself another year thinner against a rock that will not move." Something went across the grey eyes and did not stay. "That is the work. It is not glorious and it is not clever and it has cost me every friend I had. I am not putting it down for nine animals going somewhere else."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Velenn Ithalan, still writing: "Nine over six days is a migration."

"Yes."

"Migrations come back."

"Yes," said Ruvaen Aloreth. "Which is why the child is copying the crossings-out."

---

Ithuran Melloran had been in the room when they brought the Empress's body up the causeway and he had been standing close enough to see what the brother's face did, which was nothing.

That was the eleventh day of the withheld season, and the water had been flat, and the gulls had been on the stacks in their thousands and screaming, and the whole of the household had come down to the sea gate and stood there in the wet, and Ruvaen Aloreth had walked out along the arches to meet the barge alone and had put his hand on the covering and had left it there for the time it takes to say a name in full form, and had not said one.

He had not said one since. Eleven years. Not in council, not in the chapel, not in the sealed entries, not once in Ithuran's hearing. The Reckoners had a phrase for it in the register and the phrase meant a name withheld by the holder, and Corvienne Sorreth had entered it eleven times in eleven years and had written a private note beside the eleventh, and Ithuran knew about the note and had never read it and never would.

*The Drow call him Kinslayer, which is a slander and is also, in their grammar, an accounting term. A debt incurred and not tallied. They mean that a man who takes that many of another house and does not enter what he owes for it is running a ledger nobody can audit.*

*They are wrong about the kin and they are not wrong about the ledger.*

Vengeance was not the word Ithuran would have used, and he had heard the Vessel factors and the Court clerks and the ballad men use it for eleven years, and every one of them had the shape of the thing wrong. Vengeance finishes. Vengeance has a last name on the list and a morning after it. What the Prince of Eresse had been doing on that wall for eleven years had no morning after it in it at all. He had simply decided that the northern argument would be worn down against him personally, at whatever rate his body permitted, for as long as his body permitted, and he had begun, and there was no provision anywhere in the design for stopping.

Ithuran had been a soldier for two hundred and sixty of his two hundred and eighty-nine years and he had met perhaps four men he could not read after a season.

*He is the only one I have ever failed to read after eleven.*

---

## IV. THE CROSSINGS-OUT

The session broke at the seventh bell and the room emptied the way it always did, Velenn first with the tallies against her hip and the Reckoner behind her, and Ithuran last because the knee wanted the stair taken one tread at a time.

He got as far as the door.

"Marshal."

"My lord."

Ruvaen Aloreth was standing at the seaward window with his back to the room and the light off the water coming up onto the underside of his jaw. The window looks south along the chalk and down onto the causeway and out over four leagues of open green water to nothing at all.

"The stacks are empty," he said.

Ithuran Melloran stood in the doorway with his hand on the frame.

"Since the second night," Ruvaen said. "No die-off. No carcasses. They went in the dark and they went all at once and they went inland, which gulls do not do." He had not turned around. "I have been waiting all morning for one of the six of you to say it. You came up the western stair. You saw it."

"I saw it."

"Why did you not say it."

"Because a marshal who reports birds, my lord, gets asked about birds for a year."

Ruvaen Aloreth laughed. It was a short, unused sound, and it had rust in it, and Ithuran had heard it perhaps twice.

"Fair," he said. "Come here."

Ithuran came and stood at the window, and the two of them looked down at four leagues of empty water going grey under the turn of the light.

"Nine in six days, southbound, high, in the hour before dawn," Ruvaen said. "And every bird on this headland went inland in the dark on the second night. Both of those things are true and I do not know what sits between them and neither does the Watch and neither does the Court and neither, I would wager the winter charter, does Ilthára."

"Then why did you take the report from the child as though it were the weather?"

"Because she is twelve, and she came through that door frightened, and the first thing she will remember about carrying a report in her life is going to be whatever happened in the two seconds after she finished speaking." He turned from the window at last. "She is going to be carrying worse than that one day. I want the muscle in her that says *say it anyway*, and you do not build that muscle in a room that goes quiet."

Ithuran Melloran opened his mouth and found nothing in it.

"I thought you were unconcerned," he said.

"I know what you thought." Ruvaen Aloreth pulled the vambrace strap tight with his teeth on the tongue of it, the way the Landward companies do, because a man wearing half-harness at the seventh bell is a man going up to the wall. "Get your knee seen to. Then double the Cliff Watch and take the south curtain lamps for Velenn's clerks and put the boys on the seaward face where they can see something that is not a ridge for one night in their lives."

"My lord. The crossings-out."

"Bring them to me on the wall."

He went out past the Marshal and down the stair to the sea gate two treads at a time, and the sound of him going down it went on for a long while after there was anything to see.

# The Summons

*The drovers' road north of the border, under the aurora, the first night out of the Pit*

The summons came at dusk, and it came from below.

Shōki was three hours north of the dead village by then, on a drovers' road that ran up a long white valley between black pines, and the sun had gone down behind the ridge into a sky of washed apricot and rose. The snow on the road had been packed hard by hooves and sledge-runners and was ribbed with old ruts gone to ice, and where he walked it softened and gave and ran, so that behind him there was a long dark wet line on the white like a wound that would not close. Ahead, where the valley opened, the first green of the aurora was coming up over the northern ridge in slow curtains, green and then violet at its hem, folding and unfolding against the dark the way silk folds when someone shakes it out. The air was so cold it creaked.

He felt the hook move.

It was in him under the Crystal, deeper than the Covenant Scar and deeper than the heart that had started beating again that morning. He had carried it so long he had stopped noticing it, the way a man stops noticing the old ball lodged against his shoulder-blade until the weather turns. It was a thread, if it was anything. A thread that ran out of the back of his Attraction Layer, out past the edge of him and down, through the snow and the frozen ground and the rock under the ground and the long dark places under the rock, all the way to the bottom of everything, where something held the other end.

And something was pulling it.

Not hard. Not yet. It was the pull a man gives a dog's lead when he wants the dog to look round: a tug, a little tension, a reminder of who holds which end. But it came up the thread out of the deep places and set in under his breastbone like a hook setting in a fish's jaw, and for a moment the whole of the bright cold valley tilted, very slightly, toward the ground.

*Come,* the pull said. Not in words. In weight. *All of you. Come down.*

Shōki stopped on the road.

He stood there in the dusk with the steam going up off him into the green light, and he looked at nothing, and he let himself feel it, all of it, the whole length of the thread from the hook to the hand at the far end of it.

He had made that pact three hundred years ago, and he remembered making it the way he remembered everything, exactly and without the feeling in it. He had walked up out of the Hall of Verdicts with Jinsuke's blood drying on the irons and the Plane's bargain still burning in his chest, and he had gone south and down, because there was nowhere left for him to go that was up. And at the bottom of the going down there had been a voice, and the voice had offered him what he wanted.

Not peace. He had not wanted peace. A place to burn. A bed in the deep, where the heat of him would not kill everything it touched; somewhere to put the wrath while it grew, and the promise that it would be let out again when the time came, and that when it came out, every debt would be collected. He had wanted that. He had wanted it so badly he had not read the rest of the terms.

The rest of the terms were simple. When the Grand Demon called, the Wrath answered.

He had sworn nothing. He could not have sworn anything; the Kurenai oath had broken on the black floor, and what was left of the faculty that holds a vow in a man could not have held a promise to a child. That had not mattered. The pact was not held at his end. It was held at the other one, the way a debt is held by the lender and not the debtor, and a debtor who tears up his copy of the bond still owes.

The pull came again, a little firmer.

"No," said Shōki aloud, to the valley.

His voice went out over the snow and came back flat off the pines. Somewhere up the slope a lump of snow slid off a branch and fell with a soft sound.

He did not care what the Grand Demon wanted. He had never cared. Whatever was being planned down there at the bottom of the thread, the thrones and the kingdoms and the breaking of worlds, the long patient ambitions of the thing that held the other end, none of it was anything to him. He had not gone down into the Pit to serve anyone's war. He had gone down to wait. And the waiting was over, and he was in the cold air with a beating heart and a road under his feet that ran north, to the Seat, to the red chair.

He started walking again.

The hook pulled. He walked into the pull the way a man walks into a headwind, his weight forward, his feet setting hard, and with every step it cost him something. He could feel it go: a little heat out of the reserve with each stride, bled down the thread into the dark to pay for the defiance, the way a mill-wheel bleeds water to the race. Not much. He had a great deal. But it would not stop, and he knew it would not stop, and he knew that at some point, a day or a month or a year from now, the hand at the far end of the thread would stop tugging and start to haul, and on that day he would go down whether he walked or not.

*Then let it be after,* he thought. *After the Seat. After the chair. After I have read him.*

Muken Moto.

He turned the name over as he walked, the way a headsman turns a blade in the light before the morning's work, looking for the flaw in it. A sellsword, the old man had said. Came up out of nowhere. The Kōkan blood come back. Seven chairs that could not agree on the weather for a thousand years, and one boy made them kneel.

Shōki had stood in the Hall of Verdicts for twenty-two years with his back to the black floor and his face to the seven chairs, and in all those years he had never once seen the seven lines agree on anything that did not cost somebody else their head. He knew what it took to make the Moto kneel. He knew it better than any man living. It took a weight heavier than every chair together. It took a judgment that ranked all the others beneath it and did not ask them first.

The Kōkan had always had that weight. That had been the whole trouble. The crown line, with its crown eye, the Kamigan, the eye that took what all the other eyes saw and ranked it, and ranked the people who saw it, and ranked the people who sat in the chairs, and settled the matter. He had stood in that Hall the morning they voted on Tsuyu and he had looked up at the red chair and the red chair had said nothing. Nothing. It had let the silver chair speak and the gold chair speak and the copper chair weep and it had said nothing at all, and its nothing had weighed more than all of their words, and the blade had come down.

And now one of them had come back out of nowhere and put the whole house under that nothing.

He wanted to see it. He wanted to stand in front of this boy and look into his chest with the Kurenai eye, through the bone and the blood and the Crystal to the place where the record is kept, and see what shape a Kōkan was underneath, when you took the crown off. Whether there was something in there that deserved to rank other men's lives. Whether there was anything in there at all.

He would know. The eye always knew. It had known Tsuyu was clean before the vote was finished.

And if the shape was wrong, he would end it. If the shape was the same shape as the red chair's silence, he would end it, and the house that had grown up around it, chair by chair, line by line. His own line too, if it stood in the way. The Kurenai had struck his name. He owed them nothing. He would walk through the black and the ash and the hidden crimson as he would walk through the silver and the gold, and if at the end of it there was no Moto left in the north to sit on any chair, then at least there would be no one left to vote a woman to the black floor for a bargain made in a corridor.

The thought did not frighten him. Nothing frightened him. He noticed, distantly, that it should.

The aurora was full now, overhead, the whole sky moving with it: long green curtains hanging from nothing, their hems burning violet and rose, sliding and folding over the valley and throwing their light down onto the snow, so that the road ran ahead of him between pines that were black on one side and green-silver on the other. His own fire answered it, a little. The purple at the cracks of his skin pulsed with the violet in the sky. Somewhere in the pines an owl called and was not answered.

The hook pulled.

He kept walking.

---

Vaashuuk caught up with him a mile further on, which was a thing nobody who knew Vaashuuk would have believed.

He did not walk. He had made himself a long low slab of glassy air, a chaise with a bit of a back to it, and he was lying on it with his ankles crossed and his hands folded on his belly, drifting up the road behind Shōki at a steady amble a foot above the snow, the scorched sleeve of his plum coat trailing over the side. The little glass bubble bobbed along at his shoulder like a lantern. He had found a fur hat somewhere and put it on over the pompadour, very carefully, so as not to crush it, and it sat on top of his hair like a lid on a pot.

"Ugh," he said, by way of greeting. "You feel that?"

Shōki did not stop.

"The tug," said Vaashuuk. "The ol' roll call." He made a face. "Down there. Down-down. Big man's whistlin' for the dogs." He drifted up level with Shōki's shoulder, which on the chaise put his head about level with Shōki's hip, and looked up at him from under the fur hat. "Means the whole set. All seven of us. The sisters'll be there already, I bet. Fightin'. They're always fightin'." He yawned, hugely. "Word is the floor's comin' out of the whole world inside a fortnight. Hell and here, all one big room. Everybody's real excited." He rolled his eyes so far that the yellow went right up under the lids. "Real excited."

"You are going," said Shōki.

"Am I *goin'*." Vaashuuk looked scandalised. "Handsome. Do I look like a guy who goes places when he's called?" He waved a claw at the chaise, the hat, the road. "I'm here, ain't I? I got sent to fetch you. I fetched you. You're fetched." He settled deeper into the glass. "Captain said bring you alive. You're alive. I'd say that's most of the job done. The rest is detail."

"You were told to bring me down."

"I was told to bring you *to him*. Didn't say when." The gold canine showed. "I'm gettin' there. I'm workin' on it. Real slow." He let his head fall back. "Besides. You don't wanna go. I seen your face when the tug come. You looked like a man stepped on a nail." He watched the aurora for a while. "You got a thread in you too, huh."

Shōki said nothing for twenty paces. The bells on his hakama ties rang, very faintly, as he walked.

"Yes," he said.

"Yeah. Everybody got one. That's how he holds the set." Vaashuuk held up one claw, pinched an invisible string, and gave it a little twitch. "Tug tug. Mine's real thin. I barely feel it. Guess I'm not worth pullin' hard." He grinned. "Or maybe he knows if he pulls hard I'll just lie down and he'll have to drag me the whole way and that's *work*." He chuckled, a long low wheezy sound. "Laziness is a shield, handsome. Nobody likes carryin' dead weight."

"I am going north," said Shōki.

"I figured. The red chair." Vaashuuk said it lightly. Then, less lightly: "You leavin' bodies all the way up?"

Shōki did not answer.

The goblin looked at him a while from under the hat. Then he looked back down the valley, the way they had come, where the long dark wet line of Shōki's road ran back south through the snow toward the village they could no longer see.

"I went back," he said. "Down there. After you left. Lookin' for a coat." He was quiet a moment. "Kid in the last house past the bridge was alive. Little girl. She was sittin' on the step with the dog. The one with the white ear." He shrugged inside the enormous velour. "I didn't do nothin'. I don't do nothin'. I just figured you'd wanna know."

Shōki walked on. The steam came off him into the cold. He said nothing for a long time, and when he spoke it was not about the child.

"What do you want," he said, "if you will not go down."

Vaashuuk blinked at him. Then he laughed, delighted, so suddenly that the chaise bobbed.

"Ouuuu. Nobody asks me that. Nobody *ever* asks me that." He pushed himself up on one elbow, and for once his heavy lids came all the way up, and the lamp-oil yellow under them was bright and sharp in the green light. "You wanna know? Okay. Okay, I'll tell you, 'cause you asked nice, in your scary murder way."

He pointed back down the valley with one black claw, past the pines, past the ridge, to where, very far off, a single light burned on a crest above the road: the watchtower, the basalt fist, the banner of the Iron Mandate invisible in the dark but there.

"See that? Them. The Imperium. The Empire in the North. Big hobgoblins, big walls, big laws carved on a big rock." He spat, neatly, over the side of the chaise. The spit hissed in the warm wet snow of Shōki's track. "You know what's under the laws? Us. Goblins. Down in the gorge, down in the yards, filin' valve-seats, solderin' glyph-plates, buildin' the little brass hearts that go in every gauge and every standpipe and every draw-lamp from here to the coast. Fourteen hours on, a bunk and a bowl of soup, and up again. That's the whole north, handsome. That's the whole world. Every light in every Moto hall and every Yukari tower and every hobgoblin fort runs on somethin' a goblin made with his eyes goin' bad, and not one of them ever looked down to see who."

He lay back again. His voice dropped, slow and drawling, but there was nothing lazy in it.

"My ma made valve-seats till she was blind. My pa worked the bellows till the roof come down on him. And you know what nobody in that whole gang ever got? Not once, not ever, in their whole lives?" He held up a claw. "A day off. A lie-in. One mornin' where nobody was yellin' at 'em to get up. That's what they never got. Not money. Not thanks. *Rest.* Time to be lazy. Time to lie in the sun like a cat and do nothin' and have it be nobody's business." He smiled at the aurora. "Everybody thinks sloth's the worst of the seven. The joke one. The one that don't do nothin'. But I'll tell you what, handsome. Every goblin in the north would give his right arm to commit my sin for one single day."

The chaise drifted on.

"So that's what I want," said Vaashuuk. "I'm gonna go down to the gorge and the yards and tell 'em to lie down. All of 'em. Every goblin in every workshop. Put down the file. Lie down on the floor. And then I'm gonna stand on the floor next to 'em, real slow, and anybody who comes in yellin' get up, get up, they're gonna find out what it's like when the whole world goes thick around 'em." He wiggled his claws. "The hobgoblins first. Then the Moto. Then the Yukari, and the elves, and the Dawi, and whoever else got rich off goblin hands. One by one. All the way round. Till every last one of 'em says it out loud: *goblins worked. Goblins built this. Goblins get to rest.*" He yawned again, and this time the yawn was long and slow and satisfied. "And respect. Respect'd be nice. Never had that neither."

They went on for a while without speaking. The aurora moved. The snow squeaked under Shōki's boots and hissed where the heat of him touched it. The bubble at Vaashuuk's shoulder bobbed and glowed.

"It will take a long time," said Shōki at last.

"Oh, *years*." Vaashuuk sounded pleased about it. "Decades. I ain't in a hurry. I never been in a hurry in my life. That's the beauty of it." He tipped the fur hat down over his eyes. "You go burn your chair, handsome. Read your Kōkan. Do your big angry thing. I'll be down in the gorge, lyin' on a floor, startin' a revolution real slow."

He rolled off the chaise.

He did it lazily, the way a cat rolls off a sunny wall, and landed on his feet in the snow beside the road with his platform boots sinking to the ankle, and the chaise behind him sagged gently into the ruts and melted into a puddle of clear syrup and was gone. He stood there in his scorched plum velour and his stolen fur hat, small and wiry and half Shōki's height, with the aurora green on one side of his face and violet on the other.

"When the big tug comes," he said, "the real one, the haul. You'll feel it. We all will." For a moment he was not smiling. "Don't fight it all the way down. It's a long way and it hurts and you get there anyway. Trust me. I tried." Then the gold canine flashed. "And if you see the Captain before I do, tell him I'm workin' on it."

"You will not be working on it."

"I will *absolutely* not be workin' on it." Vaashuuk tipped him a two-claw salute off the brim of the hat. "See ya around, Old Furnace."

Shōki stopped.

He turned, slowly, and looked down at the goblin, and the purple at the cracks of his skin came up bright and the air around him shimmered and the snow for a yard in every direction went to water in a single breath.

Vaashuuk held up both claws, grinning.

"Kiddin'. Kiddin'. Jeez." He backed away up the slope toward the pines, his boots sinking in the drifts. "I read the book too. Found one in the noodle shop. Lousy rhyme." He was quiet a moment. "Her name wasn't in it. I noticed that."

Then he turned his back and walked away into the trees, slowly, the way a goblin walks who has never once in his life hurried, and the little glass bubble went bobbing after him through the black pines like a lantern carried home late, and then there was only the dark between the trunks and the aurora above it.

Shōki stood alone on the road.

The hook pulled. Down. *Come down.*

He put his hand flat on the knot of the white robe at his hip, where the chapbook was, against his skin, soft and warm. Then he turned north again, toward the Seat, and walked on up the long white valley under the moving sky, into the pull, paying for every step, and behind him the snow did not settle on the road where he had been.

The slow, unhurried, absolute motion of a body standing the way a wall stood, by refusing to not stand, and the refusal was the inheritance, and the inheritance was the blood, and the blood was the thing that the word bastard could not reach, because the blood was deeper than the word, and deeper was the place where the cold lived, and the cold was hers.

"So here is what I want you to carry back to your house, Pietro Ashmore. Carry it back to every lord who calls me Ice and thinks the calling is the dismissal. Carry it back to every family that chose the Tenrai because the Tenrai have an army and I have a broken throne and dangling feet and a name that sounds like winter."

The cold in the room deepened one final degree. The deepening was the blood's final statement, the Kokan inheritance's closing argument, delivered through the stone and the air and the frost on the floor and the specific, undeniable, eleven-year-old presence of a girl whose feet did not touch the floor of the throne she sat in and whose voice reached every corner of the hall her grandfather had built.

"The name is Ice. The name sounds like winter. And winter is the thing that the north was built to survive, and winter is the thing that tests what survives, and winter is the thing that is coming, and winter is the thing I am, and the thing I am is the thing you will kneel to or the thing you will freeze under, and the choosing is yours, and the choosing is now, and the name is Ice, and Ice does not melt."

She turned from Pietro. The turning was the dismissal. The turning was the back of an eleven-year-old girl presented to a man in chains who had called her a bastard and who was now kneeling in a room that was colder than the room had been when he entered it and who would be escorted from the hall and returned to his house and who would carry the story of what had happened in the throne room into every corridor of Ashmore's holdings, and the story would travel the roads the way all stories traveled, carried by the Hallenfelds and the runners and the wind, and the story would arrive in every hall in the north within a week, and the story would be this:

The bastard spoke. The hall went cold. The cold was hers. And the name she carries is the name of the season, and the season does not ask permission, and the season does not melt.

Hild walked back to the throne. Climbed the platform. Sat. Her feet dangled. The dangling was the age. The voice was the blood. The cold was the name. The name was the crown.

She looked at Renard. At the Greymane guard whose hand had tightened on his sword and whose hand had not drawn because the hand had been told not to draw, and the telling had been the child's hand lifting, and the lifting had been the command, and the command had been: mercy.

She looked at Lorn. At the uncle whose grip had raised her and whose grip held the chains and whose eyes were wet, and the wetness was not the cold. The wetness was the specific thing that uncles produced when the child they had raised said something that the child's mother would have said, in the voice that the child's mother would have used, at the age when the child's mother had been learning to braid hair, and the not-braiding was the war, and the war was the thing that had made this child into this.

She looked at the door. Through the door. Through the stone and the snow and the distance, north, toward the polar shelf, toward a man she had never met who was walking south with glacier-crystal armor and a blade named Black Verdict and a cadence she had inherited without ever hearing it.

Her right hand found the broken armrest. Her fingers settled into the fracture. Small fingers. Large gap. The fingers held.

The room breathed.

The cold stayed.

Sonzai looked at his brother with the expression of a man who had just been accused of something he considered both inaccurate and deeply irritating precisely because some portion of it might eventually prove accurate. The Chains of War rested against his hips and forearms again, their newly tuned links responding to every small shift of his posture with an ease Borin had not allowed them before, and one link clicked softly when Sonzai folded his arms. “I am not complaining about a woman,” he said. His voice carried the dry, bruised quiet that had become natural to him over the exile. “I am reporting that an unknown person appeared half-dead in the snow, gave me an unverifiable name, recovered suspiciously quickly in my presence, then annexed my sleeping quarters.”

He let the distinction sit as though it settled the matter completely. His purple eyes narrowed when Sodoku said he was glad someone wanted to remain close to him, and for a moment Sonzai seemed prepared to answer with something sharper. Instead he glanced toward the igloo where Selvara remained, the hide flap shifting slightly beneath the wind. “She does not want to be close to me. She is frightened, displaced, and has decided the person who dragged her out of the snow is presently the least dangerous option available.” A pause followed. “Her judgment is questionable.”

The word truly irritated him more than the rest. Sonzai looked back toward Sodoku, understanding perfectly what his brother had buried inside it and resenting him for being obvious without actually saying anything obvious. “Do not have Nella or Yoko move her.” The answer came too quickly.

Silence followed.

Sonzai’s face hardened by a degree.

“Not because I want her there.”

Another click came from the chains.

“Because I found her. If she is dangerous, I would rather the danger remain somewhere I can observe it than wake up three shelters away beside Dunstan.” His eyes shifted toward the camp. “And because she nearly collapsed when she tried standing. Moving her for the sake of proving that I am sufficiently bothered would be stupid.”

He stopped there, but the pause stretched long enough to betray the fact that his reasoning had been assembled after the answer rather than before it. Sonzai knew it. Sodoku certainly knew it. That made his irritation deepen, and somewhere behind the closed hide flap Selvara unknowingly received another small pulse of the chaotic pressure that sustained her.

Sonzai exhaled through his nose. “Do not look at me like that.”

Sodoku had barely changed expression.

“That one.”

Another silence.

“The one where you decide you understand something about me and then stand there until I become annoyed enough to prove it.”

The deadpan in Sonzai’s voice made the complaint sound almost ceremonial. He adjusted the links around his left forearm and turned partly toward the north, letting his attention settle on something considerably easier than intimacy: a broken kingdom, hostile houses, the Tenrai, old graves, and the possibility of war. Those things possessed rules. A strange woman who became healthier whenever he wanted to kill somebody did not.

“She stays until we determine what she is actually doing here,” Sonzai said. “Matheus watches her signature. Nella watches her condition. Afasoa checks the area where I found her. If any part of her story fails under examination, we address it before she enters Kharven with us.” His mouth tightened slightly. “Until then, she is under my responsibility.”

The phrasing made him pause.

He disliked that one too.

“Temporarily.”

Sonzai’s eyes shifted back toward his brother. “You may enjoy this while it lasts. Eventually something humiliating will happen to you again, and I intend to remember every second of this conversation when it does.”

Then Sodoku said he was ready.

That stripped away the smaller irritation immediately. Sonzai’s posture changed, the brother receding behind the Commander as his attention moved across the camp. Borin’s forge was already being reduced. Garret had the final rift measurements. Soren was pulling down the last bracing lines. Robin and Dunstan were closing supply loads. Behind all of it waited Kharven, the country that remembered their father, feared their names, and had spent eleven years becoming accustomed to their absence.

Sonzai looked north for several breaths.

“I am ready.”

The Chains of War tightened faintly around his forearms, not restraining him but settling into position as though the metal understood the difference between waiting and departure. Sonzai glanced one final time toward his igloo and gave the hide flap a long, suspicious stare.

“After I retrieve the woman occupying my home.”

A beat passed.

“Apparently.”

He started toward the shelter, crimson hair moving behind him in the polar wind. “Get everyone moving. I will bring Selvara.” His voice lowered as he walked away, enough for Sodoku to hear the last part. “And if you say anything resembling I told you so before we reach Kharven, brother, I will throw you into the rift.”

Sodoku gave his brother a smile.

Not the cadence's smile, which was rare and iron and carried the weight of everything the cadence carried. A different smile. A younger smile. The smile of a boy who had just watched his older brother, the Commander, the man whose chains carried something that slept and whose purple eyes carried something that did not sleep, tie himself in knots over a woman who had stolen his igloo, and the tying-in-knots was the funniest thing Sodoku had seen in eleven years of exile, and the funniness was the thing the smile was made of, and the smile was wide and warm and absolutely unhelpful and Sodoku knew it was unhelpful and the knowing made it wider.

"If you say so, Commander."

Four words. Delivered in the cheesiest register the iron cadence had ever been asked to produce, the register that lived at the intersection of brotherly love and brotherly cruelty, the register that said: I heard everything you said and I believe none of it and the none is the thing I am smiling about, and the smiling is going to continue for the rest of the day, and the rest of the day is a long time, and the long time is the punishment for lying to me about why the woman is staying in your igloo.

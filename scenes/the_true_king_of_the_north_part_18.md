# The True King of the North, Part 18

Her eyes went past his shoulder.

Sodoku knew what she had found without turning, because there was only one thing standing behind him worth that particular stillness. He watched his daughter run the comparison across his brother's face, feature by feature, the way a factor checks a delivery against a manifest. Same architecture. Same height off the ground. Crimson where he was dark, purple where he was pale, and the whole of it hung on a frame that carried itself in a register she had clearly filed under something familiar, because her eyes flicked once toward Lambert and back.

Whatever conclusion she drew, she kept.

The wind came through the open gate and took the last of the warmth out of the yard.

Lambert had waited through all of it without shifting his weight, and when he answered the question that had been put to him he answered it at the speed of a man who had prepared the figures four days ago and had been holding them since, which was the entire function of his office and the only apology he had ever offered for existing.

"Three thousand two hundred inside the walls." His voice did not rise for it. "Eleven hundred bushel of rye, four hundred of barley, ninety of oats and a granary floor I would not swear an oath on. Forty barrels of salt pork, which is eleven fewer than the ledger says, and I know which eleven and I know who took them and I have chosen the timing of that conversation deliberately. Sixty-one sound horses. Eight hundred and forty men capable of a day's march in this weather, and I want the phrase *capable of a day's march* understood as the low estimate it is."

He turned his hand over. The burn scar caught the tallow light.

"At the ration we are currently issuing, one hundred and sixty days. Call it five months and a fortnight and be pleased about it. At a true ration, the ration a man needs to swing an axe in cold like this, ninety." The thumb went once against the index finger and stopped. "We are issuing two thirds. We have been issuing two thirds since the equinox."

Snow ticked against the gate leaves.

"The timber towns eat full," Lambert said. "Understand that before you form an opinion about it. Ironwood is the only thing this kingdom still sells that anyone still wants, and a man cannot fell ironwood on two thirds, and if the felling stops then the last thing we can trade stops with it. So Brackenhold eats and Whitmoor eats and Cold Cross eats, and the people inside these walls go to bed on a heel of rye and a mug of hot water with a pork rind in it, and they have been doing that for four months, and they do it without a great deal of complaint because they are northern and because complaining does not fill anything."

*And because they have decided the child is worth it, which is a resource that does not appear in any ledger I keep and which I have been spending faster than I can replace it.*

"The Thin Weeks came early this year," Lambert said. "They came in the second month instead of the fourth. Nobody in this city has used the phrase correctly since, because the phrase assumes an end."

He let that stand a moment, and Sodoku understood that the old man was building toward something and was giving him room to arrive at it first.

"The death-house has not been staffed in four years," Lambert said. "There is no one to keep the Waiting. The families keep it themselves now, in their own front rooms, with the body on the table and the window open and the children sleeping in the same house for three days because there is nowhere else to put them. Ada Foss kept her husband three days in a room ten feet by twelve with four children in it, and she did it correctly, and when I asked her afterward whether she wanted the Crown to find her a room she told me the Crown should find her a granary." His face did nothing. "We have lost the trick of burying people properly, your highness. It took less time than I would have predicted. Four years, and a thing this kingdom did for six hundred years is now performed badly by amateurs in their own kitchens."

The tallow flames leaned in the draft and straightened.

"Then tell me what is missing that should not be missing," Sodoku said, because his brother had asked for that too and Lambert had not yet given it.

Something moved behind the heavy lids.

"Grain is missing," Lambert said. "That you know. What is not missing is inscription substrate. Stannvaard has cut our grain allocation by three quarters over two years and has delivered inscription substrate on schedule, in full, every quarter, for eleven years. Through the collapse. Through the factional fighting. Through two winters when the corridor was closed to everything else moving on wheels." He paused, and it was a pause with weight in it. "I have written to the Ore Council four times asking them to explain the discrepancy. They have answered three of those letters promptly and courteously and without ever once addressing the question."

Sodoku turned that over and set it down where he could reach it later.

"They want us armed."

"They want us armed and they want us hungry," Lambert said. "An armed frontier that can feed itself is a neighbour. An armed frontier that cannot is a tool." The thumb resumed against the index finger. "I have been unable to decide, over eleven years, whether that is the Dawi being Dawi or whether somebody has been standing at the Ore Council's elbow the entire time explaining to them what a useful shape looks like. I dislike questions that have two answers of equal quality. I dislike them enough that I have stopped sleeping properly."

He stopped there.

The stopping was too clean. Sodoku had spent his adult life in rooms where the shape of a silence carried more freight than the sentences on either side of it, and this one had a corner in it, and he read the corner as the man's dead wife. Eleven years of not sleeping. A name he had not said out loud in front of the girl. It fit so well that Sodoku did not examine the fit.

"Though do not mistake me," Lambert said, and his eyes went to Hild and came back. "She has eaten. Every day. Full ration, meat twice a week, and I have fought her over it in that hall more times than I have fought her over anything else in her life, because she wants to eat what her people eat and she is entirely correct that it would be the honourable thing and she is entirely wrong about what it would cost us." He said it flatly. "She is eleven and she is not finished growing and this kingdom has exactly one thing that people will still walk three days through compromised territory to stand near. I will not have that thing die of a principle. She has called me a coward for it. She will call me worse before she is done. I have written it in the ledger under acceptable losses."

Hild's ears had gone red. She did not look away from the middle distance.

*Say it in front of him. Say it in front of all of them. Old man, I hope your knees hurt.*

"I am glad you are back," Lambert said. "That sentence is not sentiment and I would like it received as it is issued. This land needs you. I need you. I have been running a kingdom out of a lockbox and a memory for eleven years and I have run out of both, and if there is going to be a war, and there is, then somebody has to stand at the front of it who the Banner Houses will follow without first requiring an argument."

"You will get one argument," Sodoku said.

"I expect several."

"You will get one from me. The rest will come up out of the Houses." He looked at the patched course in the wall. "Six of them, and every one has spent eleven years learning that the Crown is a thing you survive rather than a thing you serve. I am not walking into that hall assuming an oath transfers. Oaths expired with my father. We will be re-swearing this kingdom one house at a time and paying for each one, and I want to know what we have to pay with before I stand in front of the first of them."

"That," said Lambert, "is the correct order of operations, and I have been waiting eleven years to hear somebody say it in that order."

Sodoku turned his head.

Emira was still standing where she had come to a halt, and the aura she carried had gone down into its dormant state, and she had said her piece, all of it, the way she always did: straight through, no gaps, nowhere for him to put a word until she was finished. He let a breath go out of him. It steamed and the steam went sideways off the wind.

"You are right about the debt," he said. "You are wrong about which of us is carrying it, and I am not going to argue that with you in a wind at this hour, so I will concede the ground and take it back later when I have somewhere warm to do it from."

He picked a stone up off the gravel with his bare hand and weighed it and let it drop.

"And I never said I did not need it. I said you owed me nothing. Those are two sentences and they only sound alike to somebody who has already decided which one she is going to be angry about."

Yoko's ears came around. She had absorbed the sister-in-law remark without any visible reaction at all, which anyone who knew her understood to be the reaction.

"I am not throwing myself into anything," she said. "I have a child asleep on the third sledge and a husband with a documented tendency to walk toward large problems in order to see them better. My entire function on this expedition has been to stand behind that tendency and describe what it is walking toward." Her tail moved once. "I would appreciate the assistance."

*She is teasing me, which means she has decided I am family, which is a promotion I did not apply for and will not be returning.*

"As for quiet," Sodoku said.

He looked at the gate lamps. Tallow, guttering, throwing that thick yellow light that never reaches a corner.

"Quiet is what a thing sounds like when it is not currently pointed at you. I never saw a body. Neither did you. Neither did anyone we can name." He said it without heat. "I will not build a plan on a corpse nobody has produced. If he is dead then the world is a smaller and better place and I have lost nothing by having been careful. If he is not, then we walk in expecting weather and we are not surprised when it snows."

Beyond the yard, three streets up, past the broken fountain and the black stumps of the counting-house, a lamp burned in an upper window of a house that had no business having lamp oil.

Sodoku found it without meaning to. Oil, not tallow. The flame stood straight and white and small. He held on it for perhaps two seconds.

The lamp went out.

The flame did not gutter and nobody carried it back from the glass. Somebody had put a hand over it, and the hand had been quick, and Sodoku stood in a ruined courtyard with his daughter's frost around his boots and filed the window and could make nothing whatsoever of it.

Taulagi had seen it too. The old man's chin had come up perhaps a quarter of an inch.

*Third house past the fountain. Second floor. Left of the chimney.* Taulagi's hands stayed exactly where they were. *I will find out who lives there before I sleep, and I will not be telling anyone that I intend to.*

"Please come this way."

Hild's voice came out with the wall behind it, and it carried, because she had learned to make it carry in a hall full of men who wanted her to be small.

"It is cold and you are all standing in it, and half your column has been standing in it since the gate came off the bar, and there is nothing at this gate that cannot be discussed indoors." She caught herself. Adjusted. Came down half a register. "The palace is not habitable. The east wing has no roof and the wells under it are fouled. I have been living at the Hallenfeld factor's house on the low street, which has a kitchen that works and a table long enough for all of us, and it is nine minutes' walk and the walk is uphill and I am telling you that now so that nobody complains about it later."

She turned to Lambert.

"Edward, you will accompany us. We plan tonight." The chin came down again. "While we can."

Lambert bowed shallow.

"Your grace."

The girl put her hand into the pocket of the slate overdress and closed it around something and Sodoku watched her do it. He knew the shape through the wool before he knew he knew it: a river-smoothed hearth stone, palm-sized, the kind that goes into the fire at supper and comes out wrapped in flannel and goes to the foot of a bed. Somebody had given her a night-stone. She had been carrying it since the messenger came, gripping it through her clothes, and it had gone stone-cold hours ago and she had not put it down.

She started walking.

She got four paces and stopped and did not turn around, and stood there in the frozen ring with her back to all of them, waiting, and would not ask.

Sodoku crouched, picked his gauntlet up out of the gravel, and went after her.

Sonzai went to his old room without asking for anyone to guide him.

He remembered the way even after eleven years, remembered the narrow corridor turns, the warped floorboard outside the third door, the section of wall where winter always found a way through the mortar no matter how many times it was patched. Kharven had changed, but not enough to hide itself from him. When he pushed the door open, the room greeted him like a place that had survived by being forgotten. Dust lay thick across everything. Old blankets had been thrown over furniture and left there so long that their folds had hardened into shape. A broken spear leaned in one corner beside two warped storage chests. One of the shutters did not close properly, and cold air pressed in through the gap with a thin steady whistle. What had once been his desk was buried beneath clutter, old ledgers, cracked practice weights, a rusting lamp, bits of leather, discarded tack, three loose nails, and a wooden training knife with half its handle split. The bedframe still stood, though one leg had been crudely braced with stacked stone. Nothing in the room looked kept. It looked abandoned in stages.

He stepped inside and said nothing.

His purple eyes traveled over the mess slowly, taking inventory the way they always did, but there was something quieter beneath the assessment. This had been his. This had been where he had slept, where he had sharpened steel, where he had planned, where he had been a son before he had become a weapon. Now it was a storage graveyard wearing the shape of a room. His jaw tightened. He could smell old dust, stale cloth, mouse droppings, damp wood, and beneath it all the faint mineral scent of stone that had held winter too long. Behind him, he heard a second set of footsteps pause at the threshold.

Selvara entered after him and closed the door.

The latch clicked softly into place. That sound made the room smaller. More private. More dangerous.

For a moment she only looked at him, and Sonzai did not turn. He stood with his back to her in the half-cold room, crimson hair resting against the black fur at his collar, armor still on, chains at his hip faintly still, as if even they understood that something inside the room had shifted. Selvara watched the angle of his shoulders, the discipline in the stillness, the violence held together by habit and structure and pride. When she spoke, her voice was low.

“I know what touched you.”

That got his attention.

Not all at once. Not with a jerk or a flinch. His head turned first. Then his eyes. Then the rest of him followed, slow and exact, until he was facing her fully. The sharpness that entered his face did not arrive in pieces. It arrived complete.

Selvara did not look away.

“I know what lives in your blood and behind your eyes,” she said. “I know the shape of that pressure. I know the taste of it when your anger rises. I know why it feels familiar.” She inhaled once, steadying herself, though not out of fear. Out of decision. “You’ve been touched by a demonic deity, Sonzai. You are Malakar-touched.”

The room went still.

Sonzai’s expression hardened past anger and into something colder. The air around him seemed to draw tighter. His face sharpened with suspicion so immediate and severe it almost looked like hunger.

“How do you know this, Kusho.”

The word left him like a blade.

Selvara held his stare. There was no use lying now. Not after everything his instincts had likely already been circling, not after the strange familiarity between his Calamity and her presence, not after the way she had endured him, fed from him, stayed close to him without withering beneath what he carried.

“Because,” she said quietly, “I know Malakar better than you think.”

Sonzai said nothing.

That silence was worse than a shout.

Selvara took one step closer. “Because I am his daughter.”

Nothing changed outwardly for a heartbeat.

Then everything inside him moved at once.

Anger surged up through Sonzai so hard it nearly altered the room. It was not simple anger. It was layered, old, fresh, inherited, earned, sharpened by grief, by memory, by the murder of his father, by the ruin of his family, by every corpse Malakar’s name touched whether directly or by shadow. It rose in him like a furnace finally given air. And because Sonzai was Sonzai, because Calamity lived in him the way other men housed breath, the anger did not only burn.

It fed him.

The pressure in the room deepened. His Essence thickened. The old junk around them seemed suddenly fragile, as though a sharper movement from him might split wood, crack glass, and scatter the bones of the room’s remaining past across the floor. The chains at his hip gave a soft metallic clink. His eyes darkened. The muscles in his jaw flexed once. Twice. Still he said nothing.

Selvara saw it all.

Saw how close he was to speaking and choosing violence instead. Saw how much of him had already turned predatory. Saw the fury that did not need motion to announce itself. So she gave him proof rather than further argument.

Her body shifted.

It did not happen theatrically. It happened like something true surfacing.

Flesh reformed in layered wrongness and terrible elegance. Pale, sinewed white gave way to exposed crimson lines beneath the skin. Two more arms unfurled from her sides. Her form lengthened into a demonic anatomy that was both feminine and monstrous, beautiful only in the way certain nightmares were beautiful when they were too complete to be denied. Bone rose over her face in a crest-like crown, obscuring the upper half of her features in that blind, god-touched architecture. Her hair became a mass of dark red living strands spilling behind her. Her tail extended, long and fleshy and heavy, trailing across the floorboards. Every contour of her body declared infernal lineage without requiring another word.

Sonzai stared at her. His silence deepened.

There it was. No lie. No metaphor. No half-confession softened for his comfort. Malakar’s blood made visible.

Then, as quickly as she had changed, Selvara folded back into the shape he knew. The monstrous geometry withdrew. Flesh smoothed. Extra limbs vanished. The tail disappeared. The bone-crest receded. In only a few breaths she was herself again, standing in his old room with the cold slipping through the broken shutter and the dust unmoving around them.

Still he said nothing.

His anger remained where it was—high, boiling, alive. Feeding him in hot violent waves.

Selvara stepped into that silence carefully. Not timidly. Carefully. She came close enough to feel the heat of his fury beneath the cold structure of his control. For a moment, she simply looked at him. At the man he was. At the wound he carried. At the power that had every reason to hate her now.

Then she rose slightly, pressed a kiss to his cheek, and let her lips linger there for only a second.

When she drew back, her voice was soft.

“You know now.”

She turned before he could answer, if he had intended to answer at all and walked to the door. Her hand rested on the latch briefly. Then she opened it, stepped into the corridor, and left him alone in the room that had once belonged to a prince and now belonged to a ghost.

Sonzai did not move after she was gone.

He stood in the middle of the junk and dust and old failed preservation, with Malakar’s daughter’s kiss still warm against his cheek and rage boiling through him so fiercely that it felt almost clean. His old room had never felt smaller. Never felt more crowded. Not with furniture, not with debris, but with the weight of things he now understood.

The Blood Hound said nothing.

But in the silence, his anger kept feeding him.

Her face melted into something almost offended, her eyes shifted as she looked into Yoko's with a mix of disappointment and unamusement. Her eyes shifted back to Sodoku for a moment, seeing as how focused her brother was about this.

"You know that was a joke, right?" She said, her words blunt and yet flat with disappointment. "Nevermind... but regardless you understand the point. I do owe you, and you may not recognize that, but I can't just let your kindness go unpaid for... I'm not an ingrate." She said, a soft smirk on her face. "I suppose I take more off you than I do father." She said, a chuckle came from her own dry humor, her eyes drifting. The mood felt too awkward now as she looked around for something to quickly change it.

"You know after being with you for a while I'd think that Yoko would've at least taught you how to laugh, though I suppose teaching her how to harden herself does work too." She said with soft sigh following as she shifted herself looking up to the walls around the gate.

"Anyways, don't you think it's time we got moving? I think it would be rude to keep Kharven waiting for us, I mean... they are expecting us to show up, right?" She asked Sodoku, her eyes glimmering softly as she stepped to his opposite side from Yoko, a glare catching over on Yoko for a moment as if to rub salt into her possibly numb wound. 

"Have you spoken to Sonzai in a while? Or has he been chasing that demon woman again? I swear... when will he learn to keep his pants on?" She said, shaking her head as she let her hands rest on her hips. "I hope he ends up showing up, I mean... it's been rough since dad died. It's not good to keep running away from us." She said, though her hopes probably meant nothing in the end, but still... she could only hope to see the brothers united again.

"B-But I digress of course," She said with a bit of a stutter in her voice, an awkward chuckle coming from her as she looked off, clearly growing impatient with standing here.

Warmth got into the armour before it got into him.

That was the order it always came in. The Glacium plates had been holding polar temperature for eleven days and they gave it up grudgingly, sweating in the kitchen air, beading water along the lames of the fauld and dripping it onto flagstones that had been swept but not scrubbed. Under the metal his gambeson stank. Wool and old sweat and the ammoniac sharpness that eleven days in harness produces in any man regardless of bloodline, and over that the smell of the room itself: peat smoke, boiled rye, hot iron off the range plate, wet wool steaming on the drying rail, and something scorched at the bottom of a pot that somebody had scraped and put back on the fire anyway.

Sodoku sat on a bench built for a factor's clerks and started on the buckles.

Pauldrons first. Then the gorget, which came away with a sucking noise and a rush of trapped heat that fogged in front of his face. He set each piece down on the flags in the order he would put it back on, because that was a habit his father had beaten into him at nine years old and because a man who has to hunt for a vambrace in the dark has already lost whatever is happening in the dark.

The fire cracked. A door banged somewhere in the back of the house and a woman's voice called up the passage asking how somebody's stack was, and a man's voice answered that it was four ricks and all of it green, and the woman said something short that got a laugh out of two people.

Green wood. In the second month.

"Whatever he decides in his personal life is his business," Sodoku said. "He is twenty seven years old and he has never once taken advice about a woman, including from our father, who tried twice and stopped." He worked the left couter free. "If he wants to keep a demon in his room he will keep a demon in his room, and if it becomes a matter that touches the Crown then it stops being personal and becomes mine, and he knows exactly where that line sits because we drew it together when we were still children with nothing to divide."

The couter went down on the pile.

"He is here. That is what I asked for. I did not attach conditions to it."

Across the kitchen, Hild had planted herself in front of Yoko with her hands behind her back and her chin at that same borrowed angle, and Sodoku made the mistake of reading the posture as curiosity.

"So you must be the lovely woman who has kept him safe," Hild said.

The tail moved once behind Yoko and stilled.

"I try." She looked down at the girl and did not crouch to her height, which Sodoku noticed, and which Hild noticed harder. "Your father is arrogant. He does not listen. He walks toward large problems in order to see them better and he calls it assessment." A pause exactly one breath long. "I have assumed it was the prince in him. I have no evidence. I have not met enough princes to make a comparison, and one is a poor sample."

*She did not lie to me.* Hild kept her hands where they were. *Everyone lies to me. Osric lies to me kindly and Pietro lied to me cruelly and Edward tells me the truth in a way that is built to make me act, which is a third thing. This one just answered the question.*

The girl looked at her a moment longer, and Sodoku watched his daughter take the full inventory. Silver-white at the roots of the fur ruff where the winter coat had come in thick. Dark grey through the crown. A face with too much bone in it to be soft.

Fern would have liked her.

Fern would have liked her and been jealous of her in the same breath and would have said both out loud at the table, in front of company, because she had never in her life been able to hold two feelings without narrating them. He had heard her do it about a Hallenfeld factor's wife, a horse, and once, memorably, about a bowl.

*The child is testing whether I will lower myself to her height, and I am not going to, because she is the only person in this house who has been talked down to since she could stand and I refuse to be the twelfth.* Yoko's ears stayed forward. *She will work out what that means on her own. She is quick. She has already worked out three things about me that her father did not notice in a year.*

"Emira." Sodoku got the last strap on the cuirass and the front and back plates came apart and the cold air in the gap between them made him grunt. "I am glad you are safe. I have wanted to say that for four days and there has been no hour in which saying it would not have sounded like a preamble to an order."

He set the breastplate against the bench leg.

"Robin went south nine days ago. Fast horse, no escort, sealed pouch, and instructions to burn it if he was stopped by anyone wearing anything at all." He rubbed his forearm where the vambrace had been. "He carries a request for aid to the Holy Sea of Alabaster."

Lambert's head came up.

He had taken the chair nearest the range, coat still on, and had been warming the burned hand against the iron in a way that suggested it hurt him in cold and that he had spent eleven years declining to mention it.

"You expect the Alabasterines to answer that."

"Yes."

"Your highness." Lambert set the hand flat on his knee. "I have written four letters to the Ore Council in eleven years. I have received three replies. All three were courteous. None of them contained a fact. Alabaster is farther, poorer in grain than Stannvaard, and has never once moved a granary north for a kingdom that was not in its charter. I would like to know what you think you are holding that I have not been able to see from this chair."

Sodoku took his time.

The fire went down and somebody in the back passage put a log on it and the log was green and hissed.

"The Alabasterines keep our traditions," he said. "Ours, and they have never once pretended otherwise in a document that carried a seal. The Holy Sea's rite for testimony descends out of the same body of practice my mother spent nineteen years teaching, and she taught it in the Holy Sea's own halls, in a language she never learned properly, to men who wrote it down and filed it under a heading she would not have recognised." He looked at the fire. "She was not a missionary. She went because the Saishiki was dying and Alabaster was the only place that would sit still long enough to be taught. She did not do it for Kharven. Kharven inherited it anyway."

"And she is dead."

"She is dead," Sodoku agreed. "Along with the last four people who could bind an oath into anything harder than breath. There is no Saishiki in this kingdom. There has not been one since she died. Six Banner Houses swore to my father in a rite that made the swearing structural, and when the rite died the oaths went back to being words, and words came apart in a winter." He rolled his shoulder in the socket. "Ask yourself why the Houses fragmented along exactly the lines they fragmented along, and then ask yourself what was holding those lines before."

Lambert said nothing for a moment. The thumb had stopped.

*He has put his finger on the load-bearing member and he has done it in one sentence, which nobody in this kingdom has managed in eleven years including me, and I have been sitting in the ledger the entire time looking at the grain.*

"Stannvaard," Sodoku said.

He said the name and let it sit in the room and picked up a rag and started on the water beaded across the breastplate, because the plates spotted if you left them.

"Cut a kingdom's grain by three quarters and deliver its inscription substrate in full for eleven years, and you are not trading. You are dressing a war measure in a ledger so that nobody has to write the word down." The rag went around the neck of the plate. "Ask what my father would have done. He would have taken four thousand men down the corridor and stood them at the Ring gate and asked the Ore Council to explain the discrepancy in person. He would have got an explanation. That is what men mean when they say he was vicious. They mean he was fast."

"And you propose to be slow."

"I propose to be correct first and fast afterward." He turned the plate over. "But do not mistake what I think of them. What Stannvaard is doing is a proposition of war conducted with a quill, and it is a disgrace to the standard the Scale claims to hold, and I am willing to say that out loud in a room with a Measurewright in it."

"They do not worship," Lambert said. "Sancta Lux. Whatever you are about to build on, they will not stand on it with you. The first line of their catechism is a refusal. They strike the word church out of every filing that carries a seal, they call the Archons crowned principles, and they call anyone who kneels to a measure a man who has already agreed to be lied to. I have read the Catechism of Equal Breath. Twice, and the second time was to be sure I had understood the first." His voice stayed level. "You will send them a letter about the True Religion and they will file it as a category error and answer it courteously and it will contain no facts."

"I know what is in their catechism."

Sodoku set the rag down.

"I am not sending them a sermon. I know they do not kneel. I respect the refusal more than I respect most prayers, and I will tell any Lector who asks me that a body which declines to be adored is the only body in four worlds I would let near my kingdom's accounts." He looked up. "Here is the thing they will not enjoy hearing, and it is true, and it is why I think it will move them.

"The Moto did not build the Scale. We are one of the things it was calibrated on.

"The Works are older than the charter. Older than Izayoi, older than the Great Spirit War, older than anyone's chapter hold. They were written before time and they are the meanings stripped down to the bone, and the Archons are those same meanings crowned. Sancta Lux measures against a reference that descends out of that. So does my blood. My family has carried Hataraki in the eye for longer than that order has existed, and it did not come to us through a charter, and no one issued it to us, and there is no procedure by which it can be revoked." He said it flatly. "That is what the labours are. The Works are the labour and the Archons are what the labour became when it needed a sovereign to keep it running. My mother spent her life on the difference. You people see the settlement and the blood in the corridor and you call it savagery, and you are entitled to, and I have never once argued it. Do not confuse it with what we believe. We hold to the high religion of the labours. It is older than the north, it is older than the Ashen Crown, and it does not require anyone's permission to be true."

The kitchen had gone quiet. Somebody in the back passage had stopped talking.

"I can freeze the water in a man's chest from nine paces," Sodoku said. "I can put a verdict through plate. Every one of those is a physical thing and all of it is the smaller half. The power that held this kingdom together was a woman in a hall with her hands open, binding six oaths into something that would still be load-bearing when everyone in the room was dead, and she could do it because she understood what nature is at the level where it can be spoken to. She is dead and the binding is gone and here we are, three thousand two hundred people on two thirds and green wood."

He let a breath out.

"But you are right about the letter."

Lambert's eyebrow moved one degree.

"None of that goes to Alabaster," Sodoku said. "You are correct and I will not waste a courier proving you wrong. What goes to Alabaster is testimony. Their Wellspring is testimony, their whole apparatus is built to weigh a sworn account, and I am sending them a sworn account: three thousand two hundred inside the walls, one hundred and sixty days at two thirds, ninety at a true ration, and a substrate delivery arriving on schedule through two closed winters. Signed. Sealed. Attestable. Let their chapter hold read it and let them forward it to Altherion, because a body that exists to measure consequence has to weigh a starvation conducted through commerce or admit out loud that its instrument does not reach a ledger."

Lambert sat back in the chair.

"That," he said, "is the letter I would have written, and I would have taken four days to arrive at it, and I would have arrived by the wrong road."

"You arrived at it in this room in nine minutes."

"I arrived at it by disagreeing with you. That is a different skill and a cheaper one." He flexed the burned hand. "I will want the numbers checked against the granary floor before anything is sealed. I do not trust that floor and I will not swear to a figure I have not stood on."

"Do it tonight."

"I intend to do it before I sleep, your highness, and I want it on record that I said so first."

Sodoku stood, and stretched his back until something in it moved, and while he was upright he found himself looking at the shelf above the range without having decided to.

Sixteen jars, most of them empty, one of them holding rendered fat. And at the end, standing apart from the rest with its wax seal intact, a squat brown bottle of burn-salve. Properly made. Rendered clean, no separation at the neck, the kind of preparation that takes an alchemist with a graduated glass and nine hours and a reagent supply.

In a house with tallow in the lamps.

Sodoku looked at it for four seconds and could make nothing of it at all.

Hild had gone to the range and put both hands flat on the warm iron and stood there with her shoulders up around her ears, and after a moment she took the night-stone out of her pocket and set it down against the plate to heat.

"Sit down and eat," she said, to the room, in the voice she used in the hall. "All of you. There is rye and there is pork and there is enough tonight because I said there is enough tonight, and Edward is going to tell me tomorrow what tonight cost, and tomorrow is when I will care."

She dragged a bench out from the table with both hands and both feet and got it into position, and sat.

Sodoku came and sat down beside her.

Sonzai did not tell anyone.

Not Sodoku. Not Yoko. Not Lambert. Not Taulagi. He left the room with the knowledge lodged behind his ribs like a shard he could neither remove nor permit anyone else to touch. Selvara Kusho was Malakar’s daughter. The thought repeated without changing shape, and that was almost worse than disbelief. Malakar had touched his Soul Crystal when he was a child. Calamity had lived inside him ever since, poisoning what should have been his own inheritance and forcing him to learn how to make use of something that had never belonged there. Now the woman who had followed him across the ice, slept in his igloo, drawn strength from his anger, and ridden behind him into Kharven carried Malakar’s blood naturally. Every strange reaction between them suddenly had an explanation Sonzai wished he did not possess.

He walked through the restored portion of the palace without acknowledging anyone who crossed him. His expression made questions die before they reached the mouth. The Chains of War rested against his hip, his sword on the opposite side, and every step carried him farther from his old room without carrying him any farther from what Selvara had shown him. He should tell Sodoku. That was the obvious answer. His brother was returning to reclaim a kingdom already surrounded by Tenrai enemies, fractured Banner Houses, starving territories, and political knives. A daughter of Malakar inside their company was not information a commander concealed. Sonzai knew this. He also knew he had concealed it anyway.

That realization irritated him almost as much as Selvara did.

He emerged into one of Kharven’s old interior gardens. Winter had reduced much of it to skeletal branches and dark evergreen hedges, though someone had kept the central paths cleared and the stone channels free of ice. The garden had once belonged to Ayame more than anyone else. She had planted winter-blooming shrubs along the walls and insisted that Muken leave several crooked old pines untouched because she liked what the wind did through them. Sonzai remembered thinking it ridiculous as a boy. Now he stood beneath those same trees eleven years later and hated that they had survived when she had not.

He stopped beside the frozen reflecting pool and looked down into it. His reflection stared back through a thin veil of ice: crimson hair, hard purple eyes, a face older than the years had any right to make it. For several seconds he considered the possibility that keeping Selvara’s secret was simply tactical. He wanted to understand what she was before alarming everyone. He wanted to know what she wanted. He wanted to know whether Malakar knew she was here, whether she had been sent, whether the weakness on the polar shelf had been genuine, whether her attraction to his Calamity was instinctive or deliberate.

All reasonable.

None sufficient.

The truth was uglier.

He had not betrayed her confidence because some part of him did not want to.

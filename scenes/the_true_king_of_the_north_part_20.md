The cell was clean. That was the detail Aurelian Prudentius kept returning to and could not account for. Morning had not fully committed itself to Alabaster when Dawn Guard crossed the church grounds.

The Holy Sea lay beyond the terraces in a sheet of pale steel, morning light touching only the highest edges of the water while mist still clung to the lower cliffs. Bells had not yet begun their first proper call, but the grounds were already awake in quieter ways. Acolytes carried baskets between the kitchens and infirmary. Stablehands checked tack beneath the long eastern arcade. Two Alabaster clerks stood beside a wagon counting sealed travel cases for the third time because neither trusted the other’s arithmetic. The great church behind them held the last of the night in its white stone, its towers disappearing into low coastal cloud.

Zuberi looked different among it.

His hair had grown enough that wearing it loose had become an inconvenience, and sometime during the previous evening he had finally surrendered it to the hands of someone more patient than himself. The result was a set of clean, close Kusharan braids running back from his forehead, larger braids following the crown and smaller patterned rows worked carefully along the sides before joining behind his head. It changed the shape of his face more than he had expected. Nothing obscured the severe line of his brow now. His reddish-brown eyes appeared narrower beneath it, his cheekbones sharper, the long gold earrings more pronounced against bare skin.

The clothes were different too.

He had abandoned the indigo wraps he had returned from the Paths wearing and dressed for travel in black.

The tunic was Kusharan in cut rather than Alabastrine: long through the body, fitted through the shoulders and chest, with a high structured collar and layered panels descending nearly to the ankle. The outer cloth was matte black, heavier along the shoulders and forearms where subtle reinforcement had been woven directly into the garment. Beneath it ran a narrow central panel of charcoal-black fabric that caught light differently whenever he moved. The embroidery was nearly invisible unless one stood close, geometric Zettari patterning worked in black thread against black cloth, appearing only when the angle changed. Gold was kept spare: his earrings, a pair of heavy wrist cuffs, the blue-stoned necklace resting beneath the collar rather than over it. No cape. No ceremonial white mantle. No display meant for court.

North required clothing that could travel. It also required a child who had packed properly. Which was apparently the more difficult undertaking.

“Asante.”

Zuberi stood in the doorway of the boy’s temporary chamber and looked at the bed.

Then at Asante. Then back at the bed. There were three separate travel bundles.

One contained clothes. One contained what appeared to be half of Alabaster. The third made a suspicious metallic sound when Zuberi nudged it with the side of his boot.

Asante, dressed already, looked back at him with the solemn expression of a child who had committed himself completely to the position that everything presently packed was essential.

His outfit had been cut from nearly the same pattern as Zuberi’s, though tailored for his smaller body and made in a deep royal blue rather than black. The high collar carried fine gold stitching at its edge, and the layered lower panels stopped well above his ankles so they would not interfere with travel. A short dark mantle sat folded beside the bed for colder weather farther north. The resemblance between their clothes was unmistakable without making Asante appear dressed as a miniature version of him.

Zuberi folded his arms.

“What is in that one?”

Asante looked toward the metallic bundle. There was a pause. Zuberi’s expression became flatter.

“Asante.”

“Things.”

“That is normally what bags contain.”

Another pause.

Zuberi crossed the room, crouched, loosened the ties, and discovered two small practice weights, a blunt training knife, three polished stones, a broken piece of old Scriptia plate someone had apparently allowed him to keep, a cup, a second cup for reasons known only to the child, dried fruit, a wooden animal carving, two pieces of chalk, a folded blanket much too large for the bag, and something wrapped carefully in cloth.

Zuberi touched the wrapped object but did not open it.

“If that is personal, it stays wrapped.”

Asante nodded.

Zuberi began removing the practice weights.

“No.”

“I need those.”

“You need functioning shoulders when we arrive.”

“I can carry them.”

“I know.”

That ended that particular argument more effectively than telling him he could not.

Zuberi placed the weights beside the bed instead of taking anything else. The chalk remained. The knife remained. The ridiculous second cup remained because he had learned recently that selecting every battle available to him was merely another way to become exhausted before breakfast.

He retied the bundle.

“Your clothes are packed?”

“Yes.”

“Cold layers?”

“Yes.”

“Boot wraps?”

Asante hesitated. Zuberi stared at him. Asante immediately went to retrieve them.

“Food for the first leg?”

“In the other bag.”

“Medicine Amara gave you?”

Another hesitation. Zuberi closed his eyes briefly.

“Get it.”

Asante disappeared toward the washstand where the small packet had apparently been left beside a comb. Zuberi remained crouched near the bags, one hand resting over his knee, watching him without commenting on how much better the boy looked compared with the child who had first left Vaeloris. Better was not the same as healed. Zuberi had no intention of confusing those words.

The dark-crimson strands of Obsession Force had not shown themselves that morning.

He was grateful for that without pretending gratitude meant the problem was gone.

Asante returned with the medicine. Zuberi held out his hand. The boy placed it there. Zuberi put it inside the clothing bag himself.

“There. Now you are packed.”

Asante looked toward the weights on the floor.

“No.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You looked at them.”

Asante frowned. Zuberi stood.

“That was enough.”

The faintest offense crossed the boy’s face, but it could not entirely conceal the amusement underneath. Zuberi reached out, adjusted the high collar where it had folded under itself, then smoothed one of the blue shoulder panels into place.

“You stay close when we leave the church grounds.”

“I know.”

“You stay close when we reach the docks.”

“I know.”

“You do not wander because something looks interesting.”

“I know.”

“You do not follow a strange animal into an alley.”

Asante looked personally insulted by the specificity.

“I wouldn’t.”

“You have done things equally unreasonable.”

“When?”

Zuberi considered him.

“That question is evidence.”

Asante’s mouth twitched.

Zuberi rested his palm briefly against the crown of the boy’s head, careful not to disturb what little arrangement had been made of his hair.

“Eat before we leave.”

Then he stepped away.

Wax, wet stone, and milk.

The last one did not belong and Verinus noticed it before he noticed anything else, because the lower chapel had smelled of exactly two things for four hundred years and a third arriving meant a household had come in with all its noise attached. Beeswax off the standing candles, forty of them, the good tapers and none of the tallow they burned upstairs. The permanent damp of white stone with a harbour underneath it. And now milk, and warm wool, and the faint sourness of a cloth that has been used and not yet washed.

The bell in the outer court rang the half and stopped.

Verinus stood on the low step below the basin with the cap in his hand and his sleeves turned back, and his fingers were chalked to the second knuckle because he had come straight down from the slope and had forgotten, and he was seventy-one years old and had been forgetting that particular thing for about six years now.

"My lord Palatine."

Charles Lambert came up the aisle with his hat off and his son on his forearm, and the resemblance arrived a full three seconds before the name did.

The frame was Edward's frame. Tall and gone narrow through the shoulder, stooped a few degrees at the neck, the same long face and the same hard widow's peak, except that Charles was forty-seven and the hair had not gone through to iron yet, so it read as a man in his prime wearing a shape that in his brother read as a man in decline. The bishop's charcoal, well cut and well kept. A pectoral on a chain that had all its links matched, which was the first difference, and Verinus filed it.

Then the face. Edward Lambert's face did nothing at all and had done nothing for eleven years. Charles's face was pleasant. Open at the eyes, quick at the mouth, the face of a man people told things to without deciding to, and Verinus had been in enough rooms to recognise a Lambert with charm as a considerably more dangerous object than a Lambert without.

"Bishop."

"You remembered."

"I have never once forgotten a name and it is not a virtue, it is a condition. Bring him."

The woman came up behind her husband and stopped two paces back, which was the correct distance and which she held without any evidence of having thought about it.

She was mid-thirties and northern, from far enough north that the Concord had no name for it beyond a compass point. Broad flat face, high cheek, dark eyes set deep. Blue-black hair in two plaits pinned across the crown, and a coat of soft-tanned hide under the good wool the Court had lent her at the door, which she had accepted and had not fastened. Two fine tattooed lines from her lower lip to her chin. Her hands were down at her sides. She had not looked at the basin once. She was looking at her son and at nothing else in the room.

Verinus read the stillness as shyness in a strange house.

"Your name, madam."

"Anyu." A pause of exactly the right length. "Anyu Qilak, my lord. Lambert since the spring."

"Then you have been Lambert for eleven months and you are already better at this room than your husband, which I would like entered somewhere."

Charles laughed. It was a good laugh, easy and low, and it went up into the vault and came back thin.

"Dabney," he said, and turned the bundle so that the child's face came out of the wool. "Dabney Charles Lambert. Four months on the ninth."

Verinus put the cap down on the step and held out both arms.

The child came across without complaint. He was heavier than he looked, the way they all are, and he was awake, and he had the dark unfocused eyes of an infant who has not yet decided that faces are interesting. A fist worked its way out of the cloth and closed on the front of Verinus's grey.

And the floor moved.

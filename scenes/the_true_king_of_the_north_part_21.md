"If she is going to be in every room I am in, then she is going to see things about my household that I have not told her, and I would prefer to tell her myself, badly, at a time of my choosing, than to have her work them out in a corridor." He looked up. "I am asking your leave to do that."

"You do not require my leave. You require your wife's." Verinus picked up his cap from the pew and turned it once in his hands. "Ask her tonight. And Bishop, when you do tell it, tell all of it. A secret told in halves is a debt with interest on it, and you are about to spend a great deal of time on a very small ship."

He put the cap on and settled it.

Then he walked down the nave to the fourth row on the left, and stopped at the pillar where the acoustic is worst, and put his hand flat on the back of the empty seat.



The letter from Kharven arrived beneath enough wax to pretend desperation was still diplomacy. Ser Luthor of Rainmere stood alone in one of Stannvaard’s private council chambers, the forge-light of the mountain city burning red beyond the high windows as he read the northern request from beginning to end. Grain corridors. Metal contracts. Merchant permits. Freight. An offer to renegotiate terms without ever writing the simpler truth beneath them: we are running out. When he finished, Luthor lowered the parchment and laughed softly to himself. “Still asking politely,” he murmured, running one thumb across Kharven’s seal. “How civilized.”

The Queen no longer needed to be present for him to know what she would decide. That was the elegant part. He had spent weeks placing ideas inside structures she already possessed, control inside responsibility, isolation inside sovereignty, dependence inside strength until he scarcely needed to suggest anything at all. Her Obsession Force had wrapped itself around the need to control Stannvaard so thoroughly that resistance now felt dangerous to her and his advice felt like clarity. The Queen was not a puppet. Kujo considered puppets crude. She still thought, desired, feared, and chose. She had simply been taught to choose along the road he had already prepared.

Luthor unfolded the letter again and looked over the careful promises of payment. Kharven had grain shortages, fractured Banner Houses, competing Moto claims, and a population entering winter with too little margin for pride. Reopening trade would let them breathe. Breathing would give them time. Time would let Stark choose, Greymane recover, Tenrai consolidate, and Muken’s returning sons inherit a kingdom still capable of deciding its own future. Kujo had no intention of granting them that luxury. “No,” he said quietly, almost pleasantly. “You do not get to recover before the question is asked.”

His finger moved across the map beside him, tracing the commercial veins leading outward from Stannvaard. Grain. Aethersteel. Forge substrate. Tool shipments. Merchant charters. Freight agreements. The whole Inner World had built assumptions around Dawi production without realizing how much of its stability rested upon those roads remaining open. Luthor smiled. **“No more trade for Kharven,”** he whispered, then reconsidered the sentence and gave a faint shake of his head. **“No more trade for anyone.”**

That was cleaner.

Let every kingdom count what remained in its warehouses. Let rulers discover which principles became flexible when a child’s ration shortened. Let armies learn how quickly weapons deteriorated when replacement metal stopped arriving. Let merchants raise prices. Let allies accuse one another of hoarding. Let every supposedly independent state reveal how much of its independence had been subsidized by Stannvaard’s mines and caravans. Kujo did not need to conquer the Inner World with an army if deprivation could force every political structure to expose its load-bearing points for him.

Kharven interested him more than the others.

Luthor rested two fingers over its place on the map and stared down at it. “You’ll be difficult,” he said to the ink. Moto pride. Stark stubbornness. Banner Houses who still remembered that an oath to one sovereign did not automatically transfer to his children. A kingdom capable of choosing hunger simply because kneeling would taste worse. His smile widened by a fraction. “Good.”

A direct invasion would have been wasteful. Soldiers gave Kharven an enemy, and enemies gave fractured people a reason to stand together. Hunger was better. Hunger argued inside families. Hunger made allies measure one another. Hunger transformed principles into numbers. “I’m going to make you bleed,” Luthor murmured, his voice almost thoughtful. “Not enough to kill you. Not yet. Just enough that every promise starts costing something.”

He imagined Stark counting grain against independence. Greymane counting surviving soldiers against winter. Tenrai discovering that seized roads were worthless when nothing traveled along them. And when Muken’s sons finally came home, they would not inherit merely a broken crown. They would inherit thousands of decisions made under deprivation, each one leaving behind resentment, debt, compromise, and accusation. Kujo wanted to see what men who spoke so highly of judgment became once there was no clean answer left.

Then his thoughts moved briefly toward the Holy Sea.

Dawn Guard.

The smile stopped changing.

The Old World sovereign was near enough to interfere and powerful enough to create alternate supply. Worse, Zettari material doctrine had begun approaching physical law with the sort of jurisdictional intelligence Kujo disliked: Form, Space, Sequence, Dominion, Reality, languages that could eventually become inconvenient if aimed correctly at what he did to continuity. “And perhaps you come north,” Luthor said softly. “That would make this more interesting.”

For one heartbeat, the disguise loosened.

The handsome face of Ser Luthor did not disappear so much as become insufficient. The warm complexion seemed too shallow for what occupied it. His eyes lost their carefully cultivated humanity, turning pale and impossibly still, and the elegant proportions of his features briefly hinted at something far more beautiful and far less mortal beneath them. A shadow widened behind his shoulders against the wall vast, feathered, wrong for the dimensions of the room before folding back into nothing.

*Kujo looked at his reflection in the darkened window. Ser Luthor looked back. The mask settled perfectly.*

He gave himself a small smile, returned to the brazier, and held Kharven’s letter above the flame. Fire climbed through the parchment slowly, eating its careful appeals, its promises, its signatures, and finally its seal. Kujo watched until the last surviving corner blackened between his fingers.

“No more trade,” he said to the empty room. **“Let them learn what they are when necessity stops being polite.”**

Then he released the ash.

"Travel with the Kusharan Party? Why is it that I always find my way back to the Zettari?" She muttered to herself, almost annoyed with how the number continues to rise. Her eyes shifted to the Bishop, his tone spoke of forgiveness even if it was spoken in a once sour tone. 

"Do not speak to me for forgiveness, you can beg judger once you've finally passed on, but do be fair... that I am more than aware of where threats lead to when they are spoken out of place. Not that it matters much to me, of course. As the righteous hand of judger, I accept your proposal, Lord Palatine... if you do mind me calling you that." She said, looking over at Verinus' tranquil frame.

"But I do not need the solace of a Bishop who speaks in sour tones, I am able to withstand myself on my own path quite fine. I have navigated the lands of Kushara when I was younger, I am quite familiar with it's landscape and routes just as much as a local would be. Perhaps even he would take some benefits from my own presence." She said, a soft but yet cunning smile curving on her lips. Her frame shifted, her knee took to the ground as she lowered her head in silence. Her fist clenched tightly, the memories remained in her, but the energy lied in her fist. 

"I understand your people see me as a hero, and for that I have stood long enough to watch my family become hurt by the claws of monsters and men. I hope you understand that I am not doing this out of the goodness in my heart, but of the protection of the people. Holy men charish charity as if it was virtue, I do not mistake that for duty, m'lord. Darius had his faith in me, so I ask you lend me your faith just as he did. I am not a Martyr, I am a sword pointed in the direction of all who dares to defy the light." She said, her frame rising as she allowed the runic symbols to shift and stirr with their light, allowing her presence to be the judge of who stands in the room as the sanctified. Darius had taught her well, even if her art was still young

Her frame shifted as she looked over at the Bishop. "Come now, we have work to attend to. The will and testament of the divine judger cannot be wasted. We must act immediately." She said, her frame aimed towards the dark wooded doors of the room.

The knee going down on hardwood made a sound the whole nave took and passed along.

Four hundred years of oath into the same stone, and the stone kept every word, and it kept the small ones too. Verinus heard it come back off the vault a half-second late, doubled and thinned, and behind it the suppressants shifted their note for the length of two breaths and settled again. Somewhere up in the clerestory a pigeon went along a ledge and stopped.

He let her finish.

Then he came down off the step, and he did it slowly because the pelvis had been complaining since the second hour, and he stopped at a distance where nobody had to raise their voice.

"Stand up."

He said it without heat.

"I am not going to make a lesson of it and I am going to say it once. This order does not kneel and it is not knelt to. That is the first clause of the Catechism of Equal Breath and it comes before everything else we have ever written, and the reason is not modesty. A man who is knelt to begins, within about four years, to believe that the weight in the room is coming off him. It is not. It never was. It comes off the thing being measured and I am only the instrument that reads it, and an instrument that has been knelt to has been quietly recalibrated by everybody who did the kneeling."

He put his hands behind his back.

"Call me Palatine. It is not a courtesy and I do not mind it, because it is a job and that is the word for the job. I was Osric Peyser for thirty-one years and I answer to that in exactly two houses on this water and neither of them is this one."

*Nineteen years and I have never seen him take that tone with anybody who was not already standing.*

Charles Lambert had not moved from the front of the pew.

"You asked me to lend you my faith as Darius did." Verinus's voice did not change. "I am going to refuse that too, and this one matters more than the kneeling, so I want it heard properly.

"Darius gave you faith because faith is what the Will has to give. The Will decides. It looks at a thing entire and it commits, and commitment without faith is paralysis, and I have watched that man carry the weight of committing for six years while the investiture sat unsigned in a drawer and nobody in this Court asked him how he was managing it.

"I hold the Scale. The Scale does not have faith to lend. It has never had any, at any point, about anything, and the day it develops some it stops being able to read." He let a breath out through the nose. "So no. You will not get my faith and you should not want it and Darius would tell you the same if he were standing here, probably less kindly.

"What I will give you is the reading. Every time. Accurate, whether or not it flatters you, whether or not it agrees with what you have already decided, and delivered to your face rather than into a file. That is worth more than faith and it is considerably less comfortable, and you will hate it inside a year."

The light through the south clerestory had moved another finger's width across the boards.

"Now the other thing," Verinus said.

He walked four paces down the nave and stopped with his back half to her, looking at the empty fourth row.

"A sword pointed at all who defy the light."

He turned the sentence over the way he turned a coin over, without any visible reverence for it.

"I want you to understand something about my Domain, because you are going to be near it for one more day and then not again for a long time. Everything said inside the Sounding acquires weight. Density times depth. A rehearsed speech weighs nothing. A true thing said at cost sinks the floor. I have read four thousand statements in forty-one years and I can count on one hand the ones that surprised me."

He turned around.

"That sentence weighed nothing."

He held up a hand before anything could be said into the gap.

"I am not calling you a liar. Attend to the distinction, because it is the whole of what I have to teach you. A statement weighs according to what it claims about the world and what it costs the speaker to claim it. *There are forty in the death-house at Coldbeck and they cannot be buried* sank this floor a foot last week, out of the mouth of a seventeen-year-old courier who did not know what he was doing. *I am a sword pointed at all who defy the light* makes no claim that can be weighed at all, because there is no arrangement of the world in which it turns out to be false."

He came back two paces.

"Every creed I have ever put on the Scale has read the same way. Weightless, and true for all I can tell, and the Scale has nothing whatever to say about the difference. And I have never told anybody that, and I am telling you, in front of a bishop, because you are going to spend the next year in rooms where men say things of exactly that shape and mean them absolutely."

*He is doing it now. He is putting the whole of it on her at once and he is doing it because he will not see her again before she sails, and he has decided that one uncomfortable morning is cheaper than a comfortable letter.*

"So here is the question and I do not want it answered today. I want it carried."

Verinus stopped in front of her.

"Who holds the hilt."

The suppressants hummed and went under hearing.

"A sword is an object that does exactly what it is aimed at and has no opinion about the aiming. You said the sentence as though the pointing were settled. It is not settled. It has never once been settled for anybody who has said that sentence, and I have sat in this building long enough to read the accounts of four of them." He did not look away. "Darius knows the answer for himself and it cost him six years in a drawer to get there. Aurelian knows the answer and he is currently somewhere in Altherion not answering his correspondence, which I suspect is the answer being paid for. You are nineteen. Nobody expects you to have it. I expect you to have noticed the question exists."

He stepped back.

"That is the whole lesson and there is not another one. Take it north."

Charles Lambert cleared his throat, which in that family was a considerable event.

"On the matter of sour tones."

He came round the pew and stopped at a courteous distance, hat still in his hand, and he had put the pleasant face back on and it did not quite sit level.

"You were correct and I am not going to dress it up. I looked at you and said a word about you before I said one to you, and my brother would have described exactly what I did in exactly those terms, and he would have written it down, and the fact that he has not spoken to me in nine years does not make him wrong about my habits." He turned the pectoral chain one link. "So. Withdrawn, and not asked to be forgiven, because you have already told me what you think of being asked and you were right about that too."

He put the hat on the pew back.

"One thing in return, and then I will take the instruction and be useful.

"You said you have travelled Kushara and can read the routes as well as a local. I believe you. I have read the same maps and I will not pretend to your ground knowledge." His voice went flat in the way the family's voice went flat. "What I have and you do not is the room. Nine years on that bench, and every single week of it spent watching men decide something and then spend two hours constructing the reason they will give for it afterward. I can tell you which one of the twelve has already decided before he sits down. I can tell you when a courtesy is a delay and when it is a refusal that has not been drafted yet. That is not a virtue and it is not wisdom. It is a trade, and I have done it for nine years, and it is the only thing I have that is worth carrying onto that ship."

He picked the hat back up.

"You do not need my solace. Agreed and understood. You may find you have some use for my ear, and I am offering it as a tool and not as comfort, and if you never take it up I will not raise the subject again."

*And I am about to spend four months on a small ship with a sword pointed at everything that defies the light, and there is a four-month-old in the cabin below whose eyes went gold in a chapel this morning, and I have not told her, and I am going to have to.*

*Anyu first. Tonight. Before the tide.*

# The True King of the North, Part 22

She turned for the doors.

Verinus let her get three paces and then spoke to her back, which was a thing he did on purpose and had done on purpose for nineteen years, because a man's shoulders answered before his face did.

"One more."

He waited until the boots stopped.

"You said you are not doing this out of goodness. You said it as though it were a confession and you expected me to be disappointed." He put his cap on and settled it. "I am not. I would have been considerably more worried if you had told me you were doing it out of goodness, because goodness runs out around the fourth month of a bad winter and duty does not, and every person I have ever watched fail in a long errand failed at the point where the warm reason ran dry and there was nothing structural underneath it.

"You have something structural underneath it. Hold on to it and stop apologising for it."

The stained glass had gone another finger across the floor.

"And when you find the thing in the north that makes you want to move immediately," Verinus said, "which you will, and it will be obvious, and every instinct you have will be correct about what it is. Write to me first. Permission does not come into it. You do not require mine and I would not presume to offer it. Write because putting a thing into a sentence for somebody else forces you to say what it actually is, and half the disasters I have read in the Archive were men who never once had to write it down before they did it."

The north door mechanism turned over, two hundred years of maintenance in the sound of it.

Verinus stood in the middle of his own cathedral with his hands behind his back and listened to the suppressants come back up to full and settle into inaudibility.

"Bishop."

"My lord."

"How much of that did she take."

"All of it." Charles was still looking at the door. "And she will do the opposite of at least one of it, because she is nineteen and because you told her four difficult things in eleven minutes and the human ear takes three."

"Which one will she drop."

"The hilt." Charles picked his hat up again and turned it round by the brim. "She will carry the weightless-creed lesson because it was an insult and insults keep. She will carry the writing-first because it is a procedure and she strikes me as a woman who respects a procedure. She will drop *who holds the hilt*, because it is the only one of the four that requires her to consider that she might be being used, and she has spent this entire morning telling us with her whole body that she has already worked out who she serves."

Verinus was quiet for a moment.

"That was an extremely good answer, Bishop."

"It was my brother's answer," Charles said. "I have simply been carrying it around for thirty years without permission."

Charles looked at him for a moment, and then he did a thing that only somebody raised in that household would have thought to do, which was to say nothing at all and wait.

"I have spent nineteen years," Verinus said eventually, "being extremely careful about what this Court aims at anything. I have declined four requests for a Reading that would have ended careers and one that would have ended a house, and every single time I declined it I entered the reason and I slept afterward.

"This morning I attached a nineteen-year-old to a legation, sent her into a country I have never walked, and told her four things she will remember badly. And the arithmetic I did to justify it was sound and I could set it out for the Curia tomorrow and win. And I would like it recorded somewhere, Bishop, that the arithmetic being sound is not the same as my having done a good thing, and I know the difference, and I did it anyway."

"You want me to hold that for you."

"I want somebody to be holding it who is not me. That is the entire office and nobody explains it to you before you take it."

Maud Thole was still at the north door. She had let it come to behind the departing boots and had not gone out after them, and she had her hands folded in front of her buckle where everybody could see them.

*He has told a bishop the true reason out loud in a room that weighs things, four paces from a Lord Knight Commander whose job is to write down what he says. Which means he wants it written down. Which means he is frightened.*

"Commander."

"My lord."

"Add a fifth name to your four. Somebody who can read and who will not be missed from the muster, and who reports to you and not to the Bishop and not to me."

"Watching which of them?"

"I have not decided," said Verinus, "and I want you to notice that I have not decided, and to enter that as well."

Thole's chin came down half an inch.

"Entered."

Verinus walked down the nave to the fourth row on the left and sat down in the seat at the pillar, in the worst acoustic in the building, where nobody could see him from the door.


"I do not kneel because it is submission to the chain of command, I kneel because I understand what my mentor saw inside of you. That is not persuasion, that is an observation." She said, her eyes looking up at Palatine.

"I do not kneel to anyone, but to those who understand the cost. For that, I thank you." She said her head raising as she raised herself off the ground, the weight of her presence did not hinder her, but rather counteracted what was held onto her shoulders. A soft exhale left her lips seeing as how humble and insistent the man was. It almost made her feel offended, like he did not embrace her respectful gesture. 

"The blade does not determine where it is aimed, it is the arm that decides where it should cut. I am no different, I am but a humble servant of judger's will. We are no different from the indifference that morality brings to our door. I do not see in shades of black and white as do most people, I am very aware of the evil lurking around us. I have seen it for myself, and as should you as the scale." She said, her tone not deviating from her respectful and kind gestures. Though her eyes narrowed when he spoke of her as an object, her fist tightened but then released as she understood the consequences.

"I never expected you to be disappointed, I only wanted you to understand the forces beyond myself. That I am not simply a blade because of the purpose that it brings as a soulless object. Understand that I too have others I am looking out for, that I have sacrificed and cared, even if they do not embrace me as their own." She said, her head looking back at them, a tinge of weight pressed on her gaze even if she didn't intend it to be that way.

"It does not excuse abandonment. I know what I must do, and I will do as I must. That is not hollow, that is honor. Be proud, you stand beside me as a witness and testament to what I am willing to give for the holy sea." She said her cloak flowing behind her as she moved towards the door.

"I am not as prideful as my mentor, but I understand my place in the world just as well as any man does. I understood that when I was Fourteen, Palatine. Your authority may hold, your wisdom may speak on terms of alignment, but I am not a force that can be controlled. I am a servant, as is anyone else in the room. I do not resent you, or your kindness. You have shown me more than what the world has offered to me, but I do not accept it. It would be wasted if it were given to me." She said, her eyes shifting away as she looked at the stained glass shine it's verdant colors. The shade of blue and purple came over her figure, not out of will, but out of response.

"That is the purpose a blade holds, not that it is right to give kindness, but it will be wasted when you speak to a woman who has already chosen herself to become a martyr for her people." She said, her eyes shifting away from him as she faced forward. A soft sigh escaping her lips. She continued her paces, softly and with a heavier weight than she needed to express for Verinus' needs. 

"I will begin preparations, but do not see me as a woman who is broken, see me as I am. A weapon, a servant, simply one who is an extra pair of hands to extend and raise those who are in need."

The floor sank.

Verinus felt it arrive under his boots and go out from her across the nave in a long low displacement, and the pigeon in the clerestory went off the ledge, and somewhere back in the understructure the suppressants took the load and came up audible for the second time in one morning and the second time in nineteen years.

Nobody had asked her a question. She had been walking toward the door.

He stood where he was and let it pass through the building.

*That one weighed. That one weighed more than anything I have read in this room since the crate, and I have spent eleven minutes teaching her to notice which of her own sentences carry, and the one that carried is the one I would have given a great deal for her to be lying about.*

It had been the martyr sentence. He was entirely sure and there was no craft in the certainty. Every other thing she had said in the last quarter of an hour had gone up rather than down, because a creed makes no claim that can come out false and the Scale is silent on creeds. *I have already chosen to become a martyr for my people* was a different object altogether. It could be tested. The world could turn out otherwise. And it had cost her, visibly, in the saying, and the room had taken it and put it on the record.

Charles Lambert had come round the end of the pew and was standing in the aisle with his hat in both hands.

"We all have reasons."

He said it to her back, which was not discourtesy, because she was already moving and he had evidently decided that a thing said late was worse than a thing said to a shoulder.

"Mine are not better than yours and they are not more interesting, and I stopped believing they were about four years ago. I want to be clear about the shape of what I am offering, because you have already caught me once this morning putting a word in front of a person, and I would rather be caught early the second time."

He turned the hat by the brim.

"I will support you because I have been instructed to. That is the first reason and it is the smallest one and I am not going to dress it as fellowship." He looked up. "Here is the second, and it is the one that will still be standing when the instruction runs out.

"I have a wife and a four-month-old son going onto that keel with me tomorrow. My brother is in a kingdom that is one bad winter from eating itself. I am forty-seven and I have never been further north than the Sisters and I am, if I am honest in a building that makes honesty expensive, frightened of nearly all of it." His voice went flat in the family way. "And you are the most survivable object on that ship. That is a self-interested reason. It is also a durable one, and durable reasons are worth more on a four-month crossing than noble ones, and I would rather you knew exactly which sort you were getting from me."

He put the hat on.

"So. I will make the preparations, and I will stand where you need me to stand, and when I disagree with you I will say so once, in private, at the time, and then I will do what you have decided. That is the whole of it and I will not be revisiting the subject."

Verinus did not argue with her about the martyrdom.

He wanted to. It sat there behind his teeth fully formed, every clause of it, nineteen years of accumulated argument about what a pre-spent person becomes and what they cost the people who love them and what an institution does with one once it discovers it has acquired one. He had it. He could have delivered it standing.

And delivering it would have been aiming her.

That was the whole difficulty and he saw it while he was still holding the breath. He had spent the morning telling her that a blade has no opinion about the arm, and asking her to notice the question, and refusing her the kneel on the grounds that being knelt to recalibrates the instrument. If he now spent his authority moving her off a decision she had made at fourteen and had walked a very long way carrying, he would have proved his own point against himself in front of two witnesses and the building would have kept it.

She had asked him for nothing. She had specifically and deliberately declined the kindness in advance and given her reasons for declining it, and the reasons had been coherent.

So he let it go.

He would think about that decision for the rest of his life, and would never once be certain of it, and would not have changed it.

"One correction," Verinus said, "and then I will stop delaying you.

"I refused your knee out of habit and I was wrong to. The clause exists because most people who kneel here are kneeling to the office, and I have watched the office swell on that for nineteen years and I keep the door shut on it hard. You were not kneeling to the office. You told me what you were kneeling to and you told me accurately, and I refused it anyway before I had listened, which is precisely the error I spend most of my working life invoicing other people for."

He inclined his head, about the depth a Palatine gives an equal in a corridor and no further.

"Taken back. With thanks."

The south clerestory threw its colours down and the blue and the violet moved across the boards, and they moved across her too, and they did not move the way the light had moved.

Verinus watched that happen and could make nothing of it whatsoever. The sun stood where it had stood all morning. The glass was the glass. Something in the room had responded to her and had chosen a colour to do it in, and he had been Palatine of this cathedral for nineteen years and had never once seen the building answer a person.

He filed it beside an infant who had displaced a floor without speaking, and did not put the two things next to each other, because it was not yet the hour to be frightened.

"Go then," he said.

Charles stopped in the doorway, which was a thing men did in that building because the mechanism took four seconds and four seconds is an awkward quantity of time to hold.

"My lord."

"Bishop."

"You did not argue with her." He said it without turning round. "You had the argument. I watched you have it standing up, and it went past your mouth twice, and you swallowed it both times."

"I did."

"I want to know why, because I am going to be the one in the room when the same moment comes round in four months, and it will come round, and I would rather borrow your reasoning than construct my own badly at sea."

"Because the argument was good and I would have won it," Verinus said, "and winning it would have been me aiming her, in the same hour I told her to watch for exactly that, and she would have noticed inside a week. And then everything else I gave her this morning would have been retrospectively suspect, including the parts she needs." He put his hands behind his back. "You will face a cheaper version of it and you will face it more often. My advice is that you spend the objection when the cost is measurable and swallow it when the cost is only that you dislike the shape of the thing. And Bishop. You will get that wrong. Everybody does. Get it wrong on the side of saying it once, at the time, in private, which is what you already told her you would do."

Charles was quiet a moment.

"That is the second time today you have quoted my own sentence back to me as an instruction."

"It is an efficient method and it costs me nothing."

The north door mechanism turned over and let two sets of boots out and came to behind them, and the suppressants went back down under hearing, and the Grand Cathedral of the Alabaster Court settled into being a large cold room again.

Maud Thole came off the wall.

"That was the floor, my lord."

"It was."

"I have felt that twice." She stopped at the end of the front pew with her hands folded in front of her buckle. "The crate, and now. I want that entered because I do not trust my own memory on a thing I have only felt twice, and I would like it written down before I start rounding it."

"Enter it. Which sentence?"

"I have no idea, my lord. I was watching the doors." A pause exactly one breath long. "I would guess the last one, because it was the only one she said walking away, and people say the true thing walking away. My mother did."

*And I will not ask him which it was, because if he tells me I will spend four months knowing it, and I have to look those two in the face on a mole at the sixth hour tomorrow and wave them off.*

Verinus went down the aisle and sat, eventually, on the second step, which was cold and too low and which he had never in nineteen years been able to stop using.

"Commander. A different thing."

"My lord."

"She said she understood her place in the world at fourteen. She said it in passing, as a date, the way people say a year that has fixed itself in them so hard they assume everyone has one." He put his forearms on his knees. "I want to know what happened to her at fourteen. Not from her. She will tell me eventually or she will not, and either is her right and I am not going to purchase it out from under her by asking her at a disadvantage."

"Deacon Barrow, then."

"Deacon Barrow. Quietly, out of the Archive and the chapter registers, and no conversation with any living person who knows her." He rubbed the chalk from one knuckle with his thumb. "And Commander, tell the Deacon this from me, in these words. She is to look for what was done to the girl and she is to look just as hard for what was done in front of her and not to her, because in my experience a decision made at fourteen is more often made watching than bleeding."

"And if what she finds is Darius, my lord?"

Verinus was quiet for a while.

"Then it will be Darius," he said, "and I will have been right about the wrong thing for six years, and I will go and sit in the fourth row and work out what I owe him."

*It will be Darius. It is always the teacher. Six years that investiture sat in a drawer and I never once asked him what he had been doing with a child while the Curia dithered.*

Thole did not move.

"There is a fourth thing you have not said, my lord, and I am going to say it, and then I am going to go and requisition cold-weather issue and pretend I did not."

"Say it."

"You could stop this. Now. Before the tide. One sentence to the Bishop and she stays here and learns weight in a warm building for two years and goes north at twenty-one with something under her." Thole's flat face did not change. "You are not going to. I want the reason, because when I am standing on that mole tomorrow morning I would like to be carrying the reason instead of the question."

Verinus looked at the alabaster chair against the wall.

"Because she would go anyway," he said. "In the fifth month, give or take. She would accept it, she would work, she would be exemplary, and about the fifth month she would hear something out of the north that she could not sit still through, and she would go, and she would go alone, and she would go without a bishop who reads rooms and four of your people on the manifest as hands." He let a breath out. "A thing that is going to happen can be attended or unattended. I have never in nineteen years found a third option and I have looked for it in every single one of these."

"And there is the part I swallowed," Verinus said, "which you may as well have, since you are the one who will be standing on the mole.

"A martyr is the most useful person an institution can acquire. I want that heard flatly. Buying one is impossible, because they have already spent themselves and there is nothing left to bid on. Frightening one is impossible, because the worst outcome available is one they have already priced and accepted. They want no career, no pension, no seat, and none of the eleven things I use to steer everybody else in this Court. Put one of them in front of a problem and the problem gets solved and the bill goes to them.

"I have watched this order do that four times in nineteen years. Three of the four are dead and the fourth is in Altherion not answering his letters. Every single time, the Curia recorded it as devotion, and every single time what had actually happened was that a body found something it did not have to pay for and used it until it stopped working."

Thole's hands had come apart.

"And you are doing it anyway."

"I am doing it with my eyes open, which is worth exactly nothing to her and is the only distinction I have left." He set his palms on the step. "Watch me for it, Commander. That is an instruction and I want it in the same book as everything else. If I begin spending her because she is cheap to spend, you will tell me, in front of witnesses, in those words."

"I will use worse words than that, my lord."

"Use whichever ones work."

"That is a reason," said Thole. "I do not like it."

"Neither do I, and I would think less of you if you did, and I would like both of those entered as well."

She went out.

Verinus sat on the second step in the empty nave with the colours crossing the boards and the suppressants humming below hearing, and after a while he leaned over and picked his cap up off the stone where he had left it an hour ago, and turned it in his hands, and put it on.

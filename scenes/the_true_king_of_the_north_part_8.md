# The True King of the North, Part 8

The apology received another long silence. Sonzai did not tell him that it was forgiven. Forgiveness offered too quickly would have made the eleven years meaningless, and Sonzai had never learned to lie merely because truth was inconvenient between brothers. He stepped closer instead, lifted the chain between them, and closed his hand around several links until the metal disappeared inside his fist. “I am angry with you,” he admitted. “I will probably be angrier tomorrow. Perhaps significantly angrier when I finally understand everything you have just told me.” The faintest deadness touched his expression, the old Moto humor stripped almost entirely of warmth. “I am attempting maturity by delaying that problem.”

But when the conversation reached the Tenrai settlement, whatever remained personal inside Sonzai’s face changed. The softness disappeared. The boy who had learned he was an uncle withdrew behind the man who remembered Ruin. His chains gave a low metallic sound as his hands lowered to his sides, and for an instant the polar air around him seemed wrong, as though Calamity itself had recognized the memory and leaned toward it. Sonzai remembered what they had done. Not the version preserved in accusation, not the version their enemies had made useful afterward, but the actual thing, the moment grief stopped asking what was deserved and began asking only what remained standing.

When Sodoku asked him to stop him, Sonzai stared at his brother for a long time. “I will.” No hesitation softened it. No promise of gentleness followed. “If you lose yourself in Kharven, I will stop you. If Ruin takes your judgment and leaves only the blood behind, I will put you on the ground myself. I do not care which crown you are wearing when I have to do it.” His fingers tightened once around the chains. “But you will give me the same promise. Do not make me your conscience while pretending I no longer require one.”

The last words came harder because Sonzai knew his own architecture too well. “You were not alone in that settlement. Do not speak about what happened as though I merely followed you there. I chose it too. I remember every choice I made, and there were moments when you were the more merciful brother.” His purple eyes held Sodoku’s without wavering. “If we return to Kharven and I begin calling cruelty necessity, stop me. If I begin calling civilians collaborators because it makes the killing easier, stop me. If I ever look at somebody’s child and see a bloodline before I see a child, break every bone required to make me remember Tomuka.”

The chains sagged between his hands after that, their links clicking softly together. Sonzai looked toward the old marriage approval when it appeared, and for the first time the discipline around him visibly faltered. He did not reach for the parchment. That felt too close to touching his father’s hand through an object that belonged first to his brother. Instead he stared at the weathered folds and understood that Muken had known. Muken had approved. Somewhere inside the final morning of his life, their father had still been building a future in which his son married Fern Stark and a little girl named Hild was born into a kingdom that remained intact.

“Keep it,” Sonzai murmured. “And when we find her, give it to her.” His eyes remained on the place where the parchment disappeared back against Sodoku’s chest. “Not because paper decides what she is. Because she deserves to know her grandfather chose her before he ever saw her face.”

Then came the oaths.

Sonzai listened to every one without interruption. Robin Ice received the longest look, because after everything Sodoku had just confessed, that surname had suddenly acquired another dimension. Borin’s oath drew a minute inclination of Sonzai’s head. Matheus, Garret, Afasoa, Nella, Dunstan, Soren, each offered something different, and Sonzai accepted the differences without asking any of them to become warriors merely because warriors were easiest for commanders to understand. By the eighth oath, the chains had stopped moving altogether. They hung from his right hand in a dark line against the ancient ice, and Sonzai understood with uncomfortable clarity that command was no longer an abstraction shared between two exiled princes. People had just placed their lives inside it.

Then Sodoku called him Commander.

Sonzai closed his eyes briefly. He had been called many things since Kharven fell. Kokan. Monster. Survivor. Ruin-born. Exile. Son of Muken. Some had been titles and others accusations, and often the difference depended entirely upon who survived the conversation. Commander was different. Commander meant that when he made an error, somebody else bled for it.

When his eyes opened again, the softness was gone.

Sonzai stepped forward until the semicircle could see him clearly against the blue-black rift. Crimson hair moved violently in the polar wind, the two narrow braids beside his ears striking softly against his jaw, while the chains hung from one hand with their ancient inscriptions turning beneath frost-light. He looked across the eight people who had sworn themselves to the brothers, and when he finally spoke there was no speech prepared for them. Sonzai Moto had never trusted men who needed paragraphs to tell soldiers what mattered.

“Then hear my first order.”

His gaze moved from face to face before settling beyond them, northward, toward a kingdom none of them could yet see. “We are not returning to Kharven to punish it for surviving without us. We are not burning villages because one lord resists. We are not killing children because their fathers chose the wrong banner. We are not turning the Tenrai settlement into a doctrine and calling repetition justice.” The chain tightened softly around his fist. “Anyone who raises a weapon against us may discover what our blood remembers. Everyone else gets the chance our brothers were denied.”

He let the wind carry that across them before continuing. “We take information before territory. Roads before castles. Loyalties before banners. We learn which houses endured, which collaborated because they believed, which collaborated because their children needed food, and which men used eleven years of disorder to become something that deserves removing. We do not confuse those categories because confusion is easier than judgment.” His eyes hardened. “Ruin is not permission to become stupid.”

Then Sonzai looked toward the north again, and the uncle who had existed for only minutes stood beside the commander who had existed for years. “And somewhere in that country is a girl named Hild. If she lives, we find her. Nobody uses her to bargain with Sodoku. Nobody uses her Stark blood against the Moto or her Moto blood against the Stark. She is not a claim, not a bridge, not evidence that one dead kingdom was right about another.” His voice lowered. “She is my niece. That will be sufficient.”

The chains moved once when he turned his wrist, winding themselves more securely around his forearm. “We will be ruthless where ruthlessness is required. Precise where precision is possible. And when those two things conflict, we choose precision.” Sonzai looked at his brother then, holding the gaze long enough for the promise made moments before to become part of the command itself. “Neither of us gets to become our grief simply because Kharven gives us an excuse.”


He faced the gathered company again. The violet in his eyes seemed almost gentle until the faint corruption beneath it shifted and reminded them what rested behind the gentleness. “Pack the camp. Check the wounded, provisions, inscriptions, lines, and whatever Garret found in that rift. Nobody leaves equipment because they think we will not need it. We survived exile by carrying too much; we can survive one more journey by doing the same.”

Sonzai drew the final length of chain from the ice and wrapped it into his palm. His gaze went north, toward Kharven, toward Muken’s dead kingdom, toward Hild, toward the place where the boys who had committed Ruin would have to prove that men had returned instead.

“Then we go home.”

The camp moved.

Not all at once. Not with the sudden coordinated efficiency of a military column breaking shelter. With the specific, distributed, unhurried competence of eight people who had been doing this for long enough that the doing did not require coordination, because the coordination had been internalized weeks ago, each person's function slotting into the sequence without instruction, the way a body's organs functioned without the mind telling them to function. Afasoa pulled the watch gear and secured the sighting equipment in its oilskin wrap. Soren descended from the roof and began striking the rigging that had been repurposed as wind-bracing between the igloos. Garret walked the rift's edge one final time with the sounding line, recording the measurements in the small waxed notebook he kept inside his coat. Dunstan banked the cooking fire and packed the galley crate with the economy of a man who had packed galley crates in worse conditions and who would pack them in worse conditions again and who did not consider the packing remarkable.

They moved on Sonzai's orders. The Commander had spoken, and the speaking had become the structure, and the structure was being executed, and the executing was the thing that made the oaths real rather than ceremonial, because oaths that were not followed by function were noise, and the ice did not tolerate noise.

Sodoku sat inside the igloo and let them work.

The sitting was not idleness. The sitting was the specific act of a man who had delegated the operational execution to his brother and who understood that delegation was not absence but trust, and the trust was the thing he had asked for at the rift's edge, and the thing he had received, and the receiving required him to prove that the receiving was not a performance by actually stepping out of the operational register and into the personal register that the operational had been interrupting for weeks.

Yoko sat across from him.

She had the gambeson in her lap, spread across her crossed legs with the interior facing upward, and her hands were working. The needle moved through the quilted fabric with the precise, economical motion of a woman whose hands did everything with the specific quality of accuracy that was not learned but innate, the beastkin's fine motor control expressing itself in the needle's path the way it expressed itself in everything Yoko's hands did, which was correctly, the first time, without adjustment. The stitching was tight. The thread was waxed. The repair was being applied to the left sleeve where the seam had opened during the crossing, the same seam that had been repaired twice before, and the third repair was the one that would hold because Yoko had decided the third repair would hold and Yoko's decisions about physical materials were not contested by the materials.

Her ears were flat.

Both of them. Pressed against the skull in the specific configuration that Sodoku had learned to read the way he read everything about her, which was carefully, because Yoko's body communicated on frequencies that her voice did not use, and the frequencies were the truth, and the truth preceded the speech by the margin that the body preceded the mind, which was always, which was the beastkin condition, which was: the body knows before the person knows, and the knowing is in the ears and the tail and the nose and the specific arrangement of fur along the forearms that rose or settled depending on the emotional temperature, and the emotional temperature right now was cold.

Not angry-cold. Hurt-cold. The specific temperature of a woman who had received information that she had not been given the opportunity to prepare for, and the not-being-given was the wound, and the wound was not about the information. The wound was about the withholding. Yoko did not process deception the way humans processed deception, as a violation of trust that could be argued about and negotiated and eventually reconstructed. Yoko processed deception the way her nose processed a wrong scent in a familiar room, as a categorical disturbance, a thing-that-should-not-be-here, and the thing-that-should-not-be-here was not the daughter. The thing-that-should-not-be-here was the silence that the daughter had been wrapped in for the duration of Yoko's knowledge of the man she loved.

Her nose was working. It was always working. The nose read before the eyes read and the reading was continuous and the continuous reading was processing Sodoku right now, processing the specific olfactory signature of a man who was sitting across from her carrying guilt and love and the residual adrenaline of the oath ceremony and the particular biochemical profile that Yoko's nose filed under: he is about to try to fix something with words, and the words will be partially correct, and the partial correctness will be the problem.

She did not look up from the gambeson. The needle moved. The thread pulled. The repair continued.

Sodoku watched her hands. Watched the needle. Watched the precision that was love expressed as maintenance, the specific Yoko form of caring that did not announce itself through words or gestures but through the act of making the thing she was touching more functional than it had been before she touched it. She was repairing his clothing. She was furious with him. The two facts coexisted without contradiction because Yoko did not permit her emotions to degrade the quality of her work, and the not-permitting was the discipline, and the discipline was the woman.

"You do not need to be worried," Sodoku said. The cadence was softer than the cadence he had used at the rift. Not the iron register. The other register, the one that existed beneath the iron, the one that only Yoko heard because the one was only used when Yoko was the audience. "I already told you, Yoko. I love you."

Her left ear twitched. The twitch was involuntary, the specific auricular response that Yoko's body produced when a statement was emotionally significant and the body was processing the significance faster than the mind could decide whether to accept or reject it. The twitch said: I heard you, and the hearing registered, and the registering is real. The ears that remained flat against the skull said: the registering does not resolve the thing that flattened me.

"That is not the point, Sodoku."

Her voice was Yoko. Precise. Offering exactly what was accurate and nothing more, the specific vocal economy of a woman who had decided that inaccurate speech was a waste of breath and that breath was the medium of scent and that scent was the medium of truth and that wasting the medium of truth on inaccurate speech was a form of self-vandalism that she refused to practice. She did not raise her voice. Raising the voice was the human method of communicating intensity, and Yoko's method was the opposite: the voice dropped, became quieter, became more precise, each word arriving at a lower volume and a higher density, the compression producing something that was not loud but that was undeniable.

"You lied to me about your past." The needle stopped. The stopping was the emphasis, because Yoko's emphasis was not vocal. It was operational. The cessation of function was her exclamation mark, the specific Yoko signal that the thing being said was load-bearing and that the load required the hands to stop moving so that the mouth could carry the full weight without assistance. "You were not honest with me about what you did. With that other woman."

The words that other woman sat in the cold air of the igloo with the specific temperature of a phrase that was not a name. Yoko had not said Fern. The not-saying was deliberate. The not-saying was the wound expressing itself in vocabulary, the specific decision to reduce the woman who had preceded her to a category rather than a person, because the category was manageable and the person was not, because the person had a name and a face and a history and a daughter, and the daughter was the thing that made the person permanent, and permanence was the thing that Yoko could not negotiate with, because permanence was a fact, and Yoko did not argue with facts. She argued with choices, and the choice Sodoku had made was the choice to hide the fact, and the hiding was the thing she could argue with, and the arguing was what was happening.

Her tail was wrapped around her own waist. Tight. The self-wrapping was the beastkin's involuntary protective posture, the body's response to emotional vulnerability, the physical act of holding yourself together when the thing you usually held, which was the man sitting across from you, was the source of the thing you needed holding against. The tail's position told Sodoku everything the voice had not yet said, which was: I am not leaving. I am not rejecting you. I am hurt, and the hurt is real, and the real requires you to sit inside it with me rather than trying to talk your way around it, because talking around it is the thing you do, Sodoku, the thing you have always done, the compression of the difficult thing into the silence and the silence into the carrying and the carrying into the distance that you put between yourself and the people who need you to be present, and the distance is the thing I will not tolerate, because I chose you, and the choosing was the end of distance, and you are trying to reinstall the distance inside the thing I chose to close.


The sentence was the truest thing he had said since the rift. Truer than the confession to Sonzai, because the confession to Sonzai had been about facts and the facts were external, the daughter, the approval, the name, the eleven years. This sentence was internal. This sentence was the man looking at the woman he had chosen and admitting that the choosing was not enough, that the love was not enough, that the cadence and the Kamigan and the Kokan blood and the iron and the silence were not enough, because enough in a relationship was not measured by what you were. It was measured by what you showed. And Sodoku had not shown Yoko the thing she needed to see, which was the entirety of the man she had given herself to, because the entirety included a daughter and a dead lover and a signed approval against his chest and a grief that was older than the grief Yoko knew about, and the older grief was the thing he had hidden, and the hiding was the lie, and the lie was the thing that had flattened her ears.

"I apologize for not telling you about Fern." He said the name. The saying was the correction. Yoko had said that other woman and Sodoku said Fern, and the saying of the name was the refusal to reduce the person to a category, and the refusal was the respect, and the respect was not for Fern. The respect was for Yoko, because Yoko deserved to hear the name of the woman who had preceded her spoken without shame, because shame about the past was a lie about the past, and Yoko did not tolerate lies. "I did not know how to carry it and carry you at the same time. She was the first person I ever loved, and the loving was real, and the real does not stop being real because a new real arrived, and the new real is you, and you are not a replacement. You are a different thing entirely. A different architecture. A different foundation. Built on different ground with different hands, and the building is the thing I am committed to, and the commitment is not the same shape as the commitment I made to Fern, because I was fifteen when I made that commitment and I am twenty-seven now and the twelve years between the two commitments are the twelve years that made me capable of understanding what I am committing to."

He pressed her hand. Not hard. The pressure of contact, not the pressure of persuasion.

"I am going to commit myself to you, Yoko. Not because the committing erases what came before. Because the committing is the thing that comes after, and the after is the thing I am choosing, and the choosing is you."

Her ears moved.

The movement was small. A fraction of a degree, the left ear lifting from the skull by the width of a single hair-shaft, the auricular equivalent of a door opening a crack, and the crack was the beginning of the processing's next phase, the phase where the body began accepting what the mind had not yet finished evaluating. The tail remained wrapped. The ears were beginning to lift. The two signals were in disagreement, and the disagreement was Yoko, the body running two assessments simultaneously and arriving at two different preliminary conclusions, and the conclusions would converge eventually because Yoko's conclusions always converged, but the convergence required time, and time was the thing Sodoku needed to give without rushing.

Her nose flared. Once. Reading him. Reading the specific olfactory signature of a man who was telling the truth, because the truth had a smell, and the smell was different from the smell of performance, and Yoko's nose could distinguish between the two the way a jeweler distinguished between gold and gilt, by the weight of the thing when it sat on the tongue of the assessment.

*He is not lying. The biochemistry is consistent with genuine emotional disclosure, not with the managed honesty he uses when he is governing. The cortisol is elevated but stable, which means the stress is old, not acute. He has been carrying this for long enough that the carrying has become baseline. The oxytocin signature is directed, not diffuse, which means the attachment is specific, not performed. He loves me. He also loved her. The two facts are not in competition. They are sequential. And the sequence is the thing I can accept, because sequences are honest. Simultaneities are the things I cannot accept, and this is not a simultaneity. This is a man who loved one woman and then loved another, and the and-then is the architecture, and the architecture is sound.*

Her tail loosened. A fraction. The self-wrapping eased by a single coil, the body's preliminary acceptance arriving before the mind's, the way the body's acceptance always arrived first in Yoko's system, because Yoko's system was beastkin, and the beastkin system trusted the nose before it trusted the words, and the nose had rendered its verdict, and the verdict was: he is telling the truth, and the truth is enough, and enough is the word that this family uses when other families would use the word everything.

"As long as you promise me something."

Her voice was still quiet. Still precise. But the precision had shifted from the register of accusation to the register of negotiation, and the shift was the concession, and the concession was Yoko's way of saying: I am choosing to move forward, and the forward requires a condition, and the condition is the thing that will make the forward possible, and the thing is this:

"You will bring her back to us."

The word us.

Not me. Not you. Us. The word that contained Yoko and Sodoku and the life they were building and the space inside that life that Yoko was opening for a child she had never met, a child who was not hers, a child who carried another woman's blood and another woman's name and another woman's jaw, and the opening was the word us, and the word us was the thing that Yoko was offering, and the offering was larger than forgiveness, because forgiveness was about the past and the offering was about the future, and the future was the thing Yoko was choosing to build with room inside it for an eleven-year-old girl whose existence she had learned about an hour ago.

"I want to give that little girl the life she deserves."

The sentence landed in Sodoku's chest and stayed there.

It stayed there because the sentence was the thing he had not known he needed to hear until the hearing happened, and the happening rearranged the interior of his chest the way Yoko's hands rearranged the interior of his gambeson, by repairing the thing that had been opened, by closing the seam that the carrying had split, by placing the stitch where the stitch needed to be and pulling the thread until the thread held and the holding was permanent.

Sodoku smiled.

The smile was rare. Not the Moto deadpan's asymmetric twitch, not the fractional movement that preceded humour or threat. A smile. A real one. The expression that his face produced when the face was not governing and not commanding and not performing the hundred functions that a Kokan prince performed, the expression that belonged to the man underneath the functions, the man who was twenty-seven and sitting in an igloo on an ice sheet and holding the hand of a fox-spirit woman whose ears were slowly rising and whose tail was slowly loosening and whose heart was on a child she had never met.

"I promise."

Two words. The same weight as Afasoa's oath. The same compression. The same frontier economy of a man who had learned that the most important promises were the ones that fit inside the smallest containers, because the smallest containers held the most, and the most was the word that Yoko deserved, and the word was the promise, and the promise was: I will bring her home, and the home will include you, and the including is the thing that makes the home a home rather than a house, because a house is a structure and a home is a family, and a family is the thing we are building, and the building has room for an eleven-year-old girl who has been waiting for the room her entire life without knowing the room existed.

Yoko's ears lifted. Both of them. The full ascent, from flat-against-skull to upright-and-forward, the beastkin's signal of engagement, of acceptance, of the emotional sequence completing its biological architecture and arriving at its conclusion, which was: the processing is finished, and the conclusion is forward, and forward is the direction we move.

She picked up the needle.

The repair resumed. The stitching was tight. The thread was waxed. The gambeson would hold. The holding was the love, and the love was in the repair, and the repair was Yoko, and Yoko was the thing that Sodoku had found after Fern and that was not a replacement and not a correction and not a second attempt but a different architecture entirely, built on different ground, with different hands, in a different season of a man's life, and the season was the one where the man understood what he was building and why, and the why was: because the building is the only answer to the ruin, and the ruin was the past, and the past is behind us, and behind us is the only direction we do not walk.

Borin's forge sang.

The sound was not music. The sound was metal. Iron meeting iron in the specific frequency that meant the aethersmith was working, and the working was Borin's natural state, the condition he returned to the way other men returned to sleep, by necessity and by preference and by the specific Dawi understanding that the body at rest was a body wasting heat, and wasted heat was wasted work, and wasted work was the one sin the Dawi recognized.

The makeshift forge was a thing of ugly ingenuity. Borin had constructed it from the materials the ice sheet provided, which was nothing, and from the materials he had carried, which was everything, and the everything was contained in a leather roll that weighed more than Afasoa and that Borin carried on his back without complaint because the carrying was the forge and the forge was the function and the function was not negotiable. The bellows were hand-operated, the fuel was a combination of rendered fat and the specific alchemical accelerant that Borin had mixed from materials Matheus had provided, and the heat was sufficient for repair work if not for primary forging, and the repair work was what mattered, because the repair work was Kurosetsu.

Sodoku's blade sat on the improvised anvil, a slab of ironwood that Borin had brought aboard the ship for exactly this purpose and that had been carried across the ice sheet by two men who had been instructed by Borin that the carrying was important and who had believed the instruction because Borin's instructions about metal and wood were believed the way Yoko's nose was believed, as a primary source that did not require secondary confirmation.

Borin's hands moved across the blade's surface. Reading. The hands read before the eyes, in the Dawi tradition, the fingertips processing the metal's grain and temperature and stress-history the way Yoko's nose processed scent, at a resolution that non-Dawi could not replicate and that Borin could not explain, because explaining the reading was like explaining breathing, a description of a process that operated beneath the level of conscious instruction and that became worse when the consciousness attempted to instruct it.

He grunted. The grunt was the diagnosis. The metal was sound. The inscriptions along the blade's spine were holding, the Aetheric empowerment stable within the tolerances that the original forging had established. The blade did not need repair. The blade needed what Borin called tuning, which was the specific adjustment of the inscription array's resonance to match the wielder's current Essence signature, because the wielder's signature changed over time, changed with each stage advancement, changed with each year of use, and a blade that was tuned to the wielder's signature at the time of forging became gradually less efficient as the wielder outgrew the tuning, the way a coat that was fitted at twenty became tight at twenty-seven, not because the coat had shrunk but because the body had changed.

*The boy has outgrown the blade's calibration by two stages. Maybe three. The inscription array is still functional but the resonance lag is costing him between six and nine percent efficiency on every channeled technique, and six-to-nine percent is the difference between a clean technique and a technique that bleeds heat at the edges, and bleeding heat is bleeding Essence, and bleeding Essence is bleeding life, and the boy cannot afford to bleed life because the boy is going home and home is where the bleeding gets expensive.*

Borin set down the hammer. Wiped his hands on the apron, which was the gesture that preceded social interaction, because social interaction required clean hands in the Dawi tradition, not as a matter of hygiene but as a matter of respect, the specific acknowledgment that the person you were about to speak to deserved the same quality of attention that the metal had been receiving, and the attention required the hands to be clean, because dirty hands were distracted hands, and distracted hands were disrespectful hands.

He walked to the igloo where the Commander sat.

The walking was Borin's walking, which was the negotiation between a Dawi frame and a surface that was not stone, each step a small argument between the boots and the ice that the boots won by force of habit rather than by grace. He stopped at the entrance. Did not push through the flap. The not-pushing was the protocol. The Commander's igloo was the Commander's space, and the space was entered by invitation, and the invitation was requested by standing at the entrance and waiting, and the waiting was the asking, and the asking was Dawi, and the Dawi form of asking was: I am here, and the here-ness is the request, and the request does not require words because words are slower than presence, and presence is the Dawi's primary communication tool.

"Commander." Borin's voice carried through the flap with the dense, compressed quality that a forge-room produced in every voice that spent decades inside it. "A word, when you have one to spare."

He waited. The waiting was patient. Borin was always patient, because patience was the primary virtue of a man whose craft required him to wait for metal to reach the correct temperature before striking, and the waiting-for-temperature was the waiting-for-everything, and the everything included the Commander's attention.

When the flap moved and Sonzai's face appeared, Borin looked up at the Commander with the specific assessment that the aethersmith applied to every material he worked with, which was: I am reading your condition, and the reading is professional, and the professional is the respect.

"Those chains."

Two words. The Dawi opening. No preamble. No social lubrication. The thing, stated, the way metal was stated, by being placed on the anvil.

"I have been watching them since the ship. The way they move. The way they respond to your Essence field. The inscriptions along the links are old. Older than anything I have worked with, and I have worked with things that predate the Guild Seat's founding." His hands, cleaned and still, hung at his sides, and the stillness was the presentation, the smith offering his hands as instruments the way Nella had offered hers at the oath. "I do not know who forged them. I do not know what tradition the inscription language belongs to. I know that the empowerment is running at a fraction of what the metal can sustain, because the metal is better than the empowerment, and better metal with inferior inscription is like a warhorse wearing a saddle built for a pony. The horse still runs. The horse runs badly."

He folded his arms. The folding was the proposal's frame, the physical arrangement that preceded the offering.

"My job is to make sure all of you are properly equipped. That is the oath I gave. The oath included the word hammer and the word forge and the word yours, and the yours includes your chains, Commander." He paused. The pause was the smith assessing whether the next sentence would be received as offer or as criticism, and deciding that the distinction was the Commander's to make, not the smith's to manage. "I would like to improve them. Not change them. Not reforge them. Improve. Expand the Aetheric empowerment to match what the metal can actually hold. Bring the inscription array into alignment with your current signature rather than whatever signature you were carrying when the chains were last tuned, which was, based on the resonance lag I am reading from six paces away, a very long time ago."

His eyes, the grey-green of unpolished malachite, held the Commander's purple ones with the specific directness of a craftsman who was not asking permission to exist but asking permission to perform his function, and the function was the thing, and the thing was the chains, and the chains were the Commander's, and the Commander's things were the aethersmith's responsibility, and the responsibility was the oath, and the oath was the metal, and the metal did not break.

"I have never seen anything like them before," Borin said. "That is not flattery. That is a professional assessment. Whatever they are, they deserve better than they are currently receiving, and better is the thing I do."

He unfolded his arms.

Waited.

The waiting was the asking, and the asking was Dawi, and the Dawi form of asking was: I am here, and my hands are clean, and my forge is hot, and the work is the only thing I know how to offer, and the offering is everything.

# The Truth of Muken

The question lay on the low table where the Sovereign had set it, between the clay cups and the single lamp, and for a long moment nobody on the western side would pick it up.

Under the floor the stream went on talking to itself against the foundation posts. Somewhere at the back of the hall a lamp-wick needed trimming and was spitting about it, a small dry tick every few breaths, and the smoke from it went straight up in the still air and flattened against the beams. The Yukari built their long halls of cedar over limestone footings, and the whole room smelled of both, clean wood and cold stone, with the steam off the tea gone thin and grassy as it cooled.

Kiyome Byakuya sat with her hands open on her knees. The white of her sleeves had picked up a gray line of road dust at the cuffs that had not been there when she came in, a long smear where she had braced her forearm on the northern gatepost dismounting, and she had not brushed it off. Her sword lay on the mat at her right side with its hilt toward her and the white knot of her house bound around the guard. Her eyes went to the knot once. Then they came up, pale gray and very steady, and settled on the Sovereign of Kushara.

"You asked whether the boy is why we came," she said. "He is. You have been honest with us, Sovereign, more honest than our own council has been in years, and I won't sit in this house and answer you with half of it."

She drew a breath and let it go.

"The truth of Muken is this. He is my nephew. He is the son of my sister, Kasane Byakuya, who took the name Yukari at her Telling and never gave it back." A pause, short and exact. "And he is the direct son of Gimbzo Kōkan Moto."

Tetsugen Akagane's head came up off his chest as though someone had pulled a cord through the back of his neck.

He was a heavy man gone heavier with age, the kind of broad that had been muscle once and was now mostly the memory of it, and he had been sitting since his bow with his chin down and his scarred hands folded on his belt over the horn handle of his meat knife, the only steel he had carried through the gate. Now the knuckles on those hands had gone white. Beside him, Hayato Amagiri had been halfway through lifting his cup, and the cup stopped where it was, an inch off the table, with the tea in it trembling in a fine ring.

Neither man said anything. Neither of them had to.

*And she lays it on a Zettari table like a coin.*

That was Tetsugen's thought, and it was on his face for anyone with eyes, and he did not trouble to hide it. Sixteen years Kagenori and his kept that name off every tongue south of the Seat, and Tetsugen had known it the way you know a draught is coming from somewhere, without once going to look. Hayato's face did something more careful. He set the cup down without a sound, turned it a quarter on the wood so the glaze crack faced away from him, and looked at Kiyome the way a man looks at a ford he has crossed a hundred times and has just watched swallow a cart.

Across the table, Naiser Yukari had gone very still.

Kiyome had given her sister two houses in a single breath, and the second was his own. He did not move. He did not look at his sister. But the corona at the edge of his pupils, violet-black even at rest, brightened by the width of a hair, and the Quiet Weight of the house came into the room the way it always did when a Yukari stopped breathing to listen: every other person present found themselves leaning a little toward him without knowing why.

Beside him Naevra had lowered her eyes to the table. She was not reading, not in any way a guest could have objected to. She did not need to. A Yukari of her line saw some of the threads at rest the way a weaver sees the warp on a loom she is not working, and she had been watching Kiyome's since the woman sat down. When a person speaks a thing they hold to be true, the thread of the claim settles. It lies down along the speaker's commitment the way a plumb line settles under its own weight, and nothing in the room tugs at it. A lie does the opposite: it has to be held taut against what the speaker knows, and it hums. Every thread off Kiyome Byakuya had lain flat and still as she spoke.

Kiyome let the silence stand a moment longer. Then she went on, and her voice was lower.

"The truth is I should have been there for him." She looked at her hands. "Kasane wouldn't have it. The night she went, a runner of our house brought me four lines in her hand. I was to stay behind with our house. I was needed at home more than I'd ever be needed at her side. Yusif Stark would keep her and the boy safe." Her fingers moved on her knee, as if they were still holding the paper. "And then she was gone, and I never found her after. Not once, in all the years I looked." The corner of her mouth moved, not quite a smile. "Maybe she knew how it would end. She could always see further down a road than the rest of us. She saw things coming that the rest of us didn't believe until they were standing in the door."

She was quiet for a breath.

"She tried to make Gimbzo understand. For years, she tried. But after his mother died there was no more reasoning with him. Whatever had been between the two of them, the love, the patience, all of it, it went out like a fire nobody fed." Her hands closed on her knees. "She died the morning the news of Shimane came down to her, the Starks say, of the salt-throat that takes the cutters. I think she had been dying of him for years before anyone ever killed him. The war broke her family in half, and she was the one standing in the crack when it opened."

The lamp at the back of the hall ticked. Under the floor the stream went on.

Kiyome lifted her head.

"But that is our own fault." She said it to the Sovereign, and then her eyes moved past him to the young man at his side and held there. "The Moto let our pride tell us we could dictate the future of everyone. Every people, every road, every house under the Sky. And when we met people we couldn't control, we tried to destroy them." She did not look away from Dougou. "Yours were a people we failed to control. You know better than anyone in this hall what we did about it."

Tetsugen made a sound in his chest like a stone settling in a riverbed.

"I never liked the Tenrai."

He said it to the table at first. Then he lifted his eyes and said it again, to the Sovereign, in the flat iron voice of a man who had given orders on fields where nobody could hear anything gentler.

"I never liked them. Not when Gimbzo's father was alive, and not after. They were the ones in his ear every night once his mother was in the ground. Filled his head with the road to ruin and called it destiny, and he drank it because grief makes a man thirsty for anything that tastes like a reason." His jaw worked. "And they did well out of it. The Tenrai were one of the families that grew fat on this war. Fat on the suffering of their own people as much as yours. Every year the levies went up and every year the Tenrai granaries got deeper. Kagenori sits on a larder and calls it a seat."

He turned his whole body on the cushion, slowly, until he faced the Sovereign square.

"You asked me what changed my mind. I'll give you the truth, since you said you'd rather have that from a man who's made terrible decisions than a polished apology." The scarred hands came off the knife and lay flat on his thighs. "Nothing honorable changed it. I'd like to sit here and tell you I woke one morning with the Sennen road on my conscience. I didn't. I supported that campaign. I sat in council and argued for it. My granaries fed the columns that went down that road, and I signed the provisioning orders myself, in my own hand, with my own seal, and I knew what those columns would do to every settlement standing between them and the river. I knew, and I signed."

Hayato shut his eyes for half a breath.

"What changed me was that the Tenrai learned the lesson I helped teach them." Tetsugen's voice did not rise. "Three winters ago they withheld seed grain from the Akagane tenancies along the Kurogane valley because my eldest spoke against a levy in council. One winter. Just one. I buried forty-one of my own tenants that spring, most of them under twelve, and I stood at the edge of the pit and thought of nothing but the Sennen road. That's what turned me. The same bill I helped write for your people came to my own door with my own name on it."

He looked past the Sovereign, then, to the son.

"Prince. You said saying you've had enough is easy in somebody else's house." He inclined his heavy head, an inch, no more. "You're right. It is. So I'll say it again in mine. There's a proverb in the north: wet wood burns eventually, and a liar never dries. I've been wet wood for forty years. It took me that long to catch. I don't ask you to think well of the fire."

His hands went inside his coat and came out with a packet wrapped in oiled cloth and tied with black cord, the size of a small ledger. He set it on the table in front of him and did not push it across.

"The Akagane muster rolls and provisioning books for the Sennen campaign. Names of every commander who took grain from my stores. Names of the settlements on the route, in our own clerks' hands, with the dates we marked them cleared." His thumb rested on the cord. "Kushara can have them for its records. You'll know who did what, and you won't have to take my word on any of it." He paused. "If Kagenori ever gets his hands on a copy, it's enough to hang me in the Seat's square, so I'd ask that your clerks keep it closer than mine did. That's the first thing I'm prepared to lose. The second is simpler. The Akagane won't answer Kagenori's next muster. When the call comes, my levies stay on their land. Which means when he wants an example, he'll come to the Kurogane valley first." He sat back. "He's welcome to try."

Hayato let out a breath through his nose.

"This is true." He said it quietly, nodding at the packet, and then lifted his eyes to the Sovereign. "All of it. And yet everything the Sovereign has said is also true. Every word."

He was the youngest of the three, a few years under Kiyome and a whole generation under Tetsugen, lean in the way of men who spent their lives in the saddle, with a weathered, narrow face and a horseman's squint that never quite left the corners of his eyes even indoors. The rain-cape he had not taken off was still dark across the shoulders where the snowmelt had soaked through on the northern approach, and a sprig of blue-black juniper had caught in the toggle at his throat and stayed there.

"We can't afford to pull our families out from under the Tenrai so easily," he said. "That's the knot of it, Byakuya-dono, and you know it as well as I do. There are men in all three of our houses who eat from Kagenori's stores now. Their children are fed from Tenrai grain because ours ran out. Tell them their lords have walked out of council and they'll go hungry for it, and see how long their loyalty lasts. We need something to hold them that's older than a granary." He turned his cup another quarter. "We need Muken to step up."

Then he turned to the Sovereign, and his voice took on the plain working cadence of a man discussing distances.

"You asked about the roads, Sovereign. Whether they're truly closed, or closed only to people without the knowledge to go round. I'll answer that honestly, because it's the one thing I'm good for." He touched the table with two fingers, drawing a line. "The main roads out of Kharven are closed to carts, closed to commons, and closed to anyone without a Tenrai pass. The Hiekawa cut and the old salt road over the Shiogane shoulder are open, because the Tenrai's own smugglers use them to sell Moto grain to the hill clans at four times the price, and they don't want them watched. They know the cut as far as their mules walk it and not a yard to either side. The side tracks off it are mine. That's how we came: up the cut, off it at a herders' shoulder the smugglers have never had a reason to find, and down a deer track onto your own north hill. That's how I can bring grain in." He drew a second line beside the first. "A mule carries two sacks in snow. The cut takes thirty mules abreast at its widest and single file at its narrowest. It's a feeding road, too narrow for an army."

His mouth tightened.

"And no, it doesn't help the families we've described as things stand. It helps nobles leaving in a hurry. You're right about that too. The only way it helps a village in the southern valleys is if somebody who isn't Moto stands at both ends of it counting sacks."

He looked down the table, past the Sovereign, to the two Yukari.

"So here's what I'm prepared to lose. Those side roads are all my house has that the Tenrai don't. The Tenrai can have the cut and welcome; it's what runs off it that they've never found, and that's why the Amagiri still have a seat at all. I'll give them up. Every pass, every ford, every hut with a woodpile in it between the Seat and the southern border, drawn out for whoever you name as witness. The day Kushara's grain goes up the Hiekawa cut and off it onto my tracks with Yukari eyes on it, those tracks stop being mine. Kagenori will learn them inside a season, and he'll close them, and after that I'm just another lord who knows the weather." He lifted one shoulder. "Better it carries grain for one winter than smugglers for twenty."

He hesitated, and the hesitation was honest.

"And since you asked who we speak for. I'll not pretend. Byakuya-dono speaks for her house, and Lord Tetsugen speaks for his. I speak for the Amagiri roads and the twenty riders I brought. My uncle will sit the indigo chair for the Amagiri when Kagenori calls the full council, and he'll sit it at Kagenori's left hand in everything but the furniture. I don't speak for him, and he will not thank me for any of this."

*There. Said. Let the Zettari weigh a man who can't promise his own table.*

Kiyome had been listening with her eyes on the white knot at her sword. When she lifted them, they went first to Hayato, and something in them was very tired.

"But we cannot expect a child who doesn't even know his own bloodline to fix all of our problems." She said it gently, which made it heavier. "Muken is sixteen years old. He's a mercenary. He sleeps where he's paid to and he fights whoever the coin tells him to, and nobody alive has ever sat him down and told him what he is. You want him to step up, Hayato. Step up onto what? A crown he's never heard of, held out to him by the same families who killed his father's line and his mother's peace?" She shook her head, once. "Nor can we expect the Zettari to fix them. They didn't break us. We did that ourselves, with both hands."

She turned back to the Sovereign.

"You warned us against putting our ambitions on a boy's shoulders and calling it destiny. You said it better than I would have. I'll hold you to it, and I'll hold myself to it first." Her hand moved to the white knot and rested on it. "The Byakuya are the white line. For as long as there have been Moto kings, we have been sworn to the red: to keep the Kōkan heir, to hold his seat when he can't, and to put him on it when he can. That oath is why I'm alive. It is the whole reason my house exists." Her fingers lay flat on the knot. "So here is what I am prepared to lose. I'll go to Muken myself. I'll tell him everything I told you, and everything I didn't, and I will not ask him for a single thing. If he says no, if he wants nothing to do with the Moto and their crown and their thousand years of blood, then the Byakuya will serve him anyway, as a mercenary, in a mercenary's tent, and the crown my house kept for him will go to nobody." She took her hand off the knot. "That's the oath, read the only honest way. It binds me to the boy. It doesn't bind the boy to us."

Naevra lifted her eyes from the table.

She had not spoken since the delegation came in, and when she did, she spoke to Kiyome first, softly, in the old courteous register of the house, before turning the same words toward the Sovereign.

"My house was asked that same question this week, Lady Kiyome. About me." She folded her hands. "Whether what a bloodline needs is a thing the person carrying it has agreed to. I've had the word destiny said at me a great many times in the last few days, by people who love me and some who don't." A small pause. "I've come to think fate is only arrogant because nobody ever taught it manners. Yours is a good answer. I hope he hears it from you before he hears the other version from Kagenori."

Then she turned to the Sovereign.

"You spoke of the southern springs. Of whether they can be put right, and whether the work can be done without being made into somebody's leash." She laid one long hand on the table, palm down. "They can be read. Water keeps a record of everything poured into it. Your own well-diggers know half of this already: a poison in groundwater travels with the flow underground, spreading as it goes, so that the further you are from where it went in, the fainter and wider it runs. Walk upstream against it and the taint thickens until you're standing on the place it entered. What my house adds is Anamnesis. The Wellspring of memory holds the record of what the water has carried, and a Yukari who reads it can tell you what went in, when, and whether it is still going in. We can walk the southern springs from the mouth to the source and tell you where the harm is, and whose hand is on it." She looked at Kiyome. "But we need to walk them. All of them. Without escort, without a Tenrai factor waiting at the next spring with a story ready. If anyone tells us where we may not go, we stop, and we tell everyone at this table why we stopped."

Naiser spoke then.

He had let every word in the hall run its full length before he moved, the way his house had always done, and when he spoke the room came down to meet him.

"Lady Kiyome. You said your sister took the name Yukari."

"I did."

"That name is ours."

"She heard the Telling in this valley when she was fourteen," Kiyome said. "She sat in front of Senri's stone and listened to the whole account of what the name costs, and then she asked for it. Your house gave it to her. She never once stopped answering to it." Her eyes did not leave his. "I assumed your archive would remember. I thought you might not have read that page."

"I hadn't." Naiser was quiet a moment. "Then the boy is owed something from this house as well, and he doesn't know that either."

He turned to the Sovereign.

"You told me to draw a line between hospitality and commitment before somebody else draws it for us. I'll draw it now, in front of everyone, so it can be quoted back to me." He set both hands flat on the table. "The Yukari are hosts. We are not allies to any side present. We do not lend soldiers, we do not recognise governments, and we do not sell what we see. What we do is witness. A crow perches. A crow watches. A crow remembers. That is the lawful function this house was left alone to perform, and it is the only one I will put our name to."

He lifted one hand from the table.

"So. If Kushara purchases grain, and House Vokari consents to keep the accounts, the Yukari will stand at both ends of Lord Hayato's road and count every sack that goes up it and every mouth it reaches. If the Byakuya and the Akagane open their granaries to the same count, we'll count those too. Naevra will walk the southern springs and put her finding before every house here, whatever it says. And every promise made in this hall today is going into our record, word for word, with the name of the one who made it. If any of it is broken, by anyone, it'll be the Yukari who say so out loud." He let the pause finish. "Including the promises made by Kushara. I'd ask the Sovereign to agree to that plainly, since it binds his house the same as theirs."

He looked at Kiyome again, and something in the Quiet Weight shifted.

"And I'll tell you now, since you have been honest with my house: your sister's name makes this harder for me. A witness is meant to stand outside the thing he's watching. It seems I have a cousin in it I didn't know about." He inclined his head to her. "I'll keep the line anyway. But you should know it's there, and that it cost something to keep."

Kiyome bowed from the waist, low, her forehead nearly to the cedar, and held it a long breath before she came up.

When she straightened she turned back to the far side of the table, to the young man sitting beside his father.

"Prince Dougou." Her voice was quite level. "You said your people are starving too, and that you won't pretend their suffering went away because ours got too loud to ignore. I'm not asking you to. I'd think less of you if you did." She folded her hands in her lap, over the gray line of road dust on her sleeve. "You asked what we're prepared to lose. You've heard it. Lord Tetsugen's books and his valley. Lord Hayato's roads. My house's oath, and the crown it was built to keep. None of that buys your dead back. I know it doesn't."

She looked at him for a long moment, and the white knot lay still at her side.

"So when the Yukari go up the Hiekawa cut to count our graves, I'd ask you to send someone of your own with them. Somebody who'll count the small ones. Somebody who hates us enough to count them honestly." A breath. "Better still, come yourself."

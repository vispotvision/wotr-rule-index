# The Turtle's Boy

## Chapter One: The Way of Slow Things

Xanelor Rafiminar.

What did that name mean? What did it mean to anyone?

Nothing, was the honest answer. Not to the traders who passed the island twice a year with their salt and iron nails. Not to the fisherfolk on the northern strand who knew the boy only as *the turtle's ward*, a pale and too-thin child hauling driftwood up a hill that no human should have bothered settling. Not to the world beyond the fog bank, which did not know the island existed and would not have cared if it did.

The name meant something to exactly two people. One of them was six years old and still mispronounced it. The other was very, very old, and had given it.


The island was called Omoro by the people who fished its shallows, and nothing at all by anyone else. It sat three days south of the nearest charted coast, a crooked tooth of volcanic rock softened by centuries of moss and rain. The jungle ate everything that held still long enough. Wooden posts. Stone walls. The ruins of something ancient and nameless along the eastern ridge whose purpose even Drogo had forgotten, or claimed to have forgotten, which with Drogo amounted to the same thing.

The hut sat halfway up the mountain's leeward slope, where the wind broke against a shelf of basalt and the rain fell at an angle that kept the cooking pit dry. It was not a good hut. Drogo had built it for a body shaped nothing like a human child's. The doorway was four feet wide and three feet tall. Fine for a creature who carried his home on his back and entered every room by pushing through it. Less fine for a boy who cracked his forehead on the lintel each morning until he learned not to stand up straight.

The garden was better. Drogo's garden was the most disciplined thing on Omoro. Twelve raised beds of black volcanic soil, each one composted with kelp and fish bones and turned by claw. Those massive blunt claws could split a coconut or lift a boulder, but they moved through the herb rows with a surgeon's restraint. Sage, wild garlic, three varieties of ginger, lemongrass, a squat and ugly tea plant Drogo had been cultivating since before the boy was born. The tea was terrible. Drogo brewed it every morning without exception.

"Drink," Drogo said, the way he said it every dawn, holding out a cup the size of a human soup bowl in a hand that engulfed it entirely. His voice came from low in the chest, vibrated through the shell, and arrived unhurried, the way a tide arrives. "Good for you, it is. The body thanks what the tongue complains about."

Xanelor took the cup. He was six. He had learned not to argue with the cup.

"It tastes like dirt, Grandpa."

"Dirt." Drogo settled onto his haunches. A slow, geological process, his shell tilting backward, the moss along its upper ridge catching the first of the morning light. An arrow, old, its fletching long since rotted to a nub, still jutted from the shell's crown. A stonechat had built her nest against its shaft. She did not move when Drogo moved. She had learned the schedule. "Dirt, the boy says. And what is dirt?"

"It's... dirt."

"Everything that ever lived," Drogo said. He blinked, one heavy lid descending and rising with the patience of a season turning. "Trees that stood a thousand years. Creatures who loved their young. Leaves that caught the last sun of autumn and held it as long as they were able. All returned. All become the soil. You drink their memory, boy. Little respect, hm?"

Xanelor sipped. It still tasted like dirt.

He did not say so.


Drogo was, in the most literal and immediate sense, a turtle.

Not a man who resembled a turtle. Not a spirit wearing a turtle's shape. A turtle, or something so close to one that the distinction had ceased to matter somewhere in the deep backward of years. He stood when he chose to, which was not often. Upright, he reached perhaps five feet at the crown of his shell, though his neck could extend another foot when he wished to examine something on a high shelf or make a point. His forelimbs were arms the way a blacksmith's tongs are fingers. They gripped, they manipulated, they gestured with slow precision, but they had been built for a different purpose and the adaptation showed. His skin was the grey-green of lichen on wet stone, creased and thick, cool to the touch in a way that made Xanelor press his face against Drogo's forearm on the nights when fever found the boy. The shell was a landscape. Moss grew in its grooves. A seam near the left shoulder plate had been cracked and healed so many times it looked like river clay. The arrow had been there longer than Xanelor had been alive, and when the boy had once asked about it, Drogo had said only: "A reminder, it is. That even the patient may be found by someone less so."

He walked with a staff. A piece of driftwood that had been a living tree once, salt-bleached and smooth, its head a gnarled fist of root wood that fit Drogo's grip as though it had grown to meet him. Perhaps it had. Drogo said things about trees that made it difficult to be certain.

Around his neck, on a cord of braided sea-grass, hung a pendant: a small copper hammer, green with age, its head no larger than a child's thumbnail.

Xanelor had asked about the pendant too.

"Belonged to someone who built things," Drogo had said. "Gone now. The hammer remains. Hm. Funny thing, that. The builder passes. What was built endures. But what endures longest is the tool that did the building. Think on this when you are older."

Xanelor had not thought on it. He was six.


The trouble began on a morning in the wet season, when Xanelor came back from the tide pools with blood on his knees and fury in his throat. As Drogo would have said: the lesson presented itself.

Three boys from the fishing village. Older, all of them. Eight, nine, ten. They had found Xanelor poking through the shallows for the sea cucumbers Drogo used in his broths, and they had decided, in the way that boys decide such things, that the turtle's ward was a suitable target for the afternoon. The details were ordinary. Shoving. A rock thrown, not quite at him but not quite past him either. *Shell-boy. Freak-child. Why don't you crawl back under your rock.* Words that were ordinary too, and cut precisely because of it.

Xanelor had not fought back. He did not know how.

He came up the hill with his fists balled and his jaw set and his eyes dry, because he had decided somewhere between the tide pools and the garden that he would not cry about it. He stood in front of Drogo, who was turning soil in the third bed with a focus that suggested the soil had personally offended him, and he said:

"Teach me to fight."

Drogo did not look up.

"Grandpa. Teach me to fight."

Drogo pressed a clove of wild garlic into the loosened earth, covered it with the back of one claw, and patted it down with the gentleness of someone putting a child to sleep.

"Why?" he said.

"Because they... because I need to... because I couldn't..."

"Hm." Drogo sat back. The shell scraped against the basalt shelf behind him, a sound like a mill wheel turning. He regarded the boy with one eye, the left, which was darker than the right and saw, Xanelor sometimes thought, rather more. "Too young to understand how to fight, you are. Yet you want the tools to protect what you cannot."

Xanelor blinked. "I just want to hit them back."

"Yes. That is what I said." Drogo reached up and adjusted the stonechat's nest with one claw, gently, without disturbing the bird. "Sit."

Xanelor sat. He did not want to sit. He sat anyway, because Drogo had a way of making the word *sit* sound like the ground itself was asking.

"The fist," Drogo said, "answers the question that was asked of it. Someone pushes, the fist pushes back. Simple. Satisfying. Wrong."

"How is it wrong?"

"Because the question was wrong." Drogo extended one thick arm and pointed at the garden. "This garlic. Planted it, I did, six months ago. Harvested it last week. Planted it again today. Does it fight the soil?"

"No..."

"Does the rain fight the sea, when to the sea it returns? Does the mountain fight the wind that wears it, hmm?" The old turtle shook his head, a motion that moved his entire upper body. "The strong thing remains, boy. The thing that strikes spends itself against what outlasts it."

"But they *hit* me, Grandpa."

"Yes." Drogo picked up his tea. He drank. He set it down. The pause was long enough that a second stonechat landed on his shell, considered the nest, and departed. "And here you are. Remaining. Hm. Funny thing, that."

Xanelor opened his mouth to argue and found that he had nothing to say.

Drogo flicked the boy's forehead with one blunt claw. Not hard. Just enough.

"Listen now, and the listening itself will teach you," the old turtle said. "Gifted with magic, you may not be. But understand this. The gift itself, *power over nature it is not*. Nature speaking through a mouth that learned to ask, that is what it is." He tapped the earth beside him. "All things exist in the flow. The river does not try to be the river. Trying, it would drown itself, hm?"

Xanelor rubbed his forehead. "You always talk like this."

"Because understanding, you have not yet arrived at. When you do, repeat myself I will not need to." Drogo's mouth did something that, on a human face, would have been a smile. On a turtle's face it was more of a continental shift. "Now. Weed the fourth bed. Hands only. No tools."

"That's not fighting."

"No," Drogo agreed pleasantly. "It is not."


Xanelor weeded the fourth bed.

He weeded it the next day, and the day after, and every day that wet season until his fingers were stained green to the second knuckle and he could tell crabgrass from purslane by touch alone in the dark.

He did not understand what he was learning. He would not understand for seven years.


The island had moods.

This was not poetry. This was something Drogo said plainly, the way he said that the tide was in or the tea was ready, and Xanelor learned to believe it the way he believed those things. Not because he understood the claim, but because Drogo had never been wrong about the tide or the tea.

"Feel it, do you?" Drogo asked one evening, when the boy was seven and the sky had gone the colour of a bruise and the wind had dropped to nothing in a way that made the silence loud. "Omoro is thinking."

"Islands don't think."

"Hm. And turtles don't teach. Yet here we are." Drogo was brewing his terrible tea over the cooking pit, stirring it with a stick that he held the way a calligrapher holds a brush, from the shoulder, not the wrist. "The world is not what you have been told, boy. The world is what it does when no one is telling it anything."

"Nobody told me anything. You're the only one here."

"Precisely." Drogo poured two cups. "And even I know less than the garden. Wisdom is not the collecting of answers, hm? The collecting of better questions. The fool sees the mountain and asks how tall. The sage sees the mountain and asks what it remembers."

He handed Xanelor his cup. The boy drank without complaint. He had stopped noticing the taste somewhere around the four hundredth cup.

"Grandpa," Xanelor said. "The fishing boys say your people used to live here. That there used to be more of you. On the eastern ridge, the ruins."

Drogo was quiet for a long time. The cooking fire popped. The stonechat, she had a name now, *Pīpī*, which Xanelor had given her and Drogo had accepted with a grave nod, as though the bird had been waiting for precisely that name, shifted in her nest.

"True, that is," Drogo said at last.

"What happened?"

"What happens to all things that hold still long enough." He looked at the boy, and for a moment his eyes, both of them, the dark and the light, held something that Xanelor would not have a name for until he was much older. Not sadness, exactly. Sadness implied surprise. This was the thing that came after sadness had stayed so long it became the room's furniture.

"Gone, they are. The way of things, it was. Held to the old patterns, my people did, long after the world moved on. Believed that patience alone would be enough." He tapped his shell. "Strong, this. Very strong. But the arrow finds the gap, hm? Always the arrow finds the gap."

He touched the shaft protruding from his shell. The old wound.

"Is that why you're teaching me?" Xanelor asked. "Because your people couldn't..."

"Teaching you," Drogo interrupted, and his voice was firm in the way that stone is firm, "because you are here. Reason enough, that is. Purpose comes after. Purpose always comes after."

He drank his tea.

The silence that followed was not uncomfortable. Drogo's silences never were. They had texture to them, weight and colour, like good clay. You could sit inside one and feel held rather than ignored.

Xanelor sat inside this one until the fire burned low and Drogo carried him to his sleeping mat. The boy did not remember being lifted, only the smell of moss and mineral and earth-after-rain that meant, in every language his body knew, *safe*.


By eight, Xanelor could name every plant on the island's leeward slope, set a bone splint from memory, and hold his breath underwater for four minutes. This last because Drogo's idea of swimming lessons was to wade into the cove and sit on the bottom, and if the boy wanted company he could come down and get it. He could not fight. Drogo had not mentioned fighting again.

By nine, Xanelor could read the weather three days out by the colour of the lichen on the basalt shelf, brew a tea that was merely bad rather than actively hostile, and move through the jungle without snapping a single branch. This last because Drogo had told him, once, that every broken branch was a sentence the forest would never finish, and the thought had settled into the boy's chest like a fishhook and stayed.

He still could not fight.

By ten, the fishing boys had stopped bothering him. Not because he had grown large. He hadn't. He was lean and brown and moved with a careful, considered quality that made adults uneasy in ways they could not articulate. They stopped because he had learned, without being taught it directly, the thing that Drogo had been teaching since the garlic bed.

How to remain.

They shoved; he was there. They swung; he stepped. Not away. Just *elsewhere*, the way water steps around a stone without acknowledging the stone's opinion on the matter. They grew bored. They grew uncertain. They stopped coming. One of them, the youngest, the nine-year-old who had thrown the rock four years ago, started following Xanelor to the tide pools instead of ambushing him there, and the two of them spent a whole afternoon cataloguing sea cucumbers in a silence that felt, to Xanelor, earned.

He told Drogo about it that evening.

"Hm," Drogo said. He was carving something, a piece of driftwood he'd been working for three months, and which was beginning to resemble, in a way that defied the material, a flower. "Remained, did you?"

"I just... moved. When they swung."

"Moved how?"

Xanelor thought about it. "Like you do. In the garden. Around the plants."

Drogo set down the carving. He looked at the boy for a long time. The stonechat, it was Pīpī's daughter now, the original having departed the nest the previous spring in what Drogo had called *a graduation*, sang two notes into the quiet.

"The lesson," Drogo said, "arrived."

"What lesson?"

"The one I planted when you were six, hmm." He picked up the carving again. "Told you. All things in the flow. The river does not strike the stone. But go and find the stone, in a hundred years. See what remains of it."

He blew a curl of wood from the flower's petal.

"Now," he said. "Teach you to fight, I will."

Xanelor stared at him. "You said I was too young."

"Were," Drogo agreed. "Past tense, that is. Understand it, do you? The young learn the fist. You have learned what comes before the fist. *This* is the only order that does not produce a bully." He planted the staff and rose, a process that took the better part of a minute, accompanied by sounds that suggested his joints had opinions about the schedule. "Tomorrow. Dawn. After tea."

"After the terrible tea."

"Hm. Getting better, it is."

"It is *not*."

"The tea is not what changes." Drogo's mouth shifted again, the continental smile. "The tongue learns what to forgive."


*What Drogo taught was not combat. Not at first.*

*The boy would not understand this until much later, standing over the body of someone who had tried to kill him, realizing that his hands had done nothing he had not practised in the garden a thousand times. The uproot. The transplant. The cut-and-redirect that Drogo called simply "the way of growing things." The violence had been in the other man. Xanelor had merely been the soil that decided where the seed would land.*

*But that was later. That was fourteen, and the blood, and the ship, and everything that came after the island.*

*This was still the beginning.*


Dawn. The terrible tea. The garden, and beyond it the mountain, and beyond that the sea, which did not care about any of this and was beautiful anyway.

"First lesson," Drogo said. He planted his staff. The ground acknowledged it, a faint shudder, no more, as though the basalt had been asleep and the staff had touched its shoulder. "Tell me what you see."

Xanelor looked at the old turtle. Moss on the shell. The arrow. Pīpī's daughter in the nest. The pendant, the little copper hammer, green as the sea.

"I see you, Grandpa."

"Hm." Drogo nodded, slowly, the way the moon nods across the sky. "Wrong answer. But the right place to begin."

He moved.

Xanelor did not see how. One moment Drogo was in front of him, five feet of moss-covered shell and geological patience, and the next he was beside him, and the staff had swept the boy's feet from under him so gently that landing felt like being set down.

Xanelor lay on his back, staring up at the dawn sky, and Drogo's face appeared above him, upside down, one dark eye and one light.

"What you saw," Drogo said, "was what I am. What you did not see was what I was about to do. This is the difference between knowing and understanding, hm? The knowing looks at the mountain. The understanding watches where the mountain's shadow falls."

He offered a hand. The boy took it. The grip was cool and immense and unutterably careful.

"Again," Drogo said.


*He said it four thousand times.*

*Xanelor counted.*


## Chapter Two: The Mask and the Mountain


The first thing Drogo taught him was the bow.

Not the sword. Not the staff. The bow, which Xanelor had not expected, because a bow was a thing you used from far away and Drogo had spent four years teaching him to remain.

"Hm. Think about it," Drogo said, sitting cross-legged in the clearing above the garden with a length of blackwood across his knees and a bowstring of braided sinew coiled beside him. His claws moved over the wood with that impossible delicacy, shaving curls so thin they dissolved in the breeze. "The sword. What does the sword say?"

"I don't know. Cut?"

"I am here," Drogo corrected. "The sword says: I am here, and I will not leave until this is settled. Very honest. Very brave. Very much about *you*." He held up the half-shaped bow. "The arrow says something different. The arrow says: I have seen what needs correcting, and I do not need to be present when the correction arrives. Hm? You see?"

Xanelor did not see.

"The sword is the ego," Drogo said. He was patient about this the way he was patient about everything, which was to say absolutely and without visible effort. "The bow is the eye. Train the eye first, and it will tell the hand what to do. Train the hand first, and it will never learn to listen."

He held out the unfinished bow. The wood was warm. Xanelor could feel the grain under his fingers, tight and clean, sanded by claw-work finer than any blade.

"Yours," Drogo said. "Finish it."

"I don't know how."

"The wood knows. Ask the wood."

Xanelor stared at him.

"With the knife, boy. Ask the wood with the knife."


It took Xanelor three weeks to finish the bow. The first attempt split. The second warped. The third held, and Drogo examined it for a long time, turning it in his massive hands, sighting down its length with one dark eye, and said: "Adequate."

From Drogo, this was the highest praise Xanelor had ever received.

The arrows came next. Then the shooting. Then the long, humbling weeks of shooting at coconuts from thirty paces and hitting the tree behind them, the tree beside them, and on one memorable afternoon the roof of the hut, which Drogo repaired without comment while Xanelor stood beside him wishing the mountain would swallow him.

"Hm. The roof needed fixing anyway," Drogo said. "Grateful, you should be. Found the problem before the rain did."

"I put an arrow through it."

"Problem-finding. Different methods, same result." The continental smile. "Again. Tomorrow."


The Sage Arts were not magic. Drogo was clear about this from the start, the way he was clear about the things that mattered, which was to say once and without raising his voice.

"Magic, a gift it is. Given or not given. You were not given." He held up one claw. "But the world was given to you. Same as to everyone. The sage does not bend the world. The sage pays attention until the world bends itself, and then, hm, he is standing in the right place."

The breathing came first. Hours of it, cross-legged in the garden at dawn, matching his breath to Drogo's, which was so slow that Xanelor's vision greyed at the edges before he found the rhythm. Then the listening. Eyes closed, naming every sound on the mountain slope from the garden to the shore, in order of distance, until he could hear the tide-line crabs clicking over wet stone from half a mile up the hill.

Then the stillness. Which was the hardest thing.

"Move nothing," Drogo said. "Nothing at all. For one hour."

"I can do that."

He could not do that. He lasted nine minutes the first day, fourteen the second, and twenty-two the third before his left knee betrayed him. By the end of the first month he could hold forty minutes. By the end of the second, an hour. By the end of the third, he sat for two hours without moving and a gecko climbed his arm and nested in the crook of his elbow and did not know he was alive.

Drogo, who had been watching from the garden with the expression of someone whose tea had finally brewed correctly, said: "Hm. Now you are ready to learn how to move."


The fighting lessons began in the fourth month and were nothing like fighting.

Drogo planted stakes in the clearing, twenty of them, at irregular intervals, each one wrapped in a sleeve of palm bark. "Walk through them," he said. "Touch nothing."

Xanelor walked through them. He touched three.

"Again."

He touched two.

"Again."

One.

"Again."

He made it through clean and stood at the far end, breathing hard, and Drogo said: "Good. Now do it with your eyes closed."

The stakes multiplied. The spacing narrowed. Drogo added ropes between them at shin height, then chest height, then both. He hung coconut shells that swung when the breeze caught them, creating corridors that changed shape while Xanelor moved through them. The boy learned to feel the displacement of air against his cheek, to hear the faint creak of rope-on-wood that meant a shell was swinging toward his ribs, to trust the map his body built without his eyes.

"This," Drogo said, "is how you fight for others. You learn where everything is. You learn where everything is going. And you put yourself, hm, in the place where the harm would have landed, before it arrives." He tapped the earth with his staff. "The hero does not look for the fight, boy. The hero stands in the gap."

The word settled into Xanelor's chest. *The gap.* He thought of the arrow in Drogo's shell and did not ask about it.

"For others," Drogo repeated. "Understand? The strength that serves itself is just appetite wearing armour. The strength that serves another, that is the only kind worth the cost of keeping."


Drogo had begun the mask before the fighting lessons started, though Xanelor did not know it then.

The boy found him working on it one evening after a long day in the stake-field, when his whole body ached and his shins were mapped in bruises from the ropes he'd misjudged. Drogo sat by the cooking fire with something in his lap, and his claws moved over it with a slowness that was different from his usual slowness. More careful. As though the thing under his hands was something that could not be asked to wait.

"What's that?" Xanelor asked.

Drogo angled it away. "Not yet, it is. Ask again later."

"When?"

"When it is ready, you will know. Because I will give it to you." He set the thing aside, wrapped in oilcloth. "Tea?"

Xanelor drank his tea. He did not ask again that night.

He caught glimpses over the following months. A curve of something pale and green-grey, the colour of Drogo's own shell. Markings being carved into its surface with a bone awl, patterns that looked like tide-lines or wind-scoured rock. The shape of it, taking form through the oilcloth: a face. Not a human face. Not a turtle's face. Something between. Something that could be either, or neither, depending on the angle of the light.

Drogo worked on it in the evenings, always. His pace was glacial. One evening he made three cuts and wrapped it again. Another evening he sat for an hour with the bone awl in his hand and made none at all, just looking at the thing with both eyes, the dark and the light, as though the mask were telling him something and he was listening.

The second year, Xanelor understood that the mask was made from shell. From Drogo's shell. He had seen the old turtle pressing a claw against the plate near his left shoulder, the one with the old crack-lines, and a thin flake had come away. Drogo had held it up to the firelight, examined it, and set it with the others in the oilcloth.

Xanelor's throat closed. He said nothing.


In the second year the cough arrived.

It came quietly, the way things came to Drogo, without announcement. A hitch in his breathing during the dawn tea. A pause in the garden where he leaned on the staff and the staff, for the first time Xanelor could remember, bore real weight instead of keeping rhythm. The boy noticed because Drogo had trained him to notice. The old turtle's skin, which had always been cool and firm, grew papery along the neck. The moss on his shell thinned. Pīpī's granddaughter, the third generation to nest in the arrow's shadow, shifted her nest lower on the shell, closer to the warmth of Drogo's body, as though she knew something the boy was refusing to know.

Drogo did not slow the training. He adapted it.

"Show me," he said, more and more often, from the stone where he sat. "Show me the third form."

And Xanelor would move through the stake-field, or draw the bow, or sit in the stillness while Drogo watched and his breathing rattled like pebbles in a dry creek bed. The corrections came less often. When they came, they were shorter.

"Elbow."

"Breathe."

"Hm. Better."

By the end of the second year Drogo no longer rose without the staff. His hands, those immense and impossibly gentle hands, trembled when he poured the tea. He poured it anyway. He never missed the cup.

Xanelor understood, in the way that a child understands the thing he will not say aloud, that his grandfather was dying. That the lively spark in the dark left eye was burning fuel that would not be replaced. That every lesson was also a leaving.

He trained harder. He did not say why. Drogo did not ask.


The third year was the longest. Drogo barely left the basalt shelf. His breathing came in shallow pulls that whistled through the chest cavity, and the staff leaned against the wall of the hut because his hands could no longer grip it for a full walk. Xanelor ran the garden alone, cooked the meals alone, brewed the tea and carried it to Drogo and held the cup steady when the old turtle's claws shook too badly to lift it. He trained alone too, running the stake-field and the bow course and the breathing exercises at dawn without correction or company, and the absence of Drogo's voice in those mornings was the loudest sound on Omoro.

The mask, though. Drogo still worked the mask. Even when everything else had been surrendered to the boy or to gravity, the bone awl moved in those trembling hands with a precision that seemed borrowed from some earlier, stronger version of himself.

The fourth year. The mask was almost finished.

Drogo could no longer walk to the garden. He sat on the basalt shelf outside the hut, the staff across his knees, and Xanelor brought him tea and tended the twelve beds alone. The wild garlic was coming in strong. The ginger had spread to a thirteenth bed, which Xanelor had built without being asked, turning the volcanic soil with hands that were calloused and brown and sure.

The mask sat between them most evenings now, unwrapped. It was beautiful. Xanelor had not expected that. He had expected something rough, something practical, the way everything Drogo built was practical. But this was different. The shell had been shaped into something that was half-face and half-landscape, the brow-line carved to suggest a mountain ridge, the cheek-planes worn smooth as river stone, the eye-holes shaped like leaves or like tears depending on the light. The tide-line markings flowed across the forehead and down the jaw, and where the mouth would be there was no opening at all. Just smooth, closed shell.

"No mouth," Xanelor said.

"Hm." Drogo ran one trembling claw along the jaw-line. "A good thing does not need to announce itself. The mouth speaks. The deed, hm, the deed is silent. When you wear this, boy, no one will know your name. No one will see your face. They will see what you did, and they will know that someone chose to do it, and that will be enough."

"Why can't they know it was me?"

Drogo looked at him. The patience in that look was bottomless. "Because the moment you do a good thing and take the credit, you have done two things. One good. One for yourself. And the second, over time, hm, it eats the first. The man who saves the village and stays for the feast learns to save villages for the feast. The man who saves the village and walks into the dark, he saves the village because the village needed saving."

He set the mask in Xanelor's hands. It was lighter than he expected. It was warm from Drogo's grip.

"Piece of me, this is. From the shell. Understand? Part of me goes with you. Protect it, hm, the way I protected you."


The letter came with the supply traders in the wet season. Xanelor did not know Drogo had written to anyone. He did not know Drogo could write in the human script, though looking back he should have guessed, since the old turtle knew everything else.

The letter was addressed to the Headmaster of the Aetherion Academy, and its reply, folded inside a second envelope, was addressed to *The Guardian of Xanelor Rafiminar, Omoro Island, Southern Reach*. The seal was blue wax pressed with the shape of a tower.

Drogo held the reply for three days before he gave it to the boy. Xanelor found him sitting with it on the basalt shelf one morning, the unopened letter in one hand and the mask in the other, turning them both over as though weighing something that could not be measured in grams.

He handed Xanelor the letter without a word. The boy read it. Read it again. Looked up.

"They accepted me."

"Hm."

"You... you wrote to them?"

"Last year. When the cough started." Drogo coughed, as though the word had summoned it. A long, wet sound that came from deep in the chest cavity and echoed inside the shell. He wiped his mouth with the back of one claw. "Knew then. Knew it was time."

"Time for what?"

"Time for you to go, boy."

The word *go* landed in the clearing the way the staff had once landed, the ground acknowledging it with a faint shudder that was mostly in Xanelor's knees.

"I'm not leaving."

Drogo ignored this. He patted the stone beside him, and Xanelor sat, because he still could not disobey that gesture, not even now, not even with the letter crumpling in his fist.

Drogo placed his hand on the boy's face. The mask was up, the way Xanelor wore it most days now, pushed back on his head like a visor. Drogo's fingers found the edge of it and lifted it gently, as though unveiling something fragile.

Beneath the mask, the scars.

They had been there when Drogo found him. Two years old, washed up in a piece of wreckage on the northern shore, screaming in a language the turtle did not speak, his face a ruin of fresh cuts that had healed into ridges of scar tissue the colour of ripe plums. Drogo had never asked about them. He did not know what had made them and he had decided, long ago, that the knowing mattered less than the tending.

The scars had grown with the boy. They mapped his left cheek from the temple to the jaw, three parallel lines that had darkened from pink to purple over the years, vivid against his brown skin. His hair, longer now, a tangle of black and umber-brown that fell past his shoulders, covered the worst of them when he let it. He did not always let it.

Drogo traced the longest scar with one claw. The touch was feather-light and very steady, which cost him something, because his hands had not been steady in months.

"Sit," Drogo said, though the boy was already sitting. The word meant something different this time. It meant *listen and do not interrupt*.

"You must go now, boy. To this academy. For yourself." He held up one claw before Xanelor could speak. "For yourself. Understand? Not for me. Not because the old turtle asked. Because the world is larger than this island, and you, hm, you are meant for the larger thing. Felt it when I found you. Feel it now."

"Grandpa..."

"Take care of you any longer, I cannot." The words came slowly and they cost him. Xanelor could see the cost in the way Drogo's chest moved, the breath shallow, the shell listing slightly to one side the way a ship lists when the ballast shifts. "Time for me to go. Reunite with my people, I must."

The ruins on the eastern ridge. The empty halls. The moss-covered doorways leading into rooms where no one had spoken in a hundred years.

*Reunite with my people.*

Xanelor understood. The understanding hit him in the chest like the coconut shells on the rope-line, and he could not step around this one, could not find the gap, could not flow or remain or redirect. It just hit him.

"Please don't say that, Grandpa." His voice cracked. The tears came and he did not wipe them. Drogo had taught him that the body says what it says and a man does not argue with his own body. "You don't have to go. I can stay. I can take care of you. I can..."

"Hm." Drogo's hand was still on his face. The thumb, that massive blunt claw worn smooth by a century of garden work, traced the scar-line one more time, and then moved to the boy's chin and tilted it up.

"Look at me."

Xanelor looked. One dark eye. One light. Both of them steady and clear, clearer than they had been in months, as though Drogo had been saving this clarity for this moment.

"A good thing, you are, boy. Best thing I ever grew." The continental smile, one last time, slow and geological and warm. "Remember what I taught you. The river does not try to be the river. The strong thing remains. Stand in the gap." His voice dropped. "And wear the mask, hm? Let them see the good. Not the face behind it."

His hand fell.

Pīpī's granddaughter sang three notes into the silence. The tea sat between them, growing cold, and neither of them touched it.


*Xanelor Rafiminar left Omoro on a trading vessel two days later.*

*He stood at the rail in the gear Drogo had made for him across the four years, piece by piece, the way the old turtle did everything. The helm was slatted iron with cross-shaped visor slots, forged by Drogo in the last year he could still work the small anvil behind the hut, and from its crown a length of green silk trailed in the harbour wind, the only colour Drogo had ever called beautiful. The longcoat was heavy grey canvas, double-breasted, brass-buttoned, cut long enough to break the wind on open water and fitted close at the waist so it would not catch on a bowstring. Drogo had sewn it with sail-needles held in claws that shook. A green cross was stitched over the heart. On his left forearm, a white bracer stamped with a compass star. On his hands, green leather gloves, dyed with the same lichen that grew on Drogo's shell. The recurve bow rode his back, its limb-tips capped in worked bronze that Drogo had shaped from melted-down fittings off the old ruins. The broadhead arrows in the hip quiver were fletched with stonechat feathers.*

*Around his neck, under the coat, a copper hammer pendant no larger than a thumbnail.*

*He did not look back at the island. Drogo had told him not to.*

*He looked back anyway.*

*The mountain was there. The garden. The hut with the low door. And on the basalt shelf, very small from this distance, a shape that might have been a stone, or might have been a turtle who had finally decided to hold still.*

*Behind the helm, where no one could see, the tears ran down the purple scars on his cheek and fell into the collar of the coat that smelled, still, faintly, of moss and earth-after-rain.*

*No one on the trading vessel asked him anything. He looked like a boy who had already answered every question that mattered.*

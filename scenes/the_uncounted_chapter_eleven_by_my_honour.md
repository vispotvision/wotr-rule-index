# The Uncounted, Chapter Eleven: By My Honour

## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Eleven: By My Honour

---

### I · The First Bowl

Lorn Stark had not expected to be spoken to.

He had said his piece from the wall and gone back to the floor three feet in front of him, as a Warlord did when he had spoken in front of a king, and he had stayed there through all of it. Through the mark in the dust and Kasane's name, and the three minutes, and Muken's mother in the last gallery with the lamp. He had stood with his weight even on both feet and his hands still at his sides and he had not let his face move, because his father had told him on the wall-walk, in the dark, that the day it came out he was to stand where the boy could see him and not look away. *He'll look for us, Lorn. When it lands. He'll look to see who's still standing where they were.*

So he had stood where he was. And now the Zettari had turned to him across the long room, and said his name, and said *you were right.*

Lorn listened to all of it. The battle and the riders. *I spoke carelessly about your people.* *I hope your riders return. All three hundred of them.* He listened with his chin up and his eyes, Stark-blue and raw at the rims, fixed on the big man's face, and when it was finished he did not answer at once. He was not a man who answered at once. His father had used to say that a quick answer was wet wood: it smoked, and it filled the room, and it gave no heat.

"Thank you, Prince," he said at last.

His voice was rough, and lower than he meant it to be.

"My father had a saying. Every Kharven father has it." He did not look away. "The first bowl goes to the one who cannot fill it himself. It's about the pot, mostly. Who eats first in a lean year. But he used to say it meant the dead as well. That the first of everything you have, your grief and your anger and your good words, goes to the ones who can't ask for it anymore." He paused. "You said you've carried your own dead home. Then you know where the first bowl goes. I'll not ask more of you than that."

He put his right hand flat on his chest, over his heart, and closed it into a fist against his breastbone, and pressed it there once. Then he let it fall to his side, and looked at the floor three feet in front of him again, and was quiet.

At the head of the table, Muken's mouth moved very slightly at the corner. He did not look at Lorn. He did not need to.

---

### II · I Killed Him

He had been listening to the prince the way he listened to a man across a ditch.

The confession about the measuring, and that he had not stopped. *Whether Gimbzo's son can become a better king than his father ever was.* *I don't know if I would either.* All of it had gone into him and down, and settled, and he had kept his hands flat on the rough pine on either side of the mark in the dust, and his face had not changed.

Then the prince said: *Something that concerns your father.*

And then he said it.

*I killed him.*

The wolfhound was on its feet before the words were finished. It had been lying across Muken's boots again, with its chin on his instep, and now it was up and between his chair and the room, enormous and grey and shaking, with every hair along its spine standing like a brush and its lips drawn back off teeth as long as a man's thumb, and the sound that came out of it was nothing like the door-on-a-bad-hinge sound it had made before. It was low and continuous and it came up through the stone floor into the soles of everyone's feet.

Muken did not put his hand on its head.

He did not move at all.

Godfrey Greymane, at his right hand, saw the gold seam in his left eye go from a hair's breadth to the width of a thread, all at once, like a crack running across ice when a man's weight comes down on it. He saw the edges of Muken's knuckles go white on the pine. He saw nothing else, because there was nothing else to see. Muken sat in Shigure Tenrai's carved chair with his face quite still and his grey eyes on the Crown Prince of Kushara, and let him say the rest of it.

*I won't apologize for it. He needed to be stopped.*

*I wanted to fight him.*

*When we fought, I was happy.*

Down the long room, Kiyome Byakuya had closed her eyes.

She had known. She had sat across from the prince in the cedar hall at the enclave when he first said it, *I killed his father,* in that deep careless voice, and the silver rings in her eyes had moved once and then held. She had carried it up four hundred miles of road beside him and not said it, because it was not hers to say. Now it was said, and she stood at the foot of her nephew's table with her hands folded in front of her and the white knot at her hip, and did not open her eyes, and her lips moved very slightly over something that might have been the oath of her house.

Tetsugen Akagane had gone still in the middle of the room with his scarred hands on his belt. His heavy face showed nothing, the way a stone shows nothing. But he had shifted his weight, very slightly, onto the balls of his feet, and his right hand had come to rest on the horn handle of his meat knife. An old soldier's hand did that on its own when a room went wrong.

Hayato had stopped turning the corner of his cape.

At the wall, Lorn Stark had lifted his eyes off the floor.

*I'm not a particularly good person, King Muken.*

*I'll fight you because I want to know how strong you are.*

*Become whatever kind of king you want to be.*

*And when the snow goes, we'll see what kind of warrior you've become as well.*

The prince stepped back to where he had stood before.

And still Muken did not move.

It went through him, again, the way the other thing had: water under ice, black and fast and a long way down. But it was a different water this time, and it went somewhere different.

*Shimane,* he thought. *The crier in the galleries. The king is fallen at Shimane.*

*This is the man. This one. He stood in the mud at Shimane and put my father into it, and that morning, three hundred feet under Halcrest, my mother turned her face to the wall.*

*And he was happy.*

The black hot thing turned over under his breastbone. He felt it come up. He felt it come all the way up behind his left eye and press there, like a thumb against the back of the socket, and the edges of the room went gold, and in the gold he could see the prince's throat, and the line of his jaw, and the long open drop of the black-and-gold robe off his shoulders, and the place under the arm where the robe hung loose, and the three steps of floor between them.

He looked at all of it.

Then he made himself look at the other thing.

*Hiroe. The granaries, with the children still inside. Shigure on the stair. I watched it burn them.*

*The cellars. With a lamp. Every Kōkan he'd left breathing. His own blood.*

*Harrowmere. Tullock. Brannick ford. The bark in the long houses. The levies going south and the carts coming back.*

*Her lungs. Her eyes. The last year on the pallet. Kiyome said it: she'd been dying of him for years before anyone ever killed him.*

He sat with all of that for a long breath. And then a longer one, in through the nose until the ribs ached and the cut in his side pulled under Ulf Stark's mail, out through the mouth until there was nothing left, the way Yusif had taught him in the back of a worked-out gallery with a stick of ash in his hands. *You're thinking. Stop thinking. Let your feet do it.*

The thumb behind his eye eased. The gold drew back from the edges of the room, slowly, like a tide.

He lifted his left hand off the table and put it on the wolfhound's back.

It did not stop growling. But it stopped shaking.

"Down," said Muken.

The dog looked back at him over its great grey shoulder. Then, slowly, with the hackles still up along its spine, it lay down across his boots again, and put its chin on his instep, and watched the prince with its yellow eyes and did not close them.

"You killed him," said Muken.

His voice was very quiet. It was the gallery voice, flat and even, every word set down like a foot on a bad ledge. But under it, for anyone with ears, there was something that had not been there before, a kind of grain in it, like wood that had been split along a line it did not want to go.

"At Shimane," he said. "In the mud. And that morning, under Halcrest, when the crier came down the shafts, my mother turned her face to the wall and didn't turn it back." He held the prince's eyes. "I don't know if those two things were the same hour. I don't suppose you'd know either."

He let it sit.

"I want you to know I thought about it," said Muken. "Just now. Sitting here. I thought about three steps of floor and the place under your arm where the robe hangs loose." He said it without any heat at all. "I've sold my sword since I was fourteen. You'd have seen me think it. You were watching for it."

Godfrey's long grey hand had come to rest on the table again beside his, flat, not touching him.

"And then I thought about Hiroe," Muken said. "And the cellars. And a village in the hill road called Harrowmere that had eighty people in it in the summer and twenty-six in it last week. And I thought about what my mother died of, which wasn't you." He was quiet a moment. "So no. I don't hate you for it. I'd like to. It'd be easier. I'd have somewhere to put all this." He touched his own chest, once, with two fingers, over the place where the black hot thing lay banked. "But you did what somebody had to do, and nobody else did it. Not the seven lines. Not the Hall. Not any of the men who stood behind him for twenty years and watched." He did not look at Kiyome, or Tetsugen, or Hayato. He did not need to. "You did. I'll not hate a man for doing the one thing everybody else was too frightened to."

The fire cracked. A spark went up.

"As for being happy," said Muken.

He looked down at the scrap-iron sword on the table for a moment.

"I don't know that feeling," he said. "I've been in a lot of fights. The Veyrath ditch. The Vessant road. The stair at the Foot with Shigure Tenrai's sword at my throat. I've never once been happy in any of them. I was too busy trying not to die." He lifted his eyes again. "Maybe that's the difference between us. You fight to find out how strong you are. I fight because the other man's between me and something I need." His mouth moved, very slightly. "I can't say which of us is the worse for it. But I'd not call you a bad man for being honest about yours. Every one of us in this room has done worse than enjoy a fight. Most of us did it for twenty years and called it duty."

Tetsugen Akagane let out a breath through his nose, very slowly, and took his hand off the horn handle of his knife.

---

### III · Warn Him Properly

It was Hayato who spoke into the silence after, because the prince had told him to.

He came forward a pace, into the firelight, with the juniper still caught in the toggle at his throat and his restless fingers for once quite still.

"He's right," said Hayato. "The prince. We came to warn you, sire, and I've only told you half of it." He glanced, once, at Kiyome, and back. "Karogane's only the knife on that ice. The hand on it is Kagenori's, and the arm behind the hand belongs to someone else entirely."

Muken looked at him.

"Every letter Karogane's carried onto the tundra these six weeks," said Hayato, "has two seals on it. Tenrai gold. And Shirogane silver." He drew a line on the air with one finger, then a second beside it. "And the silver goes on the left. Always. The place of precedence. I've had three of those letters through my hands, sire, in the last month, on my own roads, opened and closed again by a man of mine who's very good at it. Kagenori doesn't sign as a lord writing for himself. He signs as a lord writing for his queen."

"Shizuha," said Kiyome softly.

"Shizuha Shirogane." Hayato nodded. "The silver chair. She's sat one step from the red one her whole life, and her mother before her, and hers. The Shirogane have been the Kōkan's rivals for a thousand years. While there was a red line, they could never sit." He looked at Muken steadily. "Now there's one of you left. If Karogane can break you on the ice with your father's name, if your own men put you on a post for it, then there is no red line. And the next blood in the Hall with a claim to the north is silver." His fingers had started moving again, folding and unfolding the corner of his cape. "Everybody's thought for twenty years that Kagenori wants the red chair, sire. He wants to stand behind the silver one."

"And the Keld?" said Muken.

"Bought." It was Tetsugen who answered, in his flat iron voice. "I'd lay my valley on it. The Keld have never in their lives fought a war for the Moto that they could get out of. They fight for the ice. Karogane will have given them the ice." He grunted. "No tithe. Seed grain. No Moto boot on their snow. That's what I'd have offered, in his place, and the boy would have taken it, because his people are starving like everyone else's."

"The boy," said Muken.

"Aksel Keld." Tetsugen's heavy face did not change. "Eighteen. I've never seen him. I fought his grandfather, once, on the ice under the Grey Teeth, in the second year of the war, when I was young enough to think I could." He was quiet a moment. "Old Harald Keld moved four hundred riders through a whiteout so close to my column that I heard their horses blowing, and I never saw one of them until they were in my baggage train. I lost every cart I had and a third of my men, and I never found out how he did it." His scarred hands lay flat on his belt. "They say the grandson is better. They say he lost his right hand to a Tenrai sword at thirteen and the Keld smiths made him one of iron, and that he kills with it. I believe that too."

"He'll come in the snow," said Godfrey quietly. "If he's anything like Old Harald. He'll wait for weather. And when it comes he'll be in among us before anyone on our side can see his banners."

"Then we'll not wait for weather," said Muken.

Kiyome Byakuya had been listening with her eyes on the mark in the dust. She lifted them now.

"There's one more thing," she said. "Since the prince has said we should warn you properly." Her voice was low and even. "You said tonight you'll tell the Foot. Every Stark and every Greymane, by the woodpile, before Karogane can shout it on the ice." She looked at him. "Do it. It's the right thing, and it's the clever thing, which doesn't happen often. But know what comes after it." She did not look away. "The moment the north knows what you are, so does the Hall. And on the day the Hall knows that the red line lives, and that it sits at Kharven's Foot under a white banner and calls itself king without leave of the seven, Kagenori will not need the Keld any more. He'll call the full council. And he'll have the Hall name you what Gimbzo was. A traitor to the Moto, and a danger to the inner world." She breathed. "And then every line that wants to keep its chair will march north to prove it."

"Every line," said Muken.

"Not every line," said Kiyome Byakuya.

It was very quiet in the long room.

---

### IV · Under My Rule

Muken looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked down the table, past the barrel and the stools and the bench from the stables, to where the Crown Prince of Kushara had stepped back to stand by the stone wall in the shadow beyond the firelight, with his great arms in his sleeves.

"You said become whatever kind of king I want to be," said Muken. "And you said you don't know if you would have told your people the truth. And that mine chose me after seeing what kind of man I was." He paused. "I'll answer you, Prince. All of it. And then I'll answer my aunt, since she's waiting."

He set both hands flat on the pine.

"Under my rule," said Muken.

He stopped, as though the words were heavier than he had expected, and weighed them, and found that he could carry them.

"Under my rule," he said again, "no Moto will ever march on Zettari lands again."

Tetsugen's head came up. Hayato's fingers stopped on the corner of his cape. Kiyome did not move at all.

"Not a levy," said Muken. "Not a column. Not a single tax-cart. Not while I'm breathing." His voice did not rise. "Under my rule, we will control the north, the way we did for hundreds of years before the war. Our own snow. Our own salt. Our own ice. And we'll keep it well, which is more than we've done in my lifetime." His grey eyes moved, slowly, from one Moto face to the next. "And we'll reach for nothing else. We let ourselves believe we could control the whole of the inner world." He was quiet a moment. "We couldn't even control ourselves. My father went down into the cellars with a lamp after his own blood, and the seven lines watched him do it and wrote it down. That's what we were. That's what we made of the world when we had it."

He paused.

He found, to his own surprise, that his hands had begun, very slightly, to shake. He pressed them flatter on the wood until they stopped.

Then he looked back at the prince.

"And I'm sure you'll make a great king," said Muken.

He said it plainly, the way he said everything, and it was so plainly said that for a moment nobody in the room was sure they had heard it.

"You said you don't know if you'd have the courage to stand in front of your people and tell them the truth." Muken held his eyes. "You just stood in front of me and told me you killed my father, and that you were happy doing it, and that you're not a good man. In my own hall. With my own dog showing you its teeth. Nobody made you. You could have let me find out from some soldier's story six months from now and never said a word." He let that sit. "I've known a great many men who'd sooner die than say a true thing that made them look worse. You're not one of them. I think that's most of what a king is."

He was quiet a moment.

"It isn't right," he said, more quietly, "for them to force the whole of your country onto you because of whose son you are. I know a little about that, now. More than I did this morning." His mouth moved very slightly at the corner. "But I hope for the prosperity of the Zettari, Prince. I mean that. The same as I hope for the Yukari's. And not for the sake of the families. Not your house or theirs or mine. For the people." He touched the edge of the table. "The ones who fill the bowls. The ones who carry the dead home."

Muken knew how it sounded.

He could hear it himself, in the long low stone room, with the Tenrai pine spitting in the hearth and the three Moto lords standing at the foot of his table in their copper and indigo and white: a Kōkan, Gimbzo's son, telling a Zettari prince that he wished his country well. He could hear how it sounded to them, and how it must sound to the prince, and to the old woman with no teeth by the barracks door if she had been in the room, and to every Zettari mother who had ever buried a son on a Moto field.

"It may sound ironic," said Muken. "I know it does. I'm a Moto. Mine spent forty years killing yours, and twenty of them were my father's." He held the prince's eyes. "But I will not continue it. That idea. That the Moto are owed the world, and anyone who won't give it to us is owed a burning. It ends here. It ends at this table."

He looked down, then, at the mark in the dust. At the long down-stroke, and the hook, and the crossing, and the small closed loop at the foot of it like a knot in a rope.

"I was given this life for a reason," he said slowly. "I didn't think so, this morning. This morning I thought it was just the life I had, the way a man has the weather. But a woman cut salt with her lungs going for sixteen years so I'd live to sit in this chair. A Stark gave up his name and his place among the Seven to come down four galleries every season and teach me to hold a stick." He breathed. "Two Warlords of the north rode south to the Seat knowing what was waiting for them, so that their sons could stand in this room today and not look away." He did not look at Lorn or Godfrey. He did not need to. "Weather doesn't do that. Somebody meant it."

He lifted his eyes.

"So this is my fate," said Muken. "And I'll claim it. I'll claim my destiny, since everyone keeps telling me I have one." The corner of his mouth moved again. "But I'll claim it my own way. I will not force my powers onto the weak, or the innocent, the way he did. Not with a sword, and not with whatever this is." He touched the corner of his left eye, once, with two fingers, where the gold seam lay. "I'll stand in front of them. And I'll defend them with honour, or I'll not stand there at all."

At the foot of the table, Kiyome Byakuya's eyes had filled again.

She did not wipe them. She unfolded her hands. And then, slowly, as though the movement had been waiting in her body for sixteen years, she untied the white knot from the guard of her sword, one turn and then another, and drew the cord out from the hilt, and let it hang from her fingers.

"Purity before power," said Kiyome Byakuya.

Her voice was very low. It was not meant for the room. It was meant for one person in it.

"Peace before the blade." She went down on one knee on the stone floor of the Tenrai mess, in her near-white robes grey with the road, and laid the katana flat across her knee with its hilt toward him. "And the red line before the white. Until the end of the red line." She bowed her head over the sword. "That's the oath of my house, nephew. My grandmother taught it to me over the lamp, and to Kasane before me. It binds me to you. It doesn't bind you to anything at all."

Nobody moved.

"I told the Sovereign of Kushara," said Kiyome, without lifting her head, "that if you said no, the Byakuya would serve you anyway, in a mercenary's tent. You haven't said no." She breathed. "So the white house will serve you in a king's hall. However mean the hall. However few the chairs."

Muken looked at her for a long time.

"Get up, Aunt," he said at last, very quietly. "Please. You've ridden four hundred miles. The floor's cold."

She did not get up at once. When she did, she rose the way she had risen at the enclave, all at once, with the glass beads at her temples chiming faintly, and the white cord still hanging from her fingers, and she did not tie it back onto her sword.

Tetsugen Akagane had been watching from under his heavy grey brows. Now he grunted, low in his chest.

"I've no oath to give you," he said. "The Akagane never swore to the red. We only ever obeyed it." He looked at Muken with his flat stone face. "But I'll tell you this, boy. Sire. If you meant that, about the Zettari lands, then you'll want my books. The Sennen muster rolls. Every commander's name who drew grain from my stores to march south." He hooked his thumbs in his belt. "A king who means to stop his own people marching on their neighbours had better know which of his own people did the marching."

"I'll take them," said Muken.

Hayato Amagiri had been very still. Now he laughed, once, under his breath, and it had no humour in it at all.

"My uncle," he said, "is going to have an apoplexy." He shook his head slowly. "*No Moto will march on Zettari lands again.* He'll call that treason in open council before you've finished saying it." He looked at Muken. "I'd say it louder, sire. Say it tonight, by the woodpile, after the other thing. Say it where every Stark rider can hear it, and every one of them will carry it home to a long house that buried a son on a southern field." He drew a line in the air with one finger. "And I'll carry it down my roads. Every hut with a woodpile in it between here and the border. Before the Hall can say you didn't mean it."

### V · The Only Thing That Can Be Held

Muken looked down at his own right hand on the table.

There was a callus across the base of the fingers that had been there since he was eight years old. A thick ridge of hard skin where a length of ash had rubbed it raw in the back of a worked-out gallery, season after season, while a big quiet grey-bearded man stooped under the low roof with his own hands closed over the boy's on the end of the stick.

*Again. Again. You're thinking. Stop thinking.*

And once, at the very end of a lesson, with the lamp going down and the stick put away against the gallery wall, the big man had crouched in front of him so that their faces were level, and put one great rough hand on the side of his head, and said the other thing. He had only ever said it once. He had said it as if it were the most important thing he had ever taught him, more than the stick, more than the feet, and Muken at eight years old had not understood a word of it and remembered every one.

He said it now, aloud, to the room, in Yusif Stark's own words.

"*Always swear by your honour,*" said Muken. "*Because it's the only thing you have that can be held to account.*"

At the wall, Lorn Stark made a sound in his throat, very small, and looked at the floor.

"Yusif said that to me," said Muken. "When I was eight. In the dark, under the salt, with nobody but me to hear it. He said a man's gold can be taken, and his land, and his name. They took his. He gave up his place among the Seven and his house's name to come down those galleries, and nobody ever let him have them back." He breathed. "But nobody can take a man's honour off him. He can only put it down himself. So it's the one thing anybody can ever hold him to." He looked at Lorn. "I never knew what he meant. I think I do now."

Lorn did not lift his eyes. But his right hand came up off his side, slowly, and lay flat on his chest, and closed into a fist against his breastbone, and pressed there once, hard. And stayed.

Muken pushed back Shigure Tenrai's carved chair.

The legs scraped on the stone. It was the loudest sound in the room. The wolfhound lifted its head off his boot and got up with him, unhurried, and stood at his left knee. Godfrey Greymane did not try to stop him. Godfrey had told him an hour ago to sit, and stay sat, and let them come to him, and he had, through all of it. He had let them come all the way. And now there was no one left to come.

So the king of the north stood up.

He was not tall. He had never been tall. He stood at the head of his split pine table in a dead sergeant's coat gone the colour of tea, with Ulf Stark's mail under it and his left arm still held a little close to his side, and his white hair tied back with a strip of leather, and the dead bronze token on his belt, and the gold seam lying quiet now in his left eye like a vein of ore in grey stone. He came round the end of the table, past the barrel, past the stools, past his aunt with the white cord in her fingers and the two lords of the seven lines, and stopped three steps from the Crown Prince of Kushara.

The same three steps of floor he had looked at, ten minutes ago, through the gold.

He held out his right hand. The callused one. Open, palm sideways, the way the Starks offered a hand, not the way the Moto did.

"By my honour," said Muken, "we shall fight."

He did not lower his hand. He did not look away.

"When the snow goes. No armies. No politics. No ancient quarrel. You and me, as you said." He held the prince's pale eyes. "And I hope our fight can be something all of our people remember. Yours and mine. Something the long houses can tell their children about, and the Zettari can tell theirs, and nobody has to bury anybody's son over it." The corner of his mouth moved. "I'll try to make you happy, Prince. I'll not promise. I've never done it on purpose."

He stood with his hand out, in the firelight, in the long low hall at Kharven's Foot, and waited.

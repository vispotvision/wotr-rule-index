# The Uncounted, Chapter Four: Three Days

## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Four: Three Days

---

Muken closed his hand round the dead token and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where nobody would see him hold it. It was cold there. It stayed cold.

He sat on the stool by the fire for a while longer without speaking. The wolfhound had gone back to sleep with its nose on his boot. Across the hearth Ulf Stark was watching him the way a man watches weather coming in over a ridge, patiently, without hope of changing it, only wanting to see what it would do.

"Lord Stark," Muken said at last.

"Ulf. In here, it's Ulf."

Muken did not take him up on it. He was not sure he ever would.

"You keep saying *your army*," he said. "*Your men.*" He turned the thought over, carefully, the way he would have turned over a stone at a ford before he put his weight on it. "Are you sure they'll want to follow somebody like me? Somebody my age?" He looked up. "I'm sixteen. I've got men in the Lanternless who were swinging a sword in the war before my mother ever came down into the Cuttings. Half of them still call me *lad* when they think I can't hear. And they've had a year of me. Your riders don't know me from a hole in the road."

Ulf smiled. It was a tired smile, and there was nothing amused in it at all.

"You've not seen the resistance," he said. "Have you."

"No."

"No." Ulf leaned back in the great chair. "I'll tell you what it is, then, so you don't walk into it with your eyes shut. Most of the men carrying spears for the north this winter are your age, lad. Sixteen. Seventeen. Some of them younger, and lying about it, and I let them lie because I've no one to send home in their place." He turned the iron ring on his beard-braid with his thumb. "Their fathers are dead. At Kessen. In the Moto cells. On the posts in the Tenrai yards. Their uncles went with Gimbzo in the long middle of the war, when the north still thought he meant to set things right, and most of those never came back either. There's a whole generation of men missing out of the north, Muken. Missing like a tooth. And the boys have come up into the gap."

He was quiet for a moment.

"So it's not the spearmen you need to worry about," he said. "They'll follow you because you're the first man they've seen in three years who looks like them and still wins. It's the others. The ones at the top." He spread his maimed hand on the arm of the chair. "Me. Wystan Greymane. Old Harald at the Keld fires, if he can be got to come at all. The Seven, what's left of them. Do you know how old we are?"

Muken shook his head.

"I'm fifty-seven," Ulf said. "Wystan's sixty-one. Harald was too old to sit a horse when my father was a boy. Every man on the council of the north is past fifty-five, lad, and every one of us is a man who fought the war from its first winter to its fortieth, and buried his sons in it, and has nothing left in him now but the stubbornness to not lie down." He let a long breath out through his nose. "We can hold the north for a few years more. That's all. We can't win it. Not on our own. We're too old, and too tired, and the boys who'd die for us can see it on our faces every time we ride past."

He leaned forward.

"If they won't follow you," he said, "then they were never any use to our cause in the first place, and I'd rather know it now than on the field. But they will. I've watched Rob look at you. I've never once in his life seen Rob look at anybody like that." He held Muken's eyes. "We need someone young. Young enough to still be alive when this is over. Young enough to be the one the tales are told about, and young enough to be the one who tells them. The north doesn't rise for an old man's grudge, Muken. It never has. It rises for a story. And it's been a long time since anybody gave it one worth getting up for."

The fire cracked.

Muken did not argue. He could not have, honestly, if he had wanted to. He had seen the men Ulf meant without knowing he was seeing them: the grey heads in every Stark hall the convoy had passed, the stooped backs at the fords, the old riders with no sons beside them. He had seen the boys too. Riding point on the Vessant road. Holding the gate at the Marrow crossing with spears longer than they were. Sixteen. Seventeen. Some younger, and lying about it.

He had not put the two together until now.

"I'll think on it," he said.

"You'll do more than that." Ulf sat back. "You've given me your word already, in this room, and I'll hold you to it. But think. Take three days. Sleep on it, if you can sleep. When you've stopped asking yourself whether you can, come and find me, and we'll talk about how." He nodded at the door. "Go on. Rob'll let you out. And Muken."

Muken stopped with his hand on the bolt.

"Your sister," said Ulf Stark. "Tell her. She'll know anyway, by the look of her. Better she hears it from you than reads it off you."

---

He did not sleep much, those three days.

The Vessant post was a small place and an idle one once the convoy was paid off, and the Lanternless did what free companies did when they were paid and idle, which was drink. They drank the post's beer and then the post's spirit, and then whatever the teamsters had brought up from the south in the bottom of their kit, and they sang and fought and slept it off in the straw of the long barn, and on the second night one of them put a knife in another over a game of bones and Yukazuri sewed the man up on the trestle in the factor's outer room while the factor's clerks watched with their mouths open. Nobody asked where their sergeant was. Sergeants were allowed to be somewhere else.

He was on the stockade.

There was a walkway round the inside of the wall at the height of a man's shoulder, plank laid on log brackets, where a sentry could stand and see out over the sharpened tops of the stakes, and Muken stood on it, those three days, more than he stood anywhere else. He stood at the north corner, mostly. From there he could see the river, black and fast between its shelves of ice, and the timber going away on the far bank in long blue-grey ranks toward the edge of the world, and the sky over it, which in the third week of the Thin Weeks was white all day and went the colour of a bruise at evening. In the mornings the frost on the stake-points glittered like ground glass, and the hoar on the far timber caught the first sun and burned pink along the tops of the ranks for the length of a breath before it went white.

He kept the token in his coat. He took it out, sometimes, when he was sure nobody could see, and held it in his palm and waited for it to hum. It never did.

*What's your oldest?* the steward had asked him, every time, at Orrel, when he was a child. And laughed. And every time, the answer had been: nothing. No contract. No history. No credit. No name the clerks would take. He had spent his whole life since believing that was the thing that was wrong with him. That he was nothing, born of nothing, the son of a cutting woman and of a father nobody had ever named to him, and that everything he had done since, every season's pay, every contract honoured, every road walked to the end, was the slow work of making something out of that nothing by hand.

And now a Warlord of the north had handed him a contract, sealed under the Chain Light, and it had lain dead in his palm. And the Warlord had not looked surprised. He had looked as if something he had been told for years had finally turned out to be true.

He thought about Yusif.

He had not let himself think about Yusif in a long time. It had hurt too much, after the visits stopped, and then it had stopped hurting, which was worse, and he had put the big quiet grey-bearded man away somewhere at the back of himself, in a worked-out gallery where he did not go. Now he went. He sat down in it, in his head, on the cold floor, and let it come back.

The salt-meat wrapped in oiled cloth. The new wool. The way Yusif had always had to stoop under the gallery roof, and never once complained of it. The stick, a length of ash the right weight for a small boy's hands, and the big rough hands closed round his own on the end of it. *Again. Again. You're thinking. Stop thinking. Let your feet do it. There. That's it. Again.* The way Yusif had looked at their mother, the few times he stayed long enough to look: carefully, with his head a little bowed, the way you might look at something in a shrine. The way he had looked at Muken, sometimes, when he thought Muken was not watching. As if he were waiting for something.

*You protect the weak. You defend the man next door. You keep the north quiet so the children can sleep in it. Everything else is just weather.*

A man who had been one of the Seven. One of the greatest warlords the house of Stark had ever put in the field. Who had turned his horse round and ridden away from all of it, his rank and his name and his place, and gone down four galleries under Halcrest once a season for years to teach a cutting woman's boy to hold a stick.

Nobody did that for a nobody.

Muken stood on the stockade with his hands on the cold points of the stakes and looked north, toward the timber, toward the end of the world, toward whatever was out there that Ulf would not name.

*Is this it?* he thought. *Is this what he was waiting for?* He did not know what the question meant, exactly, or who he was asking it of. *Is this what I'm for?*

The river did not answer. The timber did not. The sky went the colour of a bruise, violet and rose low along the treeline, and then went dark, and later the aurora came up over the timber in long slow curtains of green hemmed with violet, bright enough that the snow on the walkway took the colour round his boots. It did not answer either.

---

On the third morning his sister found him there.

She came up the ladder to the walkway without a sound, the way she did everything, and he did not hear her until she was beside him. She had her grey field coat on, buttoned to the throat against the cold, and her white hair was braided and pinned up off her neck, the way she wore it when she was working, though there was nobody left at the post who needed working on. Her small grave face was pink with cold at the nose and the cheekbones. She stood beside him at the stakes without a word and looked where he was looking, at the river and the timber and the white sky over it, until the cold had got into both their hands.

He felt his breathing slow down. It always did, when she was near. He had never once in his life wondered why.

She was thirteen. She came up to the middle of his chest. When she had been small, five or six, too small to work the stamping floor, she had used to sit on his feet in the gallery while he worked the salt face, holding onto his knees with both arms, and he had walked her up and down that way until she shrieked. He looked down at her now, at the white braid and the grey coat and the long new scar down her forearm where the sleeve had ridden up, and felt something in his chest that he did not have a word for.

He reached out and put his hand on the top of her head, the whole big cracked palm of it, and ruffled her hair, the way he had done since she was born. It came loose from one of its pins. She did not slap his hand away. That was how he knew she was worried.

"What's wrong, big brother?" Yukazuri said.

He took his hand back. "Nothing's wrong."

"You've slept with your boots on for three nights."

"I always sleep with my boots on."

"You've slept with your boots *laced* for three nights." She did not look at him. She was still watching the river. "You've eaten one meal in two days and you gave half of it to Mark Stark's dog, which isn't even Mark's, it's the factor's. Your breath's gone short and high when you think nobody's listening, right up under the collarbone, here." She touched the hollow of her own throat with two fingers without taking her eyes off the river. "And you've had your hand inside your coat on your chest every time I've looked at you since you came out of that room. Like there's something there you're frightened you'll drop." She paused. "Or frightened somebody will see."

Muken was silent.

"So I'll ask you again," said Yukazuri. "And this time you'll tell me, or I'll take that coat off you myself and look. What's wrong?"

---

He told her.

He kept his voice low, and he kept his back to the inside of the stockade and his eyes on the gate, and he told her all of it, the way Ulf had told it to him. The road that was chosen. Draycott, who was Ulf's man. Torvald's head in a cask of brine, and the note in Kagenori Tenrai's hand. The Kessen road, and the martial law that was coming down on it in two months like a door. Stark and Greymane, and maybe the Keld, against six Moto lines. The army. The offer. The word he had already given.

When he got to Yusif, she turned her head and looked at him for the first time.

"Yusif," she said.

"Yusif Stark. Ulf's uncle. One of the Seven." He swallowed. "He rode with Gimbzo. In the war. And then he stopped. And then he came to us."

Yukazuri was a while finding anything to say.

"He taught me to hold a needle," she said at last. Her voice was very even. "I was five. He brought it down wrapped in a scrap of leather with a reel of gut, and he sat me on a sack of salt and showed me how to put it through a fold of wool and draw it tight so it wouldn't pull." She looked down at her own hands on the stakes, at the small brown fingers that had closed a hundred men. "He said it was the most useful thing in the world. He said more men would live because of a needle than would ever die because of a sword." She was quiet. "I thought he was just being kind."

"So did I."

"He wasn't." She looked up at him. "Was he?"

"No," Muken said. "I don't think he was."

The wind came over the stockade from the north, off the river, and it was cold enough to make his eyes water. Yukazuri did not seem to feel it. She was looking at his chest, at the place where his hand had been, and her face had changed.

"Show me," she said.

He hesitated. Then he looked over his shoulder, at the empty yard, the closed doors of the barn and the factor's hall, the smoke going up straight from the chimneys in the still morning. Nobody. He reached inside his coat and took out the token and held it out to her on his palm.

She did not take it. She looked at it first, the way she looked at a wound before she touched it. Then she bent her head, close, closer, until her ear was nearly touching his hand, the way she bent over a man's chest in the back of a cart to listen to what was going on inside it, and closed her eyes.

He waited.

She straightened up. Her eyes, when she opened them, had gone very dark, nearly black, the deep blue swallowed up by the grey of the morning.

"It's sealed," she said. "It's a good seal. Old wall. Bright light. I can feel it in there, the shape of it, like a coin in a pocket." She frowned. "It's not broken. It's not dead. It's just..." She searched for the word. "It's just not talking to you. As if it's looking straight at you and can't see anyone there."

He closed his hand over it.

"Ulf knew it would do that," he said. "Before he gave it to me. He wasn't watching me. He was watching it."

"I know. I've been listening to that thing for three days." Yukazuri did not take her eyes off his closed fist. "Every token in this post hums, Muken. The factor's. The clerks'. Every teamster with a supply contract on his belt. I can hear them all the way across the yard at night, like bees in a wall. And yours is the only one that's quiet." She drew a breath. "I thought at first it was broken. And then I thought it was Mother. Something in her, that came down to us."

He went still.

"It isn't Mother," Yukazuri said softly. "Mother had a token once, she told me, and it hummed fine. She told me near the end, when the fever had her and she wasn't always sure which of us she was talking to. A little bronze thing on a cord, from before the Cuttings, and it sang on her like a kettle on the hob, she said, and she dropped it down a dead shaft the week she came to Orrel, because a thing that hums can be heard." She looked up at him. "It's us. I've been standing in that yard three days listening to every token in this post, and I've worked out what they're listening for. A token wants a name to sit under, Muken. A house. That's what the steward was laughing at with his *oldest*, and what Draycott meant about the book. It goes looking for our name, and we haven't got one it can find."

The mark. Their mother's mark, in burned stick on the salt wall of the gallery. *That's ours. That's our name. Don't show it to the clerks.* The Zettari surveyor at the Marrow, wiping it off his window with his sleeve, with his breath gone short and high under his collarbone.

Neither of them said it.

"What are we, Yuka?" Muken said. It came out of him very quietly, almost before he knew he had said it.

His sister looked up at him, with the white braid coming loose where he had ruffled it, and her small grave face pink with cold, and her eyes very dark.

"I don't know," she said. "Somebody's, with the name scraped off." She put her hand on top of his closed fist, over the token, and held it there. "But I don't think Yusif came down four galleries every season until I was ten to teach a needle to nothing. And I don't think a Warlord of the north hauls his own chair down out of Hollenmark on the back of a cart to talk to nothing either." She paused. "We're something, Muken. Somebody knows what. And whoever they are, they're frightened enough of it to make sure we never found out."

---

They stood together on the stockade, her hand over his, and the river went on below them, black and fast between the ice, throwing the low sun back in hard white sparks wherever it broke over a stone.

"You've already decided," Yukazuri said after a while.

"I haven't."

"You decided in the room. I could hear it on you when you came out." She took her hand away and tucked it back into the sleeve of her coat. "You've spent three days up here trying to find a way to make it not your fault. In case it goes wrong."

He laughed. He could not help it. It came out short and rough and pulled at the stitches in his cheek, and he put his hand up to them and laughed again anyway.

"Sky above," he said. "Is there anything you don't know?"

"Lots of things. Not that." She looked up at him. "And I'm coming."

"Yuka."

"I'm coming. Don't start."

"You're *thirteen.*"

"I was twelve at the Veyrath ditch," said Yukazuri, very evenly, "when I went down it on my knees through two hundred men the lords had left lying there, with my needle and my gut and the salve tin, and closed every one of them I could reach, and sat with the ones I couldn't until they went. You were up the bank holding the line. You didn't see." She did not raise her voice. She never did. "I've been the company's surgeon for a year. I've sewn your face shut twice. There are men alive in this post right now who wouldn't be if I'd been somewhere sensible. And if you go and lead an army of sixteen-year-olds against six Moto lines, Muken, then somebody's going to have to be there afterward for the ones who don't come back from it. That's my work. That's what Yusif gave me the needle for." She held his eyes. "So I'm coming. And if you try to leave me behind at some fort with a nice warm fire, I'll walk after you in the snow, and you'll have to explain to Lord Stark why your surgeon's lost three toes."

He looked down at her, and found he had no argument left that she had not already sewn shut.

Then he put his hand on her head again, gently this time, and left it there, and did not ruffle her hair. He just left it there, the weight of it, the way he remembered Yusif doing, once or twice, to both of them, in the gallery, when he was going away and did not want to say so.

"Mother would've hated this," he said.

"Mother would've packed us food for the road," said Yukazuri, "and told you to keep your boots laced."

Down in the yard, the barn door creaked. A tall shape came out of it with a stick under one arm, limping, and stood at the foot of the ladder, and looked up at them, with his breath going up white in front of his face. Rob Stark. He did not call out. He just stood there, waiting, the way the Starks waited, as though he had all the time in the world and knew exactly what the answer was going to be.

Muken took his hand from his sister's head.

He looked out once more over the stakes, at the river and the timber and the white sky going away north toward whatever it was that Ulf would not name, and the long road down to Kessen, and the two months left before the door came down on it. Then he looked down at Rob Stark at the foot of the ladder.

"Rob," he said.

"Sarge."

"Go and tell your uncle," Muken said, "that the Tokenless is coming."

# The Uncounted, Chapter Nine: The Queen's Will

## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Nine: The Queen's Will

---

### I · The Fires of the Keld

There was no wood on the tundra, so the Keld burned fat.

It went into the stone lamps in long white slabs, seal and reindeer and the yellow tallow of the great horned musk-beasts that came down off the high ice in the Thin Weeks, and it burned low and smoky and very hot, with a smell like a butcher's yard on a summer afternoon and a light the deep yellow of butter, which climbed the curve of the hide walls and turned them the colour of honey held up to a fire. The war-lodge of Aksel Keld was lit by nine of those lamps. It was a great domed tent of reindeer hide sewn over a frame of whalebone and driftwood carried a hundred miles from the northern shore, and inside it the air was thick with smoke and fat and the sharp sour reek of the fermented milk the Keld drank from horn cups the size of a man's two fists. Outside, the wind came over the white flat world from the pole with nothing in a thousand miles to stop it, and leaned on the lodge, and moaned in the guy-ropes. Through the smoke-hole at the crown of the dome a strip of night showed, and across it the aurora moved, green going to violet at its hem, slow as weed in a current. Inside, it was warm enough to sweat.

Kagenori Tenrai Moto had not taken off his outer coat. He sat on a stack of white bear-pelts across the central lamp from the Keld warlord, in gold-trimmed black that the lamplight picked out thread by thread, with his narrow face composed and his hands folded in his lap. The smoke did not seem to touch him. Behind his left shoulder, a pace back, stood a man in plain grey with no house colours at all: tall, lean, hooded, with a long pale face and very pale eyes and a stillness like a heron's in shallow water. He had not spoken since the Tenrai lord came into the lodge, and every Keld captain in the tent knew his name anyway. He had been out on the tundra for six weeks, going from fire to fire with Tenrai silver in one hand and Tenrai seed grain in the other.

Karogane.

The Iron Fist of the Keld sat on no pelts at all. He sat on the bare packed snow of the lodge floor, cross-legged, the way the Keld had always sat at their own fires, with a horn cup on his knee.

Aksel Keld was eighteen years old, and he had never lost a battle.

He had fought eleven. Some of them against the hill clans that came down off the Shiogane in the bad years to raid the reindeer herds, and some of them against Tenrai tax columns before the Tenrai learned better than to send columns onto the ice, and one of them, the one the Keld sang about, against a Thornwall warband four times the size of his own that had come out past the treeline to take the whale-oil trade by force. He had broken it on the open ice in a single afternoon, in a whiteout, by moving his riders through the snow-blind so fast and so quietly that the Thornwall men had died believing they were fighting ghosts. He had been fifteen.

He did not look like a boy. He was short, as the south counted tallness, and built the way the tundra built its people: low and wide and dense, with a barrel chest and short powerful legs and a neck like a tree-stump, all of it packed down under the weight of the cold until nothing was left on him but what the cold allowed. His face was broad and flat and wind-burned a deep red-brown across the cheekbones. His eyes were narrow and very black and set deep against the glare. Three thin blue lines were tattooed down his chin from the lower lip, the marks of a Keld who had killed a man in defence of the herds, and a fourth had been added beside them, thicker, for a chief. His hair was black and coarse and braided close to his skull in tight rows, and the braids were threaded with small rings of walrus ivory, one for each fight he had won.

His right hand was iron.

It had been a hand, once. A Tenrai sword had taken it off at the wrist in his second battle, when he was thirteen, and the Keld smiths had made him another out of black iron and walrus tusk and sinew, a heavy articulated gauntlet that strapped to the stump and the forearm and closed by a cord running up the sleeve to his shoulder. He could not feel with it. He could not hold a cup with it, which was why the cup sat on his knee. But he could make a fist with it, a fist of solid iron the size of a man's skull, and he had killed more men with that fist than with any spear. That was where the name had come from. Most of the south believed the Iron Fist was only a name for how he ruled. The Keld knew better.

He ruled that way too.

He looked across the lamp at the Tenrai lord with his black eyes narrowed against the smoke, and let the silence run as long as it liked.

"We march in two days," he said.

His voice was low and flat and rough, the way voices got on the ice, where the wind took anything that was not said plainly.

Kagenori inclined his head. "Yes," he said. "That is true."

He unfolded his hands.

"And when we meet them on the ice," Kagenori said, "I will tear the heart out of every Stark and every Greymane who has chosen to follow Muken the Tokenless." He said it pleasantly. "With a sentence, before a single spear moves. Karogane will stand between the lines, before either army has drawn a blade, and he will tell them who their tokenless king really is." The faintest smile touched his closed mouth. "Gimbzo's son. The butcher's boy. The heir of the line that burned their villages and hanged their fathers and starved their children for twenty years. Once they learn his heritage, Lord Aksel, nobody in the north will follow him one step further. Starks, Greymanes, the boys with spears, all of them. They'll put him on a post themselves."

Aksel did not answer at once.

He turned the horn cup on his knee with his left hand, slowly, a quarter-turn, and looked down into the sour white milk in it.

*It's a filthy thing,* he thought. *To break a man with his own father's name, in front of the men who love him. Before he's even had the chance to lift a spear. It's the kind of thing the Moto do. It's exactly the kind of thing.*

He knew it. He disliked it. He had disliked it since Karogane first sat down at his grandfather's fire and laid it out, six weeks ago, in that soft dry voice, between one cup and the next.

And it was true.

That was the trouble with it. Every word of it was true, and every Keld at every fire on the tundra would know it was true the moment they heard it. Nobody on the ice had any love for the Kōkan. Nobody anywhere did, any more: the Keld, the clans, the Banner families, the hill people, every one of them had given it up. The red line had been the Moto's crown for a thousand years, and for the last twenty of them the red line had meant Gimbzo, and Gimbzo had meant fire. The Keld had lost a third of their herds in the war to Gimbzo's requisitions. Aksel's own father had died on a Gimbzo levy, fighting the Zettari in a war that was nothing to do with the ice, on a field so far south he had never seen snow there. Most of the north, if you asked it honestly, would have told you that the Kōkan line was the worst thing that had ever happened to it. That its ending had been the one good thing to come out of the whole war.

And now there was one left. And the Starks had hidden him. And put a crown on him.

*Let them find out, then,* Aksel thought. *If he's worth following, he'll survive it. If he's not, better they learn now than after they've bled for him.*

He lifted his eyes to the Tenrai lord.

"If you keep your promise," he said, "you've nothing to worry about."

He let it sit.

"No tithe on the ice. Not from the Tenrai, not from the seven lines, not from anyone, while the world lasts. In writing. Under your seal and the Shirogane's." His black eyes did not move from Kagenori's face. "The seed grain you've already sent stays sent, and more comes in the spring, enough for every fire. And no Moto boot on Keld snow without the Keld's leave. Ever." He lifted the cup, finally, with his left hand, and drank, and set it down. "Keep that, and it'll be the Keld that win this battle. Our path. Our will. The ice doesn't care about the Kōkan or the Tenrai or anybody's crown. It only cares who knows how to cross it." His voice did not change. "We'll win it for you. For the true king of the north."

Kagenori Tenrai Moto laughed.

It was a soft laugh, and quite genuine, and it went round the smoky lodge and made two of the Keld captains glance at each other.

"You honour me, Lord Aksel," he said. "But you have the wrong man." He spread his narrow hands. "I am not the king. I have not become anything. Not yet." He tilted his head. "And I do not mean to."

Aksel's black eyes narrowed a fraction.

He did not believe it. Nobody on the tundra did. Every Keld fire for six weeks had talked about nothing else: that Kagenori Tenrai wanted the red chair for himself, that he had wanted it for twenty years, that the Tenrai had been waiting beside the Kōkan in their gold for a thousand years to sit in it. Everybody knew what Kagenori wanted.

Except that everybody had it wrong.

It took Aksel a long time to see it. He saw it, in the end, the way he saw a herd turning on the ice a mile off in a whiteout, by watching what moved around it. He saw it in the way Kagenori said the word *queen.* He saw it in the way the Tenrai lord's narrow face changed, very slightly, almost not at all, whenever he spoke of the Shirogane, of the silver line, the old rival of the red. He saw it in the letters Karogane had carried, every one of them sealed with two seals, the Tenrai gold beside the Shirogane silver, and the silver always on the left, the place of precedence.

Kagenori Tenrai wanted a crown for another head than his own.

He wanted a kingdom made for one woman, small and grey and hard as a hatchet, sitting very upright in a silver chair in the Hall of Verdicts, under worked lamps that burned white and steady on the Guild's draw while the whole north froze in the dark. He wanted to build it for her with his own hands, stone by stone, and set her on it, and stand at her right side, and see her content. For a thousand years the Shirogane had been the rival of the Kōkan, the hard silver line that had always stood one step from the red chair and never been allowed to sit in it. If the Kōkan were truly ended, and the last of them put on a post by his own people, then there was one line in the Moto house with a better claim to the north than any other.

And Shizuha Shirogane had the most to gain of anyone alive.

"All of the north will accept Queen Shizuha's rule," Kagenori said softly. "Every house of it. Every fire." He looked at Aksel across the lamp. "And those who do not accept it, Lord Aksel, will die. Quickly, I hope. But they will die." His voice did not change at all. "I hope you understand that. I'd not have you say later that you were not told."

The wind leaned on the lodge. The fat-lamps guttered and steadied, and the yellow light swung up the hide and settled again. Behind Kagenori's shoulder, Karogane stood exactly as he had stood all evening, and his very pale eyes had come to rest on the Keld warlord's iron hand.

Aksel Keld held the Tenrai lord's eyes across the lamp while the aurora went over the smoke-hole and was gone.

Then he lowered his head, very slightly, and lifted his iron fist off his knee, and set it against his chest, over his heart, with a dull heavy sound like a hammer laid on an anvil.

"The queen's will," said the Iron Fist of the Keld, "is absolute."

Behind his flat red-brown face, where nobody in the lodge could see it, a thought went past and was gone, like a fox crossing snow.

*For now.*

### II · The Gate

Lorn Stark had been standing at the gate of Kharven's Foot since the sun came up.

It was mended now. Two days, the outrider had said, and two days it had taken, two days and a night of every man and boy in the fort carrying stone up from the rubble of the breach and fitting it back into the gap in the curtain wall, course by course, while the Greymane masons shouted at them and the snow came down and nobody slept. The new stone was paler than the old. You could see the line of the mend from the bottom of the ridge, a long uneven seam of grey-white running down through the darker Tenrai grey, and in the early sun the frost on the new stone glittered where the old stone only darkened. Muken had looked at it, the morning it was finished, and said they should leave it that way. That every man who came up the Kessen road should see where the wall had come down.

The gate itself was new timber, raw pine, still weeping sap in the cold, and the sap froze where it ran in beads as gold as amber. Somebody had nailed the Stark wolf to it, and the Greymane wolf beside it, and above both of them, on a long pole over the gatehouse, a banner that had not existed a week ago.

It was white. Plain white wool, the cheapest the fort's stores had, unbleached and unembroidered, with nothing on it at all. The boys of the vanguard had made it the night after the crow came up from the south with the news of Ulf and Wystan, sitting in the barracks with needles borrowed from Yukazuri's kit, because nobody could agree what should go on it, and in the end they had decided that a tokenless king ought to have a banner with no token on it either. It hung over the gate of Kharven's Foot in the still morning, blank as new snow, against a sky going from rose at the ridge-line to a hard clean blue overhead.

Lorn Stark stood under it in his father's mail, which was too big for him across the shoulders, with his father's axe across his back, and watched the road.

He was twenty-two. Four weeks ago he had been his father's son and nothing more, with Hollenmark a long way off and nothing on his mind but his next hunt. Now he was the Warlord of House Stark, by his father's word on a cold road and his father's death at the Seat, and he had not yet learned what to do with his hands while he waited.

He saw the riders a long time before they reached him. Twenty-odd in plain grey, coming up the long road from the south at a steady walk, without banners. And in the middle of them, impossible to mistake even at a mile, a great black shape that moved like water over the snow and was too big for any horse.

He did not move until they were at the foot of the gate.

Then he straightened, the way his father had taught him, the way every Stark son was taught before he could walk: chin up, shoulders back, weight even on both feet, hands still. And he watched them come.

The woman in white came first. She dismounted at the foot of the gate with the ease of someone who had been riding for a long time, and her near-white robes were grey with the dust and slush of the road to the hem, and the glass beads of a veil were pinned back from her face and clicked faintly at her temples when she moved, throwing small sparks of the sun. She had a katana at her hip, bound in white cord. She looked up at the blank banner over the gate, and kept looking, before she looked at him.

Behind her came the two others. A great bald man in copper and grey, the copper gone red-bronze in the low sun, who moved like a bull and dismounted like one, heavily, with a grunt. And a slight sharp man in indigo with restless fingers, who had his eyes on the mended seam in the wall before his boots touched the snow.

Lorn bowed exactly as low as a Warlord of the north bowed to a Moto lord, which was very little.

"Lady Kiyome," he said.

She inclined her head.

He turned. "Lord Tetsugen." The bald man grunted. "Lord Hayato." The slight man bowed, quickly, and his eyes went straight back to the wall.

Then Lorn looked past them.

The great black cat had stopped a little way down the road and lain down in the snow like a dog, with its yellow eyes half closed and its long ivory fangs resting on its paws, and the rider had dismounted beside it. Lorn had heard the outrider's account. He had not quite believed it. He believed it now. The Zettari was the biggest man he had ever seen, half a head taller than Wystan Greymane had been, in a black-and-gold robe that hung open over a chest like a barrel of nails, and he was standing in the snow by his cat looking up at the gate of Kharven's Foot, and he was, Lorn thought, very young.

Lorn let himself look. He thought about the forty years. He thought about every Stark who had gone south on a Moto levy and come home in a cart, or not come home. He thought about the Zettari spears they had faced on those fields.

Then he thought about his father's hand flat on his chest in the gatehouse, and Muken's face, and what Godfrey had said.

*If you're going to be a king, act like one.*

The same went for a king's men.

"Prince," said Lorn Stark.

He bowed to the Zettari exactly as low as he had bowed to the Moto. Not one finger's width more or less.

Then he straightened.

"Our lord is waiting for you in the meeting chamber," he said.

Kiyome Byakuya had been looking at the white banner again. Her head came round.

"Lord?" she said.

Lorn nodded.

"Yes," he said. "Lord. And more than lord." He glanced up, once, at the blank white wool over the gate. "The crow came up from the south three days ago with the news from the Seat. My father, and Lord Wystan." His voice did not change. He had practised it. "The next morning every Stark and every Greymane in this fort knelt in the yard by the woodpile. Nobody told us to. Nobody gave an order. The boys of the vanguard started it, and then the old men, and then all of us." He paused. "There are no Banner Houses yet, my lady. There's no crown, and no Seat, and no chair, and no rite. But the north has a king. We've chosen him."

He looked at her steadily.

"Muken is king now," said Lorn Stark. "King Muken. The Tokenless."

Something moved in Kiyome's grey eyes. The fine silver rings in them, which had lain quiet as pond-ice the whole time she had been looking at the banner, turned once, very slowly, and were still again.

She did not say anything.

Lorn turned. "This way," he said, and led them in.

---

He took them through the gate and across the yard. It had been swept, mostly. The dead were gone from it, buried on the ridge in a long row under cairns of stone from the breach, and the burned stables had been pulled down and their timber stacked for the fires, and the slush had been shovelled into banks along the walls. But there were still dark places in the trampled snow by the well that no amount of shovelling had got out, and the barracks door still had the marks of axes on it, and the long pale wall of split wood still ran along the barracks wall as high as a man's shoulder, with children carrying more to it.

Lorn did not hurry, and did not look back at them. He walked the way his father had walked, the way the Starks walked through their own halls: heavy, even, unhurried, with his weight going straight down through his heels into the ground and his hands still at his sides. A well-trained warrior's walk. He had trained it every day since he was six years old. He had never once thought, until this morning, that it would matter more than any sword stroke he had ever learned.

He could feel the eyes of the fort on them as they crossed the yard. The Greymane men on the walls. The boys at the woodpile, with their arms full of split pine, stopped dead. The old woman with no teeth on her stump by the barracks door, in her three shawls, watching the woman in white go by with her toothless mouth set in a long hard line.

The whole of Kharven's Foot had gone silent, except for the wind, and the creak of the new gate, and a child somewhere splitting wood, three blows for every split.

### III · The King of the Foot

The meeting chamber had been the Tenrai garrison's mess hall. It was the largest room in the keep, long and low and stone-floored, with a hearth at one end big enough to roast an ox in, where a fire of split pine was burning orange and loud and throwing its colour halfway down the floor. The long tables had been pushed back against the walls. In their place, in the middle of the floor, someone had set one table from the barracks, rough pine with a split down the middle, and round it a scatter of mismatched chairs. Shigure Tenrai's carved chair from the gatehouse. A bench from the stables. Two camp stools. A barrel.

At the head of it, with his back to the great hearth, sat the king of the north.

Muken had not changed his coat. He still wore the dead sergeant's coat, cut down to fit him, brown wool gone the colour of tea, with the darns at the cuffs in his sister's neat small stitches. But there was mail under it now, Ulf Stark's own mail, taken in at the sides by the Stark armourer until it nearly fitted. The scrap-iron sword lay across the table in front of him, unsheathed, with its black pitted blade turned edge-away. The seam on his cheek from the Vessant road had healed to a long raised pink line from his jaw to the corner of his eye. His left arm was out of its sling at last, though he still held it a little close to his side. His white hair was tied back with the same strip of leather. On his belt, hanging where every merchant in Halcrest wore his oldest contract, where anybody who looked could see it and nobody would see it hum, hung a small bronze seal-token stamped with the Stark wolf.

Ulf Stark's wolfhound lay across his feet under the table, enormous and grey, with its chin on his boot.

Godfrey Greymane sat at his right hand, on the bench from the stables, very straight, in grey, with the Greymane wolf on his breast and his long hands folded on the rough pine. There were still dark circles under his grey eyes. There would be for a long time.

Muken was looking at the door.

He had been looking at it since the outrider came in from the wall to say the riders were at the gate. He had not moved in all that time. Godfrey had told him not to. *Sit,* Godfrey had said, an hour ago, standing in this room while the boys dragged the furniture about. *Sit, and stay sat, and let them come to you. Don't get up when they come in. Don't go to meet them. You're not a sellsword waiting to be hired any more. They've come up the Kessen road to you. Let them walk the last of it.*

So he sat. It was the hardest thing he had done all week.

He could feel it under his breastbone, the black hot thing that had come up in him in the gatehouse when the outrider first said *Moto* and *Zettari* in one breath. It was quieter now. He had been sitting on it for two days, the way you sit on a frightened horse, and it had stopped trying to throw him. But it was there. It sat behind his left eye like a banked coal, and every time he thought of Ulf Stark riding down that road in the dark, it glowed.

He turned his head, slightly, to Godfrey.

"Hopefully this won't take long," he said, low. "The preparations are almost done. I want the columns on the ice road the morning after next, and the Keld won't wait for us to finish being polite."

Godfrey did not look away from the door.

"Yes, sire," he said. "They are almost done."

There was a very small pause.

"And the Keld will wait exactly as long as it takes," said Godfrey Greymane, "for you to learn what these people came all this way to tell you." He still did not look at Muken. "My father used to say that a lord who's in a hurry to end a meeting usually leaves it knowing less than when he came in." His long grey hands did not move on the table. "Let them talk, sire. Let them say all of it. You can be in a hurry afterward."

Muken looked at him sideways for a moment.

"You sound like him," he said.

"I've been told." Godfrey's mouth moved very slightly at the corner. "It's the only thing he left me that the Tenrai couldn't take."

Muken did not answer. He looked back at the door.

Under the table, the wolfhound lifted its great grey head off his boot, and its ears came forward, and it looked at the door too.

Footsteps in the passage. Lorn's, heavy and even, which Muken knew. And behind them others that he didn't: a light quick tread on the stone, and a heavy dragging one, and a soft restless one that kept stopping and starting again. And under all of them, very faint, through the stone of the floor, a slow heavy weight, as if something enormous were walking very carefully in a passage built for smaller things.

The door opened.

Lorn Stark came in first and stepped aside, as Godfrey had told him to, and stood against the wall with his hands still at his sides.

And the woman in white came through the door after him.

Muken had been ready for a Moto lord. He had built one in his head for two days: someone hard and narrow and cold, in gold and black, with rings turning in their eyes the way they had turned in Shigure's on the stair. He had been ready to sit like stone and look at that face across his table and not let the black hot thing out from behind his eye.

He was not ready for her.

She stopped just inside the door and looked down the long room at him, and the pale gold morning light from the narrow windows came across her, and he saw her hair.

It was white. Silver-white, whiter than the robes, a white so clean it looked deliberate.

He had seen that white every day of his life.

He had seen it on the pillow beside him in the cart-sized room under Halcrest when his sister was small and frightened of the dark. He had seen it braided down her back in the room over the tea-house and pinned up off her neck at the surgeons' line under the Foot. He had seen it this morning, at dawn, when she came to change the dressing on his side.

He had seen it on his mother. Bending over him in the dark of a gallery when he was small, with a lamp behind her, so that her hair caught the light and seemed to burn. Drawing a mark on a wall with a burned stick and saying *that's ours.* Lying on the pallet in the last gallery with her face to the salt, the morning the news came down from Shimane, gone white as the wall.

Muken did not get up.

He did not know how he did not. Every muscle in his body had gone to stand. But Godfrey's long grey hand had come down on the rough pine beside his own, flat, not touching him, only there, and he stayed in his chair.

The coal behind his left eye had flared, all at once, white-hot, and the edges of the room went gold.

The woman in white was looking at him. At his face. At his eyes. At the left one. And her own face, which had been calm and composed as still water all the way down the long room, did something very small, and broke, and was mended again so fast that only a man looking straight at her would have seen it.

He was looking straight at her.

Behind her, the bald man in copper and the slight man in indigo had stopped in the doorway. And behind them, stooping under the lintel, came the Zettari, enormous, in black and gold.

Muken did not look at any of them.

He sat at the head of his split pine table, with the scrap-iron sword across it and the dead token on his belt and Ulf Stark's wolfhound standing now beside his chair with its hackles up, and looked at the white-haired woman by the door, and did not say any of the things that were in his mouth.

What he said, in the end, was very quiet.

"You'll forgive the chairs," said King Muken. "The Tenrai took the good ones." He nodded at the scatter of benches and stools and barrels round the table. "Sit. All of you. You've come a long way up a cold road to see me, and I've a war to march to the morning after next." He paused. "So say what you came to say."

## Author Notes

**What this is.** Isaac's two scenes, every line and beat kept and in his order, expanded into Chapter Nine. **I** is Aksel Keld and Kagenori in the Keld war-lodge. **II** is Lorn at the gate. **III** is the meeting chamber, stopping where the guests enter. Past tense, third person: close on Aksel in I, on Lorn in II, on Muken in III. About 5,100 words of prose.

**Your beats, kept.**
- **I.** Aksel Keld, the Iron Fist, one of the youngest and greatest warlords, countless battles won against bigger and better-prepared armies, ruling with an iron fist. "We march in two days." Kagenori: he'll rip the heart out of the Starks and Greymane who follow Muken, because once they learn his heritage no one will follow him. Aksel finds it nefarious but true, since nearly every people has lost faith in the Kōkan. "If you keep your promise you've nothing to worry about; the path and will of the Keld will win this for the True King of the North." Kagenori laughs: "You honour me... I have not become the king yet." His ambition isn't for himself but to make a kingdom for Shizuha; the Shirogane are the Kōkan's old rival and have the most to gain. "All will accept Queen Shizuha's rule; if not they'll die. I hope you understand." "The queen's will is absolute."
- **II.** Lorn waits for the Moto lord and the young Zettari prince. Kiyome comes out first with Tetsugen and Hayato behind. Lorn greets each, then "Prince." "Our lord is waiting in the meeting chambers." Kiyome: "Lord?" Lorn: "Yes. Muken is king now. King Muken, the Tokenless." Lorn's warrior's bearing.
- **III.** Godfrey sits beside Muken. "Hopefully this won't take long; the preparations for the war campaign are almost finished." "Yes they are, sire."

**One spelling I changed.** You wrote **"Queen Shizuka"**. The Shirogane head was named **Shizuha** in the council scenes, so I used Shizuha. If you meant to rename her Shizuka, or meant a different woman, tell me and I'll swap it through.

**Dougou is never written.** He appears only as Lorn and Muken see him: the cat lying in the snow, his size, his robe, how young he looks, stooping under the lintel. No lines, actions, thoughts or reactions. Lorn bows to him exactly as low as to the Moto, and that's Lorn's choice.

**Originated here (yours to keep or strike):**

- **Lorn.** Twenty-two, Warlord by his father's word and death, in his father's mail with his father's axe.

# The Uncounted, Chapter Sixteen: Tsugigane

## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Sixteen: Tsugigane

---

> *Ask a Dawi what a blade is and he will tell you it is a decision. The iron does not want to be a sword. It wants to be ore, asleep in the mountain, as it was before anyone dug it up. Every fold is an argument against that wish, and the sword is only what is left when the argument is won.*
>
> *From the sayings of the forge-halls of Varrak-Thur, as written down in Stannvaard*

---

### I · The Cairn

They buried her on the ridge at first light, in the row with the dead of the Foot.

The ground was frozen as hard as iron, and there was no digging it. There never was, in the north, between the first frost and the thaw, and so the Kharven did what the Kharven had always done in winter: they laid their dead on the bare rock and built over them. Stone from the breach. The same pale gray-white stone the Foot had carried up the hill course by course to mend its wall, the stone that still lay in heaps in the yard where the masons had left it. Every soul in the fort who could walk carried one stone up the ridge path, in their arms or on their backs, in a long silent file through the snow, and laid it on the cairn, and walked down again for another.

Muken laid the first.

He had carried her up himself, in Godfrey's gray cloak, because nobody would have dared offer to do it for him. He had laid her down on the bare rock at the end of the row, where the ridge looked south over the Kessen road and the white country beyond it toward the Seat, and he had knelt beside her while the sky over the Kessen road went from blue to rose, with his hand on the gray wool, and then he had got up, and gone back down the path, and come back up it with a stone of the mend in his arms so heavy that his strapped ribs tore loose under the linen and he had to stop twice on the path to breathe. And he had set it down at her head.

Then the others came.

Lorn. Godfrey. Kiyome Byakuya, carrying her stone in both hands against her near-white robes as if it were a child. Tetsugen Akagane, who carried two, one in each fist, and set them down without a word. Hayato, whose stone was too big for him and who would not let anybody help. Rob and Mark Stark. The two Greymane women from the surgeons' line, and the Stark boy who could carry, and every Greymane in his long gray file, and every Stark rider who had come back off the Skeldmere, and the boys who had sewn the banner. The women from the long houses. Their children, carrying stones the size of their fists. The old woman with no teeth, who could not climb the ridge path on her two sticks and was carried up it on a door by four Stark riders, and who sat on the door at the top with a stone in her lap no bigger than a hen's egg, and when it was her turn, leaned over and set it down on the cairn very carefully, in the exact center, where it would be seen.

By the time the sun cleared the curtain wall, the cairn stood as high as a man's chest, pale against the snow, and the first light lay on it warm and pink as the inside of a shell, and every rime-crusted stone in it sparkled. There was nothing on it. No name. No mark. The Kharven did not carve names on cairns; the Waiting kept names in mouths.

But when everyone else had gone back down the ridge, Muken stayed.

He knelt at the head of it in the snow, and took off his right glove, and with the end of his forefinger, slowly, in the frost that had already begun to form on the face of the stone he had carried, he drew a mark. A long down-stroke. A hook. A crossing. A small closed loop at the foot of it like a knot in a rope.

His hand had never once been able to make it come out right in sixteen years, on the gallery wall or the tea-house window or in the dust of the Tenrai mess. She had always laughed at him for it.

It came out right.

He knelt and looked at it a long time, the mark in the frost, perfect, the way she had always drawn it. And then the sun came round onto the face of the stone, and the frost began to go, and the mark went with it, line by line, until there was only gray stone, wet and shining in the sun, and nothing on it at all.

He put his glove back on, and got up, and went down the ridge.

### II · The Dawi

The forgeman came up the Kessen road at noon on the same day, alone, on foot, with an anvil on his back.

The Greymane watch on the wall saw him a mile off and did not know what they were seeing. A short dark shape on the white road, very broad, moving at a steady trudge that did not change on the hills, with something on its back so big and so square that it looked from a distance as if a piece of the road had stood up and was walking north. When he came closer they saw that it was a man, and then that it was not a man, and then that the thing on his back was an anvil of black iron the size of a small table, strapped to a frame of oak and iron, and that he was carrying it the way another man might carry a pack of blankets.

He stopped at the foot of the gate and looked up at the blank white banner, cracking in the wind against a hard blue sky, and took his time about it.

Then he looked at the mend in the wall, the long pale seam of new stone running down through the old, and something in his broad ruddy face moved, very slightly, as if he did not entirely approve of the coursing. Then he put back his head and called up to the gatehouse in a voice like a rockfall in a deep mine.

"*I'm told there's a Byakuya in there,*" he called, "*with a blade on her hip that I made, and she's untied the knot on it.*"

---

They brought him into the long room because there was nowhere else big enough.

He set the anvil down by the great hearth, and the stone floor of the old Tenrai mess cracked under it, a long fine line from the hearthstone to the table, and he looked at the crack and grunted as though it were the floor's fault. He was a head and a half shorter than Godfrey Greymane and twice as wide. Barrel-chested and heavy through the trunk, with arms that hung at a slight bow as if they were still holding something, and a face flushed so deep across the nose and cheekbones that it looked like the color had been baked into it in a kiln. In the firelight his forearms and knuckles had a faint granular sheen, like stone. His hair was black streaked with iron-gray, pulled back in tight braids. His beard was the main event: charcoal and gray, thick enough to insulate a chimney, braided into three great trunks bound with old rings of a pale gray metal that did not catch the firelight so much as hold it, each one etched round with fine angular marks.

Kiyome Byakuya came in, and stopped, and stared at him.

"Master Ironheart," she said.

"Lady Byakuya." The Dawi did not bow. Dawi did not bow, as far as anyone in the north knew. He looked at her, and then down, at the katana bound in white cord at her hip, and at the white cord itself, which was no longer bound. It hung loose from the hilt in the firelight. "You've untied it."

"I have."

"Three hundred and twelve years," said Borin Ironheart, "that knot's been on that sword. Your grandmother's grandmother's grandmother came down into the Varrak-Thur on her own two feet to commission it, and she stood at my anvil for sixteen hours while I worked, and when it was done she tied that knot round the guard with her own hands and swore an oath over it I could feel in the steel." He folded his great arms. "And nine days ago, in the middle of the night, at the bottom of my mountain, I felt it come undone." He looked at her with eyes like wet slate. "A Dawi forge remembers what it makes. I've felt that oath on that blade every day for three centuries the way you feel your own heartbeat. Then it stopped. I wanted to know why. So I came."

"Four hundred miles," said Kiyome softly. "On foot."

"I don't care for horses." Borin looked round the long room, at the split pine table and the mismatched stools and the barrel. "And I don't care for being kept waiting. Why did you untie it?"

Kiyome did not answer him. She turned her head.

Muken was standing in the doorway.

He had come down from the ridge and gone to the smithy, and the Stark armorer had told him, and he had come. He stood in the door of the long room in the dead sergeant's coat with his strapped ribs and his stitched forearm and the snow of the ridge still white on his boots, and his face gave nothing, and the Dawi turned round and looked at him.

Borin Ironheart looked at him as he would have looked at a bar of steel come out of a stranger's forge.

He looked at the white hair and the gray eyes and the gold seam in the left one. He looked at the boy's hands. At the way he stood. And then, slowly, he unfolded his arms and took one step toward him, and stopped, and his broad ruddy face did something it had perhaps not done in a long time. It went quiet.

"Ah," said the Dawi.

He did not say anything else for a moment.

"Your oath came home," he said at last, without looking away from the boy. "Didn't it, Lady Byakuya. The red line before the white. Until the end of the red line." His slate eyes were very steady. "I'd heard the red line had ended."

"Not yet," said Kiyome Byakuya.

"No." Borin Ironheart was still looking at Muken. "No, I can see it hasn't." He tilted his great head. "You've a crack in you, boy. Under the breastbone. I can feel it from here, the way I'd feel one in a bar of steel. It's hot." A pause. "It's very old. Older than you."

Muken said nothing.

"My father's," he said at last.

"Aye," said Borin. "And his father's. And his." He looked at the boy a moment longer. Then he turned away, as if he had seen what he came to see, and looked at the hearth. "Well. I've walked four hundred miles. Somebody had better feed me before I start breaking things."

"Master Ironheart," said Muken.

The Dawi stopped.

"Our armorer says you looked at my sword. In the smithy. When you came in."

"I did." Borin did not turn round. "Scrap iron. Bad scrap iron, forged by men who didn't know what they were doing, and then reforged, and reforged again, a hundred times, by men who knew even less. Sharpened so many times there's hardly a blade left. And now it's in three pieces." He shrugged one heavy shoulder. "I've seen nails I respected more."

"It held a note," said Muken. "On the ice. It held it until it broke."

Borin Ironheart turned round slowly.

"Did it now," he said.

"I need a new one," said Muken. "Made out of the old. All three pieces. And I need it made in a day."

The Dawi looked at him, and his beard twitched.

Then he laughed.

The laugh had no cruelty in it. It came up out of the great barrel of his chest like full casks rolling down a cellar ramp, low and rolling and enormous, and it went on for some time, and the fire in the hearth leaned toward it.

"A day," said Borin Ironheart, when he had finished. "A *day.* Out of three pieces of scrap. Boy, I've spent forty years on a single blade. I spent sixteen hours on that one" (he jerked his beard at Kiyome's hip) "and that was a short job, and I slept two days after it." He shook his head. "You don't know what you're asking."

"I know what it'll cost," said Muken. "I'll pay it."

"Not in coin, you won't. You haven't any."

"No," said Muken. "Not in coin."

He reached inside the dead sergeant's coat, slowly, with his stitched right arm, and brought out something small, wrapped in a strip of linen, and held it out on his open palm.

Borin looked at it. He did not move to take it.

Muken unwrapped the linen.

There were seven needles in it. Thin steel surgeon's needles of three different lengths, two of them curved, one of them bent very slightly at the tip where it had been used once too often on something tougher than skin. And with them, still threaded through the eye of the longest one, a short end of gut, gone dark.

"My sister's," said Muken.

The long room was very quiet.

"She was a surgeon," said Muken. "Thirteen. The best in the north. She closed half the men in this fort with these, and some of them twice." He did not look away from the Dawi. "A man called Yusif Stark gave her the first one when she was five. He told her more men would live because of a needle than would ever die because of a sword." He breathed. "She's on the ridge now. Under the stones. The Shirogane sent her home to me down the river."

He held them out.

"I want them in it," said Muken. "Folded in. In the marrow of the blade, where the old crack was, by the guard, where it broke. So that the place it broke is the place it holds." His voice did not change. "That's what I'm asking."

Borin Ironheart looked down at the seven needles on the boy's palm.

He did not look up from them. And something went across his broad ruddy face, fast, and was gone, the way a crack goes across ice when the weight comes down on it, and when it was gone his face was different. Older. Something in it that his students in the Varrak-Thur had a name for, and would not have said in front of him.

He held out his hand.

It was enormous, square, granular-skinned, scarred across the palm and the knuckles by four centuries of hot iron, and it was quite steady. Muken laid the needles in it, linen and gut and all, and Borin closed his fingers over them as gently as if they might fly.

"What's the blade to be called?" said the Dawi.

"Tsugigane," said Muken.

Borin looked up at him.

"Joined iron," said the Dawi slowly. "Aye. And the other meaning." He turned the closed hand over, very slightly, as if feeling the weight of what was in it. "*Tsugu.* To join. To mend a break. And to take up what someone left you. To inherit." His slate eyes held the boy's. "You know both, don't you."

"I know both."

Borin Ironheart nodded once.

"Then you know what you're asking," he said. "A blade with a name like that isn't a sword. It's a legend, or it's nothing. There's no middle way with a name like that." He closed his fist on the needles. "I don't make nothing."

He turned to the hearth.

"Get me charcoal," he said, to the room at large, without raising his voice. "All the charcoal in this fort. And every bellows. And clean snow, a cartload, packed in a trough by the anvil. And somebody to work the bellows who won't stop when his arms go, because they will." He was already shrugging off his coat. "And get that boy out of my sight. I don't want him breathing on it."

He looked back over his shoulder at Muken, once.

"A day," said Borin Ironheart. "You'll have it at dawn the day after tomorrow, and not an hour sooner. And I'll sleep for two days after, and if anyone wakes me I'll break their arms." His beard moved. "What will you do with your day?"

### III · All Will Be Revealed

Muken looked round the long room.

At Lorn Stark by the door, in his father's mail, with his jaw working on the left side. At Godfrey Greymane by the hearth in his shirt, because his cloak was on the ridge under the cairn and he had not yet found another. At Kiyome Byakuya, standing very straight with the white cord hanging loose from her hilt and her eyes swollen. At Tetsugen Akagane, broad as a door, with his scarred hands on his belt. At Hayato Amagiri, with his wet indigo cape and the juniper still caught in the toggle, his restless fingers for once quite still.

He looked at each of them, one after another. Then he spoke.

"I'll be ready in a day," said Muken.

His voice was quiet, lower and slower than the gallery voice, and each word in it carried weight, like stones laid one on another on a cairn.

"One day," he said. "And then all will be revealed. And all of this will come to an end." His gray eyes did not move from their faces. "The Tenrai. The Shirogane. The silver chair. All of it."

The fire ticked in the hearth.

"I must find myself," said Muken. "First. Before anything. I've been cutting at nothing in the snow for two days and the world's been cracking under my feet, and I don't yet know how to stop it. I have to learn. And I have to heal." He touched his strapped side, once, very lightly. "I can't take the Seat with two ribs stove in and an arm that won't close. Before the battle, I have to be whole." He was quiet a moment. "Or as whole as I'm going to be."

"Sire," said Godfrey quietly. "The Seat's three days' march. The Tenrai have the Hall and the cliff and the conduits, and whatever's left of the Keld. They'll have had a week to wall themselves in by the time we reach them. And they'll know we're coming. They sent her down the river to make sure of it." He did not look away. "Forgive me. But how can you know we'll win?"

Muken held his eyes.

"Because the tides of fate have shown me," he said.

He said it quite simply. He did not raise his voice. He said it the way another man might have said *because it's going to snow*.

"I've seen it," said Muken. "I don't know how else to say it to you. Every crack in the Seat. Every flaw in their walls and their wards and their oaths. Every Tenrai lord who's stood behind Kagenori these twenty years and hated every minute of it. They're humming, all of them. I can hear them from here." His eyes went, very briefly, to the window, and south, toward the ridge and the white country and the road. "The Seat is already broken, Godfrey. It's been broken since the night she died. It only hasn't fallen yet." He looked back. "We will win. I'm not asking you to believe it. I'm telling you what I've seen."

The long room was silent.

Kiyome Byakuya was staring at him.

She had heard a great many Moto speak in her life. She had heard them in the Hall of Verdicts and the white house and the councils of the seven lines, lords and ladies and heirs, arguing and threatening and lying and bargaining. She had heard her grandmother speak over the lamp at the hour of the evening bell, the oath of the white line, in the old high register of the house that the children were taught before they could walk. And once, long ago, across a field under a flag of truce, she had heard the voice of a Kōkan king.

She had never heard anything like this.

The words were simple. The weight was in the thing under them. The way every one of them seemed to settle into the air of the room and stay there, the way a stone settles into snow. It was the voice of a man who was not telling you what he wanted to happen, or what he hoped would happen, or what he would make happen if he could. He was telling you what was going to happen, because he had seen it, the way you would tell a man the river was frozen because you had walked across it.

*That is a Moto,* thought Kiyome Byakuya. *That is a Moto speaking. The first I have heard in my life. Seven lines forgive us, that is what we were supposed to sound like.*

She did not say it. She could not have said it.

She looked at Tetsugen. The old Akagane lord was staring at the boy from under his thick gray brows with his heavy face gone slack with something near wonder, and his scarred hands had come off his belt. She looked at Hayato. Hayato's lips were moving slightly, as if he were trying to work out the distance to something.

"I don't understand," said Lorn Stark.

He said it roughly, from the door, without shame.

"I'll be honest with you, sire, I don't understand a word of that. Tides of fate." He shook his head slowly. "But I'll have the vanguard ready in a day. And whatever you need. Name it."

"I need you to keep everyone out of the death-house," said Muken.

They all looked at him.

"The death-house," said Muken. "It's empty now. It's the coldest room in the Foot and the quietest, and nobody goes into it unless somebody's died." He breathed. "I'm going in there. And I need nobody to come in after me until I come out, whatever they hear, whatever they see under the door. Not you, Lorn, nor Godfrey, nor my aunt." He looked at Kiyome. "Not even if I stop breathing."

"Sire..." said Godfrey.

"Not even then," said Muken. "There's someone I have to go and see. And it isn't anywhere you can follow."

He looked at them all once more. Then he turned, and walked out of the long room, past Borin Ironheart, who was standing by his anvil with his coat off and his great arms folded and seven needles still closed in one fist, watching him go with his slate eyes narrowed. And across the yard. And into the death-house in the north wall, where the sun never reached and the stone shelf at the back was empty now, with only a faint dark stain on it where she had lain. And he shut the door behind him.

They heard the bar go down on the inside.

### IV · The Forge

Borin Ironheart forged Tsugigane through that day and that night and the next, and the Foot did not sleep.

They had never seen anything like it. Nobody in the north had. The Stark armorer, who had been beating out horseshoes and mail-rings at Hollenmark for thirty years and thought himself a fair smith, stood in the door of the long room for the first hour with his mouth open, and then went away and sat on the woodpile with his head in his hands, and then came back and asked, humbly, if he could work the bellows.

Borin let him. He let six of them, in shifts: the Stark armorer, two Greymane masons, Rob Stark, Tetsugen Akagane, who took off his copper coat and worked a bellows for four hours without stopping or speaking, and the boy with the red ears, who could not be made to go away. They worked the great bellows of the Foot's smithy, carried into the long room and set either side of the hearth, until their arms gave out and the next shift took over, and the fire in the hearth went from cherry red to a roaring orange to a yellow-white so bright that nobody could look at it, and stayed there, throwing the shadows of the bellows-men huge and leaping up the old Tenrai beams and turning every face in the room the color of new brass. It did not flicker. It wanted no tending. It simply burned at the heat the Dawi wanted, as though it knew.

He began with the three pieces of the old sword.

He laid them on the anvil, side by side, the hilt and the long broken middle and the point, and put his great granular hands on them, and closed his eyes, and stood like that for an hour without moving or speaking. The men at the bellows looked at each other.

"He's listening," said Kiyome Byakuya quietly, from the doorway, where she had stood since he began. "A Dawi reads iron the way we read a face. He's finding out what it was before it was a sword."

When he opened his eyes, he took the pieces off the anvil and put them in the fire.

He did not forge them. Not at first. He took the old scrap iron apart. He heated each piece until it glowed, deep cherry and then orange and then the yellow of a buttercup, and folded it, and beat it out, and folded it again, and watched the scale come off it in black flakes and the slag run out of it in bright spitting threads that skittered across the stone floor like shooting stars and winked out, and folded it again. Over and over. The men at the bellows lost count. The iron got smaller each time, and grayer, and quieter, and the sound it made under the hammer changed, from a dull clatter like a pot lid to a clear high ring like a bell.

"Bad scrap," said Borin, once, to no one, as he drew a glowing bar out of the fire. "Bad scrap, from a dozen different smelts. Some of it's older than this fort." He turned the bar in the tongs, looking at it. "Some of it's very old. Some of it's had blood in it more than once." He put it back in the fire. "It's all got to come out. Everything that isn't iron. Everything that was put in by men who didn't know what they were doing." He folded it again. "Then we'll see what's left."

By midnight of the first day, what was left was a single bar of gray steel no longer than a man's forearm and no thicker than two fingers, ringing under the hammer like a struck glass.

"That's it?" said Rob Stark, at the bellows, staring at it. "That's all that was in it?"

"That's all that was worth keeping," said Borin Ironheart. "Most things are mostly not."

He laid the bar on the anvil and let it cool, and then he took the linen out of his shirt.

---

He did not let anyone watch the folding of the needles.

He sent the bellows-men out of the long room. He sent Kiyome out. He barred the door behind them, and the Foot stood in the yard in the snow and the dark with the light of the forge coming yellow-white through the cracks round the door and the narrow windows, and listened to the hammer.

It went on for six hours. It was slower than the earlier hammering, and lighter, and very even, a single stroke and then a long pause and then another, the rhythm of a man working at the very edge of what his hands could do and not daring to hurry. Sometimes there was a sound that was not hammering, a long hiss like water thrown on hot stone, or a high thin ringing that went on and on and made the old woman with no teeth, sitting on her door in the yard, put her hands over her ears. Once, near the fourth hour, the light through the cracks went out entirely, and the yard was dark, and nobody breathed, and then it came back, brighter.

And once, toward the end, they heard the Dawi speak. It was in no tongue they knew, low and harsh and very old, and it went on and on, and it sounded like a man arguing with something that was arguing back.

At the sixth hour the hammering stopped, and the door opened, and Borin Ironheart stood in it with the forge-light behind him, and he was gray to the lips.

"Bellows," he said. "Now. I'm going to quench it."

---

He quenched it in the snow.

The Stark armorer quenched in water and the Moto smiths of the south in oil. Borin had them pack the long trough by the anvil with clean snow from the top of the curtain wall, where nobody had walked, a cartload of it, white and dry and so cold it smoked. And when the blade came out of the fire for the last time, long and slender and curved now like a crescent moon on its side, glowing the color of a winter sunrise from the spine to the edge, he did not plunge it. He laid it down in the snow.

All along its length, all at once.

The sound it made was like nothing anyone in the long room had ever heard. A scream and a roar and a singing all together, and a great white cloud of steam that went up to the beams and filled the room, and through it, very faint, high and clear and sweet, a single note that went on after everything else had stopped. On and on. Quieter and quieter. Until it was only a hum, felt in the teeth and the long bones more than heard.

When the steam cleared, Borin Ironheart was on his knees by the trough with both his hands flat on its edge and his head bowed, breathing like a man who had run up the ridge path with a stone in his arms.

Tetsugen Akagane went to him and put a hand under his arm. The Dawi let him.

"Is it done?" said Tetsugen.

"It's done," said Borin Ironheart. "The hard part. Now it's only polishing and the hilt and the saya, and any fool can do those, and I'm the fool who's going to." He did not get up. "Give me a moment, Akagane. I've just argued with three hundred years of bad iron and a dead girl's needles and the boy's cursed blood, all at once, and I'm not as young as I was."

Tetsugen did not let go of his arm.

"What did they say?" he said. "When you argued."

The Dawi was quiet a long moment.

"The iron wanted to go back to sleep," said Borin. "Iron always does. The blood wanted to break things. It's very old, that blood. Very angry. It's been angry for a thousand years and it doesn't know why any more." He breathed. "And the needles." His great hands moved slightly on the trough. "The needles wanted to close things. That's all. They didn't argue at all. They just kept closing. Every crack I opened, every place the blood wanted to break, the needles went in after it and closed it." He lifted his head and looked at the blade lying in the snow, gray and plain and curved, with a single thin bright line running along it from the guard to a hand's width up the blade, where the old break had been, like a seam of silver in gray stone. "She's in there now. In the marrow. Where it broke." He looked away. "I've made a great many blades, Akagane. Kings' swords. Relics. Things the Guild would sell its own mothers for. I've never made one with a surgeon in it before."

Tetsugen Akagane looked at the blade in the snow, with the copper-red of the forge-light moving on his scarred face.

Then he helped the Dawi up.

---

It took the rest of the night and the morning after for the tempering and the polishing and the fitting, and he did it all himself.

He drew the temper first, laying the blade spine-down over a bed of coals raked out from the hearth and turning it slowly, a finger's breadth at a time, with his eyes a hand from the steel. The colors came up out of the gray and ran along the spine like oil on water: pale straw first, then gold, then a deep bronze, then the purple of a ripe plum, and at the very back of the spine, where he let it go furthest, a clear peacock blue. He watched each color come and stopped it where he wanted it, and the edge he never let past straw. When he plunged it into the snow again, the colors stayed, a band of gold and bronze and blue along the back of the blade that the polishing would take off and that the bellows-men, who saw it, would talk about for the rest of their lives.

The hilt he bound in black rayskin and over it a wrap of plain dark red silk cord, the red of the red line, in the old diamond pattern that Kiyome Byakuya told him, standing at his elbow, the Kōkan had used. The guard he made from the old one, the iron guard of the scrap-iron sword, which had come off it in the breaking: he cleaned it and filed it and polished it until it was dark and plain and round, a simple disk with a single small hole worked through it, off-center, in the shape of a needle's eye. The collar was iron. The pommel cap was iron. There was no gold on it anywhere, and no silver, and no gem.

The saya he carved himself from a single length of black ash out of the Foot's woodpile, the long pale wall of split wood that ran along the barracks, because Muken had once said it had held his whole life up. He lacquered it black, seven coats, and dried each in front of the forge, and bound it at the mouth and the foot with iron. He tied the sageo, the cord that bound it to the belt, in white.

Kiyome had brought him the white cord. It was the cord from her own sword. The knot of her house.

"Are you sure," said Borin, holding it.

"It's his," said Kiyome Byakuya. "It was always his. I only kept it."

The Dawi tied it on.

At dawn on the morning of the second day, he laid Tsugigane in its saya on a length of clean linen on the split pine table in the long room, in front of Shigure Tenrai's carved chair, where the scrap-iron sword had lain. He stood looking at it for a moment with his great arms folded and his slate eyes very tired.

"There," said Borin Ironheart. "It's a legend, or it's nothing. And I don't make nothing."

Then he lay down on the stone floor of the long room in front of the hearth, with his head on his anvil, and was asleep before anyone could bring him a blanket, and the Foot tiptoed round him for two days, and nobody woke him.

---

On the other side of the yard, in the cold of the death-house in the north wall, the bar was still down on the door.

It had been down for a day and a night and a day. Nobody had gone in. Lorn Stark had sat with his back against the outside of the door the whole time, in the snow, in his father's mail, with his father's axe across his knees, and let nobody near it, and slept in snatches where he sat. Twice in the night he had heard something from inside. A voice, very low, that was not Muken's. Once, toward the second dawn, a sound like a great bell being struck very far away.

And all that time, under the door, in the crack between the wood and the stone sill, there had been light.

There was no fire in the death-house. The light was like noon through cloud, steady and warm, with a thread of gold running in it, coming out under the door onto the snow of the yard, so that the snow there had never once frozen in all that time, and lay soft and wet, and in the soft wet snow, if you looked closely, very faint, there were the marks of fine branching cracks, coming and going, coming and going, like breath on a window.

# The Uncounted, Chapter Two: Big Bertha

## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Two: Big Bertha

---

As acting sergeant, Muken knew the cargo was his to get where it was going. Eleven wagons of Vessant iron stock and lamp oil, one covered carriage, twenty-one fighting men of the Lanternless and the company surgeon, and every salt bar of it his responsibility from the Halcrest gate to the river post in the timber. He had known that when he shook Draycott's hand. He had known there would be fighting.

He had not known what kind.

They came out of the trees on the sixth morning, where the road narrowed between two shoulders of granite and the snow lay in the ruts packed hard and grey. There were three of them. At first, through the spruce, he took them for goblins, and for a moment, before he saw them properly, he was nearly relieved. Goblins he knew. Goblins you could frighten, or pay, or kill.

These were wrong.

They had the shape of goblins the way a burned house has the shape of a house. Something had been done to what was inside them. He could feel it from thirty paces, a sick pressure behind the breastbone, the way the air felt near a seal that was going bad in the Cuttings: a sourness, a wrongness of weight, as if the essence in them had gone off like milk and kept them moving anyway. Their skin was black. It was black all over, wet-black, the colour of a lamp's soot when it is fresh and still greasy, and it moved over the muscle beneath like oil over water. Their eyes were small and set too far forward, and they were red. The whites were shot through with crimson, every vessel burst, as if they had been weeping blood for days.

They were covered in blood. Most of it was old and had dried brown and flaked at the joints. Some of it was not. The middle one had something grey and soft hanging from the corner of its mouth, a long string of it, and as Muken watched it slid loose and dropped into the snow, and he knew what it was, because he had seen the inside of a man's head before. Brain. It had been eating somebody's brain on the way here and it had not finished.

The biggest was half again the height of a man. Its shoulders were humped like a bear's and its arms hung to its knees, and its face had come out of the corruption as something that should never have been put together: the broad heavy muzzle and lower jaw of a bear, full of yellow teeth, and set into the front of it, where the nose should be, a hooked black beak like a hawk's, as long as a man's hand and sharp as a gaff. The second was nearer a man's size, long and narrow, with fingers that ended in claws as long as table knives, and from the claws of its right hand hung a pale, wet, rope-like sheet of something that Muken's eye skated off twice before his mind would name it. Gut. The lining of somebody's belly, still hooked on where it had been pulled out.

The third was small. It came no higher than his hip. It was the fastest of the three, and it was the one he liked least.

*A fucking demon*, he thought. *That's a demon. That's what they look like.*

He put two fingers in his mouth and whistled, three short blasts, and four of his men peeled off the column at a run toward the head of it, where the lead wagons were already slewing to a stop and somebody was shouting about the horses. The rest of the line knew the signal and drew in around the wagons. Behind him he heard the carriage door bang open.

He did not need to turn. He knew it would be his sister. She would be leaning out of the door with one hand on the frame and her white hair coming out of its pins, and her face would be doing nothing at all, because that was what it did when she was frightened for him.

"Stay in," he said, without looking round.

"I'm in."

"Stay further in."

The carriage door did not close.

Three men had stayed back with him. Oskar Pell, who was fifty and had a spear and a bad hip and no intention of going anywhere near the front, had already planted himself at the carriage wheel, and Muken left him there. The other two were the Starks.

Rob Stark was the elder by four years, a big rawboned northerner of twenty-six with a beard the colour of wet rust and a face like a closed door, who had come south out of the Stark holdings with his brother two winters back for reasons neither of them had ever told anyone. He wore a mail shirt that had belonged to someone shorter and fought with a boar-spear, a heavy ash haft with an iron crossbar below the head, and a hand axe at his belt. Mark Stark was twenty-two and looked younger, with the same rust in his hair and none in his chin yet, freckled, quick, with a round shield on his left arm and a short sword in his right. He talked when he was frightened. He was talking now, under his breath, a steady stream of it, and Muken could not make out the words and did not try.

The three things on the road had stopped. They stood in a ragged line across the ruts, perhaps twenty paces off, and they were watching. The big one's beak opened and closed with a small wet click.

"Rob," Muken said. His voice came out flat and steady, and he was glad of it. "I'll take the two big ones. You and Mark handle the cub."

The Stark did not complain. The Starks never did. Rob only shifted his grip on the spear, and spat to one side, and said, "Aye," and his brother's muttering stopped.

Muken looked at the two big ones. He reached over his shoulder and drew the scrap-iron sword, the black pitted blade sliding out of the scabbard with a dry rasp, and brought it down and round into both hands and fell into a stance, left foot forward, weight sitting low in the hips, the point at the height of his own throat. He felt the cold of the hilt through the wrappings.

"You two ugly motherfuckers," he told them, "are fighting me."

---

At that time in his life, Muken's essence was nowhere near what it would become. Neither was his aether. He had no school, no teacher and no name for anything he did. What he had was a body that had cut salt from the age of six and swung a sword, of one kind or another, since he was eight, and a trick he had found on his own the way a child finds that it can whistle: when the danger came close enough, he could feel something warm move in him, down out of his chest into his arms and back and legs, and for a little while everything in him was heavier, harder, faster, a little less likely to break. He did not know it was called reinforcement. He did not know that what he could do was the very least of it, a candle's worth against a bonfire. He knew it had kept him alive in other people's wars, and he thought it would be enough to kill these.

He thought.

The big one came first. He named it in his head as it came, the way he named everything that tried to kill him, because it helped. *Big Bertha.* It lurched forward out of the line with a heavy rolling gait, the hump of its shoulders rising and falling, and swung its right arm up and over in a long arc like a mantis bringing down its foreleg, the hooked claws at the end of it falling toward him faster than anything that size had a right to.

His body moved before he decided to. That was how it always went. The warmth flooded down into his legs and his back and he went sideways and round, turning on the ball of his left foot, spinning clear under the falling arm so close that the wind of it lifted his hair. The claws went into the frozen ground where he had been standing and the road broke under them like a dropped plate, chunks of ice and gravel bursting up into the air.

And the other one was already there.

They had done this before. They had done this a great many times. Even as Bertha's blow fell the narrow one had come in low under it from the other side, timing its rush to meet him at the end of his turn, and its right hand came straight for his belly, claws first, the gut-lining still swinging from them, a wet pale flag. It meant to open him the way it had opened the last one. He saw it coming the way you see a cart coming downhill with no driver on it, and there was no time to bring the sword round.

"Nah," Muken said through his teeth. "It won't be that easy."

He pushed hard off his left foot. Big Bertha's arm was still driving down into the road beside him, all that weight still moving, and something in him, something he had no name for yet, read the line of it the way he would read a fault in a salt face before he set the pick: the shoulder, the elbow, the wrist, the planted claws, the whole arm locked straight and loaded like a beam under a roof. There. *That* was the place. He used it. He threw his weight against Bertha's locked forearm as if it were a post set in the ground and swung his right leg out off it, a wild flailing kick with everything his reinforced hips could put behind it, and the heel of his boot came down on the narrow one's wrist from above and drove the claws down past his belly and into the frozen ground, smashing the hand flat against the road.

He felt the small bones in it go under his heel like a fistful of kindling.

The creature shrieked. The sound was high and wet and very nearly a word. Its pinned arm held it bent over for half a breath with its neck stretched out in front of him, long and black and bare, and Muken brought the sword up and round in both hands, over his head and down, and put all of it into the neck.

---

The blade went in a hand's depth and stopped.

He felt it stop. It went through the black skin and through the thick ropes of muscle at the side of the neck, and blood came out round the steel, very dark, almost purple, and smoking faintly in the cold, and then the edge hit the bones of the spine and lodged between two of them with a jolt that went up through his wrists and elbows and into his shoulders like a hammer on an anvil. It did not go through. Scrap iron and a candle's worth of essence did not take a head off in one stroke, whatever the songs said.

The creature did not die. It thrashed. It ripped its broken hand up out of the ground and swung it at him backhanded, and the shattered fingers caught him across the left side of the face and tore his cheek open from the hinge of the jaw to the corner of the eye. He felt the skin part. He felt his own blood go hot down his neck and into his collar. The sword was still stuck in its spine and it was trying to wrench it out of his hands by turning its whole body, and behind him, he knew without looking, Bertha was pulling its claws out of the road.

He put his boot on the narrow one's shoulder and shoved and pulled at the same time, and the blade came free with a sound like a spade coming out of wet clay. Black blood sprayed across the snow in a long fan. The creature staggered, its head lolling to the right where the neck was half cut through, and still it would not go down. It turned toward him, head hanging, crimson eyes rolling, and opened its mouth.

He cut again. Lower, flatter, into the wound he had already made. This time the spine was already parted and the edge went on through the meat and gristle on the far side and the head came off and fell into the snow and rolled once, face up, and the jaws kept working. The body stood for a moment longer with black blood pumping up out of the stump in thick pulses, and then the knees went and it folded down onto them and pitched forward onto its chest.

There was no time to watch it. Bertha's arm was coming round again.

He heard it more than saw it: the hiss of the claws through the air at the height of his head. He threw himself flat. The claws went over him and took the top off a snowbank and sprayed it white across the road. He rolled. The warmth was still in his legs but it was thinner now, a thread of it, and when he came up onto one knee his thighs were shaking as if he had climbed four galleries of the Cuttings with a full load, and there was a coppery taste in his mouth that was not only the blood from his cheek.

*Not much left,* he thought. *Make it count.*

Bertha came on. It came on the way it had come at the start, with that heavy rolling lurch, and this time Muken watched how it moved instead of where its claws were, and he saw it. Every step it took, it came down on the right leg. The left it set down lightly and lifted fast, the way a horse favours a bad hoof. Something in that left leg was wrong: an old break, or a wound from whoever had fought it before him, or just the way the corruption had put it together. Whatever it was, every ounce of that great humped weight was going through the right knee. He could see it now, the knee itself, the joint where all the loading met, standing out to his eye as clearly as a crack in a wall.

The beak opened, the bear-jaw under it opened, and the whole head came down at him like a falling bell.

He went in under it. He went in low and to its right, so close he could smell it, rot and wet fur and something like hot pennies, and he swung the sword in a flat hard arc at knee height with both hands and every scrap of warmth he had left poured into his arms. The edge took Bertha across the front of the right knee, just below the cap, where the thick tendon ran down over the joint, and went in deep and grated on bone.

The knee buckled. The weight on it was too much and the tendon was gone, and the leg simply folded the wrong way, bending forward at the joint with a crack like a green branch, and all of Bertha came down after it. It fell forward onto its hands. Its head came down level with his.

He did not try for the neck this time. He had learned that much. He brought the point up from below, under the hinge of the bear-jaw, where the throat met the skull and the bone was thin, and drove it upward with both hands and his shoulder behind it and his legs straightening under him. The point went in under the jaw and up through the roof of the mouth and on into whatever Bertha had for a brain, and he felt it go through, a soft give and then a harder one, and then nothing.

The hawk's beak opened very wide and stayed open. Black blood ran out of it over his hands. The red eyes rolled up white, or what had been white, and the whole great weight of it sagged down onto the sword, and he could not hold it. He let go of the hilt and stepped back, and Bertha slid down onto its side in the snow with the blade still standing up out of its throat like a stake in a field, and its legs kicked twice, and it was still.

Muken stood there with his empty hands at his sides and his breath going in and out of him in great white plumes. His cheek was hanging open and his own blood had run down inside his coat to his belt. His hands would not stop shaking. The warmth had gone out of him entirely, all at once, the way a lamp goes when the oil is gone, and what it left behind was a cold so deep that his teeth started to chatter.

"Two," he said aloud, to nobody. His voice cracked on it. "That's two, you ugly bastards."

Then he heard Mark Stark scream.

---

The cub had gone for the brothers the moment Bertha moved, and it had been fighting them the whole time behind him, and they had been losing.

It was small. That was the problem. It was small and it was quicker than anything Muken had ever seen move on two legs, and it fought like a stoat in a henhouse, low and fast and everywhere. Rob had met its first rush the right way, the boar-spear levelled and the butt planted in the snow, so that the thing would run itself onto the head. It had not run onto the head. It had gone under the crossbar and up the haft, climbing the spear itself with its claws, hand over hand, as fast as a squirrel up a trunk, and Rob had had to throw the whole spear sideways to shake it off before it reached his hands. It hit the snow and was up again before the spear did.

Mark had come in then, with the shield up and the short sword low, the way they had both been taught by someone a long time ago in a northern yard, and the cub had gone straight at the shield. It took the rim of the round shield in its jaws and bit down, and the wood split under its teeth with a crack, and its head came on through the split, and its jaws closed on Mark's left forearm through the leather of his sleeve.

That was when he screamed.

Muken was already running. His legs were wrong under him, numb and heavy and shaking, and he had no sword, and he was too far away. He saw it all happen in front of him with that terrible clearness that comes in a fight, where everything is slow and there is nothing you can do about any of it.

Mark went down on his back in the snow with the cub on top of him, still clamped to his arm, shaking its head from side to side the way a dog shakes a rat, and with every shake Mark's arm went with it and he screamed again, high and cracked, like a boy. Blood was coming out from between its teeth and running down Mark's sleeve. Its back claws were raking at his belly. The mail shirt was saving him, just; the claws skidded on the rings and snagged and tore and skidded again, and links were flying loose into the snow like spilled coins.

"*Mark!*" Rob roared.

He did not use the spear. There was no room; he would have gone through his brother. He dropped it and drew the hand axe from his belt and came down on the cub from above, both knees on its back, his whole weight on it, and grabbed it by the back of the neck with his left hand. The black skin slid under his fingers like greased leather and he could not hold it. He brought the axe down anyway. The blade hit the cub between the shoulders and bit in and stuck, and the thing let go of Mark's arm and twisted round under Rob's knees with its whole body, impossibly, like something with no bones in it, and sank its teeth into Rob's thigh just above the knee.

Rob did not scream. He made a sound like a man lifting something too heavy, a long grinding groan through clenched teeth, and he wrenched the axe free and hit it again. And again. The axe came down on its back and its shoulders and the side of its head, a butcher's rhythm, and black blood flew up in spatters across Rob's face and beard, and still the thing would not let go of his leg.

Mark was on his knees beside them. His left arm hung at his side, the sleeve torn open and the forearm under it torn open too, so that Muken could see the white of something inside it. His face was grey. He had kept hold of the short sword. He had kept hold of it the whole time. He got his right hand round it and shuffled forward on his knees and put the point against the cub's side, just behind where its foreleg joined the body, and leaned on it with all his weight.

It went in. The short sword went into the cub up to the hilt, and the cub stiffened all over and let go of Rob's leg and opened its mouth, and Mark held it there, leaning on it, sobbing, his face a hand's width from the thing's red eyes, and the cub's claws scrabbled at his chest and his face and his good arm, and he held it there.

"Hold it," Rob said hoarsely. "*Hold it.* Hold it, Mark, hold it, don't you let go."

He took the axe in both hands and raised it high over his head, and brought it down on the cub's neck where it lay pinned between the sword and the snow, and the blade went through and buried itself in the frozen ground beneath. He stayed there on his knees with both hands still on the haft and his head bowed over it, breathing.

The head lay in the snow. Its jaws opened and closed, opened and closed, slower each time, and then stopped open.

Muken reached them at last and went down on his knees beside them because his legs would no longer hold him up. For a while none of them said anything. The three bodies smoked on the road in the cold. The snow around them was black and red and trampled down to the frozen gravel, and here and there in it lay the bright scattered rings of Mark's mail like coins somebody had thrown away.

Mark was crying. He was doing it quietly now, with his face turned away from his brother, holding his ruined arm against his chest with his good hand. Rob was bleeding steadily through his breeches from the bite above the knee, a dark wet stain spreading down to his boot, and he did not seem to have noticed it yet.

"You're all right," Muken said. His own voice sounded very far away. "You're both all right. You did it."

"That was the *small* one," Mark said. His voice shook so badly the words came out in pieces. "That was the small one, Sarge. That was the *cub.*"

"Aye," said Rob, and spat black into the snow, and wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, and looked at the carcass of Big Bertha lying on its side with Muken's sword standing up out of its throat. He looked for a long time. Then he looked at Muken, at the open cheek and the blood down his coat and the hands that would not stop shaking, and something in that closed northern face shifted, very slightly, and settled again in a different place.

He did not say anything. The Starks never did.

Behind them, quick light footsteps were coming across the snow from the carriage, and Muken did not need to turn round to know whose they were. He heard the clink of glass and brass in a deep coat pocket. He heard his sister's voice, low and level, already giving orders, the voice she used for telling men to hold still.

"Mark first," Yukazuri said. "Rob, lie down. Muken, sit before you fall."

"I'm sitting."

"Sit lower."

He would have laughed, if his face had let him.

Then, from the head of the column, far up the road past the lead wagons and round the shoulder of the granite where the four men he had sent forward had gone, came the sound of a horn.

It was the column's horn, the Lanternless call, one long note and two short. He knew it the way he knew the chain at Bridgemarch. It meant the cargo had stopped.

It blew again, one long and two short. And then, before it had finished, a third time, ragged, and much too soon, as if whoever was blowing it had no breath left to blow it properly with.

Muken looked up the road. Then he looked down at his empty hands, and at his sword standing up out of the dead thing in the snow, and began, very slowly, to get to his feet.

## Author Notes

**What this is.** Isaac's fight, polished, kept beat for beat and in his order, then carried on to the end he asked for: Muken kills both of his, Rob and Mark struggle to kill the cub, and then the horn means the cargo has stopped again. Past tense, third person, close on Muken. No audio tags.

**One call I made on your draft.** You wrote that *Naevra* looked out of the carriage worried for her brother. Naevra is Naiser's sister, from the other piece, so I took it as a slip and used Yukazuri, who is riding with the convoy as surgeon. If Naevra really is in that carriage, tell me and I'll rewrite the carriage beats around her.

**Who's who.**
- **Rob Stark** (26) and **Mark Stark** (22), as you named them. Rob is the elder: boar-spear, hand axe, a mail shirt made for a shorter man, a beard like wet rust, says almost nothing. Mark: round shield, short sword, freckled, talks under his breath when he's frightened. House Stark is canon in the north, and on Muken's card they are the house that later takes his terms at the boundary stones without being beaten. Two Starks bleeding beside him on this road is a seed for that. Neither has a card yet.
- **Oskar Pell**, the third man who stayed back, posted at the carriage wheel. Invented.
- **Big Bertha** is your name for her. Muken names everything that tries to kill him, because it helps. He gives the narrow one no name.

**How the fight decides (the combat floor).**
- **Muken's first kill.** It is your kick, made physical: Bertha's locked, loaded forearm is the "fracture point" he pushes off, and his heel shatters the narrow one's wrist into the road. Scrap iron on a minor reinforcement lodges in the neck vertebrae on the first stroke. The second stroke, through the parted spine, takes the head. The narrow one's broken hand opens his left cheek from jaw-hinge to eye before it dies.
- **Muken on Bertha.** He has nearly run out of essence, so he stops watching the claws and reads her gait instead. She favours the left leg, so all of her weight is going through the right knee. He cuts the tendon below the kneecap, the knee folds the wrong way, and when her head comes down he drives the point up under the jaw through the roof of the mouth. This is the same unnamed sense from Chapter One, a salt-cutter reading a fault in a face before he sets the pick. It is the Shingan coming, unnamed (his card's Fractura and Eye of the Splintered Work).
- **The brothers on the cub.** It climbs Rob's spear haft past the crossbar and bites through Mark's shield into his forearm, deep enough to show bone. Mark's mail saves his belly. Rob's axe sticks between its shoulders, and it bites Rob above the knee. Mark pins it with the short sword behind the foreleg and holds it there sobbing, while Rob takes the head off into the frozen ground.
- **The cost.** Muken's cheek is opened to the eye, and he is drained of essence: shaking, the cold coming in after, a coppery taste. Mark's left forearm is torn to the bone. Rob's thigh is bitten above the knee and bleeding steadily, and he hasn't noticed yet. No numbers are narrated in the fight.

**What the creatures are.** These are the things from Chapter One's map, what fell through when the spirit side broke, now corrupted goblins. Their essence reads to Muken like a seal going bad in the Cuttings: sour, the weight wrong. The gut-lining on the narrow one's claw and the brain in the middle one's mouth are what is left of Draycott's last escort, twenty-two dead or missing.

**What the horn means.** One long note and two short is the Lanternless call for "cargo stopped". The third blast is ragged and comes too soon, which suggests whoever is blowing it is hurt. The four men he sent forward are up there. That is the next chapter.

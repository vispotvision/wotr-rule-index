# The Undercroft — Aetherion Academy

*Aetherion Academy. The old cold room under the south range. The same hour.*

The grille had been iron when Mu-jin reached it. It ran down the steps now as something bright and slow, the lock and three of its bars gone soft as candle wax and pooling on the flags, giving up a hiss and a smell of scorched rust that filled the stair. He stepped over the spill where it was already greying at the edges and came down into the cold.

It was a real cold. The Academy had laid its dead here once, before the infirmary had a mortuary of its own, and the room still held the habit. A low vault of dressed stone, ribs sweating. Niches along both walls where coffins had waited for the ground to soften, empty now and swept. The lamps burned low and bluish, their flames leaning toward the far end of the room as though a draught pulled them, though no air moved. Mu-jin's breath stood in front of his face. The man at the far end of the vault had none.

Six cots stood in a row down the middle of the floor, each with a grey blanket folded at its foot in the infirmary's own way, corners squared. Three of them were occupied. A girl in a village smock sat on the nearest, hands in her lap. A young man in a carter's waistcoat lay on the second with his eyes open on the ceiling. On the third, a boy of perhaps twelve sat cross-legged, rocking a little, his lips moving over something too quiet to hear. None of them looked round at the noise of the grille. All three were breathing slowly and evenly. None of their breath showed either.

On a hook by the farthest niche hung a faculty gown, Academy black, with the silver lapel pin of the Department of Restorative Practice still fastened to it. The hem had been brushed. Someone had cared for it.

Beside the last table a broad man in a cheap coat had half risen from a stool. A burn scar ran from his left ear to the hinge of his jaw, the skin drawn tight and shining in the lamplight, and an unlit cigar had stopped halfway to his mouth. Velder Crace looked at the slag on the steps, then at Mu-jin, and very carefully put the cigar down on the table and his hands flat beside it.

Malphas did not rise at all.

He sat on a bench against the end wall with his long legs crossed at the knee and a ledger open across them, a tall man folded small. The same long face Mu-jin remembered from the Division's reading floor, the same skin the colour of steeped tea gone cold, the grey gone further back from the temples than it had been. His left hand lay on the open page. The healed lattice ran from his knuckles to his wrist in fine raised lines, a small repeating pattern under the skin, and there was frost now in the creases of it, white as salt, though the lamps were burning six feet away.

*He is cooling.* Mu-jin knew the sentence before he had finished looking. *Keep that sentence by you. He wrote it himself.*

"You can still turn yourself in, Malphas." Mu-jin stopped at the foot of the steps. His voice came out level, the teaching voice, the one that carried to the back row. "I know you are behind all of this. All of it. The students, the Crystals, the girl upstairs whose name does not turn her head. I don't want to kill you."

He looked at him. He made himself do it properly, the whole of the man and not only the hand, because Malphas had once written that the living who will not look at the dead are the ones to fear, and Mu-jin was not going to give him that.

"You are the only one living with this much knowledge of the dead. Nobody else could have built this. I read your book three times." He heard himself beginning to explain and did not stop it. "The first manuscript and the sealed edition both. I glossed your Aphorism for the fourth chapter, and you cited me. I thought you wrote the Necrocursica to push people away from bringing the dead back. That was the whole of it, as I read it. A complete architecture with the warning written into every page, so that anyone who went down that stair would know exactly what he was walking into." He took a breath that showed white. "So tell me. What has driven you into this madness?"

Malphas chuckled. It was a dry sound, almost fond, and he shook his head slowly the way a lecturer does when a student has asked the right question in the wrong frame.

"Madness?" He closed the ledger on one finger to keep his place. "Is that the word? Is it madness to want to save the one you love?"

The boy on the third cot stopped rocking.

"Is it madness to do anything, and everything, to keep a mother from having to watch her child die?" Malphas's voice did not rise. It never had. "You have been outside the grid, Mu-jin. You carried men down off a mountain when you were a boy. You know what a village looks like when a core opens three days' ride from the nearest Guild office and nobody comes. I have sat with those mothers. I have sat the night with their children after." He tipped his head toward the cots without looking at them. "Is it mad to remove death? We are growing past it. As a people, as a craft. Past the norms these gods put on us by force and called nature so that we would not argue."

He set the ledger aside on the bench, square to its edge.

"No. This is not madness, Mu-jin. This is truth. This is enlightenment." For the first time something moved behind his face, and it was not anger. "I won't let them die. I won't stand idly by and call myself a doctor if I cannot save even the most hopeless of my patients."

The lamps leaned further toward him. The temperature in the vault dropped another degree, and Mu-jin felt it in the old scars along his forearm before he felt it on his skin.

He reached up and took off the lens.

The room came apart. Sight Through the Glass did not ask permission; it never had. The vault's stone came up as a tally of load and damp. The lamps showed him their oil, half spent. And the three on the cots showed him what they were, layer under layer, the way a cut stone shows its strata when the light falls right. The Shells were intact, every one, and holding. Behind each Shell the architecture had been laid in by a careful hand. And behind that, where the person should have sat, there was a formula. A good one. Elegant, even, the glyph chains closed with a neatness he would have praised on any student's slate. Each of the three ran a thread, fine as spider silk and very cold, back across the floor to the lattice on Malphas's left hand. The boy's thread was the thinnest. At the boy's sternum, under the smock, one glyph sat open, its last stroke never drawn.

The migraine started behind his eyes at once, a slow heavy weight like a hand closing. He let it come. He had paid for this sight his whole life, and he could pay for it now.

"You would destroy yourself doing this." It came out in pieces, the way it always did when he could see too much at once, each piece another part of the mechanism. "You know it. You wrote it. Whoever borrows the current cannot step back out of it without a cost to his own Crystal. That is the Aphorism. You are holding three of them. Three threads, Malphas, back to your own hand. You are cooling toward the boundary and you cannot feel it. Equilibration. Chapter one of your own book."

He drew the grimoire from inside his coat. The Open Crucible came out heavy and warm, the old leather softened by his hand and by the years, and it fell open by itself to a page that had been opened many times.

"You have committed treason against everything we alchemists are supposed to stand for. Everything. We arrange conditions and the Continuum runs what it was already running. We authorise. We do not take." His voice steadied as the lecture took it over, the old reflex that turned fear into explanation. "You think you can break out of the laws of this world. What you have built is nothing more than Mechanica. A working that takes what it was never given, and calls the taking a cure. Every cot here is an Open Shell. The ground under this Academy will not heal from what you have opened in it. Not in our lifetime, not in theirs."

He held the book open on his palm, the page toward Malphas.

"We are not gods. We are not the creators. We should not permit ourselves these changes, because we could fracture the very world we live in, and I do not mean that as a figure of speech. I mean the road. I mean the ground." He breathed. "And because of that fact, Malphas, I cannot allow you to escape."

The words sat in the cold between them.

Then, quieter, the teacher and not the Archmagus, Mu-jin said the thing he had not planned to say.

"You asked for this. In the Averted Regard. You put yourself under that head because it belonged nowhere else, and you wrote that if you were ever found describing what the dead leave as more interesting than the people in the room, we should treat your book as a confession. Read this chapter back to me from its head, you wrote, and do not let me answer until it is finished." He did not look away. "I can read it to you. I know it by heart."

Malphas looked at him for a long moment. The frost in the lines of his hand did not melt.

"Nobody read it back," he said softly. "I waited for years, Mu-jin. I wrote it in the margin when nobody came. The living remain the duller company." He uncrossed his legs and stood, unhurried, and he was taller standing than Mu-jin remembered. "You are not here to read to me. You are here to stop me. Those are different errands, and I would rather you were honest about which one you came on."

At the far table Velder Crace had not moved his hands. His eyes went from his master to the slag on the stair and back, and a muscle worked in his scarred jaw.

Malphas walked to the third cot. He laid his lattice hand on the boy's head, gently, the way a man settles a child who has woken in the night.

The boy stood up.

He stood straight and lightly and put himself between the bench and the steps, the open glyph at his sternum facing Mu-jin under the thin cloth of his smock, and he looked at Mu-jin with perfectly ordinary eyes and no breath in front of his face. Above them, through the vault and the stone and the floors of the south range, somewhere across the grass, a bell gave one low tremor that no one had rung.

"I will not run from you," Malphas said. "I have never run from anything in a room with the dead in it." His hand stayed on the boy's hair. "But you will have to decide, Teacher, whether you can stop me without killing my patient. He is twelve. His name is Ansel. He asked me this morning when his mother was coming."

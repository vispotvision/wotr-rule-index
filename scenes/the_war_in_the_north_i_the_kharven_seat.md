# The War in the North I — The Kharven-Seat

Rind-water had been on the range since before light and the smell of it had got into everything, pork fat and hot iron and the flat mineral sweetness of melted snow, layered under wet wool steaming off the backs of four chairs and the green ironwood hissing in the firebox because the dry stack was down to its last two courses and nobody had said so out loud yet. Frost furred the inside of the shutter in a band four fingers deep. It had not come off that shutter in nine days.

In the yard a woman was counting the woodpile for the second time in a week. Her count came up eleven short of the last one. She swore once, quietly, and started again from the bottom course.

At the far end of the kitchen a boy and a scullery maid were conducting a slow, vicious argument about a cracked bowl and whose fault the crack was, and neither of them lowered their voices, because the low street had not yet learned that the house had a king in it.

The long table carried three things. A tallow lamp with a bad wick. A pot of rind-water going cold in the draft. And Muken Büri's north wall survey, unrolled and pinned flat at the corners with a whetstone, a horn cup, and two river-smoothed hearth stones borrowed off the range without asking.

Edward Lambert had one hand flat on the plat and the burned one closed around his cup for the heat.

"You have had several days to settle now, my lord."

His voice went down onto the boards the way it always did, each word set in its exact position, no warmth performed and no coldness performed.

"What should our next move be. It appears you were right. The Holy Sea of Alabaster has sent an envoy." The thumb began against the pad of the index finger. "They have sent my brother, who acts as an Archbishop for the Holy Church of Sancta Lux, and before anyone at this table repeats that word to a man wearing the grey, understand that the order does not have archbishops. They have Lectors and Arbiters and a great deal of impatience with the north's vocabulary. Charles is an Arbiter-Lector of the chapter hold at the white shelf. He will answer to Archbishop in the street and strike it from anything he signs, and he will hold that distinction to be a courtesy he is extending to us."

"And your other worries," Lorn Stark said.

He had not sat down. He never sat down before the report was finished, which was a habit out of a different court and one he had declined to lose. Forty-four, built long and lean, with the sword-teacher's specific ruin in both shoulders and grey coming into the beard in two stripes off the corners of his mouth.

"Confirmed," Lambert said. "All of it. The Mönggön have begun putting the substrate Stannvaard granted them into anti-fortification work on what is left of Kharven. Confirmed by a woman who walked it, who is standing in our yard counting the woodpile because she declined to come in until she had done something useful."

Temür looked at Edward. Then he looked at his daughter.

Hild had the range at her back and the night-stone in both hands. Dark hair off her mother's side, the single crown braid gone fuzzy at the temples because Brida had done it fast that morning, brown eyes moving between the two men at the rate of a person who has been told to listen and has decided to do more than that. The mail hem swung against her thighs when she shifted her weight. She had been holding the stone since the count came in and it had gone cold in her hands twenty minutes ago and she had not put it down.

*He is looking at me to see whether I flinch, and I am not going to, and the not-flinching is going to cost me the whole of my breakfast.* Hild set her jaw and held it. *Ask about the grain. Somebody has to ask about the grain and it is going to have to be me again.*

"Edward." She said it to the room. "How many days between your letter leaving and your brother leaving."

Lambert's thumb stopped.

He turned that over for a moment with his face doing nothing at all, and then he set the cup down and put the burned hand flat on the plat next to the other one, which was as close as the man came to visible disturbance.

"Eleven days on the fast road to the water," he said. "Nine more if the Malani boats were sitting where they were supposed to be sitting, which they never are. Twenty days minimum for that letter to reach a desk at the white shelf. Twenty more for a party to be assembled, provisioned, and put on the road north with a chapter escort." The thumb resumed. "Charles is at Brackenhold. He has been on the road twenty-six days."

The green log in the firebox found a pocket of sap and cracked like a small bone.

"Then he left before you wrote," Lorn said.

"He left fourteen days before I wrote."

Nobody filled the gap after that. In the yard the count went on, patient, out loud, a number every three seconds.

*Twenty-six days at this time of year with nineteen in the party and a chapter escort, and no order in the four quarters moves that fast on a hunch.* Lambert looked at the wick and did not trim it. *They knew something was coming north before we did. The question is whether they knew about the grain or about the Mönggön, and there is a third answer I do not like and cannot rule out, which is that they knew about him.*

"It is not a reply," he said aloud. "Whatever it is, it is not a reply, and I would like that understood before we lay a table for it. We have been assuming we are the party making a request. We may be the party receiving one."

"Or they were told to go," Yoko said.

She was in the corner nearest the door with her back against the plaster, which was where she put herself in every room and had never once explained. The ears had come forward and stayed forward through the whole exchange. She had not touched the rind-water.

"Two days ago the courier from the Hallenfeld junction carried a satchel that had been packed in a hold with alabaster dust in the seams," she said. "It was on the leather. That is all of it. I am not telling you what it means."

*He wants me to tell him what it means. He always wants that, and the wanting is the thing that gets people killed, because the day I guess for him is the day he stops checking.* Yoko's tail moved once against the plaster and stilled.

Lorn made a sound in his throat.

"Bring her in," he said. "The one in the yard. I would rather hear it from the boots than the ledger, no offence intended to the ledger."

"None taken. The ledger agrees with you." Lambert raised his voice half a register without changing anything else about it. "Mistress Foss. Leave the stack. It will be eleven short whether or not you count it a third time."

The door came open on a slab of cold that walked across the floor and took the heat out of the room at knee height, and the woman who came through it filled the frame side to side.

Rannvei Foss stood a hand shorter than Lorn and carried twice his weight through the shoulders and the back, the kind of build that comes off twenty years of felling and dragging and no other kind of life at all. Hair the colour of wet ash, cut with a knife at the jaw, gone flat under a hood she pulled back and did not smooth down. A broad, sun-scarred face across the cheekbones and dead white at the brow-line where the hood sat, with deep vertical creases bracketing the mouth and a nose that had been broken sideways and reset by somebody competent. Grey eyes under heavy brows. A short neck, a barrel chest under three layers, a waist that was simply the same width as everything else, and thighs that strained the wool where it had been patched at the seam twice with different thread. Her deel was Cold Cross work, boiled and greased and black with old resin at the cuffs. The belt was undyed. No Line at all.

Two fingers gone off the left hand at the second knuckle, old, healed clean.

She stopped four paces inside the door and bowed the way a woodward bows, which is to say she inclined her head and got it over with.

"Rannvei Foss. Woodward of Cold Cross." Flat northern vowels, and the words came out short and hard and stacked one on the other with nothing between them. "Ada Foss is my brother's wife. I mention it because you will hear the name twice today and I would rather you heard it from me first."

"Sit," Lorn said.

"I will stand. If I sit I will stop." She turned her head to Lambert. "Your stack is eleven short. It is not the count. Somebody is taking a billet a night off the north course and they are taking it off the north course because the north course is out of the lamplight, and I would look at that before I looked at anything else in this city."

*Six days I walked and the first thing out of my mouth is the woodpile. My mother would have laughed until she was sick.* Rannvei put her back to the wall next to the door. *Say the rest of it plain. They are all going to want it dressed up and it does not dress.*

"Cold Cross to the Whitmoor cut is a day and a half," she said. "I do that walk every eight days for the coppice count. On the sixteenth I came over the shoulder above the old gravel road and there was a spoil heap where there was no spoil heap on the eighth. Fresh. Twenty paces long, and growing at the north end."

"How deep," Lorn said.

"I did not measure it. I counted the barrows." She held up the ruined hand and folded down what was left. "Nine barrows in the hour I lay there. Two men on each. That is a gallery, my lord, and it is a proper one, and it is being cut by people who have done it before."

Lambert had gone very still over the plat.

"The spoil," he said. "What colour."

"Dry," Rannvei said. "Grey and dry and it broke in flakes when it came off the barrow. There was no water in it at all."

The thumb stopped against the index finger and stayed stopped.

Lorn looked from one of them to the other. "Say it in words the rest of us have."

"Everything on that approach is wet clay to a depth of thirty feet," Lambert said. "It has been wet clay since the world was made. A man digging on that line hits water in an afternoon and spends the next month pumping, which is why nobody has ever mined the north wall and why the north wall has never once needed a countermine cut against it." He turned the plat a quarter turn and put one finger down on it. "Except along a shelf of bedrock that runs from here to here, which is nine feet wide, which does not surface anywhere, and which appears on precisely six documents in the world. This is one of them. My father-in-law drove stakes for it. I drove stakes for it. Muken walked the line himself and corrected us twice."

"And now somebody is digging along it," Lorn said.

"Somebody is digging along it in the dark, in winter, at a rate of nine barrows an hour."

Hild had come off the range without anyone noticing her move.

"Who has the other five," she said.

"Two burned with the east wing. One went into the ground with Halvard Ness at the Reading four years ago, which I witnessed. One is in a Stannvaard strongbox under the terms of the original works contract, which they are entitled to and which I have never liked." Lambert's face did not move at all. "The sixth was held by Jorunn Mell, who factored the corridor for eleven years and who has not answered a letter since the spring."

"So it is Mell."

"It is likely Mell. It is not certainly Mell." The old man looked up at her. "Your grace, I will ask you to hold the difference between those two sentences for the rest of this winter, because the moment we act as though it is certainly Mell, we stop looking at me."

Rannvei reached into the front of the deel and put something down on the boards next to the plat.

It rang, once, flat and brief.

A brass housing the size of a fist, machined, with a threaded collar at one end and a hairline glass window let into the face of it. The glass had a scale etched behind it in fine black strokes and a needle sitting dead at the low stop. Along the shoulder, struck deep and struck clean, a proof-mark: the Ore Council's crossed hammers with the assay number under them.

"Off a dead man on the gravel road," Rannvei said. "He was three days dead and the wolves had been at his legs and not his coat. There were two more of these in the coat and I left them, because I was one woman with a hand axe and I judged that taking one would read as scavenging and taking three would read as a message." She looked at Lambert without deference. "I am telling you what I judged, in case I judged it wrong."

Yoko came off the wall.

She crossed to the table, put her hands behind her back so that they would not be doing anything, and lowered her face to within a foot of the brass without touching it. The ears went flat and then came halfway forward again. The nostrils moved twice.

"Gun oil and beeswax on the outside," she said. "Under it, on the threads, there is a resin I have smelled in exactly one other place, which was Borin's bench on the eleventh day of the crossing." She straightened. "That is the fact. I have no second sentence."

The range ticked as it cooled through a joint.

*They are going to run a conduit up that gallery and they are going to gauge it, which means it is not powder, which means it is worse than powder, and I have no vocabulary for the worse thing and neither does anyone in this room.* Lorn's hand had gone to the back of a chair and closed on it. *And I am going to have to teach eight hundred men to hold a wall that is going to be cut out from beneath them by an instrument with a window in it.*

Temür had not spoken since the count came in.

He looked at the brass, and at the plat, and at the eleven-short woodpile through the shutter's frost band, and when he did speak the words came out slow and set flat, one after another, with nothing hurried in them at all.

"It seems we are preparing for a great war."

He turned the housing a half turn with one finger so the proof-mark faced up.

"We do not have enough food to begin drafting. We are put into a hellish situation, truly."

"You are." Lambert did not soften it. "I will give you the figure and then I will give you the argument, and I want them received in that order. Three thousand two hundred inside the walls, and the granary floor I would not swear to. A drafted man eats at true ration or he is useless within a fortnight, and true ration in this cold is half again what we are issuing. Draft one thousand and you have added five hundred men's worth of eating to a store that carries us to Ice-out at two thirds, and you have taken a thousand pairs of hands out of the ironwood, which is the only thing this kingdom still sells that anybody still wants." The thumb went once and stopped. "The draft costs us the Thin Weeks and the timber both. That is the arithmetic and it does not care what any of us want."

"And a wall nobody stands on falls anyway," Lorn said.

He said it into the middle of the table, not at Lambert.

"I have watched you win this argument four times in eleven years and you were right all four. You are not right now. A gallery under the north course is a thing that arrives on a day somebody chooses, and the somebody is not us, and eight hundred men who can march a day in this weather is not a garrison, it is a funeral with a schedule." He let go of the chair. "I will say it in the room rather than in the corridor afterward, because I have watched saving it for the corridor destroy better men than either of us. You are counting bushels against a siege that has already started digging."

"I am aware that it has started digging."

"Then say what we do when the ground opens."

"I do not know," Lambert said.

He said it perfectly evenly, and the boy and the maid in the scullery had gone quiet somewhere in the last minute and the whole house heard it.

"I have run this kingdom out of a lockbox for eleven years and I have not had a single day of it where the correct answer was available, and this is not one either. I can hold three thousand two hundred people to Ice-out on two thirds. I cannot hold them and feed a levy and I cannot feed a levy and keep the felling, and there is no fourth door in this room, and I have been standing at the wall looking for it since the sixteenth."

Hild put the cold night-stone down on the boards next to the brass housing.

"Then we ask him," she said. "The Archbishop. Whatever he is called when he signs." She wiped her hand on the mail hem. "He is at Brackenhold, and Brackenhold eats full, and he will have seen that they eat full before he ever gets here and he will have drawn the wrong thing from it. Somebody should tell him what it cost to make that true, and it should be somebody he cannot dismiss, and it should be before he sits down at a table where we are pretending."

*They will say I am eleven. Let them say it. I have been the only person at this table for four years who has actually been hungry in front of the people we are asking.* Her chin came down half an inch. *And Father has not asked me a single question about the city yet. Not one.*

The door came open again without a knock.

Tabitha Hallenfeld came in with the cold behind her and the road still on her, six days of it, the hem of her riding coat frozen into a plate and cracking as she walked. She got two paces in, saw the room, and did not bow at all.

"Nineteen in the party," she said. "Grey, and a chapter escort in proofed plate, sword and firearm both. They are three hours out of Brackenhold on the low road and they are not stopping there, which means they will be at the north gate the day after tomorrow before the light goes." She was breathing through her mouth. "There is a second thing and I do not like it. They sent this ahead by their own rider at midday, sealed, and their rider asked for a written answer and said he would wait for it in the yard, and he is in the yard now, and he has been standing in it for two hours and has declined bread three times."

*Three times. Nobody in the four quarters declines bread three times unless they have been told what it would mean to accept it here.* Tabitha's hand did not quite steady as she reached inside the coat. *Somebody has briefed that boy on our stores. Somebody at the white shelf knows to the bushel.*

She put the packet down on the boards.

Heavy paper, waxed against the weather, and the seal on it was not the chapter hold's. It was the Scale itself, struck in white wax over a second impression that had been made first and had been deliberately allowed to show through at the edge, an older mark, a Kharven crown with a broken bar.

Lambert reached across the plat with the burned hand and turned the packet through a half circle until the seal faced the head of the table.

Then he sat back and folded both hands in his lap and waited.

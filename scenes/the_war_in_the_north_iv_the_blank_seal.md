# The War in the North IV — The Blank Seal

The room smelled of another family's life.

Beeswax, first, which nobody in the corridor had burned in four years and which meant the house had been provisioned from somewhere with a surplus. Under it, lavender gone brown in a dish on the sill, and under that the sour-sweet residue of a wool press that had stood in this room until three weeks ago and had left four square depressions in the boards where its feet had been. Yūgiri had lain awake her first night here counting those depressions in the dark and working out from their spacing what the press had weighed and how many people it had taken to shift it and where they had put it.

Below the window a hound was being fed and was being loud about it. Boots crossed the yard, stopped, went back the other way. Somebody with a Silver belt was explaining to somebody without one that the well was not to be used before the sixth bell, and the explaining had gone on for long enough to stop being an explanation.

Wren had a candle at the writing desk and the good chair turned to face away from the bed.

He wrote in one continuous economical motion, no adjustments and no pauses to reread, the pen going down at the top of the sheet and coming up at the bottom and the whole of it done inside two minutes. He had not taken the coat off. Greymane grey, the wool so heavy it stood away from the shoulder on its own, and the seam under the left arm let out and restitched by a hand that was not a tailor's. His boots had been cleaned and were drying at the wrong distance from the brazier and would crack.

Yūgiri came off the bed with the blanket still around her hips and crossed the boards barefoot and made no sound doing it.

She had the Yukari build entire: five foot ten and long through the shin and forearm, narrow at the shoulder, with the long spidering fingers the bloodline throws in nine births out of ten and the flat quiet gait that comes with them. Skin a cool dark brown, ashy at the knuckle and the elbow in this dry cold, with a raised keloid the length of a thumbnail sitting under her collarbone where something had gone in and been taken out again. Hair the colour of wet slate, straight and heavy and cut level with the jaw, pushed behind one ear and not the other. Small breasts, a soft low belly she had never once apologised for, wide flat hips, and the specific dense muscle down the back of the thigh that comes from a childhood of sitting seiza on hard boards for four hours a day. Her irises were so dark the pupil could not be found in lamplight, and around each pupil, faint even at rest, a violet-black corona that did not belong to any human eye. Above the outer corner of the left one, three inked strokes in a shallow arc. She had earned them at nineteen and had never explained them to him.

She put her arms around him from behind and laid her cheek against the back of his skull.

"It must be hard for you," she said. "To be hated by your brother. You know I can see it."

Wren did not answer.

The pen went on. She could feel the movement of it through his shoulder blade, four strokes and a lift, four strokes and a lift.

*He has not stopped writing to be held in eleven months and I keep doing it anyway, which says a thing about me I would rather not have said out loud.* Yūgiri closed her eyes. *And the ink is wrong. It is not the desk's ink. He brought that ink in with him.*

She looked down the length of his arm at the page.

The letter was to Sonzai, and it was not in Wren's hand.

She had watched him write it in somebody else's letters, upright where his own sloped, the loops closed where his ran open, and he had done it at the same speed he wrote his own, which meant he had been doing it long enough that it had stopped costing him anything. Below the last line he set a seal in the wax that carried no device at all, a plain blank disc, and pressed it, and held it, and lifted.

"Sworn into secrecy," she said. "But this is what your nation requires of you."

He set the pen in the tray.

"If I have to become the villain to eventually be seen as a hero, there is no greater honour." His voice came out flat and unhurried and entirely certain, the voice of the ridge, the voice of a house that had held a border for three years past the point where holding it made sense. "All those who rise against the true line of the Büri will be destroyed."

Yūgiri smiled into his hair.

She had loved him first for exactly this, and she had never been able to make that sound like anything other than a failing when she said it aloud to herself in the dark. Other men trimmed. Other men said a thing and then went around behind it and put a second thing underneath in case the first fell. Wren said the whole of it, once, in the shape it actually had, and then he went and did it.

She slid her hands down inside the front of the grey coat where it was warm and flattened them on his chest.

"But what will you do." She kept her mouth close to his ear. "The Möngön expect you to execute one of the traitors. To show your allegiance."

He stood.

The chair went back on its legs and she came away from him and did not stumble, and he turned and she got her first look at his face since the sixth bell, and there was nothing in it at all. He had the flat, incurious expression of a man reading a road he had walked four hundred times, and no rehearsal anywhere in him, and no resolve either, because resolve is what a man assembles when the thing is still in front of him.

"It will have to be done," he said. "I will regret my actions."

He took her jaw in one hand, and the hand was cold from the window side of the room, and the palm was ridged across the heel from twenty years of a shield strap.

"Many have already been sacrificed. Their deaths will not be in vain." His thumb moved once along her cheekbone under the inked strokes. "And I am sure that by now my brother Bram has found the rat I left behind for him."

The hound below stopped eating. Somebody downstairs had told the man who was shouting who was upstairs, and the yard came to order in under four seconds.

Yūgiri put her hand over his wrist and held it there.

Then she looked past him at the desk, where the letter to Sonzai sat sealed with a blank disc, and beside it, weighted flat under the inkstone, a second sheet listing the men the Möngön were holding in the byre at the far end of the yard. Nine names in a clerk's crabbed hand.

The fourth was Marten Hillborn.

She had read the first sheet upside down an hour ago while he was writing it and she had known the surname then and had said nothing, because saying it would have required her to admit she reads what is on his desk. She looked from the one name to the space where the other one had been and she felt her own pulse go up in her throat, and it went up alone. The threads did not move. Around him they never did.

"Two," she said.

Wren waited.

"There are two knots on you." She said it in the register she used for a finding and not for a conversation, and her eyes had gone black across the whole iris, and the corona had come up silver and was turning. "One of them closes this winter. That is the fact. I do not have a second sentence and I am not going to invent one for you."

"You never do."

"I never do."

The corona went down. Her eyes came back and the room came back with them, dim and yellow and smelling of another family's beeswax, and she had a headache starting behind the left orbit that would be with her until morning.

He kissed her.

It was not gentle and it was not hurried, and it was the only thing he ever did that had no purpose behind it that she could find, which was why she permitted it to matter to her. His hand went from her jaw into her hair and closed and pulled just enough. She got the coat open and both palms flat on his ribs under the shirt, warm skin over a body that was one long map of the border, the puckered entry above the hip, the two ribs that had knitted crooked, the deep dished scar under the right pectoral where a Keth lance had gone through and come out again and, by every account she had ever heard, should have finished him at twenty-three. She dragged her nails down over it. He made a sound against her mouth and his hand tightened in her hair, and she felt him go hard against her belly through the wool, and the blanket came off her hips onto the boards.

She got her leg up over his thigh.

"Wren."

"Mm."

"Stay in the room."

He stopped.

She felt it happen, the whole animal weight of him coming back from wherever he had gone, a rope going slack in her hands. He put his forehead against hers and stood there breathing, one hand still in her hair and the other spread wide across the small of her back, and for four seconds Yūgiri Yukari had the whole of him.

Then he said, "The rider goes at the sixth bell," and let go, and turned back to the desk to check the seal had taken.

She stood naked in the middle of another family's floor with the cold coming up through the boards into her heels and looked at his back.

*I am going to have that. I am going to have those four seconds and I am going to carry them like a coin in a shoe, and one day somebody is going to ask me what I got out of eleven months of this and I am going to lie to them.*

She bent and picked up the blanket.

# The War in the North V — The Shieldwarden

*The Ridgewall, the southern face. The same week.*

Pain has a schedule.

Bram had learned its timetable the way he had once learned the watch rotation, by being wrong about it repeatedly in front of other people. The shoulder was worst at the fourth hour after waking, when the swelling had gone down enough to let the joint move and not enough to let it move correctly. The ribs were worst at night. The forearm, which had taken the axe through the shield and kept it, was worst when it was warm, which was the joke of it, because the only warm hour in the day was the hour by the fire and he had begun to dread the fire.

He was on the floor of his own chamber in a shirt gone through at both elbows, working the shoulder against a rope Lilia had run through a ring bolt in the ceiling, and he had been at it long enough that the sweat had cooled on him twice.

The room held almost nothing. A cot. A stool. A table with a whetstone, a horn cup, a stub of tallow, and the letters he had not opened. The Ridgewall's southern face had been stripped for firewood in the second year and never refurnished, and the wall behind him showed the pale rectangles where four generations of Greymanes had hung the things a house hangs, and every rectangle was empty, and the emptiness had a smell to it, chalk and old plaster and cold stone that never got above freezing on the inner face.

Down the corridor somebody was singing the counting song wrong on purpose to make a child laugh, and the child was not laughing, and the singer was not stopping.

He pulled. The joint came up through its arc and caught at the top where it always caught, and he breathed out through his teeth and held it there and counted to nine and let it down.

Twenty-one times. Lilia said twenty-one and would know if he had done nineteen.

The question came in around the eleventh, on its own schedule, as it had every morning since the field.

It had worn smooth from handling. Why his brother had done it. He could recite the politics and he had reconstructed the timing four times on the table with cups standing in for companies, and neither of those had ever been the thing he was actually asking. The two of them at eight and eleven on the wall walk with their father telling them that the ridge did not move, and Wren, who had been eleven and had already worked out that a thing said twice is a thing somebody is worried about, asking whether the ridge had ever moved. Their father had not answered. Wren had taken that as the answer and never asked again and had spent thirty years being right about it, and the being right had ended two hundred paces across a field of dead men with a smile on his face that Bram had seen clearly and would go on seeing.

They had held the southern approach together. That was the whole of the argument and it was not an argument at all, because it was a fact and facts do not argue. The two of them, and eight hundred men, and a rock shelf, and the Mandate below it breathing green smoke every spring for nine years. Kharven had a north gate to worry about because the Greymanes had spent nine years making the south not worth the walk. Nobody at the capital had ever written that down. Bram had stopped needing them to.

Twenty-one.

He let the rope go and sat back on his heels and let his head hang and dripped onto the boards between his knees.

The hate was there. He had stopped pretending about it around the fourth week. It sat low and it sat warm and it was the only warm thing in the room, and it got him up in the morning when grief could not, and Lilia had taken one look at what he was doing and had made a decision about it that she had never once told him.

He knew what she thought. He knew because she had put the ring bolt in the ceiling instead of arguing.

Eight hundred men on a rock shelf watch the man in front. If the man in front loses faith, they do not mutiny. They simply begin to die, one at a time, in ways that each have a reasonable explanation, over the course of a season, until there is no one left holding the shelf and the thing below it comes up. He had watched it happen to a neighbouring company at nineteen and had never needed the lesson repeated.

So the hate could stay. It was load-bearing.

He had found the rat.

Nine days of it, and it had not taken cleverness, which was the part he kept catching on. Josse Hillborn, forty-one, farrier to the southern muster since before the split, a short thick man with a burn scar across the back of the neck from a horse that went over in a forge fire, three daughters, the eldest married to a Stormfold cooper. He had ridden out on the eighth and the twenty-second of four consecutive months to a hamlet where he had no kin and had come back each time with nothing on his mule. Renard had put it together off the farrier's own tally book, which Josse had kept honestly and completely and had never thought to hide.

That was the part. The man had not hidden it.

Bram had gone down to the root cellar on the seventh day and stood in the doorway with a lamp and asked him one question, and Josse Hillborn had looked at him with an expression Bram had been unable to read at all, and had said, *I have not sold you, my lord,* and had then closed his mouth and had not opened it since, through two days of Renard asking and one night of the men outside the door doing the thing men do outside a door.

Six words that no clerk could enter as a denial, and then silence.

*He is protecting somebody.* Bram had walked back up the cellar stair with that and had put it down where he could reach it and had never once turned it over far enough to see the other side of it.

The knock came flat and twice, low on the door, which was Lilia's knock and nobody else's.

"Lord Bram."

He got his shirt down over the ribs before he called her in, which she would notice and would not mention.

Lilia came in with the cold from the corridor and a tray she set down on the stool without looking at where the stool was, because she had put the stool there. Stormfold, and it was audible in the vowels and visible in the build: thirty-six, short, deep through the chest, with the forearms of a woman who has hauled wet wool and dead weight in equal measure since she was nine. Sandy hair going grey at the front, scraped back and pinned with something that had been a nail. A broad blunt face, sunburnt across the nose even in winter, with a white scar splitting the left eyebrow. Her hands were the part you looked at. Cracked to bleeding across every knuckle from lye and from the cold, the nails cut to the quick, and a permanent grey-brown staining in the creases that no amount of scrubbing had ever taken out.

She looked at the rope, and at the wet boards under it, and at his face.

"Twenty-one."

"Twenty-one."

"Good." She wiped her palms down her apron. "I have letters from the capital. They came up with the salt cart and the carter waited, which he was not asked to do, which tells you they told him to wait."

She put them into his hand rather than onto the table, which was deliberate.

"They expect us to handle this situation swiftly, my lord."

Bram turned the top one over. The wax was the Crown's, and under the Crown's, showing at the edge where the second impression had been laid over the first, the small square stamp of a steward's office.

*He has not eaten since the cellar and he thinks I have not noticed because I have not said. I could put it in front of him and he would eat it to make me leave.* Lilia stood where she was and did not sit. *And I am not going to, because the day I start managing him is the day he stops being able to stand in front of those men, and I would rather have him thin.*

"Say the rest of it," Bram said.

"There is no rest of it in the letter. There is rest of it in the yard." She folded her arms. "Half of them want him hung this afternoon and half of them want him let go over the ridge with a day's start, and both halves are made of men who have eaten at his fire. Your cousin has put two on the cellar door who are not from the southern muster, which I think was correct and which nobody has thanked him for."

"And you."

"I am the wrong person to ask. I have set that man's arm and I have buried his wife." Her jaw worked once. "I know one thing worth saying and here it is. A man who is hanged in a yard is not gone out of the yard. He stays in it. I have watched three garrisons go bad and every one of them went bad slowly and every one of them had a yard."

Bram broke the seal with his thumbnail.

He read it standing, in the grey window light, with his hair wet through and the shoulder beginning to seize the way it always did when he stopped moving it, and he read it twice, and the second time he read only the last four lines, which said in the flat courteous register of a steward's office that the Crown required a public and attributable resolution, and that the resolution should be effected before the Reading, and that the Crown was confident the Shieldwarden understood the necessity.

The Shieldwarden.

Nobody had used the title since the split, in eleven months, in his hearing or out of it. The men said *my lord* and the letters said *Lord Bram* and the house said nothing at all, because the title belonged to whichever brother the ridge belonged to, and nobody in the Stormfold had been willing to be the first to say which that was.

Somebody in a warm room at Kharven-Seat had now said it, in ink, over a seal.

He read the four lines a third time, and understood that he was being paid in advance, and that the price was in the same sentence as the payment, and that whoever had drafted it had put the two things in that order deliberately.

He had never killed one of his own. Nine years of it and every man he had put down had been marked out for him first by somebody else, by a horn or a banner or the green smoke coming up off the Mandate, and the marking had done a work in him that he had never had to look at directly. He looked at it now. It was not courage. It had never been courage. It had been the enormous, unearned relief of having the choosing done by somebody standing on the other side of a field.

He put the letter face down on the table.

Then he crossed to the corner where the shield stood against the plaster with its face to the wall, and he turned it round, and he put his forearm through the straps.

The elbow strap had been shortened two fingers' width and restitched with waxed thread in a hand that was not a saddler's.

Bram stood with the weight of it hanging correctly off a shoulder that had not carried it correctly since the field, and he looked at the stitching for a moment, and he did not look at Lilia. He tightened the second buckle with his teeth. He went out past her into the corridor and turned toward the cellar stair, and the singing down the passage stopped as he passed it.

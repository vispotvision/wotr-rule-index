# The Weak Grade

*The Arbitration Division, the chancery at Stannvaard, where the words cut above the door read Nemo in lingua sua mensuratur: no one is measured in his own tongue.*

[narrating, low and measured] The lamp on the examiner's table burned without smoke, and Hettrun disliked it before she had sat down.

A Dawi lamp smoked. It told you what it was eating. This one sat on a brass stem plumbed into the wall, fed off the main, and gave a flat white light with no flicker and no smell, and the only proof that it cost anything was the meter on the panelling behind the examiner's chair, where a brass needle trembled behind glass. [rain against windows] Rain ran down the tall window. Up here on the Seat it always seemed to be raining. Down in Kolmthruk it never did, and her father had said once that the surface people built their courts high so that they could hear the weather and feel important.

The room was out of true. That was the second thing. The oak panels met the floor a hair off the square on the window side, so that the line of the skirting ran downhill by the width of a fingernail over ten paces. Hettrun had seen it from the door. Every Dawi who ever sat in this room had seen it. Nobody had fixed it, because nobody who could see it had ever been asked.

She sat on the left of her father at the long table with the field Tally open on her knees, the small one, bound in boards and worn pale at the corners, and the cut-pen in her right hand. Her father sat in the middle. The chair had been made for a Concord man and his boots did not reach the floor, so he had set them on the rung, and he sat as he sat at the furnace door, with his weight forward and his hands on his thighs. They were furnace hands, the backs shiny with old burns, the left thumbnail black where a ladle had dropped on it in her childhood. The soul-runes on his forearms ran up under the rolled sleeves in the cut hand, entry over entry, forty years of his life she could have read aloud to the room. His beard was bound in three iron rings. He had oiled it this morning. She had watched him do it in the mirror by the furnace stair, slowly, as if for a wedding.

Across the table, Examiner Prudence Ashcombe of the Arbitration Division turned a page.

Hettrun looked at how she carried. Tall, as they all were, but carried narrow, the shoulders drawn in under a black chancery gown gone green at the seams and shiny at the cuffs, and the weight all low, in wide hips and a heavy seat that did not shift in the chair. Hair the grey of wire drawn cold, coiled at the back of the head and pinned with two black pins. Long face, long jaw. Heavy-lidded eyes that looked half asleep and were not, and spectacles on a black ribbon round her neck, which she put on to read and took off to look at you. An ink stain on the inside of her right middle finger, the same place as Hettrun's own. A plain ring of office, silver.

Under her hand lay the docket, a stack of papers tied with green tape, and on top of it a small book bound in oilcloth, swollen with damp at one corner.

[pen scratching] Ashcombe wrote a line, then took her spectacles off and let them hang.

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent, formal] This is a hearing of the Arbitration Division, sitting at Stannvaard, in the matter of the death of Tobias Wren, meter-reader to the Guild of Measurewrights, in the conduit gallery beneath the Hallan furnaces, on the seventh of the month.” She had it by heart and did not look down. “The witness is the foreman of those furnaces, and the witness's hold has sent its clerk. Will the witness state his name for the record.”

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent, slow] Oskrin. Of Kolmthruk. Foreman of the Hallan furnaces.”

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent] Your full name, Foreman.”

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent] That is my name in Common.”

[narrating, low and measured] Ashcombe put the spectacles back on and looked at a page. “[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent, dry] I have read Hallorin's grammar. A Dawi name has four parts when the man is sworn. Given, oath, father, byname. You've given me one and a hold.”

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent] In Common it is enough.”

[narrating, low and measured] He did not say it hard. He said it as he said everything, every word set down level and left there.

*She has read a grammar,* Hettrun thought. *She knows there is a door in the house, and she has never once been inside.*

Ashcombe wrote something. She let it go.

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent] Then let us begin with what is not in dispute. On the evening of the sixth, the Guild suspended supply to the Hallan furnaces for arrears. You were notified?”

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent] A boy came down with a paper at the second bell.”

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent] And what did you do?”

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent] I banked the furnaces. I sent the night crew up the stair, and I went down alone to the pump gallery.”

[narrating, low and measured] Hettrun cut. The field Tally took the old metre, four feet to the line, and she turned his Common into the hold's tongue as she went and said each line aloud, low, because a line not spoken was a line not entered.

“[soft, precise Finnish accent, chanting in metre] Oskrin of Kolmthruk, the furnaces banked. On. The night crew sent up the stair. On. The foreman to the pump gallery. On.”

[narrating, low and measured] Ashcombe watched her pen and not her face. “[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent] Is that necessary?”

“[soft, precise Finnish accent] It is an act. Entering it is also an act, and I enter that too.”

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent, faintly amused] Of course you do.” Ashcombe turned back to Oskrin with the air of a woman stepping round a puddle. “Why the pump gallery?”

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent] Kolmthruk is under the Hallan furnaces. Three hundred of us under. The pumps keep the water out, and the pumps run on the main.” He looked at his hands. “Four days without the main, and the lowest gallery is water to the roof.”

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent] The Guild's notice gave the hold eight days to clear its arrears.”

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent] The water does not read the notice.”

[narrating, quieter] Ashcombe's mouth moved at one corner, and she wrote, and Hettrun saw that she had liked the line and was writing it down to use against him.

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent] At the fourth bell of the seventh, Tobias Wren went down into the conduit gallery to read the Hallan meter and seal it. He did not come up. He was found at the sixth bell by the relief, under the main, upstream of the meter, by an open tap that had no business being there. The coroner's word is that he took the whole draw of a trunk main through the chest.” She turned a page with one finger. “Who opened the main upstream of the meter, Foreman?”

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent] The main was found open.”

[narrating, tense] Hettrun's pen stopped on the board.

There was no line to cut. She heard it go past and tried to scan it and it would not scan, because there was nobody in it. In the hold's tongue every act had a doer at the front and the Tally slot at the back, and the sentence her father had just said had neither. It was a Common sentence. It had been built in Common, for this room, and it had a hole in the middle the exact size of a man.

[clock ticking] The meter needle trembled behind its glass.

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent] Your clerk has stopped writing.”

“[soft, precise Finnish accent] There is nothing to enter.” Hettrun heard her own voice come out level and was glad of it. “Nobody did anything in that sentence.”

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent, sharper] Somebody certainly did something in that gallery.”

“[soft, precise Finnish accent] Yes. Not in the sentence.”

[narrating, low and measured] Ashcombe took her spectacles off. She looked at Hettrun properly for the first time, and Hettrun put her eyes on the woman's ring of office and held them there, as you do with a surface face that has decided to look into yours.

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent, slow] Let me tell you both what I think I know,” Ashcombe said, “and you may tell me where the angles are wrong. I am told that's how one speaks to a Dawi.” She folded her hands on the docket. “If your father opened that main, and your Tally enters it, then the act belongs to your hold. Your hold stands behind it. The Division bills Kolmthruk for the stolen draw and for Mr Wren's death, and Kolmthruk pays. If your Tally does not enter it, then it is one man's act. Unentered. Unowned. Your father answers for it alone, under Accord law, and Accord law for a death by theft of supply is the brand and the mines.”

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent] Then let it be one man's.”

“[soft, precise Finnish accent, too fast] Father.”

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent] It is one man's.”

[narrating, quieter] He still had not said who the man was. He would not. Hettrun had heard him not say things all her life. When her mother died he had not said her name at the death-shelf for a year, and then one morning at the furnace door he had said it, in the strong grade, as if it had always been there.

Ashcombe untied the green tape.

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent, brisk] Then let us have the arithmetic, since your people trust it more than they trust me.” She drew out a sheet ruled in red and turned it round to face them. “The Hallan meter. Arrears of four hundred and sixty silver marks over the last two quarters, drawn at the posted tariff.” She laid one finger on the sheet and squared it. “The meter was proved in the spring, as every meter on the Seat is proved, and its seal was sound. The Guild's supply was lawful, its bill was lawful, and its suspension was lawful. What happened in that gallery was theft.”

[narrating, tense] She had squared it to the table's edge, and Hettrun had watched her do it. The sheet came to rest a hair off true, the same hair as the skirting, because the table was not square to the room either, and the woman had squared her paper to a crooked thing and believed it straight.

*The finger,* Hettrun thought. *She put the finger on it before she said the spring.*

She leaned forward. The chair was too high. She put both hands flat on the table to reach.

“[soft, precise Finnish accent] May I see the proof certificate?”

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent] It's in the docket.”

“[soft, precise Finnish accent] Then may I see the docket.”

[narrating, low and measured] Ashcombe looked at her for the length of a breath. Then she slid the stack across with the flat of her hand, and Hettrun turned the papers one at a time.

She read Concord figures as every hold-clerk did, slowly and without love. Reading-sheets, the tariff schedule, a coroner's note in a doctor's bad hand. Then the oilcloth book.

It was Tobias Wren's reading book. She knew it before she opened it, from the swollen corner and the pencil in its loop down the spine, a short pencil, sharpened with a knife, chewed at the end. Inside, his hand: small, square, careful, each meter on its own line with the date and the needle's reading and a tick. Kolmthruk's meter had a line every eight days for two years. On the last page the last line read *Hallan main, the seventh, fourth bell,* and a number begun and not finished, the pencil stroke running off down the page where the hand had stopped holding it.

[narrating, slow, hushed] She looked at the half number a long while. Someone had closed the book over it afterwards and the facing page had taken a faint grey ghost of the stroke. Nobody would finish it. There was no one alive who knew what the needle had said.

[narrating, low and measured] She turned the page back, and kept turning, and at the bottom of the stack she found the certificate.

A card with a printed border and a Guild seal in red wax. *Proved and sealed, the Hallan main meter, sound within the schedule.* A Measurewright's signature, flourished. And the date stamped beside the wax in violet ink, by a machine, so that it could not be a careless hand.

The ninth.

Tobias Wren had died on the seventh.

[clock ticking] She did the sum once, and again, because a number said in a room like this was an act, and she would enter it.

“[soft, precise Finnish accent] Examiner.” She turned the card round and set it down between them, and did not square it to anything. “The meter was proved on the ninth. Two days after the man died, and not in the spring, or if it was proved in the spring, the spring proof is nowhere in your docket.” She put one finger beside the violet stamp, not on it. “If it was not proved in the spring, then for two quarters nobody knows what it read. Then nobody knows the arrears. Then nobody knows if the suspension was lawful.”

[narrating, tense, quieter] Ashcombe did not look at the card; she had no need to. She had known it was there all along, from the moment she untied the tape. That was the thing Hettrun saw, all at once, in the stillness of the low heavy body in the chair: she had known, and she had squared her finger on the red-ruled sheet and said *spring* anyway, and hoped that a Dawi clerk would not read to the bottom of an Accord stack.

[rain against windows] The rain came harder on the glass.

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent, quiet, very even] Let me propose something,” Ashcombe said, “that I think will suit everyone at this table.”

“[soft, precise Finnish accent] Say it.”

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent] Your father gives me his statement in Common. Today. As one man. It is entered in my record and not in yours, and Kolmthruk owes the Division nothing for Mr Wren and nothing for the draw. In return, the question of when the Hallan meter was proved does not appear in my record either.” She opened her hands on the table. “Nor in yours. Your hold keeps its pumps and the Guild keeps its tariff. Your father,” and here she did look at him, “keeps whatever it is he's keeping.”

[narrating, low and measured] Hettrun felt her father go still beside her. Stiller. He had been still the whole hour, but there is a stillness that is a man sitting and a stillness that is a man holding a weight off the ground, and she had grown up beside the second kind at a furnace door.

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent, almost gentle] Take it,” he said to her, in Common.

[narrating, low and measured] She did not look at him.

*He is right,* she thought. *It saves three hundred of us, and all it costs is one.* She could see every line of it, the way you see a cut before you make it. The hold dry. The pumps humming. Her father in the Accord's mines with a brand on his cheek, and his act nowhere in any book that a Dawi would ever read. Kurnur. An act that happened and was never entered. A hole in the Tally the size of a man, kept there on purpose, by her, with her own pen, for the rest of her life.

And under it another hole. The ninth. The meter that read whatever the Guild said it read, for two quarters, while the arrears ran up and the boy came down the stair with his paper.

[narrating, quieter] She closed her hand on the cut-pen.

“[soft, precise Finnish accent, firm] Kurlo,” she said. “Ei kurme.”

[narrating, quieter] Ashcombe waited for the translation.

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent, heavy] It is unbegun,” her father said, without turning. “And it is not undertaken. Among us that is how a bargain ends, Examiner, and nothing is owed on either side.” He let a breath out. “My daughter has ended it.”

[narrating, low and measured] Ashcombe sat back. For the first time in the hour the heavy seat moved in the chair. She took off her spectacles, and folded them, and laid them on the docket, and Hettrun understood that she was not angry. She had made the offer the way a smith tests a weld, by bending it, and she had found out what she needed to know.

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent, dry and tired] Then we are back where we began,” she said. “A man died, and nobody in this room has done anything.” She turned to Oskrin. “For the record, Foreman, and in full this time, your name.”

[narrating, slow, hushed] Hettrun stopped breathing.

A Dawi name says itself. There is no way to speak it that hides the grade, and the grade is the oath, and the oath-name sits in the second place, after the given name, hard or soft. Kaltekk, while the oath held. Kalte, if it had broken. Every Dawi who ever heard her father's name had heard which, in the first syllable, before the name was finished. The surface philologists had written it all down in their grammars and called it a curiosity of phonetics.

She did not know which it would be.

That was the whole of it, sitting in the out-of-true room with the rain on the glass. She did not know. He had sworn his oath at the furnace door at forty, to keep the heat in the Hallan furnaces and the water out of Kolmthruk, and she had been there and cut the entry herself, her first. And then the boy with the paper. The pumps. The gallery. A Guild man on the stone with his reading book in his coat. She did not know what the Crystal in her father's chest had made of that, and the language would tell her before he could.

Oskrin put his furnace hands flat on the table. He looked at Ashcombe, at her face, the way a Dawi only looks at someone he means to tell the truth.

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent, slow, every word set down] Oskrin Kaltekk Hallan.”

[narrating, slow, hushed] The kk came down like a hammer on a cold set. Strong. All the way.

The oath held.

Hettrun heard it and something in her chest went hot and then cold. It held. He had kept the heat in and the water out, as he swore, and he had done it by opening a trunk main with his own hands in the dark, and a man with a chewed pencil had walked into the draw. And the oath held, because the oath was about the water, and the water had stayed out. The language had no grade for the rest; it told you only whether you had done what you swore, and nothing else at all.

Across the table Ashcombe's head came up. She had heard the hard consonant. Hettrun saw her hear it, and saw her not understand what it meant, and saw her decide it meant he was innocent.

*It does not mean that,* Hettrun thought. *There is no word for what it means.*

Her father did not wait to be asked again.

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent, in the old metre, steady] Oskrin Kaltekk Hallan, the main above the meter, opened. On. Tobias Wren of the Guild, in the gallery, dead by it. On.” He turned his head, at last, and looked at his daughter. “Enter it.”

[narrating, low and measured] She cut it.

The pen went into the board at the true angle, and the strokes came the way they always came, straight and fixed, the doubled stroke for the strong grade on his name, the single strokes for the rest. Four feet to the line. It scanned. She said it aloud as she cut it, low, because a line not spoken is not entered, and her voice did not shake until the dead man's name, and then it did, and she let it.

“[soft, precise Finnish accent, chanting in metre, voice catching] Oskrin Kaltekk Hallan, the main above the meter, opened. On. Tobias Wren of the Guild, in the gallery, dead by it. [sniffles] On.”

[narrating, low and measured] Then she cut another line beneath it.

“[soft, precise Finnish accent, chanting in metre, steadier] The Hallan meter, proved on the ninth, two days after the death. On. The arrears, unknown. On. The entry holds.”

[pen scratching] [narrating, low and measured] Across the table Ashcombe picked up her own pen, looked at it a moment, and wrote one line in her record. Hettrun could not read it upside down, and did not try.

“[crisp, received-pronunciation English accent, quiet] Your hold will be billed,” Ashcombe said. “And the Guild will be asked about the ninth, in open court, at the Assize, with your book on the table in front of the Bench.” She put her spectacles on by their ribbon. “I hope you know what you've done.”

“[low, gravelly Finnish accent] It is entered,” said Oskrin. “That is what we know.”

[narrating, low and measured] Hettrun blotted the board. She took Tobias Wren's reading book from the docket, closed it over the half-finished number, and set it square on the table in front of the examiner, square to the out-of-true room, so that anyone who knew how to look would see it was the only straight thing there.

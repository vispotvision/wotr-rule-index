# The Weight of an Infant

*The Alabaster Court, the lower chapel. Mid-morning.*

---

Wax, wet stone, and milk.

The last one did not belong and Verinus noticed it before he noticed anything else, because the lower chapel had smelled of exactly two things for four hundred years and a third arriving meant a household had come in with all its noise attached. Beeswax off the standing candles, forty of them, the good tapers and none of the tallow they burned upstairs. The permanent damp of white stone with a harbour underneath it. And now milk, and warm wool, and the faint sourness of a cloth that has been used and not yet washed.

The bell in the outer court rang the half and stopped.

Verinus stood on the low step below the basin with the cap in his hand and his sleeves turned back, and his fingers were chalked to the second knuckle because he had come straight down from the slope and had forgotten, and he was seventy-one years old and had been forgetting that particular thing for about six years now.

"My lord Palatine."

Charles Lambert came up the aisle with his hat off and his son on his forearm, and the resemblance arrived a full three seconds before the name did.

The frame was Edward's frame. Tall and gone narrow through the shoulder, stooped a few degrees at the neck, the same long face and the same hard widow's peak, except that Charles was forty-seven and the hair had not gone through to iron yet, so it read as a man in his prime wearing a shape that in his brother read as a man in decline. The bishop's charcoal, well cut and well kept. A pectoral on a chain that had all its links matched, which was the first difference, and Verinus filed it.

Then the face. Edward Lambert's face did nothing at all and had done nothing for eleven years. Charles's face was pleasant. Open at the eyes, quick at the mouth, the face of a man people told things to without deciding to, and Verinus had been in enough rooms to recognise a Lambert with charm as a considerably more dangerous object than a Lambert without.

"Bishop."

"You remembered."

"I have never once forgotten a name and it is not a virtue, it is a condition. Bring him."

The woman came up behind her husband and stopped two paces back, which was the correct distance and which she held without any evidence of having thought about it.

She was mid-thirties and northern, from far enough north that the Concord had no name for it beyond a compass point. Broad flat face, high cheek, dark eyes set deep. Blue-black hair in two plaits pinned across the crown, and a coat of soft-tanned hide under the good wool the Court had lent her at the door, which she had accepted and had not fastened. Two fine tattooed lines from her lower lip to her chin. Her hands were down at her sides. She had not looked at the basin once. She was looking at her son and at nothing else in the room.

Verinus read the stillness as shyness in a strange house.

"Your name, madam."

"Anyu." A pause of exactly the right length. "Anyu Qilak, my lord. Lambert since the spring."

"Then you have been Lambert for eleven months and you are already better at this room than your husband, which I would like entered somewhere."

Charles laughed. It was a good laugh, easy and low, and it went up into the vault and came back thin.

"Dabney," he said, and turned the bundle so that the child's face came out of the wool. "Dabney Charles Lambert. Four months on the ninth."

Verinus put the cap down on the step and held out both arms.

The child came across without complaint. He was heavier than he looked, the way they all are, and he was awake, and he had the dark unfocused eyes of an infant who has not yet decided that faces are interesting. A fist worked its way out of the cloth and closed on the front of Verinus's grey.

And the floor moved.

It did not move. Verinus knew precisely what had happened and precisely what had not, and what had happened was that something in the room had acquired weight, and the something had not spoken, and an infant who has not spoken should displace nothing at all. That was the whole architecture. Statements had density. A thing that had made no statement had no density. He had held the Sounding for forty-one years and had never in that time been given a reading on a person who had not opened their mouth.

He held the child a moment longer than the rite required.

*Nothing. He has said nothing. There is no claim in the room and the room is carrying one anyway, and I do not have a category for that, and I am going to perform the blessing before I have time to become frightened of it.*

Verinus stepped up onto the basin ledge, wet two fingers, and set them on the boy's forehead.

"Sine hunc puerum maiore iudicio ornari," he said, "atque anteverte omnibus qui ei nocituri sunt."

The candles did what candles do in a draught.

And Dabney Lambert's eyes went gold.

The iris only, from the inner ring outward, a warm dense metallic gold that came up through the brown and held for perhaps two seconds and then went back down into it, and the boy did not react at all, and the fist stayed closed on the front of Verinus's coat.

Nobody in the chapel made a sound.

Verinus finished the rite because a rite abandoned partway is worse than a rite performed badly.

"Dictum est," he said. "Ponderatum est."

He dried his fingers on the cloth over the basin rim and turned around, and the mother had come forward one pace and had stopped again, and her hands were still at her sides.

*He saw it. He is going to say something now, and what he says next is the whole of the rest of my son's life.*

Verinus gave the child back.

She took him the way a woman takes a child she is not going to hand to anyone else for a while, and she said nothing, and she went four paces down the aisle and sat in the third row with the boy against her shoulder and her cheek down on his head.

"Sit with me," said Verinus.

---

They sat on the second step, which was cold and too low for a man of seventy-one and which Verinus used deliberately because the alabaster chair against the wall was for people who needed him to be the Palatine and Charles Lambert was about to be told something else.

"I have a task I would like you to encumber."

"That is a heavy verb, my lord."

"It is the correct one. I am not going to dress it as an opportunity." Verinus put his forearms on his knees. "I want you in the north. I want you travelling with the Kusharan party, alongside Dawn Guard and whoever else that Sovereign sends, and I want you to be my hand out there for as long as it takes to find out what is actually holding Stannvaard's gate shut."

Charles did not answer immediately. That was the first thing his brother would have done and it was interesting to see it in the younger one.

"You cannot go yourself."

"I cannot go myself. Deacon Barrow would file an objection before the cutter left the mole, and Lord Knight Commander Thole would simply stand in the doorway, and she has done it eleven times and she has never once been wrong." He turned a hand over. "The Ballast does not require my agreement. That is the entire point of appointing her. So I stay, and somebody I trust goes, and the list of people I trust is short and getting shorter and you are on it."

"Why."

"Because you are clever enough to decipher a thing that nobody has written down, which is a rarer competence than either of us pretends. And because I have watched you in the Curia for nine years and you are the only bishop on that bench who reads the objection before he reads the motion." Verinus looked sideways. "And because I would like you to sit in this seat one day, and a man who has never been anywhere should not have it."

Charles's fingers went to the pectoral chain and turned a link.

"You will forgive me for asking the obvious question."

"I would think less of you for not asking it."

"What is the shape of this errand." The pleasant face had not gone anywhere, and something underneath it had gone flat, and for a moment he sounded so exactly like his brother that Verinus felt the years move. "You are sending a bishop north with a foreign sovereign's party into a fracture that has already swallowed a Primate. That is not a legation. That is a man being put somewhere so that he can be seen to have been put there."

"It is both."

"Say more, my lord."

"You will carry a legation and the legation will be genuine. Grain, shipping, and whether this Court can broker anything that does not go through the corridor." Verinus let a breath out. "And you will look at Kharven with your own eyes and tell me whether the Büri are what they were, and you will find out who is standing at the Ore Council's elbow, and you will do all of that while being a bishop of Sanctum Lux in a room full of people who have every reason to lie to a bishop of Sanctum Lux. That is the errand. I am aware of what I am asking."

"And my brother is in Kharven."

"He is."

"Which is why you are asking me and not somebody safer."

"It is one of four reasons and it is not the largest, and I am not going to rank them for you." Verinus said it without heat. "Edward will be useful to you and you will be useful to him and neither of you will enjoy the arrangement, because I have met one of you and I am now looking at the other and I can see how that household worked."

Charles laughed again. Shorter this time.

"We have not spoken in nine years."

"Then you will have a great deal to catch up on and very little time in which to do it. Take the boy's mother with you or leave her here under this Court's protection, and I will support either and I will not offer an opinion, because I have never had a wife and a man without a wife who advises on the subject deserves what he gets."

"Anyu will go where the child goes. That was settled before it was discussed."

"Then that is settled."

The harbour came up through the high window. Somebody outside was calling a boat in.

"My lord." Charles's voice had dropped. "You are going to have to say the other thing. I have been sitting here waiting for you to say the other thing and you have taken a remarkably long time about it."

Verinus looked down the aisle to the third row, where a woman from the far north was sitting with her cheek on her son's head and had not moved in a quarter of an hour.

"His eyes went gold," he said. "You saw it. Your wife saw it and did not need to see it, which is a different sentence and I would like you to think about it later rather than now." He rubbed the chalk off one knuckle with his thumb. "I have seen that in one bloodline. It sits with the Altan Büri, it sits with the crown line specifically, and it has no business whatsoever appearing in a Concord bishop's son in a chapel on this water."

"I know what my wife is and I know what I am."

"Then we are agreed that neither of you explains it." Verinus turned his head. "Which leaves the interesting possibility, which is that it did not come down to him through either of you."

Charles's hand stopped on the chain.

"Say that again."

"I am not going to, because I have no evidence and I have a Domain that makes speculation expensive." Verinus straightened his back and something in it complained. "Here is what I have. I held him. There is a connection to the Plane of Fate in that child and it is not small and it is not latent, and I knew it before I opened my mouth, and I want you to understand what an unusual sentence that is coming from a man whose entire instrument requires somebody to speak first."

"He is four months old."

"He is four months old and he displaced my floor."

Charles Lambert sat on a cold step in a chapel and looked at his hands.

"What happens to him," he said.

"Nothing. For as long as I am on this earth, nothing happens to him, and nobody learns of him, and that is not a kindness and I want it received as it is issued." Verinus's voice did not rise. "If the Curia learns that an infant in a bishop's household carries Fate-plane inheritance, he stops being your son and becomes a question, and questions in this Court get answered. I have watched it done. I have signed one of them." He let that sit. "So he will not be written down. There will be no entry, no record of the blessing, no name in the day-book. Deacon Barrow will not be told and she will work it out inside a year and she will not be told anyway, and when she asks me directly I will decline to answer, which she will correctly understand as confirmation."

"You are asking me to trust you with my son while you send me four thousand leagues away from him."

"Yes."

"That is a very poor bargain, my lord."

"It is the worst bargain I have offered anyone in nineteen years, and I have offered it because the alternative was offering you a better one and lying about the second half." Verinus put his hand out flat on the step and pushed himself up, and it took him longer than it had at sixty. "You may refuse. I want that said plainly and out loud in a room that weighs things. Refuse, and you go back to your bench and nothing changes and I will never mention it again."

Charles stood. He was taller than Verinus and had been trying not to be for the whole conversation.

"When does the cutter go."

"Tide is at the sixth hour tomorrow. Fend will carry your papers and he will not know what is in them, which is the only way that boy is capable of keeping a secret and I have started building around it."

"And if I find that the thing holding Stannvaard shut is larger than a mining council."

"Then you will write it down," Verinus said, "and you will send it by three separate routes, and you will assume that two of them are read."

He picked his cap up off the step and put it on and settled it, and then he walked down the aisle past the third row, and as he passed he put two chalked fingers briefly against the back of the sleeping child's head.

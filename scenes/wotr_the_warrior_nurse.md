# The Warrior Nurse

*The teaching house at Sum-gol · The Alabaster Court*

---

## I. WHAT SHE OWED HIM

Seal oil, boiled willow, and the sour animal warmth of a room that has had a sick man in it for four days.

Frithia had the shutter cracked two fingers because a closed room breeds worse than cold does, and the draught coming through it carried salt off the gulf and the resinous smell of the pines below the terrace. Under her knees the floor mats gave slightly. Somewhere out past the wall a class was running the long count in unison, forty young voices, badly synchronised, and one of them a half-beat behind the rest for the fourth morning running because nobody had yet told the boy to listen instead of watching.

She would tell him. Later. After.

Kwon Mu-jin lay on his back with his arms outside the blanket because he had thrown them out sometime in the night, and she had put them back twice and had stopped putting them back.

He looked worse lying down. That was the thing nobody warned you about with the ones who worked past their reserve. Upright, a man carried his exhaustion in his posture and you could read it and correct it. Flat on his back with the tension out of him, the face went honest, and what Mu-jin's face said honestly was that he was twenty-four years old and had not slept properly in something like six years. Grey under the eyes so deep it looked like a bruise. Cheekbones standing out. The skin over his sternum moving with a breath that had been shallow and quick for two days and had only in the last hour started going down into him properly.

His hands were the worst of it. Ink at every fingernail, worked into the cuticle so far it had stopped washing out, and both index fingers callused along the outer edge where he braced them to draw a circle. A clerk's callus rather than a swordsman's, built up over a decade until it had become armour.

Frithia sat back on her heels and let out a breath.

"You are a foolish summoner."

She said it in her own tongue first, out of habit, and then in Accord common because he had once told her he could hear the difference even asleep and she had never entirely decided whether he was joking.

"You put yourself between a thing and forty people who were not worth what you spent, and you did the arithmetic before you did it, and that is what makes it foolish. A man who does not know the cost is only ignorant. You knew." She reached over and put the back of two fingers against his throat under the jaw, counting. The pulse came steady and slow, and she took her hand away and wiped her palm on her thigh. "That is the difference between brave and stupid where I come from, and we do not have a word for the one you did."

Eighty-eight. Down from a hundred and twelve when they brought him in.

She was thirty-six and built the way her mother had been built and her mother's mother, which was short and wide through the shoulder and heavy through the thigh, a frame that the ice country produced because the ice country killed everything else. Black hair cut blunt at the jaw and pushed back under a hat of ringed-seal fur that she had made herself and would not be parted from indoors, in council, or in front of the Sovereign. Broad flat face. Dark eyes set deep under a heavy brow. Three fine vertical lines tattooed on her chin from lip to jaw, done when she was fourteen with a bone needle and soot, and a fourth line she had added herself with a mirror and a shaking hand at twenty-two, which was not how it was done and which she had never explained to anyone here.

Her hands were split across the knuckles from the cold and from work. There was a white seam through the meat of her left forearm where a walrus had opened her at nineteen and where she had learned that a wound you can see is not the one that kills you.

They called her the Warrior Nurse in this country and thought they were paying her a compliment.

At home it had been an insult, and a specific one. *She fights and then she mends, so she is only half of each.* Her uncle had used it at a feast when she was twenty and everybody had laughed, and she had laughed too, because the alternative was worse, and she had spent the sixteen years since making it true in a way that would have shut his mouth.

"I envy you," Frithia said.

She said it quietly and to a man who could not hear it, which was the only condition under which she was ever going to say it at all.

"The strength is not the part. I have strength and it has never once been the thing that was needed. I envy that you need to be strong, and that the need has a shape, and that you know what the shape is." She pulled the blanket back up over his arm and this time she left it there. "I have watched you for two years and you have never once done a thing because it would look well. My father's people were like that. Four men in the whole settlement, and every one of them dead before fifty, and every one of them dead of the same thing."

*The ice does not take the weak first. It takes the one who keeps going back out.*

She had not said the proverb aloud in six years. It arrived on its own, in her mother's voice, and she let it sit and did not repeat it.

Out on the terrace the long count broke apart into forty voices arguing.

Mu-jin's hand moved.

Only the fingers, and only slightly, tracing something across the blanket in a short repeated arc that his body had done ten thousand times and would apparently keep doing whether or not anyone was home to authorise it. Half a circle. Lift. Half a circle. Frithia watched him draft in his sleep and felt something go through her chest that she disliked and could not put anywhere useful.

Then he said a name.

It was not clear and it was not loud and it was two syllables, and it was not Ara Min's name and it was not his own, and Frithia had been in this house for two years and did not know it. She leaned closer with her ear over his mouth and waited through four breaths and he did not say it again.

She sat back.

She would ask him. She would not ask him. She turned it over and decided she would ask him when he was well enough to lie about it convincingly, because a man's lies told you where the wound was faster than his answers did, and she was, whatever her uncle had said, a nurse.

He had saved her life at Rimward. That was the plain fact of it and she had stopped trying to have feelings about the fact and had settled instead into the debt, which was simpler and heavier and did not require her to examine anything.

More than that, he had made her see something.

She had come south believing that her people's way was the only serious way, that everything below the ice line was soft, that a discipline you could write down was a discipline that had never been tested. And she had watched a boy of twenty-two stand in front of a Legion-class working with a piece of chalk and a book, and she had watched the thing answer him, and she had understood on the spot and without any pleasure that written-down was not the opposite of tested. Her grandmother's knowledge had died with her grandmother. Mu-jin's would not die with Mu-jin. That was not softness. That was the single most durable thing she had ever seen a person do and her whole people had never once thought of it.

She owed him her life and she owed him that, and the second one was harder to carry.

Frithia leaned in and kissed his cheek.

It was quick and it was dry and it landed on the cheekbone and not the mouth, and she was upright again inside two seconds with her hands back in her lap, and she sat there afterward with her jaw set and did not pretend to herself that it had been nothing.

"That was taken," she said. "You did not give it and I took it, and I am telling you now so that it is said in the room even if you cannot hear it. When you wake I will not tell you again. You have enough being carried on your behalf without adding mine to it."

*And if that is dishonest, then it is dishonest, and I have done worse and slept.*

She got up off her knees, which took longer than it had at twenty, and put a hand flat against the small of her back while it decided whether to argue.

The water in the basin had gone cold. She carried it to the door and threw it out over the terrace edge and watched it go down through the pines, and then she went back in and set the basin on the brazier and fed the brazier from the scuttle, three lumps, no more, because the house was rationing and she was not going to be the one who broke it.

Behind her the shutter knocked twice against its frame in the wind.

Frithia reached up without looking and fixed her hat, pulling the ringed seal down snug over her ears the way her mother had done it, and knelt back down beside the bed to count him again.

## II. THE MESSENGER GOES OUT

Wax, wet stone, and the harbour coming up through an open shutter three floors below.

The bell for the second hour had rung and stopped. Verinus VII stood at the writing slope with the cap off and set on the corner of the desk, and the two vertical lines between his brows had not moved in an hour and a half, and the pen had been in his hand for most of that without going down to the paper.

Seventy-one. His fingers had started aching in cold rooms about four years ago and he had told nobody, which was the sort of thing a man of seventy-one does and then privately grades himself for.

"It is not the sending I object to," Ilse Barrow said.

She had a chair and had not taken it. She stood square with her hands behind her back, which she did when she intended to be here a while.

"I want that on the record before you write it down, because you have a habit of hearing objection and filing it as opposition, and they are different documents." Her voice did not lift. Nobody's did in that city. "You have already sat with the Sovereign. Four times, in a cabin, at his invitation. The relationship exists. Sending word is not an escalation and I am not going to pretend that it is."

"Then say the thing you are actually here to say, Deacon."

"You are about to put the Holy Sea's name on a triangle."

Verinus set the pen down.

"Say more."

"Zettari on one side. The Moto on the other. And this Court in the middle holding the pen." Barrow's shoulders stayed where they were. "Every one of those relationships is currently an advantage to us and every one of them is currently deniable. The moment you convene it, it stops being deniable. You will be the party that brought them together, and the party that brought them together owns the outcome whether or not it had any hand in shaping it. That is not doctrine. That is how the Curia will read it in four years when somebody wants your seat."

*And he knows it. He worked it out before I opened my mouth and he is going to do it anyway, and the reason he is going to do it anyway is that he has already decided the seat is a thing that gets spent.*

"You are correct on all of it," Verinus said.

"Then why is the pen out."

"Because the alternative is that we make enemies of both of them by being careful." He turned the pen once on the slope so that it lay parallel to the paper's edge. "Think about what happens if I do nothing, Deacon. A courier arrives from Kharven asking for grain and ships. Somebody else's courier arrives from Kushara asking about corridors. Both of them are refused politely by this Court, in writing, with a seal, and both refusals are accurate and neither one is remembered accurately. In four years the Moto remember that Alabaster would not move for them and the Zettari remember that Alabaster would not move with them, and the only house on this water that has kept its hands clean is standing between two powers who have each independently decided we are not worth consulting."

He picked the pen back up.

"I have been Palatine for nineteen years and I have watched this Court become extremely good at not being blamed for anything. That is not neutrality. That is a slow retreat conducted at a pace nobody notices."

Barrow's jaw moved once at the corner.

"And there is the other reason," Verinus said, "which is the one I would prefer to be wrong about."

"The Primate."

"Six weeks of silence from a man who has never in nineteen years failed to answer a letter." He wrote three words and stopped. "If the Grand Church is being used as a wall by somebody who needed a wall, then the two parties in the best position to see the shape of the thing from outside are the two parties currently being walled off. One of them holds a continent's worth of grain and shipping. The other one has been bled for eleven years by exactly this mechanism and has had four courteous replies to show for it." He looked up. "I would like them in the same conversation. I would like to be in the room when they are. And I would like all of that to have happened before somebody explains to me what I should have done instead."

Behind Barrow, in the doorway she had come through and not shut, boots hit the flags at a pace that was not appropriate for an indoor corridor.

Sub-Serjeant Elber Fend came around the frame at speed, corrected, and stood.

He was twenty and had the physical constitution of a man who has never once been tired, and he had grown out of a uniform sometime in the last eight months and nobody had ordered him a new one, so the cuffs sat above the wrist bone. Sand-coloured hair cut short and going in four directions. A long face, all forehead, with a permanently slightly surprised set to the eyes that had cost him at least two promotions and had never once cost him a friend.

"Lord Knight Commander says the fast cutter is available, my lord Palatine, and she says to tell you she is not asking what for, and she says to tell you that the fact that she is not asking should be entered as her opinion."

"Enter it," said Verinus.

"She also says if you want it to go tonight the tide is at the sixth hour and after that it is tomorrow's ninth."

"Then it goes tonight."

Barrow said nothing at all, at length.

Verinus wrote. It took him six lines and he did not cross anything out, which anyone who had watched him write for nineteen years would have understood as the difference between a difficult letter and one he had finished composing four days ago and had been waiting to be talked out of.

He folded it. He heated the stick over the lamp, laid the wax, and set the seal into it, and held the seal down a beat longer than the wax required.

"Fend."

"My lord."

"You will carry this yourself as far as the cutter and you will hand it to Captain Kesh's counterpart and to nobody in between, and if anybody at all asks you what it is, you will tell them you do not know."

Fend's face did something complicated.

"My lord, I will not know. That is true. I want to say plainly that I am aware you know I cannot lie, and I would like it recorded that I noticed you gave me an instruction I am capable of following."

*He is going to be extraordinary in about fifteen years and I am not going to be here to watch it.*

"Recorded," Verinus said. "Go."

The boots went out at a pace that was still not appropriate for an indoor corridor.

Barrow stayed where she was for a while after that, and the harbour came up through the open shutter, and a gull went over the roofline making the noise gulls make.

"I am going to write my own account of this conversation," she said at last. "Tonight. Before I sleep. And I am going to file it where it can be found."

"I should hope so, Deacon. That is the post."

"It will not be kind."

"It should not be." Verinus put the cap back on and settled it, and reached past her for the ledger. "If I am wrong about this, I would like the record to show that somebody in this room said so at the time, with her name on it, and I would like her to have been specific."

He opened the ledger to the day's page, dipped, and entered the sending in his own hand: the hour, the vessel, the tide, and the addressee.

Then he ruled a line under it and closed the book.

# The Weight of a Courier

*Kharven-Seat · The Alabaster Court · Altherion*

---

## I. THE FINGER TO BEND

The fire had burned down to the stage where it gave more smell than heat, resin and old ash and the particular sourness of green wood that has been forced. Somebody had banked it wrong. The heel of Sodoku's boot found a nailhead standing proud of the flagstone and he pressed on it twice without deciding to, and out in the passage a woman was telling a man that four ricks of green would not see him to Ice-out and that he should have said so a month ago.

"My beliefs will not move."

He said it into the cup and then set the cup down.

"That is the first thing and I am not going to soften it. The second thing is that I understand your approach, sister, and I am not pretending to. You have made an argument about weight on both sides of a scale and you made it in the vocabulary of the thing you are defending, and that is more work than most people do."

He turned the cup a quarter turn, which was a gesture he had picked up from a man sitting four feet away and had not noticed he was copying.

"I am not going to answer it tonight."

Lambert's eyes came up. Only the eyes.

*He has just declined the argument in front of the whole table and called it deferral, and his father used to do the identical thing, and his father did it because he had already decided and did not want to be talked out of it.*

"I have an answer. The answer costs an evening, and we have a courier nine days out, and I would sooner spend the evening on the thing that can still be changed." Sodoku pulled the plank of bread toward himself and then pushed it away again, untouched. "And because my brother is right, and he has been right for the length of this meal, and everyone in this room has been arguing about priests while the hand around our throat belongs to somebody else entirely."

He put two fingers on the boards.

"Sancta Lux does not hold Stannvaard. Say that out loud and hear how obvious it is. The Grand Church sits at Altherion and it has jurisdiction in four worlds and no writ over an ore quota. It cannot open a granary. It cannot close a corridor. It cannot instruct a factor house to hold a shipment for two seasons and it has never once tried, because the day it tries is the day the Ore Council remembers whose mountain the church is standing on." His fingers moved half an inch across the wood. "Twelve guild masters. A council chair. The River Crown's republic underneath all of it. That is where the decision lives, and it has lived there for two hundred years, and no theology I have ever been furious about has moved one wagon."

"Then why have you spent four minutes being furious about it," Lambert said.

"Because I am capable of both." Sodoku did not look up. "Write it down if you like. Under acceptable losses."

Yoko's ear flicked once and settled.

"Consider what the pattern actually is," Sodoku said. "Grain cut by three quarters over two years. Inscription substrate delivered in full, on schedule, every quarter, for eleven years, through the collapse, through two winters when the corridor was shut to everything on wheels. Four letters from this house asking for an explanation and three courteous replies with no fact in them." He let that stand. "That is not a trade posture. A trade posture has a price attached to it. This has no price attached to it, which means somebody is paying the price somewhere else and it is not showing up on our side of the ledger."

"Blackmail," said Lambert.

"Blackmail. Or something with a longer word." Sodoku's jaw moved once. "A council of twelve does not act as one for eleven years without either an agreement or a hand. If it is an agreement then there is a document and Tabitha can find the document. If it is a hand then the hand is on somebody's family, and there is a Dawi guild master somewhere in that mountain who has been voting the same way for a decade with his stomach turning over every time."

He picked up the cup again and found it empty.

"So we make it expensive. War is off the table, and nobody sitting at this one wants it or could pay for it. Pressure. We stop being convenient." His thumb went along the rim. "Kharven ironwood goes into Dawi tool handles because nothing else in the four quarters holds an edge-shock the way it does, and they have been buying it at a friendly rate for two centuries because they have never once had to imagine buying it at any other rate. That stops. Escort cover on the northern corridor stops. The border reports we pass to their commission for nothing, and have passed for nothing since my father signed it, stop." He set the cup down. "And the Mandate's western approaches are watched by our mountain companies and by nobody else, and Stannvaard has never once had to garrison that gap, and they should be made to think about the gap."

The room got quieter than the fire.

Lambert took a slow breath in through the nose.

"Say the last one again, your highness."

"I said what I said."

"You said you would consider ceasing to hold the western approaches in order to make a point to a mining consortium." Lambert's voice did not change register at all, which was how everyone at that table understood he had stopped being agreeable. "I want it stated plainly and not gestured at, because gestured at it reads as leverage, and stated plainly it is Bram Greymane's ridge and eleven hundred people who live behind it."

"I said they should be made to think about it."

"They will think about it. So will the Mandate." Lambert flattened the burned hand on his knee. "And then there is the ironwood, which I will take separately, because you have proposed to shut off the one export this kingdom still has in order to injure the only customer who buys it. Brackenhold eats because the ironwood moves. Whitmoor eats because the ironwood moves. Cold Cross eats because the ironwood moves. You will be cutting the throat of the only three towns in Kharven currently on a full ration, and you will be doing it in the second month, and the Dawi will feel it in their tool inventory in about a year."

Sodoku's fingers stopped on the wood.

"How long."

"Ten months before a guild master notices. Fourteen before it reaches the Council floor." Lambert did not enjoy saying it and did not disguise saying it. "Kharven will have starved the timber towns for a year in order to make a point that arrives after the point stops mattering. This is the correct instrument at the wrong scale, my lord. It is a lever built for a kingdom with two years of grain, and we have one hundred and sixty days at two thirds."

The nailhead was still standing proud under Sodoku's heel. He pressed it a third time.

"Then it goes in the drawer and it stays in the drawer until we have eaten," he said. "And we build it anyway. I want the whole of it costed. Every dependency they have on us, what it is worth to them, what it costs us to withhold, and how long each one takes to bite. We build it in order to know it and we do not touch it. A man who has not costed his own leverage is a man who will use it badly the first time he is frightened."

"That I will do tonight," Lambert said.

"And the letter goes first." Sodoku pushed the bench back and stood, and the whole room heard the joints in him. "Kushara. Grain that does not come through the corridor, ships that do not answer to the Ore Council, and a courier who arrives before the Thin Weeks close over the roads. Robin left nine days ago on a fast horse and I have been assuming nine days is enough, and I would like somebody in this house to tell me whether that assumption is worth anything."

"It is not," said Hild.

She had her chin propped on the heel of her hand and her eyes on the fire.

"The southern road is out past the fourth ford. Tabitha walked it. He will have gone the long way through the Ironwood March and that is four days added and nobody told him it was four days because nobody knew when he left." She did not move her chin. "So it is thirteen days, not nine, and you have been counting wrong since the equinox."

*And now he will look at me and work out that I have been reading Edward's road reports, and Edward will work out that I have been reading them before he redacts them, and I will lose that.*

Sodoku stood there and did the arithmetic and found that his daughter had done it first.

"Then we send a second rider on the second road," he said, "and we accept that we are paying twice for one message, and we stop pretending that is wasteful."

He put his hand flat on the table and pushed himself off it.

## II. WHAT THE COURIER WEIGHED

Salt, wet stone, and hot wax.

Robin Ice had been smelling all three for six hours and had run out of anything to compare them to. The Alabaster Court was built downhill and open to the water, and every corridor in it carried a draught that came off the harbour with the tide in it, and the white stone held that damp with the obstinacy northern granite reserves for cold, so that a man walking through it was never dry and was never quite cold either. The floors were worn into shallow troughs down their centres. Four hundred years of feet. Somewhere ahead a bell rang twice and stopped.

The Lord Knight Commander walking him in had not spoken since the gate.

Ser Marten Quay was a wide grey man in the Court's harbour-blue with a surcoat that had been let out at the sides and taken in again at some point in the last decade, and he walked half a pace ahead and slightly to the left, which put his body between Robin and every doorway they passed. He had asked for the seal at the gate. He had looked at the seal for four seconds. He had then given it back without comment and had been in a foul temper ever since, and Robin, who had spent his whole life reading which adults were angry and which adults were angry at him, could not work out which this was.

"Ser."

"No."

"I have not asked anything yet."

"You are going to ask whether I dislike you," Quay said, without turning around. "I do not. I have never met you. Save it."

They came through an arch and the air changed.

Robin felt it in his chest before he understood it. Something in the neighbourhood of cold and of pressure and exactly neither. Like the first four feet of a dive, that sensation of the world putting a hand flat on your sternum and holding it there without malice. His ears did not pop. His breathing changed anyway, went shallower and higher and he had to reach for it and pull it back down.

*It is a room. It is only a room. Get through the room.*

The meeting chamber was small and had one long table in it and no chair at its head. There was a chair standing against the wall, alabaster white, clearly heavy, clearly ceremonial, and clearly not in use, and there was a second empty chair placed at the table itself with nobody near it and nothing in front of it.

Two people were waiting.

The woman was sixty and built square, in the grey of the Court with a First Auditor's black cording at the cuff, hair iron-coloured and pinned back hard enough to pull at the temples. Broad through the shoulder, thick through the wrist, the hands of somebody who had done a trade before she had done this. Her mouth was set in a line that had clearly been there before Robin arrived and was not going to be moved by his presence.

The man was standing.

Tall and gone narrow with it, shoulders forward over a chest that was too deep for the rest of him. Grey wool to the instep. A stole down the front worked in raised gold coils the width of a thumbnail, hundreds of them, thousands, stacked in overlapping ranks from the belt to the hem, and Robin looked at them for a moment too long before he understood that they were not decoration and that somebody had put each one there for a reason. The cap was in his hands. He had taken it off. His lips carried a faint blue cast at the edges, and there were two vertical lines drawn deep between his brows that had not relaxed in some years.

"Say it at depth," said Verinus VII, "or do not say it."

Robin had a speech. He had carried it nine days on a horse and thirteen days in total and had said it aloud to himself in the dark at four separate way-stations, and he opened his mouth and delivered it.

"My Lord Palatine. I come with word from the True High King of Kharven, Sodoku Moto. He sends notice of distress and requests the support of the Holy Sea in his endeavours, specifically naval and grain support, and asks that you consider the ancient friendship between our houses in your deliberation."

It weighed nothing.

He felt it go. That was the horrible part, and nobody had warned him, because nobody in Kharven had known to warn him. The words came out of his mouth and went up instead of down, the way chaff goes up off a threshing floor, and the room registered them and did not register them, and Deacon Barrow's jaw moved once at the corner in a way that told him she had heard a thousand of those and had stopped being irritated by them around the four hundredth.

Verinus tilted his head slightly down and to the left.

"That is a correct message," he said. "Whose question was it?"

"My Lord?"

"You have brought me an accurate account of what a king wants. I believe every word of it and it has told me nothing, because nothing in it cost you anything to say." He turned the cap once in his hands. "You rode thirteen days. What did you see."

Robin's throat closed and opened.

"We will absolutely not do this," said Barrow.

She said it to Verinus and not to Robin, which Robin noticed and filed.

"The Grand Church has a standing position on the northern corridor and we have a chapter hold in this city that reports past this table to Altherion, and the moment a Holy Sea keel goes north with grain in it we are a party to a quarrel between a mining council and an exiled house, and we are a party to it with our name on the manifest." Her hands stayed flat on the table. "I have watched you weigh four hundred of these. You are going to consider it. You consider all of them. I am telling you now, in front of the courier, so that nobody afterward can say the Curia was not warned."

"Noted, entered, and I am considering it," Verinus said.

"Then hear the rest of it. You would be moving against the expressed will of the High Church of Sancta Lux." Barrow's voice did not rise, because nobody's did in that city. "That is not a censure. That is a stripping. You would not be the Palatine by the spring convocation and you know it, and whatever you think you are buying, you would be buying it with the seat and not with your own coin, and the seat is not yours to spend."

*Nineteen years and he still thinks the chair against the wall means he is not sitting in it.*

Verinus did not answer her. He looked at Robin.

"What did you see," he said again.

And Robin Ice, who was seventeen and a bastard out of a bastard's line and had been chosen for this errand precisely because nobody would miss him, discovered that the speech was gone and there was nothing behind it but the road.

"There is a death-house at Coldbeck with forty in it," he said. "They cannot bury them. The ground is shut until Ice-out and the man who reads the names is a cooper because the last one who could do it properly died four years ago and the liturgy went with him. I stopped there on the sixth night. A woman gave me half a rind because I was on the Crown's business and I ate it and I have thought about that rind every day since, because she had four children in the room behind her and she gave it to me anyway, and I did not have anything to give her back except a sealed pouch I was not allowed to open."

He stopped.

He had not planned any of it and had no idea where the rest was.

"Her name is Ada Foss," he said. "I asked. I did not have to ask. I asked because I wanted to be able to say it somewhere that mattered and I did not think this would be the room."

The floor moved.

It did not move. Robin knew it did not move. Something went through the boards under his boots the way a wave goes under a hull, a long low displacement that arrived and passed and left the room fractionally lower than it had been, and Barrow's flat hands came off the table half an inch and went back down, and the two vertical lines between Verinus's brows did not change at all.

Nobody spoke for four seconds.

"Entered at weight," said Verinus.

He put the cap on the table.

"Deacon, you are correct on the procedure and correct on the cost, and I want that recorded before I say the next part." He looked at the empty chair without appearing to notice that he had. "The Palatine is required to weigh all of it. Including the part where the office ends. That is not courage and I would like it entered as arithmetic, because a seat that will not spend itself on anything is a seat that has already been spent on nothing."

"Verinus."

"There is a second thing, and it is the reason I have not refused this in the doorway." He folded his hands into his sleeves. "This does not smell like the Grand Church. Read the shape of it. A quota held for eleven years, through two closed winters, in a matter that touches no doctrine and produces no finding. That is not an order that measures. That is an order being used as a wall by somebody who needed a wall." A pause. "And the Word of Judger has not answered a private letter of mine in six weeks. Aurelian answers everything. He answers courteously and at length and he never says anything, and he has never once simply not answered."

Barrow's head came around.

"You think the Primate is behind it."

"I think the Primate has stopped writing," Verinus said, "and I have known him for nineteen years and I cannot make those two facts sit together."

## III. THE HELD BREATH

Wet iron, lamp oil, and the smell that consecrated stone acquires when it has been used as a floor for eight hundred years.

The cell was clean. That was the detail Aurelian Prudentius kept returning to and could not account for. Somebody had swept it, and had swept it recently, and the straw was fresh and the slop bucket had been changed within the hour, and none of that was consistent with the way the two men outside the door had spoken to him when they brought him down. He had a bench, a blanket of good weight, and a lamp with oil in it, and the lamp was the part he genuinely could not resolve. Nobody gives a lamp to a man they intend to forget about.

He sat with his back against the wall and his hands folded, warm, in front of him.

The door was iron on a stone frame set into a wall two feet thick, and none of it was of the slightest consequence. He had held the Word of Judger for eleven years and the Register listed a striking force he had never expressed and an unlock he had never used, and the honest description of his situation was that he was in a room he could remove, guarded by men he could suspend before either of them finished turning, in a building he had walked into on his own feet after a note arrived that named four Attestants and a scriptorium.

The note had not made a threat. That was the craft in it. It had simply listed where four people were, in the present tense, and had asked him to come and discuss a procedural matter.

*They have understood something about me that the Curia has not understood in eleven years, and I want to know who told them, and I am not going to find out from in here.*

He had been ill for six weeks. The fever was not the cause of it, the fever being constant since he was twenty-seven. The cause was that he had stopped answering his correspondence. Fourteen letters. Verinus's among them, twice. He had read every one of them and had suspended each at the interval before the reply resolved, and they sat now where everything else sat, stacked in the order they had arrived, waiting for a release he had not scheduled.

He raised his right hand and looked at the back of it in the lamplight.

Then he closed his eyes and stopped breathing.

The lattice seated. It always seated in the same place, a little behind and below the sternum, in the interval, and it drew nothing from him because the Mahuo working had never held tethers and had never needed to. It sat in the ambient field and the ambient field here was consecrated stone, which was the only good news the room contained. His skin was warm. His skin was always warm. Under the pause it went warmer, and the straw in the corner nearest him gave off the faintest smell of a dry season.

He reached down.

The Plane of Fate is not a place and the Paths are not roads, and the two errors are the same error made at different scales. What Aurelian did in the cell had no motion in it. He found the thread of relation that already existed between a man in a locked room in Altherion and a sovereign four thousand leagues south whom he had met twice, both times in Accord chambers, both times briefly, and both times with the specific mutual courtesy of two men who have privately concluded that the other one is dangerous and has not yet decided in which direction.

The thread was thin and it was real, and Attraction Force is the law of recognition, and recognition does not require affection.

He pushed the message down it.

*Stannvaard is encumbered. The Council votes as one and has for eleven years. It is not an agreement. There is a hand on it and the hand is not the Church's and the Church is being used as the wall. I am held. I came here on my own feet and I would do it again and that is not a complaint. Move grain north if you can move grain north. Do not move soldiers. Not yet.*

That was all of it. He had spent four days composing it and had cut it to that.

And then, because a message sent is a completed act, and because he had not completed one since he was twenty-seven, something at the bottom of the stack came loose.

He had told the Grand Church's physicians four times that the pyrexia was chronic and idiopathic and he had corrected none of their reports, and what happened in the cell was that the oldest thing he held shifted a quarter turn and did not release and made itself felt, and thirty-one years of deferred thermodynamic cost moved through a fifty-eight-year-old body all at once and then settled back into holding.

His breath came in. Loud, in a stone room, involuntary.

The lamp flame stood straight up, elongated, and thinned to a blue thread, and every surface within a yard of him went to the temperature of a hand held too long against skin, and the fresh straw in the corner browned along its edges and did not catch.

Aurelian sat forward with both palms on his knees and let it pass through him.

When it was finished he was still holding everything he had been holding a minute before, minus one, and he did not know which one had gone, because a stack releases from the bottom and the bottom of his stack was thirty-one years old and he had never written down what it was.

The small closed smile had not moved.

Out in the corridor a guard's boots came four paces closer, stopped, and stood there a while, and did not come any nearer.

Aurelian wiped his palms once down the front of his black working dress, folded his hands again in his lap, and began composing the second letter.

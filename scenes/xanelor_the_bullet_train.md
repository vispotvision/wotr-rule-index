# Xanelor — The Bullet Train

The Combat Arena smelled of raked sand and old ward-chalk, and under both, faintly, of the lamp oil the groundskeepers rubbed into the gallery rails every autumn. The floor sat sunk a man's height below the first tier of benches, forty paces across and raked so fine that every tooth of the rake still showed in rows. Around its rim the wards stood up invisible. They hummed. A person felt them in the teeth before hearing them, a low note that set the small bones of the jaw faintly buzzing. Up behind the last tier a door banged twice as the rest of Class X came through it, boots on the stone steps, somebody laughing, somebody arguing about where to sit, and at the far rail a groundskeeper swore without heat at a rake that had lost a tooth.

Dabney took the second tier, where the rail gave a clean line over the whole floor, and folded his hands on his knee.

He did not lean forward. Leaning forward was something other people did. A boy who had been summoning things since before he could spell them, against a boy with no magic at all and a bow on his back. It was going to be 'interesting', to say the very least. He watched the masked boy's feet, because the feet would say what happened next before anything else did.

Two benches along, Naori sat with Akira pressed to his arm and her feet going against the bench in front.

He had stopped asking how someone without magic got into the Academy. That was the wrong question. The Academy took all sorts, took letters of recommendation and strange islands and whatever else arrived at the gate with a good enough story. Class X did not. Class X took the thin top of the top, and it had taken this one, and nobody had said why.

He let the sight open, a fraction, the way his father had taught him to open a door he did not intend to walk through.

Every thread on the floor below ran forward somewhere. Hiromi's already ran hot and bright and straight at the far end of the ring, half committed before he had moved a muscle. The masked boy's ran nowhere. It ended at his own feet.

Naori closed the sight again and found he had no idea what to make of that.

On the floor, Xanelor stood perfectly still.

He held it a long moment, the old stillness from the basalt shelf, heart slow, breath slower, until the grey canvas of the coat had stopped moving on his shoulders. Then he let it go and shifted into his stance, and the shift took half a breath he could feel himself paying. Left foot forward. Knees soft. Weight sunk and spread through both soles. Hands open and low at his sides, the gloved fingers loose. The bow stayed where it was, across his back.

Nothing in him tensed. It was the first thing Drogo had ever beaten into him and the last thing he had stopped needing to be told. A tense body can be pushed. A soft one can only be moved, and a thing that can only be moved goes where it is sent and arrives whole.

He let the vessel go malleable, joint by joint, and the ring stopped being a place.

It became a nexus. Everything in it tied to everything else, every small detail and every inconsistency carried along the ties to him. The ward-hum against the fine hairs of his forearms. A grain of sand rolling under his left heel as his weight settled. Forty-odd people breathing in the gallery, most of them fast. The groundskeeper's rake scraping one last time and stopping. Somewhere up on the second tier, a stillness with an edge on it, and he felt it and set it aside.

And thirty paces off, Hiromi. A knot of heat and restlessness in the air, shifting weight from foot to foot, alive all over.

Thirty paces sat inside the range where the Sage's Draw was worth anything. It did not help. The read took a breath, sometimes two, and he did not yet know how fast that knot of heat could cross thirty paces. The Way of Growing Things did not start fights. It waited to be told what shape the fight was, and then it answered the shape.

He waited.

Hiromi's mind had gone narrow and bright, fixed on the target and on nothing else.

Aether came off him in waves. They left his body concentric and flat and blue, rolling out across the raked sand one behind another, and each wave broke at its edge into small hard shards of colour that caught the light and went out. They smothered the floor in a calm blue haze. The gallery went quieter under it.

He needed a strategy, and a real one. Xane felt everything. The whole ring was talking to him, the sand and the air and probably the wards, and anybody who ran at a man like that was running at the entire room, which was the whole problem. The environment was his inhibitor. Every step Hiromi took would be a step the floor told on.

So the answer was to take steps the floor could not hear.

Nothing happened for several minutes. The haze settled. Somebody in the gallery coughed and was shushed. Then strings fell out of the air above the wards, dozens of them, pale and taut and fine as fishing line, dropping straight down through the blue until their ends touched the sand. Where each one touched, a point of light came up. The points found each other. Lines ran between them, point to point, chaining outward in a mass across the floor, each link pulling the next into being, until the whole ring was mapped in small cold constellations, most of them on Xane's side.

Hiromi brought up his right hand and brushed his thumb down the palm in a slow circle.

"Infinite Summoning Arts, Imprint Summoning Arts."

What he was calling up was a thing he had seen once, at nine, from a station platform, and never stopped turning over since. The bullet train. How it could go from standing to that in so short a run. He had worked it out, lying awake: the bullet train pushed the air out of its own road with aether before it ever got there, shoved it ahead and aside in one long tapering wedge, so it never had to force its way through anything at all. That was why it was quiet. And the aether packed around it went so stiff that the whole body turned rigid, and whatever hit it soaked in and went nowhere, the way water goes into a sponge. The imprint wrapped that around him. It did not make him the train. It lent him the shape of one, and only for as long as the pattern held, which was not long. Twice his speed, for a moment.

It was not the first of his measured attacks. It was only the first one Xane would see coming.

Up at the rail, Mu-jin had both hands flat on the oiled wood.

He knew what his son had just done, all at once and down to the fault. An imprint was a likeness called up out of wherever patterns are kept, and it answered because it was like the thing it copied, and it came already knowing how to be what it was. That was its gift. The price was on the same page, and it was simple: a copy carried whatever the original carried. A bullet train carried its rails.

So the boy had borrowed something that could only go straight.

The rest he read off the body without meaning to. The shards on those waves were too many and too small. The channel was spilling, the way his son's always spilled, a third or more of what went in never coming out the far end. Dozens of strings hung open up there, and each one was a load the boy was carrying in his own flesh for as long as it stayed open. A boy who forgot to eat. A boy who had gone down on a practice floor three times this year and got back up embarrassed.

*It isn't taught below the eleventh Stage. Nobody taught him. He was seven.*

Frithia would have called it at once. Hiromi had taken the Vor. The other boy would be fighting in the Nach whether he liked it or not, and a masked boy with no Crystal who needed a breath to read a thing did not have a breath. Mu-jin shifted his weight toward the signal-bell on its bracket, ready to call the match on first contact.

Hiromi went.

There was very little to see. The sand under his back foot barely moved. He was simply further forward than he had been, and then further, the blue haze tearing open around him in a long clean wedge, his white hair flat back against his skull and his mouth open on a grin.

Xanelor felt him before anything else could.

The push came first. It arrived on his face and the front of his coat as a broad soft pressure out of the right of centre, warm, a hand laid flat on his chest, and the grains of sand in front of his toes shivered and rolled away from it in a fan. He knew that push. He had knelt in the shallows on Omoro and felt the swell come up the beach ahead of a boat's bow, lifting the moored skiffs one by one while the boat itself was still out past the point. The water went first. The hull came after, and it came on the line the water drew.

The line was straight. It was also far too fast for a bow.

No draw, then. He would not get the arrow off the string before the thing behind the push arrived. So he did what the Way said to do with a wave, and went to meet it.

Irimi and tenkan, the old books would call it. Drogo had only ever called it taking the wave at its shoulder, because a wave taken at the crest drowns you and a wave taken at the trough has nothing left in it to use. Xanelor stepped forward and out with his left foot, off the line by the width of a body, and pivoted on that foot as the heat arrived, and the half-turn put him alongside Hiromi at the exact instant Hiromi reached him. His left palm came down flat on the boy's right shoulder. It joined the line, went with it, and turned.

It should have been a throw. Hiromi's own speed should have carried him around Xanelor's hip and off his feet and down.

Xanelor's palm met something that did not give.

The envelope around the boy was rigid as a hull and it took the push of his hand and soaked it in and handed nothing back. There was no shoulder under his palm to steer, only a hard slick surface travelling very fast, and the turn he put into it bit a quarter of the way and stopped. The rest of the force had nowhere to go. It came back up his own arm. His wrist bent backward past where a wrist is meant to go and something along its thumb side pulled with a thin bright pain, and the green leather of the glove split open across the heel of his hand as the envelope dragged across it, taking the skin of the palm underneath raw.

Hiromi turned a quarter and kept going. Straight.

He could not do anything else. The imprint knew one road, and when Xanelor bent it, it simply picked a new straight line off the new angle and ran it. The new line led to the edge of the ring. He hit the ward at a slant with his right shoulder, and the ward came up out of nothing in a burst of hard blue-white fracture, a hundred flat bright facets flaring and spidering outward from the point of impact. The envelope took most of it. Not all. His shoulder met the ward, then his hip, then the sand, and he rolled twice and came up on one knee spitting grit, the blue haze collapsing around him in rags.

The pattern was gone. He felt it go out of him all at once.

At the rail, Mu-jin's hand had stopped a finger's width from the bell.

He took it back. His son was up, and laughing, one hand still on the sand. Out of an old habit from the infirmary he classed the blood from where he stood and found nothing worth classing, a skinned elbow, a split lip, less than a cupful between them, and a bruise that would come up purple over the point of the right shoulder by supper. The Xanelor boy was holding his left hand in close against his coat. Radial side. A sprain, if the scaphoid had been lucky, and scaphoids were seldom lucky.

Mu-jin went back over the exchange and could not find the read in it. The boy had not had time to read anything. He had guessed the line, or been lucky with it, or been told somehow what an imprint was and what it could not do. Mu-jin did not believe the last and could not rule out the first.

Whichever it was, his son was now empty of the imprint and could not call it back cheaply. Nothing Hiromi drew from here on could be a second call. Every one of them would cost what a first call costs, and he had already paid one.

"Huh." Hiromi wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist and stared across the sand. "How'd you... no. You couldn't have heard that."

Xanelor did not answer him.

He opened and closed his left hand once, slowly, to see what it would do. The fingers closed. The wrist did not want to hold them there, and a tremor ran down into the thumb and stayed. The bow hand. The hand that had to stand still at full draw while everything else in him moved. Fifteen paces separated them now, the best range he could have asked for, and the hand that needed to be steady at fifteen paces was shaking.

He looked past Hiromi at the floor.

The strings were still hanging there, pale and taut, dozens of them dropping out of the air above the wards. The constellations on the sand had not gone out when the boy fell. They had brightened. The lines between the points were drawing tighter, point to point, as though something were being pulled closed.

And he could not feel them.

His eyes told him they were there. The nexus told him nothing. The ward-hum was there, the sand, the gallery's breathing, the rake, the blade on the second tier, Hiromi's heat. Where the strings hung, there was a hole. They moved no air. They put no weight on the sand. Every one of them came down through the ring and left the ring exactly as undisturbed as an empty floor.

Across from him, Hiromi got his feet under him, still grinning, and raised his right hand, and his thumb began the slow circle on his palm again.

Xanelor's right hand went back to his hip and closed on the stonechat fletching of an arrow.

# Xanelor — The Constellations

It was true that Xane could not feel the constellations. He had never been taught to sense aether in any capacity, and the lines drawing tight across the sand were silent to him, lines on a page. What he could feel was what they did to everything else. Drogo had explained it to him once, on the shelf above the tide pools, with a crab turning over in one enormous hand: aether was only a person's essence made solid enough to push on the world, feeling and the deepest root of a person poured out and made to run like water. You could not see the current. You could watch the weed lean.

So he watched the weed lean.

Hiromi was far from done. He had both hands up now, fingers spread and moving, small precise motions, playing an instrument nobody else could see, and every motion answered somewhere on the floor. His feet had not moved since he stood. They could not. A net this size had to be held from the middle, and holding it took everything he had to give, so he stood in the eye of it and conducted and did not try to also fight. That was the one rule of this that nobody had needed to teach him. The net gets harder the more of it there is. The one holding it gets slower.

A thin line of blood had started from his left nostril, the small vessels at the front of the septum giving way under the load. He did not notice it.

The points on the sand began to rise.

They came up as hands. Long pale spectral arms broke out of the floor at the constellation points, too many joints to each of them, wrists turning, fingers splayed, and they did not reach so much as travel. They came along the bright lines between the points like snakes, low and fast and sidelong, each one slithering down its own straight line of light toward the same place. Toward Xane.

He felt them the instant they broke the surface. Sand shifted and fanned and hissed away on either side of each one as it came, and the sand told him everything the lines would not.

One came for his footing, low along the ground from his left, fingers open to close on his ankle.

He did not step away from it. He pushed himself upward off his right foot, drew the left knee high, and brought the heel of his boot down hard on the back of the spectral hand as it arrived. It had weight. It had enough solidity to hold a boot for the instant a boot needed holding. He drove down through it and it took his full weight and held, one step's worth of stair, and he used it, propelling himself off it straight up into the air above the ring.

The gallery made a noise.

The bow came off his back as he rose. His right hand went to the hip quiver in the same movement, snapped an arrow up out of it with a flick of the wrist, and flipped it end over end once in the air in front of his face. He caught it crossways between his teeth. Stonechat fletching against his cheek, iron head out past his jaw on the other side, the taste of cedar and old wax.

He adjusted in the air. A twist through the hips turned him over so he hung face down above the floor for a long suspended moment, coat flaring, and from up there the whole net lay open under him. The lines ran straight. They had to, to join point to point. The hands ran along the lines. Three of them had lined up on one bright track, one behind the next, making for the place he had just been.

His hands went to the string. He took the arrow from his mouth with the right, nocked it without looking, and drew.

It was a short draw. There was no ground to push from and no back to put into it, only arms and the core of him, and he drove his breath down hard into his belly and pulled from there, from the centre, the way Drogo had made him pull ten thousand times with a stone in his lap. His left wrist screamed at the load, the strained radial ligament hauled tight across the scaphoid by the full pull of the bow. The tremor ran down into the bow. The arrowhead wandered a finger's width off the line and back.

At that range, down that line, a finger's width was nothing. He released.

The arrow ran the seam of the line. It took the first hand through the palm and the second through the forearm and the third through the wrist where it joined the line, and all three burst apart into loose points of light that scattered across the sand and went dim. He was already turning, the release rolling him back through the air into a backflip, sky, floor, sky, and he came down on his feet in the sand in a low crouch with the bow in his left hand.

More of them came. Four, five, from three directions.

His right hand went to his right hip and came back with a short dagger, reversed in the grip so the blade ran down along the outside of his forearm, a plain working knife with a horn handle worn pale. He moved through them. He did not fight them. He let the first one come close enough to touch and stepped around it, and the blade went through its wrist where the arm left its point on the sand, a short hard stroke that ended exactly where it meant to end. The hand went slack. It went slack in a way that seemed wrong for a single cut, loose all the way down its length, drifting, and the two nearest it slowed, the weight gone out of them too.

He cut the next one the same way, at the root. Then the next.

Up at the rail, Mu-jin had stopped breathing on the second cut.

The boy was cutting at the anchor points. Every stroke landed where an arm left its point on the floor, and every arm he cut at the root took its neighbours' strength down with it, because the net lived on how its pieces sat against each other, and pieces with a gap cut between them went back to being ordinary. That was how a net like this was beaten. You broke the shape of it. You did not kill the parts. It was the cheapest answer there was, and it was written down in a book Mu-jin himself had copied out at fifteen, and he could not tell from the rail whether the masked boy had ever read a book in his life.

It was mastery of combat, whatever it was. It was true that the boy lacked magic, and he was making up for it with his body alone, and the gallery had gone completely silent to watch him do it.

Xanelor sheathed the dagger.

He did it without looking, blade back into its sheath on his right side, and then he tossed the bow straight up into the air. Not a flip. He threw it. The blackwood turned once over his head and kept climbing, and climbing, past the height where the wards bent the light, up toward the dim arch of the dome, until it was a thin dark stroke against the lamps that only a sharp pair of eyes could still follow.

Naori's could. So could Dabney's, and so could Mu-jin's. None of them had the least idea why.

Hiromi did not look up. He was looking at Xane.

And for a moment something came up around the masked boy that had not been there before.

It began in the sand. Grains near his boots stirred and lifted a hair's breadth off the floor and hung. Then the air in the whole ring pressed outward from him, all of it at once, a slow enormous push with no wind in it, the way the tide comes in over a flat without ever seeming to move. The benches of the first tier creaked. People in the front row leaned back without knowing they were doing it. Somebody's water flask rolled off the rail and hit the sand. Around the masked boy's shoulders the light itself went strange, breaking into hard jagged planes that caught and cut against each other like panes of ice jostling on a river, and inside them his body went tight all over for the first time since the fight began. The cords of his neck stood out. The veins at both temples rose blue and thick under the brown skin. The three old scars on his left cheek darkened toward black under the fall of his hair.

Nobody had taught him this either. He did not know what it was. He only knew it had been in him the whole time, under everything, and that it was coming up now whether he had asked it to or not, and that it hurt, the deep burning ache of blood coming back into a dead limb.

Mu-jin felt it arrive in his own chest and knew it for what it was before his mind caught up. That was essence. Not aether worked through a channel into a shape, not a technique, nothing the boy was doing on purpose with his hands. That was stored essence coming out raw through a body that had no channel at all to send it down, twelve years of it by the look of the boy, all of it rising at once through every inch of him because it had nowhere narrower to go. A reserve with no road out comes up the way it was stored. This one had been stored on an island by the sea, and it came up the way the sea comes up.

The intake board had written *no detectable pressure* on that boy's sheet, and underlined it.

*Twelve years in the dark. Nobody ever asked it to come out.*

On the benches, Akira had gone rigid against her brother's arm. She could not see the boy on the floor and had never needed to. Her fingers dug into Naori's sleeve hard enough to hurt, and her face turned toward the ring and stayed there.

Naori opened the sight without deciding to. The masked boy's thread, the one that had ended at his own feet, ran forward now. One line, straight across the floor to Hiromi, committed end to end and brighter than anything else in the ring.

Two tiers up, Dabney's hands had come unfolded. He put them back on his knee, deliberately, one over the other. He had spent seven years learning to keep exactly this inside his own skin. The boy on the floor had never been taught to keep it anywhere, because nobody had known it was there to keep. Interesting was no longer the word, and he did not have a better one.

Then the wards spoke.

It started at the far edge of the ring, low down, at the place where Hiromi's shoulder had hit on the first exchange. A crack. A single bright line ran out from that point across the curve of the ward, and then it split, and split again, spiderwebbing outward in fine brilliant branches as the pressure leaned on the one place in the whole shell that had already been hurt.

Hiromi's mouth fell open. His hands stopped moving for the first time since the net went up, and across the floor every one of the constellations shivered.

*He doesn't have magic. He said so. He told the whole class.*

He could drop the net and move, and lose every point he had paid for. Or he could hold it, and stay exactly where he stood.

In that instant, Xanelor shoved off his rear foot.

The sand went up behind him in a sheet. He crossed the ring low and flat and so fast that the pressure around him went ahead of him, a bow wave in front of a hull, and hit everything it passed, and the wards on either side of his line gave way under it. The cracks ran. They raced out from the old flaw in every direction at once, through the facets and across the dome, and pieces of the ward began to come away in bright flat shards that tumbled down through the air and went out before they reached the sand. Somebody in the gallery screamed, briefly. Mu-jin's hand closed on the rope of the signal-bell.

He did not pull it. Not yet.

Xanelor came in on Hiromi's left side with his weight low and his right palm already rising, open, fingers together, lifting toward the boy's face in plain sight. It was a clean line. It was a slow one, by the standard of everything he had just done, slow enough for anybody watching to see it coming.

He wanted it seen. He was expecting Hiromi to answer it.

The palm came up into Hiromi's eyeline, and Hiromi's eyes went to it.

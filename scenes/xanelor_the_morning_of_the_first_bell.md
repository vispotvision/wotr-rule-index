# Xanelor — The Morning of the First Bell

Xanelor had been in the room for an hour before anyone else arrived.

He sat at the back row, far left, where the curved wall of the circular chamber met the window ledge at an angle that let him see the door and the eastern slope of the Academy grounds beyond the glass. The service corridor branched off six paces to his left. He had mapped it his first day. He had mapped the whole building his first week, every stair that moved and every hall that remembered insults, because Drogo had taught him that you learned the ground before you learned the names of the people standing on it.

The chamber was cold this early. The conduit network hadn't brought the heating lines to pressure, and the stone held the night's chill in its grain like a creek bed holding the last of the snowmelt. It smelled of chalk dust and lamp oil and the faint copper residue of old workings baked into the walls. Xanelor did not mind the cold. Omoro had been warm. He had learned to stop expecting it.

He sat with his hands flat on the desk, fingers spread, still as the wood beneath them.

He was not a small boy, but he was lean in a way that suggested endurance rather than deprivation, the kind of lean that came from hauling driftwood up a volcanic slope and running stake-fields at dawn for years until the body decided it would carry only what it used. Brown skin, darkened further by salt wind and equatorial sun, stretched taut over forearms that were corded and scarred at the knuckles from bowstring and bone-awl work. His hair was black with threads of umber-brown running through it, long past his shoulders, falling in a rough tangle that he did not tie back because tying it back exposed the left side of his face, and he had not decided yet how much of the left side of his face this place deserved to see.

Three parallel scars. Temple to jaw, tracing the cheekbone's ridge like furrows cut by something with claws or intent. They had been pink once, when he was two and screaming in wreckage on a northern shore and a turtle had lifted him out of the surf. They were purple now, dark as ripe plums against the brown, vivid, raised, the kind of marks that told a story he did not know and had never been told. The hair covered the worst of them when he let it fall. He let it fall now.

His right eye was dark, deep-set, steady. The left, bisected by the longest scar, was the same colour but held a different quality, something sharper, as though the tissue that had healed around it had taught it to look harder at whatever had put it there.

The mask sat pushed back on his head like a visor, resting against the crown of his hair. Carved from shell, Drogo's shell, pieces given over four years from the plate near the old turtle's left shoulder where the crack-lines ran deepest. The brow-line was shaped to suggest a mountain ridge. The cheek-planes were smooth as river stone. The eye-holes were cut in the shape of leaves, or tears, depending on the light. Tide-line markings flowed across the forehead and down the jaw in patterns that looked like wind-scoured rock or the growth rings of something ancient. Where the mouth should have been there was nothing. Smooth, closed shell. No opening.

*A good thing does not need to announce itself.*

His longcoat was heavy grey canvas, double-breasted, brass-buttoned, fitted close at the waist and long enough to break the wind at the knee. A green cross was stitched over the heart. The stitching was uneven in places, done by hands that had shaken badly, and Xanelor had never repaired it because the unevenness was the point. On his left forearm, a white bracer stamped with a compass star. On his hands, green leather gloves, dyed with the same lichen that grew on a shell he would never touch again. The recurve bow rode his back, its limb-tips capped in worked bronze, and the broadhead arrows in the hip quiver were fletched with stonechat feathers, grey-brown barbs from a bird whose grandmother had nested in an arrow shaft on a mountaintop at the edge of the world.

Around his neck, under the coat, where no one could see it, a copper hammer pendant no larger than a thumbnail.

He looked like no one else in the room. He looked like no one else in the Academy. The other students wore Academy blacks or house colours. Xanelor wore the clothes a dying turtle had sewn for the boy he'd raised, and he would wear them until they fell apart, and then he would repair them, and he would not explain why to anyone who asked.

No one had asked.

Students filtered in. Xanelor watched them the way Drogo had taught him to watch: terrain first, then movement, then people. The girl who sat upside down against the far wall, silver hair toward the ceiling, reading. The broad boy with dark scales and the luminous creature on his shoulder, arguing with it in undertones. The girl with floating rings assembling a mechanical puzzle without touching it, seven cycles, pause, seven cycles, always seven. A pale boy in solid black near the opposite wall who cast no shadow at all, whose outline sat against the lamplight like a hole in the room's logic. Xanelor noted him, filed him, moved on.

The door opened wider.

Three bodies came through at once, and the room's noise rearranged itself around them.

Hiromi Mahuo first, because Hiromi Mahuo always came first. White hair catching the overhead light like forge-ash before it cools. Red eyes sweeping the chamber with a proprietary scan. The grin already in place, settled into his face like it had been born there. His coat was open at the collar. His hands were in his pockets.

Behind him, Geturo. Brown-haired, broader, quieter. Eyes that moved to the exits. Not the way Xanelor moved to exits. Geturo was checking for people. Xanelor checked for ground.

And Lily, on the left, starched and precise, her year-rank pin catching the light.

Xanelor watched Hiromi drop into a seat three rows ahead, lean back, and put his boots on the desk.

*The Paragon of Dominion.*

Kwon Mu-jin stood at the front of the chamber, chalk in hand, writing on the broad slate board in careful script. Tall, lean, dark-haired, with the Mahuo bone structure and his right eye covered by a cloth binding tied at the back. Not a patch. A deliberate concealment.

Xanelor had noticed the binding his first lesson. He had wanted to ask about it the way he wanted to ask about the arrow in Drogo's shell, and he had not asked for the same reason. Some wounds were not questions. They were doors, and you did not open a door unless you were prepared for what stood behind it.

"Teacher." Hiromi, easy and familiar.

"Teacher." Geturo. The same word carrying different weight.

"Teacher." Lily. Clipped. A formality delivered as one.

Mu-jin's nervous laugh carried across the room. He rubbed the back of his neck with the chalk hand, leaving a pale streak.

"I heard you missed your first class." He looked at Geturo. "What did I tell you? You have to be more consistent, or your mother's gonna strangle me."

Geturo grumbled. "It isn't my fault *someone* stayed up with me most of the night refining my alchemy, *Teacher*." The last word was loaded. "What if I told her about that?"

Mu-jin's hands went up, palms out. "Now, now. Let's not be so brash." The nervous laugh again.

*He is afraid of the woman at home.*

He pressed his fingertips into the desk. Counted the grain lines. Seven under the index finger. Nine under the next.

*I'll get stronger. I promise, Grandpa.*

The temperature changed. Not the conduit lines. Not a draught. Something structural, as though the air itself had remembered that cold was an option. Xanelor felt it along the backs of his hands, against the scar tissue on his left cheek where sensation ran thinner but the nerve endings that remained were better calibrated.

Pressure. Not Hiromi's broad, unconscious projection. A drawing-in. Conversations lost their upper register. Laughter became shallow. Students shifted without knowing why.

A girl walked in. Xanelor watched her cross to a seat with her books held against her chest, her frame closed in on itself in a way that contradicted the weight she carried.

He let his gaze drift to the high windows. Beyond the practice fields, the central tower rose into the morning cloud cover, and somewhere inside it the Infinite Archive waited.

The door opened again. Two more bodies. A boy with violet eyes and a posture that read as laziness until you watched his hands, and a taller boy beside him who moved with the confidence of someone raised inside these walls.

The violet-eyed boy's gaze reached the back row. It found Xanelor.

Something happened. Not contact. Displacement. The air between them shifted the way air shifted in the stake-field when a coconut shell swung toward your ribs. A reading. An attempt. Something reaching toward him with a faculty that was not sight.

He looked at the boy. Held the gaze. Not a challenge. Not a greeting. The way a stone holds the river's gaze: by being there, and by not moving.

Then he looked away. Back to the desk. Back to the grain lines. Back to the copper hammer pendant resting against his sternum, warm from his body.

He had looked back at Omoro. Drogo had told him not to. He had looked back anyway.

Xanelor sat at the back, in the seat by the service corridor, and watched all of it. The mask rested on his crown. The scars were hidden by the fall of his hair. The green cross over his heart was stitched by trembling hands, and the stitching was uneven, and the unevenness was the whole of what he had left.

# AMENDMENT ONE
## Target: `WOTR_Combat_Craft_Guide.md` — replaces the open gap at the end of the document

### The per-character pass, and why it cannot be a guard assignment

The Combat Craft Guide's open-gaps section directs a per-character pass in the priority order Rengai, Rashani, Sodoku, Cozbi, assigning each a guard preference, a preferred master-cut, and a documented answer to plate.

**Running the pass reveals that three of the four do not fence, and that one of them explicitly cannot.**

Sodoku's Shinken-ryū is documented in canon as a verdict system with a blade attached, and the canon states in terms that it is *not* a fencing style: no blade conversation, no sentiment du fer, no probing exchanges, no parrying forms, no counter-cuts. He has three named stances of his own. Assigning him Pflug and a preferred Schielhau would contradict established canon rather than extend it.

Rashani reads first causes through the Origin Eye and answers with Zero-Point stepping and deflection. His engagement grammar is analytical, not tactile. He does not live in the bind because he does not need the bind to know what is coming.

Rengai works a hammer, and Dougou Zettai's Iron Tree is body-percussion. Neither maps onto longsword vocabulary and the guide already flagged this.

**Ruling: WOTR uses three combat vocabularies, not one. Assign the vocabulary before assigning the technique.**

### The three vocabularies

**I. Blade Grammar (Liechtenauer/Fiore, as the guide already documents).**
Applies to characters trained in a conventional sword tradition. Guards, master-cuts, the bind, Winden, Indes, Absetzen, Durchwechseln. Full existing framework applies without change.
*Assign to:* conventional duellists, Concord Aether Knights in formal training, Old World nobility, guild fencing traditions, and any character whose fighting mind is built around openings.

**II. Verdict Grammar.**
Applies to characters whose combat is a reading followed by a conclusion. There is no conversation because a conclusion does not converse. The prose skeleton is *read, rank, commit*, and the tension lives in the read rather than in the exchange.
*Assign to:* Sodoku (canonical), Rashani (canonical in behaviour if not in name), and any Kamigan-class or perception-lineage fighter.
*Prose cue:* the beat before the strike is longer than the strike. Fighters in this grammar are terrifying in the pause and anticlimactic in the motion, and that is correct.

**III. Percussion Grammar.**
Built below, per the guide's own instruction that it be built rather than borrowed.
*Assign to:* Rengai, Dougou, hammer and haft fighters, and any body-percussion tradition.

### The Percussion table

Blade grammar hunts **openings**. Percussion grammar hunts **fatigue**. That is the whole distinction and it comes straight out of Dougou's own canonical doctrine: *do not break the wall, make the wall confess where it was already tired.*

A swordsman asks where you are unguarded. A hammer asks where you are already carrying load. This changes the entire structure of an exchange, because fatigue accumulates and openings do not, which means percussion fights get more dangerous the longer they run while blade fights get less decisive.

| Strike | Description | What it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| **The Settling** | Descending, full weight, delivered into the opponent's base rather than at their body | **Stance.** Answers anyone rooted, braced, or planted. The correct answer to a man who has decided not to move. |
| **The Sweep** | Horizontal at hip or knee height, using the weapon's full arc and mass | **The base.** Answers wide, stable, low postures that a blade cannot profitably attack. |
| **The Shove** | Haft, shoulder, boss or pommel, delivered at closed distance | **Measure.** Answers anyone who has closed inside the weapon's arc, which is the percussion fighter's only real vulnerability. |
| **The Return** | The rebound taken deliberately and redirected into a second strike from the recoil | **Recovery.** Answers anyone counting on the pause after a heavy blow. The signature strike of the grammar and the one that separates a hammer-fighter from a man with a hammer. |

**Percussion answers to plate:** none of these seek to penetrate. Plate is not defeated by percussion, it is *used*. The armour transmits force inward, and the correct target is the joint, the base, the balance and the man's ability to keep standing in fifty pounds of steel. A percussion fighter's answer to plate is to make the plate expensive to wear.

**Percussion tell:** the fighter tracks the opponent's breathing and footing rather than their weapon. In prose, have them look at the wrong thing.

### Counterplay at Scale

The Mechanism Standard's seven fields (Trigger, Function, Mechanism, Numerical Effect, Target Response, Cost, Counterplay) describe what a technique does to a person.

**Three further fields are mandatory for any technique deployed against a formation. A technique that cannot be given all three does not belong on a battlefield and should not be written into one.**

- **Frontage.** How much line the technique affects, in men or in paces. A frontage of twelve men is a personal weapon used in a battle. A frontage of three hundred is an instrument of war. The distinction is width, not power, and most spectacular techniques are narrow.

- **Sustain.** How long the effect can be held before Essence cost forces cessation, *and what the practitioner cannot do while holding it.* The second clause is the load-bearing one, because it tells you what the enemy is free to do during the window and therefore where the scene's real tension sits.

- **Re-form.** How long an affected formation needs to recover cohesion, and whether it recovers at all. **This is the field that decides engagements and the one authors most often fail to decide before writing.** A technique that breaks a line for ten minutes has bought a local advantage. One that breaks a line permanently has ended the war on that flank.

**The counterplay to any scaled technique is therefore always one of three things:**
1. **Exceed its Frontage** so the war continues where it is not.
2. **Outlast its Sustain** so the practitioner pays and gains nothing.
3. **Shorten its Re-form**, which is what drill, veterancy, redundant command and a counter-Domain seated toward your own men are all purchased to do.

This closes the standing gap by which every high-Stage character was unbeatable by default.

# AMENDMENT THREE
## Target: `WOTR_Scene_Writing_Process_Guide.md` — Scene Standards carve-out

### Battle scenes

The standing Scene Standards (minimum 2,500 words, layered sensory opening, one private italic thought per named NPC, no section spacers, end on physical action) apply in full to all scenes **except** mass combat sequences, where two provisions are amended.

**Amended: the italic thought rule.**
The one-private-italic-thought-per-named-NPC standard is suspended in mass combat. It exists to make a small cast feel populated and it does the opposite at scale, producing a scene that reads as a poll of forty consciousnesses.

**Replacement standard: name three.** No more than three characters carry interiority through a battle sequence. Everyone else is exterior only: actions, voices, bodies. Interiority for named characters outside the three is **deferred to the aftermath**, where it lands harder because the reader has been waiting for it.

**Amended: the word floor.**
The 2,500-word minimum applies to the *engagement plus its aftermath treated as one unit*, not to the fighting alone. A battle sequence that ends when the fighting ends has not met the standard regardless of its length.

### Aftermath as the primary dramatic unit

New structural provision. Grimdark war narrative lives in the week after, not the hour of.

**Rule: the interior action of a battle occurs in its aftermath.** Whatever the engagement was going to do to your characters' relationships, it does in the three days afterward, over the burial detail, in the sick lines, and in the arguments about who should have done what. The fight itself is too loud and too fast for anyone to change during it.

**Structural consequence:** when planning a battle scene, plan the aftermath first and the engagement second. The engagement exists to produce the conditions of the aftermath. If you cannot state what the aftermath scene is for before you write the battle, the battle has no reason to be in the book.

**The five-stage aftermath sequence** (per the Mass Combat Craft Guide section 7): the wounded left where they fell, the stripping, the late and cursory burial, the disease, and the district that does not recover in the season. Any aftermath scene should touch at least two.

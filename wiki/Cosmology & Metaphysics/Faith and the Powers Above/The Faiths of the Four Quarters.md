# The Faiths of the Four Quarters

*What people in the four quarters actually do, on an ordinary day, about the fact that the world has owners.*
> *Ask a man his religion and he will name an institution. Watch him for a week and you will find out what he believes.*

The Bridge of Four Stakes caught fire on the second morning of the wool fair in Year 668, from a cookshop brazier and a bale of badly scoured fleece, with a thousand people on it and every stall hung with cloth: madder red, weld yellow, the good Altherion blue, all of it going up the timbers in long bright flags. The Guild's fire-rolls survive, and I have spent more evenings with them than is healthy, because they record in a clerk's dull hand what each person went back for. A Moto widow set down her basket in the middle of the crush, squared her shoulders and bowed fourteen times to the hinge of the morning with sparks landing in her hair, and then she picked up a neighbor's child under each arm and walked them off the bridge at no great speed. A lector of the Scale climbed a mooring post and began attesting ownership of the wool, bale by bale, at the top of his lungs, and two merchants stopped to pay him. A Marrowkin hold-clerk waded out under the east span with his Tally held above his head, entering the fire in scanned meter as it happened, including the part where it reached his beard. A Kharven carter with no stake in the place got a bucket line going, stood it in order of who looked strongest, and was heard to ask the man beside him, as the center span came down, how his stack was. And an old woman nobody could afterward name walked back against the whole running crowd onto the burning approach, to the post where a seep came up under the pilings, took down a string of tin hands that had hung there longer than the bridge, and carried them away in her apron.

Twenty-seven died, the bridge was rebuilt by autumn, and every one of those people would have given you a different account of themselves if you had stopped them the week before and asked what they believed. I trust the fire-rolls over any of those accounts. What follows is the five large faiths and the three kinds of cult as they are done, and then what each of them has got wrong.

## Part One: How Religion Is Actually Practiced

The error to avoid is treating a faith as a set of propositions people assent to. **Religion in the four quarters is a set of things people do**, mostly at home, mostly daily, mostly without reflection, and the doctrine is downstream of the practice rather than upstream of it.

Three findings from the Guild's visitation rolls, which have been inside more kitchens than any lector, and all three are already half-built into the faiths below.

**The house is the unit.** Domestic devotion carries most of the actual religious life of the quarters, and the building comes a long way behind. Almost every home holds some object with ritual weight: a small altar, a shelf, a figure, an amulet, a marked doorpost. These range from finely made to cheap, pressed by the tray from a village mold, and **the same practices span every social stratum**. The Sohai already runs on exactly this and says so: no congregation, no clergy in the ordinary sense, no building, and a kingdom's piety measurable by walking into its kitchens.

**The vow is the transaction.** The lynchpin of ordinary religious practice is the vow between a person and a power: help me, and I will bring you a thing. The thing brought afterward is the votive, and votives are overwhelmingly ordinary objects, including models of the body part that was healed. Over time the petition itself has moved into the home, with the shrine visit deferred until after the help arrives. **In a world with Attraction Force in it (the law of recognition and binding), a vow is structurally load-bearing, the whole transaction runs far sharper than any lector's book admits, and nobody wrote that down before this page.**

**Petition is the natural default and every organized faith fights it.** People ask. They ask constantly, for specific things, in bad grammar, at objects. The Sohai bars petition outright and calls it a heresy. Sancta Lux permits it and regulates the form. The Current is made of nothing else. **Where a faith sits on petition tells you almost everything about it.**

### The template

Every faith below is written to the same six questions, and the last one matters most.
1. What it claims happened.
1. What it asks of an ordinary person on an ordinary day.
1. Its clergy, if any, and how they eat.
1. Its heresies.
1. What it costs to belong.
1. **What it is wrong about.** A religion nobody in the setting can be wrong about is decoration.

## Part Two: The Majors

### Sohai, the Whole Bow

**Moto. The oldest, the most demanding, and the smallest by headcount.** Built in full elsewhere; here is where it sits against the others.

**Claim.** The Fourteen are crowned and the Works are older, and both are true, and the order matters. No Archon may be addressed alone because none operates alone.

**Daily.** The Bow at the hinge, fourteen inclinations in emergence order, containing no request. Then the Labor: one concrete act in the character of one Archon's principle, performed by hands. The Weighing, the Discard, the Squaring, the Telling, and eleven more. Most households perform eleven of the fourteen without knowing they are performing anything.

**Clergy.** None. The household is the unit. This is why the Moto have never built a temple and have never understood why anybody else does, and it is also why the Sohai has no institutional weight to bring against Sancta Lux in any forum where institutions are what counts.

**Heresies.** Singling, the elevation of one Archon. Petition, which converts the Bow into a transaction. The Empty Bow, observance without labor, held to be worse than neglect on the grounds that a man who has stopped bowing has at least stopped lying.

**Cost.** Everything, quietly, daily, forever, with no ceremony and no audience. The Sohai asks more of an ordinary washing day than any other faith in the four quarters and offers nothing in return that can be pointed at.

**Wrong about.** Its own immunity to Singling. The Kōkan favor Totality, Totality is the Work most easily mistaken for a crown, and the house watches for this in itself precisely because it has already happened once and the record of when is not in Kharven.

**On the calendar.** Auren's day in every month for the dead. Munahi is the one day the Bow is not performed, because there is no station to perform it at, and the house defends this vigorously against the obvious objection.

### Sancta Lux, the Scale

**The institutional faith of the Concord and the Accord's working partner.** Judicial in temperament, literate, propertied, and the only major with buildings, courts and a ladder of preferment.

**Claim.** That the cosmos is weighable, that a thing's worth can be established by procedure, and that establishing it is a sacred act rather than an administrative one. Where the Sohai says a thing read correctly has been given its Work, the Scale says a thing weighed correctly has been given its due, and the difference between reading and weighing is the whole quarrel between them.

**Daily.** Attendance where there is a house to attend. Petition, permitted and formally shaped, which is the single largest reason the Scale has adherents and the Sohai does not. The Accord's proof-marks, seals and attestations are all Scale liturgy that the Accord has secularized and now charges for, and neither institution finds this awkward.

**Clergy.** Ordained, trained, examined, kept for life and pensioned off with a corrody (bread, ale and a bed from a house of the order, for the rest of their days). They eat by benefice, by fee for attestation, and by the Accord's retainer. **A Scale cleric is a professional and is treated as one**, which is a source of both its reach and its contempt.

**Heresies.** Weighing without a scale, meaning private judgment. The False Standard, an unauthorized weight. And the one the house does not discuss, which is the suggestion that a scale calibrated by the people being weighed is not a scale.

**Cost.** Money, mostly, and it is not hidden. Attestations, dispensations, the fee for a reading. The Scale is honest that piety is expensive and holds that a thing costing nothing is worth its price.

**Wrong about.** The claim to neutrality. Every weight it uses was standardized in Sealhall by people with interests, and the Sohai's charge against it lands hard on precisely this point and has never been answered in a form anybody found satisfying.

### The Current

**Everybody's and nobody's. The largest faith in the four quarters by headcount, and it has no name for itself.**

This is the practice of people who live near Wellspring sites (the places where one of the sixty kinds of Aether pools; a Core, once somebody has worked it) and have done for longer than any institution has existed. It has no founder, no text, no clergy, no doctrine and no organization, which is why the Accord has never suppressed it: **there is nothing to arrest.** "The Current" is the Guild clerks' name for it, taken from the people themselves, who will tell you a current runs under a seep (a place where Aether comes up close to the surface). A Measurewright will explain at some length that what pools there is a kind of Aether and that nothing runs anywhere, and they will thank him and go on changing the water.

**Claim.** That the currents were here first, that they were never anybody's, and that what the Guild's Core keepers sell by the hour is a thing that was given freely to everyone who lived near it. The people who hold this do not mean it politically. They hold it as an obvious fact about the world, the way people hold facts about weather.

**Daily.** The shelf by the door with water on it, changed every morning, poured out at the threshold at night. The vow, constantly, for specific things, in the ordinary way people ask. A ribbon tied at a seep. A coin dropped in a wellhead, and every wellhead in the four quarters has coins in it, and everybody knows this, and nobody takes them.

The votives are the tell. **Small objects left where the Aether comes close to the surface**, made cheap and made everywhere: pressed clay, cut tin, a lock of hair, and overwhelmingly, models of the part of the body that was hurt. A shelf of tin hands at a seep outside a mining district is not a shrine anybody built. It accumulated.

**Clergy.** None, but there are always **women who know the seeps**, unpaid, unappointed, usually old, who are asked and who answer, and who are not called anything in particular. A district has two or three. Everybody knows which. When one dies the knowledge sometimes goes with her, and this has happened often enough that whole seeps have been forgotten.

**Heresies.** The concept does not apply, which is precisely what infuriates the organized faiths. There is no orthodoxy to depart from. Two villages nine miles apart will practice differently, know they do, and not consider the question interesting.

**Cost.** Nothing, and that matters more than anything else on this list. **The Current is free, in a world where the Scale charges, the Core keeper sells his hours and the lamp-warden wants his round-fee**, and that single fact does more for its persistence than any belief in it.

**Wrong about.** Efficacy, constantly, in ways that get people killed. A village that has been leaving tin at a seep for six generations is a village downwind of a concentration, and the strangeness in its families is a Wellspring's doing rather than a blessing, and the practice that grew up around it is a practice of standing too close to something for a very long time. On Walking Into the Current documents what that does to a body. Nobody at the seep has read it.

**Why the Accord tolerates it.** It cannot find it. There is no building to close, no roll to seize, no purse to seize it from, and a Guild searcher who breaks up a shrine has broken up a shelf and will find another shelf tomorrow. The Accord's actual position, unwritten, is that a faith with no institution is a faith with no politics, and this is the most consequential mistake anyone in the four quarters is currently making.

### The Tally

**Marrowkin. Never previously named as a religion, including by the Marrowkin, who would deny it is one.**

**Claim.** That an act either happened or did not, that the record is the world's memory of it, and that the record is sacred because nothing else is durable. The Marrowkin do not say this. They keep the Tally, and by their own reckoning they have kept it for six thousand years, a figure the Guild's Errata would cut down hard and which no clerk has yet dared carry to a hold, and the entering is itself an act that gets entered.

**Daily.** The entry. Every completed lawful act, in fixed wording, scanned to Mathematical Cadence, because a statement that does not scan has not been entered whatever it says. A hold-clerk is a metrician before he is anything else and does not consider this liturgical.

**Clergy.** The clerks, and they eat by the hold. **A Marrowkin would say these are administrators.** Every other people in the four quarters, on being shown a caste of hereditary record-keepers using a fixed liturgical meter to enter acts into a book that has never been closed, would say something else.

**Heresies.** The unentered act, *kurnur*. The broken oath, which counts as a change of grade rather than a sin, audible in the name, permanent, and published on a wall. And the deepest one, which is the Deepkin's Mirror-Sign: the only Marrowkin language in which a broken oath does not announce itself, and the only Marrowkin practice the other lineages regard as genuinely impious.

**Cost.** The future tense. A Marrowkin cannot express an intention without swearing to it or admitting nothing has begun, and there is no third option, and this is a religious constraint enforced by grammar rather than by clergy. It costs them enormously in every negotiation they have ever entered.

**Wrong about.** That the record is the world's memory. The Keth-Gorrum's archive is a room full of timber and can be destroyed with an axe, and this has happened, and there is a hole in the law where the year 411 used to be. A faith built on permanence has already lost a century and does not have a doctrine for it.

### The Sky

**Kharven. Regional, total within its region, and woven through every Kharven custom without ever having been called a religion.**

**Claim.** *The sky does not ask whether you are ready.* The sky is a condition, and the Kharven will look at you oddly if you call it a deity. It covers everyone, is indifferent, and outlasts every arrangement made under it, and the correct response to this is competence, with no worship required.

**Daily.** The woodpile. *How's your stack* is a greeting, a diagnostic and an examination of conscience at once, and it is Thalen's labor worn down past recognition, and no northern clansman alive knows that. The night-stone. Meat-sharing. The first bowl to the one who cannot fill it themselves.

**The dead.** The death-house, the Waiting, sky burial. *Gone to the sky.* A whole national institution devoted to sitting with the dead through a winter, performed by a people who believe they are being practical. Kharven-Seat is the one exception, and it is the city's own rite rather than the faith's: the Seat buries its dead in the ground, under cut stones in the upper burying ground, while everywhere else the faith still gives its dead to the sky.

**Clergy.** None. Oaths are sworn by the sky and by the fire, and there is nobody to swear them in front of but everyone present, which is the point.

**Cost.** It asks a household to be materially prepared at all times and treats unpreparedness as a moral failure rather than a misfortune. A man whose stack is low in the Thin Weeks has been judged before anyone has spoken.

**Wrong about.** That competence is sufficient. It is a theology built by people for whom it usually was, in a place where it usually is, and the Thin Weeks it does not cover are the ones the proverbs go quiet about.

## Part Three: Cults

Leave a cult's doctrine for last. **The interesting things are how it recruits and how it eats**, and the searchers' files on this are unambiguous and unromantic.

**Recruitment runs along existing social relationships.** The essential mechanism is interpersonal bonds. **Doctrine comes second**: people become close to members and take on the beliefs afterward, as part of the cost and the pleasure of belonging. The gullible and the unstable turn up less often than anyone expects. The likeliest joiners are people in the middle of a life transition who met the group through somebody they already liked, and who have few countervailing commitments holding them where they are.

**So picture the recruiter as a man's brother-in-law, and forget the stranger in the hood.** The man has just been discharged, or widowed, or moved to a district where he knows nobody, and the brother-in-law is kind to him for four months before anything is asked.

Most cults die in obscurity for want of kin and friends to recruit through, and this should be true in play. For every one that matters, a dozen never got past nine people in a back room.

### The three forms

**Audience cults** transmit to a large public and require no membership at all. Nobody joins. Everybody consumes.
> **The almanac trade.** Hand-copied sheets sold in every market, turned out by scriveners in back rooms and keyed to the sixty-count, telling a reader what their Wellspring-day portends and when to sign, travel, marry and dig. They sell by the hundred, and they are believed by nearly everyone a little and by a great many people entirely. The trade is enormously profitable, taxed as scrivening rather than as religion, and quietly consulted by two members of the Bench.

**Client cults** run on a personal relationship between a practitioner and the person paying, and sell a service rather than a membership.
> **The kind readers.** A back room, a fee, and an assay of a sort: a reading of what a person actually is, given gently, with no record kept and nothing forwarded to the Accord. What the Guild examination offers, minus the consequences. Half of them are frauds, some are competent and unlicensed, and the distinction matters far less to the client than either party pretends. The Bench prosecutes them for unregistered practice and never for what they actually do, because what they actually do is listen.

**Cultic movements** demand the whole person: commitment, money, and separation.
> **The Lease-holders.** They hold that the Archons administer and the Titans own, that all the Aether anyone has ever pulled was borrowed, and that the Withering is the term running out. Their response to this is to **pay early**, and repentance does not come into it: tithe, labor, communal living, and a discipline of pulling as little as a body can survive on.
>
> They buy dead ground. A district where the ground has gone thin, the wardens have walked off their rounds and the Core keepers have shut their doors is a district where the Accord's writ has already failed, the property is worthless, and nobody is watching, so the Lease-holders move into the quiet quarters and are frequently the only functioning institution in them. **They feed people the Accord has stopped counting**, and they are growing, and the growth is measured almost exactly by the fires going dark along the beacon line, one hill at a time.
>
> The doctrine is the least dangerous thing about them. They are right about the ground going thin and everybody can see it going thin, and the Accord's answer to the question they are asking is that the calendar is the calendar.

## Part Four: What To Do With This At The Table

**Name the faith by the practice, never by the creed.** Leave the word Currenter to the clerks. The character is a man who changes the water on the shelf every morning and has not thought about why since he was nine.

**Petition is the tell.** Where a character goes when they want something badly places them faster than any statement of belief. The Sohai bars it, the Scale prices it, the Current is made of it, the Tally has no grammar for it, and the Sky does not consider the sky to be listening.

**Every faith on this page is wrong about something specific**, and the scene is usually in the gap. The Current kills the people who practice it hardest. The Tally has lost a century. The Scale's weights were made by interested parties. The Sohai cannot see Singling in itself. The Sky's competence runs out in exactly the winter that matters.

**The votive is free texture and costs one line.** A shelf of tin hands at a seep. A ribbon at a wellhead. Coins nobody takes.

## Author Notes

Researched rather than recalled.

**Domestic devotion.** Pre-modern religious life ran substantially in the home, with most households holding objects of ritual significance ranging from fine work to cheap mold-made items, and the same devotional practices spanning all social strata. Petitions increasingly moved into the domestic setting, with a promise to visit a shrine with an offering if help arrived.

**Votives.** The vow is the lynchpin of the arrangement between the petitioner and the power, with the ex-voto given in hope or gratitude. Votives were frequently mold-made, inexpensive and used by ordinary people, and anatomical votives shaped like the afflicted body part were widespread across both pagan and later Christian shrines. The shelf of tin hands is this practice, transposed.

**Cult recruitment.** Interpersonal bonds are the essential mechanism, established quantitatively across several very different groups. Doctrine follows attachment rather than preceding it. Recruitment runs along existing networks and works most efficiently on people with few countervailing commitments, a condition the literature calls **structural availability**, and joiners are typically in a life transition and were introduced by somebody they already liked. Most new movements perish in obscurity for want of a recruitment network.

**The three forms** are the standard typology: audience cults transmitting broadly with no membership, client cults built on a personal relationship between practitioner and client, and cultic movements demanding full commitment. Tithing, fundraising, communal living and separation from non-members are the documented commitment mechanisms.

**The caveat, and it belongs on the page.** Nearly all of this literature describes one window, largely Anglo-American movements between the mid-1960s and mid-1980s, examined by sociologists arguing explicitly against the brainwashing accounts then current in the press, and that argument shaped which questions were asked. Within the model the friendship element has held up considerably better than the turning-point element, which was tested against other movement data and found weakly supported. The friendship finding is the one this page leans on.

The Current, the Tally as a named religion, the Sky as a named religion, and all three cults are originated. Sohai and Sancta Lux are summarized from existing canon and not altered.

# The Faiths of the Four Quarters

*What people in the four quarters actually do, on an ordinary day, about the fact that the world has owners.*
> *Ask a man his religion and he will name an institution. Watch him for a week and you will find out what he believes.*

### Part One: How Religion Is Actually Practised

The error to avoid is treating a faith as a set of propositions people assent to. **Religion in a pre-modern society is a set of things people do**, mostly at home, mostly daily, mostly without reflection, and the doctrine is downstream of the practice rather than upstream of it.
Three findings from how it worked in reality, and all three are already half-built into WOTR.
**The house is the unit, not the building.** Domestic devotion carried most of the actual religious life of a pre-modern population. Almost every home held some object with ritual weight: a small altar, a shelf, a figure, an amulet, a marked doorpost. These ranged from finely made to cheap and mass-produced, and **the same practices spanned every social stratum**. The Sohai already runs on exactly this and says so: no congregation, no clergy in the ordinary sense, no building, and a kingdom's piety measurable by walking into its kitchens.
**The vow is the transaction.** The lynchpin of ordinary religious practice was the vow between a person and a power: help me, and I will bring you a thing. The thing brought afterward is the votive, and votives were overwhelmingly ordinary objects, including models of the body part that was healed. Over time the petition itself moved into the home, with the shrine visit deferred until after the help arrived. **A world with Attraction Force in it, where a vow is structurally load-bearing, has a much sharper version of this and has never written it down.**
**Petition is the natural default and every organised faith fights it.** People ask. They ask constantly, for specific things, in bad grammar, at objects. The Sohai bars petition outright and calls it a heresy. Sanctum Lux permits it and regulates the form. The Current is made of nothing else. **Where a faith sits on petition tells you almost everything about it.**

#### The template

Every faith below is written to the same six questions, and the last one matters most.
1. What it claims happened.
1. What it asks of an ordinary person on an ordinary day.
1. Its clergy, if any, and how they eat.
1. Its heresies.
1. What it costs to belong.
1. **What it is wrong about.** A religion nobody in the setting can be wrong about is decoration.

### Part Two: The Majors

#### Sohai, the Whole Bow

**Moto. The oldest, the most demanding, and the smallest by headcount.** Built in full elsewhere; here is where it sits against the others.
**Claim.** The Fourteen are crowned and the Works are older, and both are true, and the order matters. No Archon may be addressed alone because none operates alone.
**Daily.** The Bow at the hinge, fourteen inclinations in emergence order, containing no request. Then the Labour: one concrete act in the character of one Archon's principle, performed by hands. The Weighing, the Discard, the Squaring, the Telling, and eleven more. Most households perform eleven of the fourteen without knowing they are performing anything.
**Clergy.** None. The household is the unit. This is why the Moto have never built a temple and have never understood why anybody else does, and it is also why the Sohai has no institutional weight to bring against Sanctum Lux in any forum where institutions are what counts.
**Heresies.** Singling, the elevation of one Archon. Petition, which converts the Bow into a transaction. The Empty Bow, observance without labour, held to be worse than neglect on the grounds that a man who has stopped bowing has at least stopped lying.
**Cost.** Everything, quietly, daily, forever, with no ceremony and no audience. The Sohai asks more of an ordinary Tuesday than any other faith in the four quarters and offers nothing in return that can be pointed at.
**Wrong about.** Its own immunity to Singling. The Kokan favour Totality, Totality is the Work most easily mistaken for a crown, and the house watches for this in itself precisely because it has already happened once and the record of when is not in Kharven.
**On the calendar.** Auren's day in every month for the dead. Munahi is the one day the Bow is not performed, because there is no station to perform it at, and the house defends this vigorously against the obvious objection.

#### Sanctum Lux, the Scale

**The institutional faith of the Concord and the Accord's working partner.** Judicial in temperament, literate, propertied, and the only major with buildings, courts and a career structure.
**Claim.** That the cosmos is weighable, that a thing's worth can be established by procedure, and that establishing it is a sacred act rather than an administrative one. Where the Sohai says a thing read correctly has been given its Work, the Scale says a thing weighed correctly has been given its due, and the difference between reading and weighing is the whole quarrel between them.
**Daily.** Attendance where there is a house to attend. Petition, permitted and formally shaped, which is the single largest reason the Scale has adherents and the Sohai does not. The Accord's proof-marks, seals and attestations are all Scale liturgy that the Accord has secularised and now charges for, and neither institution finds this awkward.
**Clergy.** Ordained, trained, examined, salaried, and pensioned. They eat by benefice, by fee for attestation, and by the Accord's retainer. **A Scale cleric is a professional and is treated as one**, which is a source of both its reach and its contempt.
**Heresies.** Weighing without a scale, meaning private judgement. The False Standard, an unauthorised weight. And the one the house does not discuss, which is the suggestion that a scale calibrated by the people being weighed is not a scale.
**Cost.** Money, mostly, and it is not hidden. Attestations, dispensations, the fee for a reading. The Scale is honest that piety is expensive and holds that a thing costing nothing is worth its price.
**Wrong about.** The claim to neutrality. Every weight it uses was standardised in Stannvaard by people with interests, and the Sohai's charge against it lands hard on precisely this point and has never been answered in a form anybody found satisfying.

#### The Current

**Originated. The largest faith in the four quarters by headcount, and it has no name for itself.**
This is the practice of people who live near Wellsprings and have done for longer than any institution has existed. It has no founder, no text, no clergy, no doctrine and no organisation, which is why the Accord has never suppressed it: **there is nothing to arrest.**
**Claim.** That the currents were here first, that they were never anybody's, and that what the Guild sells through a stopcock is a thing that was given freely to everyone who lived near it. Not a political claim in the mouths of the people who hold it. Simply an obvious fact about the world, held the way people hold facts about weather.
**Daily.** The shelf by the door with water on it, changed every morning, poured out at the threshold at night. The vow, constantly, for specific things, in the ordinary way people ask. A ribbon tied at a seep. A coin dropped in a wellhead, and every wellhead in the four quarters has coins in it, and everybody knows this, and nobody takes them.
The votives are the tell. **Small objects left where a current runs close to the surface**, made cheap and made everywhere: pressed clay, cut tin, a lock of hair, and overwhelmingly, models of the part of the body that was hurt. A shelf of tin hands at a seep outside a mining district is not a shrine anybody built. It accumulated.
**Clergy.** None, but there are always **women who know the seeps**, unpaid, unappointed, usually old, who are asked and who answer, and who are not called anything in particular. A district has two or three. Everybody knows which. When one dies the knowledge sometimes goes with her, and this has happened often enough that whole seeps have been forgotten.
**Heresies.** The concept does not apply, which is precisely what infuriates the organised faiths. There is no orthodoxy to depart from. Two villages nine miles apart will practise differently, know they do, and not consider the question interesting.
**Cost.** Nothing. This is not incidental. **The Current is free, and it is free in a world where the Scale charges and the draw is metered**, and that single fact does more for its persistence than any belief in it.
**Wrong about.** Efficacy, constantly, in ways that get people killed. A village that has been leaving tin at a seep for six generations is a village downwind of a concentration, and the strangeness in its families is a Wellspring's doing rather than a blessing, and the practice that grew up around it is a practice of standing too close to something for a very long time. On Walking Into the Current documents what that does to a body. Nobody at the seep has read it.
**Why the Accord tolerates it.** It cannot find it. There is no building to close, no roll to seize, no purse to seize it from, and a Guild inspector who breaks up a shrine has broken up a shelf and will find another shelf tomorrow. The Accord's actual position, unwritten, is that a faith with no institution is a faith with no politics, and this is the most consequential mistake anyone in the four quarters is currently making.

#### The Tally

**Dawi. Never previously named as a religion, including by the Dawi, who would deny it is one.**
**Claim.** That an act either happened or did not, that the record is the world's memory of it, and that the record is sacred because nothing else is durable. The Dawi do not say this. They keep the Tally, and they have kept it for six thousand years, and the entering is itself an act that gets entered.
**Daily.** The entry. Every completed lawful act, in fixed wording, scanned to Mathematical Cadence, because a statement that does not scan has not been entered whatever it says. A hold-clerk is a metrician before he is anything else and does not consider this liturgical.
**Clergy.** The clerks, and they eat by the hold. **A Dawi would say these are administrators.** Every other people in the four quarters, on being shown a caste of hereditary record-keepers using a fixed liturgical metre to enter acts into a book that has never been closed, would say something else.
**Heresies.** The unentered act, *kurnur*. The broken oath, which is not a sin but a change of grade, audible in the name, permanent, and published on a wall. And the deepest one, which is the Deepkin's Mirror-Sign: the only Dawi language in which a broken oath does not announce itself, and the only Dawi practice the other lineages regard as genuinely impious.
**Cost.** The future tense. A Dawi cannot express an intention without swearing to it or admitting nothing has begun, and there is no third option, and this is a religious constraint enforced by grammar rather than by clergy. It costs them enormously in every negotiation they have ever entered.
**Wrong about.** That the record is the world's memory. The Keth-Gorrum's archive is a room full of timber and can be destroyed with an axe, and this has happened, and there is a hole in the law where the year 411 used to be. A faith built on permanence has already lost a century and does not have a doctrine for it.

#### The Sky

**Kharven. Regional, total within its region, and written throughout the Standing Inventory without ever having been called a religion.**
**Claim.** *The sky does not ask whether you are ready.* Not a deity. A condition. The sky covers everyone, is indifferent, and outlasts every arrangement made under it, and the correct response to this is not worship but competence.
**Daily.** The woodpile. *How's your stack* is a greeting, a diagnostic and an examination of conscience at once, and it is Thalen's labour worn down past recognition, and no northern clansman alive knows that. The night-stone. Meat-sharing. The first bowl to the one who cannot fill it themselves.
**The dead.** The death-house, the Waiting, sky burial. *Gone to the sky.* A whole national institution devoted to sitting with the dead through a winter, performed by a people who believe they are being practical.
**Clergy.** None. Oaths are sworn by the sky and by the fire, and there is nobody to swear them in front of but everyone present, which is the point.
**Cost.** It asks a household to be materially prepared at all times and treats unpreparedness as a moral failure rather than a misfortune. A man whose stack is low in the Thin Weeks has been judged before anyone has spoken.
**Wrong about.** That competence is sufficient. It is a theology built by people for whom it usually was, in a place where it usually is, and the Thin Weeks it does not cover are the ones the proverbs go quiet about.

### Part Three: Cults

The interesting thing about a cult is not its doctrine. **It is how it recruits and how it eats**, and the research on this is unambiguous and unromantic.
**Recruitment runs along existing social relationships.** The essential mechanism is interpersonal bonds. **Doctrine comes second**: people become close to members and take on the beliefs afterward, as part of the cost and the pleasure of belonging. The people most likely to join are not the gullible or the unstable. They are people in the middle of a life transition who met the group through somebody they already liked, and who have few countervailing commitments holding them where they are.
**Which means the WOTR cult recruiter is not a stranger in a hood.** He is a man's brother-in-law, and the man has just been discharged, or widowed, or moved to a district where he knows nobody, and the brother-in-law is kind to him for four months before anything is asked.
Most cults die in obscurity for want of a network, and this should be true in play. For every one that matters, a dozen never got past nine people in a back room.

#### The three forms

**Audience cults** transmit to a large public and require no membership at all. Nobody joins. Everybody consumes.
> **The almanac trade.** Printed sheets sold in every market, keyed to the sixty-count, telling a reader what their Wellspring-day portends and when to sign, travel, marry and dig. Sold by the thousand. Believed by nearly everyone a little and by a great many people entirely. Enormously profitable, taxed as printing rather than as religion, and quietly consulted by two members of the Bench.
**Client cults** run on a personal relationship between a practitioner and the person paying, and sell a service rather than a membership.
> **The kind readers.** A back room, a fee, and an assay of a sort: a reading of what a person actually is, given gently, with no record kept and nothing forwarded to the Accord. What the Guild examination offers, minus the consequences. Half of them are frauds, some are competent and unlicensed, and the distinction matters far less to the client than either party pretends. The Bench prosecutes them for unregistered practice and never for what they actually do, because what they actually do is listen.
**Cultic movements** demand the whole person: commitment, money, and separation.
> **The Lease-holders.** They hold that the Archons administer and the Titans own, that everything anyone has ever drawn was borrowed, and that the Withering is the term running out. Their response is not to repent but to **pay early**: tithe, labour, communal living, and a discipline of taking as little from the draw as a body can survive on.
> They buy dead legs. A district the Guild has cut is a district where the Accord's writ has already failed, the property is worthless, and nobody is watching, so the Lease-holders move into the quiet quarters and are frequently the only functioning institution in them. **They feed people the Accord has stopped counting**, and they are growing, and the growth is measured almost exactly by the contraction of the signal net.
> What makes them dangerous is not the doctrine. It is that they are right about the pressure dropping and everybody can see it dropping, and the Accord's answer to the question they are asking is that the calendar is the calendar.

### Part Four: What To Do With This At The Table

**Name the faith by the practice, never by the creed.** A character is not a Currenter. He is a man who changes the water on the shelf every morning and has not thought about why since he was nine.
**Petition is the tell.** Where a character goes when they want something badly places them faster than any statement of belief. The Sohai bars it, the Scale prices it, the Current is made of it, the Tally has no grammar for it, and the Sky does not consider the sky to be listening.
**Every faith on this page is wrong about something specific**, and the scene is usually in the gap. The Current kills the people who practise it hardest. The Tally has lost a century. The Scale's weights were made by interested parties. The Sohai cannot see Singling in itself. The Sky's competence runs out in exactly the winter that matters.
**The votive is free texture and costs one line.** A shelf of tin hands at a seep. A ribbon at a wellhead. Coins nobody takes.

### Author Notes

Researched rather than recalled.
**Domestic devotion.** Pre-modern religious life ran substantially in the home, with most households holding objects of ritual significance ranging from fine work to cheap mass-produced items, and the same devotional practices spanning all social strata. Petitions increasingly moved into the domestic setting, with a promise to visit a shrine with an offering if help arrived.
**Votives.** The vow is the lynchpin of the arrangement between the petitioner and the power, with the ex-voto given in hope or gratitude. Votives were frequently mould-made, inexpensive and used by ordinary people, and anatomical votives shaped like the afflicted body part were widespread across both pagan and later Christian shrines. The shelf of tin hands is this practice, transposed.
**Cult recruitment.** Interpersonal bonds are the essential mechanism, established quantitatively across several very different groups. Doctrine follows attachment rather than preceding it. Recruitment runs along existing networks and works most efficiently on people with few countervailing commitments, a condition the literature calls **structural availability**, and joiners are typically in a life transition and were introduced by somebody they already liked. Most new movements perish in obscurity for want of a recruitment network.
**The three forms** are the standard typology: audience cults transmitting broadly with no membership, client cults built on a personal relationship between practitioner and client, and cultic movements demanding full commitment. Tithing, fundraising, communal living and separation from non-members are the documented commitment mechanisms.
**The caveat, and it belongs on the page.** Nearly all of this literature describes one window, largely Anglo-American movements between the mid-1960s and mid-1980s, examined by sociologists arguing explicitly against the brainwashing accounts then current in the press, and that argument shaped which questions were asked. Within the model the friendship element has held up considerably better than the turning-point element, which was tested against other movement data and found weakly supported. The friendship finding is the one this page leans on.
The Current, the Tally as a named religion, the Sky as a named religion, and all three cults are originated. Sohai and Sanctum Lux are summarised from existing canon and not altered.

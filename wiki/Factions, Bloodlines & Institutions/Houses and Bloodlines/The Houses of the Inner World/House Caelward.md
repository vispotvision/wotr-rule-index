| | |
|---|---|

In the spring of 702 Count Merrow Tallis of Sedgeford, who took war seriously and could afford to, armed two thousand archers for the Harl Ford with forty thousand arrows fletched in the true gray molt of Ysmay Hask, called Long Afternoon, three times champion of the Gray Kites. He paid a king's ransom, with a certificate for every sheaf, and he told his quartermaster to leave off the Windstair almanac as an economy, since the four silver seemed to him an impertinence. The morning of the battle was the kind of morning they paint, all bright banners and yellow flag along the water. Far overhead one small figure in gray and lilac hung on the wind and took no part, having been engaged by the other side for a modest fee. At the first volley the count's archers loosed as one, the whole black flight went up with the sun behind it, and at the top of its arc nine hundred arrows left the rest. They peeled off like starlings. They turned over in the air with every man on that field watching, and they came back on a long lazy curve and went into the count's own banner, his banner-bearer's saddle, and the table where his breakfast had been laid out, in a cluster you could have covered with a door. A slip of paper came down after them, on an arrow, reading *Four silver. Overdue.*

The remaining thirty-nine thousand one hundred were goose feathers, dyed. They fell on the enemy and did what arrows do, and Count Tallis won the battle by a comfortable margin. He then sued his supplier for fraud on the thirty-nine thousand and the Gray Kites for assault on the nine hundred, and the court at Caelspire informed him that the first complaint had saved his life and the second could be settled by stepping off the bridge.

Every bowman in the center will tell you the moral: a true feather can be called home at bowshot, so buy fakes for the battles that matter. That has kept people from asking the next question, which I put to an elderly molt-seller at Windstair who had been drinking for some time. Why does a Kite sell her feathers so cheaply to both sides of every war? He looked at the arrow in my quiver, which was a good one, and he said that they do not sell them, exactly.

## Who They Are

House Caelward has not been the strongest school on its own mountain for three hundred years, and it holds that mountain anyway. It keeps the Core's hours, works the hoists and judges at the Drop, so a dozen clubs that despise each other must all come and shout at the Warden, at present Vessaly Caelward, called Nobody's Landing, a tall gray-crested woman of fifty with one vane burned to the bone.

They want the stair uncut, the Core's hours kept and the Peaks left to their own quarrels. What they hold that everyone needs is the weather. Half the battles of the center have been timed off a Windstair almanac, which is right more often than one reader on one crag has any business being.

---

## Origin of the House

Nobody knows who built the bridge at Caelspire, and it was already old when the first Kin nested on it. For a long age the Caelwards were simply the best fighters on the mountain and the most resented. Somewhere about the year 415 the Warden of the day stopped entering the Long Step, and the schools were delighted. The Caelwards had just begun keeping a book of who bought which molt and where it went, and had worked out what twelve old readers with their arms in the Cymorath flow could feel of the world if the world was carrying enough of their feathers. They have been losing on Saturdays ever since, by policy.

## The House on the Crystal

The Soul Crystal is the soul, which refines Aether (mana) drawn from the world into Essence (spiritual energy). The Kin draw it out of moving air and lay it along the vanes of the forearm, and the flight they have is earned that way.

| Layer | How it runs in them | What shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Aether Shell** | Clean, light and extended along every vane. They call the aura coat *the lift*, and they argue about whether a good one should show at all | A blue spiraling shimmer and curved trails where it was spent |
| **Essence Core** | Quiet in most, and a Reader's whole life. A molted vane keeps a trace of stamped Essence, and the Core goes on hearing it | Far-off eyes, a tilt of the head |
| **Attraction Layer** | Each sold feather is the thinnest possible tie, held here. Thousands of them make a sort of permanent itch | Nothing a coil can read, and plenty a Warden can feel |

**Path lean.** Body, then Spirit, as a tendency of the untrained soul. **The eye.** None. They read with the skin. **The grip.** Aether Class, the ladder of a soul's influence on the physical world, may open before Glory (Stage VI, where the ladders first read) in a fledgling who has stepped off a sill and landed well. The cap never lifts. **On the rungs.** Aether I is a school senior, whose fletch in the hand obeys. At II, a captain's feather carries it onward wherever that feather is. At III a champion knows a true feather of her school by recognition, and IV is a Warden who has given up a good many Saturdays. The Readers are often Aether I and Essence III, and could not fight a goose. Thread stays Unthreaded.

## The Long Read

**Every feather the Kin ever shed still feels the wind for them, and there are several hundred thousand of them in quivers, hats, pillows and army stores across the center.**

**What it is.** A Kin's vane is grown with her own Essence in it, stamped with who she is, which is why the fletch flies true. When it falls out in the molt the stamp stays on it, and so does a tie as thin as a cobweb. A single feather tells its owner next to nothing, a faint draft on one patch of skin. The Caelwards' discovery was that the number of feathers changes what kind of thing you are looking at.

**How it works.** No one can forecast weather from where she stands, because a storm is the size of a country. What forecasts weather is many readings taken at once across a wide land: where the air pressure is falling, how fast, and what lies upwind. Scholars call it a synoptic chart, the view taken all together, and you would need ten thousand clerks with barometers. The house has quivers. Cymorath, the Air of Ascent, teaches a soul to read the world as a set of open ways, and twelve old Kin sit in the domed hall at the lip of its summit pool with their bare vanes laid in the flow, feeling a low front over Kingsbridge in eleven thousand fletchings. They also feel that eleven thousand fletchings have gone south by the Kessen road at an army's pace. That sitting is the Sill, and what comes off it, once the clerks of the Molt Book have matched each draft to whoever bought that batch, is the almanac. Rain Thursday, heavy, you are in a floodplain, four silver. The house also knows where you are, because you bought the arrows.

| Form | From | What it does | Costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reads the Wind** | Any Kin | Her own skin reads the air's whole shape, a front two days out | Nothing, and no way to stop |
| **The Given Feather** | Stage III | A feather shed by hand to a student or client. She feels you by it, and while she attends, Cymorath holds you up in a fall. This is the Stair Wardens' whole contract | Attention. Drop it, and you are simply a person falling |
| **The Call** | Stage VI, Aether I and up | Her own molt within bowshot leans toward her or away. A volley's true feathers can be bent by a spear-length, or brought home by a champion | A sharp bite of reserve per flight, and every shaft she turns she feels |
| **The Sill** | Stage VIII, at the Core, with the Book | The Long Read itself, for hours | Essence, sleep and a little more of herself each season |
| **THURSDAY!** | Stage X or so, and a field already full of her school's molt | The house's one great art, about once in a generation. She finds a front with every reason to arrive in two days and brings it in now, through ten thousand small leans of air, until warm wet air lifts, cools and lets go of its water all at once | A vane burned to the bone, for good. She can only call what is already coming |

Whether the rain that fell on Quartermaster-General Hasketh in 709 was forecast or called, the house has declined to say, and it billed him the same four silver either way.

**Domain: the Last Step.** A Domain is the territory where a high-Stage practitioner's own law holds, opened by a Second Release from Stage VIII. Law: *every stair ends here.* The ground stays where it is and stops being anybody's right, footing must be retaken each step, and those who have stepped off may stand on the air as they please. Fighters on the terraces sing the skipping rhyme to tell you it is coming.

**What it costs.** You feel what your feathers feel, and that includes where they end up. An arrow going into a man comes to its owner as a faint hot draft, and a champion who has sold her molt across thirty years of wars has never had a quiet night in any of them. A Reader gets the whole of it, and this is why the house's fighters have been second-rate for three hundred years: its best are sitting down.

**Its limit.** Only the real molt counts, a small and costly part of what the market sells. Far-off feathers can be read and only near ones called. The Sill reads the air and has no idea what a general has in his head, and it goes blind where the air goes dead.

**Who beats it.** Fletch in goose, as the Marrick companies have done for a century. Carry crossbows: a bolt has leather vanes, costs about three coppers and has no opinion, and every fighter in the Peaks knows someone it brought down like a goose off a marsh. Burn or salt-boil a true feather and its tie dies. Fight on a dead calm inversion morning, when half a school is grounded. Or hold one of their feathers yourself and hire a good Spirit reader, since a tie runs both ways.

## Physical Appearance and Dress

Light-boned and narrow, hard-eyed, with crests of feather from brow to shoulder and long stiff vanes down the outer forearm, in every color from sparrow to flame. They speak in high carrying voices, having learned to talk across gulfs, and do not lower them indoors.

At fifty paces it is the colors, the scarves and the sky behind them. Sleeves are slit from wrist to elbow so the vanes run free, and coats are cut short and laced flat. A Warden's violet coat is Rare work in storm-silk with the white lining showing at each turn. Top gear goes light: a pinned-steel gorget, hippogrif leather, a heel-spur with a name. The Highroost champion's is named Your Collar. A poor cousin in the lower town has one good scarf and a single true feather from a dead aunt that he will not sell.

## Lineage Traits

### [Trait] Current-Reading — The Air's Whole Shape

- The skin and vanes read pressure, draft and heat across a whole valley.
- It gives first warning of anything that moves, including you, in the dark.

*They can tell you are lying about the weather, which in the Peaks is the worst kind of lie.*

### [Trait] Hollow Frame — Very Little to Break

- The bones are light and fast and the Reflex has no equal in the center.
- The unwoken can glide on a good wind, if they are brave and have an appointment with a surgeon.
- A heavy blow stays landed, and a broken wrist grounds a champion for a season.

*Hard men with low ceilings have ended a good many careers in the indoor towns.*

### [Trait] Vanes Laid Along — The Earned Sky

- True flight comes with training, as Aether pulled from moving air is laid along the vane.
- A plucked vane grows back crooked and flies wrong for life.

*Fighters step off the cliffs each morning in their colors, as a matter of routine, and call it going to work.*

## Doctrine and Arts

Who holds the stair holds the peak. A Kin fighter stays above you, makes you pay for every step, and screams her moves down the gulf by name so the terraces can sing them by evening.

| Move | School | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **HIGHROOST STOOP!** | Shrikes | A drop from a thousand feet with everything she weighs and all the air she has packed behind her, into one heel on the join of your collar |
| **LONG AFTERNOON!** | Gray Kites | One spear-length out of reach on the wind, and one cut each time you breathe in, until you sit down |
| **NOBODY'S LANDING!** | Stair Wardens | She gets under a falling enemy, catches him as a courtesy and carries him out over the gulf to discuss terms |
| **STEP OFF!** | Wardens, house style | A hammer of descending air on one spot, the downdraft that leaves a man's footing lighter than his shoulders. The politest way a person is asked to leave |
| **FINE!** | Stair Wardens | A Warden's catch with a sharp little dislocation in it, delivered with the amount |
| **THE KING'S HAT!** | Any, in a match | Anything worn at the highest point of an enemy is suddenly in her hand, three hundred feet up |

## Culture

**Language.** Common with a great deal of school cant. Their names are light and old, and their word for anyone without feathers is *groundling*, applied with pity. Do not order eggs.

**Rites.**

- **The First Step.** A fledgling steps off the family sill with a Warden's feather at her wrist. If she does not land, the Warden catches her and sends the fine to her parents.
- **The Sill-Taking.** A Warden chooses a Reader, always someone at the height of her fighting years, and she hangs up her colors in front of her school. Most of them believe she is retiring.
- **The Wind Burial.** The dead go to the wind from the bridge, and every true feather they ever sold goes quiet at once. The Readers feel it.
- **The Bridge Hour.** A fowler taken on any stair is walked up in the sight of the assembled schools, who for that one hour wear no colors, and invited to step off. There is nobody to catch him.

**Who they marry, feud with and owe.** They marry across clubs with enormous difficulty. The Scalebond hold an old fine over a bird. House Nobody has asked for the high roads: a Kite captain has carried sixty people over the range on her back, and a Shrike master charged the same sixty.

**What commoners get.** The hoisters' company strikes once a decade, the lower town hangs from the crag on ropes and pins, and every child gets a school to hate with. A Stair Warden will catch them too, and the fine comes out of a common fund nobody admits to.

**The sore point.** The fowlers. Where the Accord's rolls read the Kin as labor stock, licensed men with nets take them for their singing and their feathers. A plucked vane is still attached in every way that matters. A woman in a cage in a Concord market town can feel the feathers they took from her go to church on someone's hat. The Caelwards sell their wind to the Accord each season and spit afterward, and the Warden has been heard to observe that a people who can leave are always the last to stop selling.

## Limitations and Soul-Costs

### [Curse] Vane-Ache — Every Arrow Ever Sold

Old champions flinch at nothing in the middle of a meal. It is a volley in a war two hundred miles away, going into someone, and there is no way to give the feathers back.

### [Curse] Never Quite Here — The Price of the Sill

Readers forget their own children's names and remember the rainfall at Pennycrown, and a Warden who sits too long has been known to give judgment on a case from the last century.

### [Curse] Glass and Dead Air — One Hit, One Calm Morning

A bolt or a maul in a low room stops the song and most of the career. Still air and bled ground strip the school and the Read at once, and on an inversion morning the whole mountain is very polite.

---

## How They Stand Against the Three Paths

**Body.** They beat him by never being in reach, for as long as the weather holds, and one good blow settles it if he closes. **Spirit.** Their fear: armor and height are no help, and a reader holding a true feather has a road straight to its owner's Core. **Fate.** A storm that should not be there shows on the Sill, and they can do nothing about it, and a pull on what flight depends on (a strap, a gust at the wrong second) needs to win only once.

---

## Final Definition

House Caelward is the people who stopped trying to win. For three hundred years they have sat on top of the only stair no army ever took, losing matches on purpose and selling their own molt to every war in the center. Anyone can buy the almanac. Your arrows send their regards.

*Step off.*

And in the Book's oldest hand, on its first page: *What we shed is still ours. Charge accordingly.*

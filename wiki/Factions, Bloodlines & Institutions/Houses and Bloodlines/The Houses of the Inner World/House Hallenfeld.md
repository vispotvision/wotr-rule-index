Chainbridge has a contraption at its eastern pier for raising the middle span in a siege. A guild-master built it, and the guild-mark on it is the most boasted-about in the valley. On a blue October morning in Year 661, with the Hallen running green and four hundred yards of plank swaying in the sun, Aldous Rook's Long Company came down the east road, nine hundred lances in black and yellow, on a contract from a Midmarch king to take the junction by nightfall. The bridge's clerk that morning was Griet Hoogstraat, sixty-three, in a blue-and-ocher striped apron, and she sent a boy to raise the span. It jammed. It has jammed twice in its history, and that was the first. So she picked up her stool and ledger, walked out to the east end of the planks, past a toll house painted from eaves to waterline with what happens to people who do not pay, sat down in the middle of the road, and had two bargemen haul the bridge's old road-chain across behind her. It lay across the planks at about the height of a horse's knee. Rook thought it was very funny. It was a chain, with an old woman in front of it.

She stood up as the first rank came into a canter, and she has a voice in the histories, because everyone on both banks heard it, and the rate is still painted on that wall for anyone who cares to check.

**"NINE HUNDRED HORSE AT THREE PENCE THE HOOF! PAY OR BE CHAINED!"**

She stepped to one side. They did not pay. The lead horses struck the chain at full gallop and stopped, as though the bridge had been a cliff. There were eighty lancers in the river before the rest understood they should pull up, and the rank behind rode into the rank in front, and the planks shook from end to end. Links as thick as a man's arm had gone blue-white along their length and stood there humming over a pile of horses, with the air around them shivering like the air over a hot stove. Two hundred and six years of carters, drovers, widows and pilgrims had each paid to come across that span, and each had gotten what they paid for, and what they gave up had gone into the book and stayed there. Griet Hoogstraat looked at the chain. Then she wrote something down, and walked up to where Aldous Rook was picking himself out of the wreck with a broken collarbone, and showed him the page.

Eleven years of receipts had just gone out of that chain. She knew precisely what was left, which was about enough to stop a good-sized cart. She did not mention it. She said that there were a great many hooves still on the east bank, that she would want it noted that the rate had been posted, and that the damage to the planks would be added. Rook looked at the chain, still humming, and at the old woman. He paid, in full, on the bridge, for all nine hundred, and then he went home. The Toll Law has never had a better advertisement. She got a raise of a penny a week.

House Hallenfeld has no glamour and produces no legendary warriors or political masterminds. It produces quartermasters, road engineers, courier captains and the category of administrator who treats six hours as a problem worth solving with the intensity other houses bring to swordwork. Ulf Stark called them clerks and couriers who would carry anybody's letters. They hold the junction where three rivers and three roads meet, and with it the speed at which the north can respond to anything. Everyone needs them, and they know it, and they would prefer to be thanked.

## Origin of the House

Nothing floats past the triple falls of the Hallen. Every barge unloads at the top or the bottom and its cargo is hauled round under the castle wall, and everything hauled pays. Maarten the Hauler charged for that before there was a castle. He kept his accounts on a stair behind the falls, where the rock stayed damp and the paper did not, and it was his great-granddaughter, going further down that stair than was sensible, who came to a flooded landing and a toll house far older than anything on the river, with its rate still painted on the wall in a script no clerk has ever read. She came back up, put a chain across the head of the stair and started keeping better books. The house has never explained the connection, and no Hallenfeld has ever tried to collect on that particular chain.

**Path.** The soul leans Fate, for the bonds and bargains, with a Spirit lean for the writing. Couriers lean Body instead, and the two halves of the house do not entirely consider one another to be the same family. **Ladders.** Most of it runs at Thread Ø, because none of this pulls. A vow spends the soul's own share, and a toll is one of those, many thousands of times. **Grip.** None. A Hallenfeld has no grip on Fate at all, and every clerk in the house will tell you so with pride. They have receipts.

## The Paid Chain

**In a breath:** a toll is a small real bargain, and what you give up to cross goes into the chain at exactly its worth, so a chain with three hundred years of honest books behind it is as strong as everything anyone ever paid to pass it.

**How it works.** Oblation, the Wellspring of the offering, keeps one law: what you give up comes back as force, and never one grain more than you gave. It favors altars, old bargain-stones and toll bridges. It has been doing this on toll bridges since before there was a house. When a carter pays three pence at a Hallenfeld span he gives something up, freely, for a known return, and that is a true Oblation, however small. The house's discovery was that the force does not have to come back to the carter. It can be left where it is, on the terms of the bargain, against the thing that was bargained for: the crossing. So the chain holds it.

Everything else about the Painted Rivers follows. **The rate has to be posted**, because a bargain nobody understood is no bargain and banks nothing. That is why every toll house is painted to the waterline with the rate in pictures for those who cannot read, and why all that paint is more than vanity. **The entry has to be made**, in a sworn hand, on the day, because a payment no one recorded left no stamp on anybody's Layer and might as well have been a coin dropped in the river. **And the book has to be true.** This is the one that surprises people. A false entry rots the balance, and the chain parts at that link. Hallenfeld clerks are not honest for love of honesty. Osbert Lambert, of the Hallenfeld cadet line, read carts against their manifests for eleven years and would not change a figure when the Tenrai beat him for it, and the Tenrai thought they were dealing with a stubborn man. They were dealing with a man who knew exactly what holds up a bridge.

### The forms

| Form | Who | What it does | What it costs |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **The Rate** | Any sworn clerk, at a post | The rate called aloud at a toll, to someone who has not paid. The cart will not roll for a moment and boots grow heavy. It is about what an honest hedge-worker's trick is worth, and it stops a chancer | Nothing. It runs on the post's float |
| **The Chain** | A clerk of a span, at that span | Closes the chain against whatever is crossing unpaid. It meets that thing with force at exact par from the balance, and the balance goes down by exactly that | Whatever it stopped, in receipts. Griet spent eleven years in about a breath |
| **The Receipt** | Anyone who has paid | Not a working of the house at all. It is the other half of the bargain, and a chain will not stop you. A bargee with a good receipt has floated through a closed river in a siege | A copper, and keeping it dry |
| **THE HALT** | The Mistress, or a tariff-master by her order | Every span and river-crossing closed in an hour by beacon. No grain moves, no message moves, and an army a week away starves on the road. Almost entirely logistics, and all the more frightening for it | Every toll in the valley stops as well, and the house's whole income with it. It has been used three times |
| **SIX HOURS** | Courier captains | The Relay. Riders sworn to one road are tied by Transference, the Wellspring of the living exchange, so that a rested rider's legs turn up under a tired one across the gap. A satchel on the Hallenfeld road moves faster than any rider who carries it, because everyone on the line is giving it their rest | A courier's body. Nobody in the room under the stair expects to be good at forty |
| **THE TOLL IS DUE** | Second Release and Domain, Stage VIII at the least | The Rate as a law of a place. Everyone in it pays reserve, posted and at par, before a working will fire, and the clerk pays the same as anyone. It is the only Domain on the north's books with a printed tariff | Her own reserve, and honest figures. Falsify it once and it comes apart on you |

**What it costs.** It costs a life of arithmetic. A chain's balance is spent the moment it is used and comes back a copper at a time, so a clerk who stops an army has just cost the next twenty years of clerks, and will be told about it. The house cannot lie in its own books, ever, and cannot get out of a bad bargain it has written down. Nobody likes them.

**Its limit.** Exact par, never one grain more. It holds only at that chain, only against what is crossing, and only to the amount in the book. A chain cannot chase you, or do anything in a field, or stop anything that has not come to cross. A Hallenfeld clerk two hundred yards from a span is a soft person with spectacles. Monsters do not read posted rates and are not bound by anything: a sump-toad in a mill-race has to be dealt with by a miller with a hoist.

**Who beats it.** The first and best answer, which it is astonishing how few armies have thought of, is to pay. Right of passage under the old terms lets a host cross, and the house will take its money and hand it a receipt. Go around: the fords are real, Hayato Amagiri's indigo roads are real, and the eastern march has bears who have never paid in their lives. Spend the chain down, as Griet very nearly let Rook do. Then there is **Free Water**. A town that stops paying stops feeding the chain, and the Toll-Breakers along the wrong side of it, whose class names run from Chain-Thief to Free Water itself, are beloved by bargemen and hanged by Hallenfeld for exactly this reason. Corrupt the books, if you can get a clerk to do it, and the link parts where he lied. The Northern Road Company has lately found another way, standing guard at bridges a house can be paid to give it, so a ferryman pays the toll in the morning and the Company in the afternoon. Nobody can tell him which is the law, and the chain itself seems to have begun to wonder.

### The money under it

Ilse Hallenfeld, Mistress of the counting-house, does not fight. When the Seat had to fall, she told a council that the Seat's wards were kept by hand by Guild keepers, paid quarterly through the Hallenfeld counting-house, on Hallenfeld credit that the Seat's treasury had been running for eleven years without paying back a copper. She bought the wardmasters. At the first bell every Tenrai ward on the rock died. That was not a working of any kind. A note of hand is only paper, half the debts of the north are in that building, each in its pigeonhole, and that is simply what the house is like when it has decided something. She wrote the ratification of the seven lines in her own hand, at a small table at the side of the Hall, and would not let a clerk touch the pen. *A debt is a debt,* she said. It is the largest single entry the counting-house has ever made, and she wanted that noted. It has occurred to at least one Measurewright to wonder what, under the Oblation's law, that entry now holds up.

## Physical Appearance and Dress

**Stature and build.** Round-shouldered, indoors, soft in the belly, and often in small square spectacles. Couriers are the opposite: narrow, sun-dried people who look as though they were packed for a journey. **Coloring.** Sandy, mousy, pale. Ilse has eyes like gray ice. **Marks.** Ink on the second finger of the right hand that no amount of beating has ever got off. **Voice.** Exact and unsurprised. Nobody in the house has ever been heard to say *I think* about a number.

**The look at fifty paces.** Blue and ocher stripes, loud enough to be seen from the far bank, on men with boathooks, short spears and the weight of the law. Bargemen's clubs and bridge companies wear theirs in every color a Dauber can get cheap. Toll houses are the true gear of the house, and they flex: a fresh coat of paint on a jettied front means solvent, and a peeling one means you should ask to see the books. The high end is restraint: Ilse's dark coat lined in wolf, a ledger under her arm and ink on the gloves. The river-reeve on the lip of the falls wears a long robe and carries a long sounding-staff, and no holder of that office has ever been seen to fall. Every morning before calling the rate up to the castle the reeve drops a copper over the edge and enters it, so the falls have been paid. Couriers carry the one thing the house spends real money on: boots. They are Grade D and Uncommon, resoled every month and worn to bed. A poor cousin has a single stripe on his sleeve and a very great deal of knowledge about the price of barley.

## Lineage Traits

### [Trait] The Sworn Hand — What the Entry Does

* Anything entered by a sworn Hallenfeld hand on the day it happened carries a small stamp, and the entry cannot be altered without showing.
* Their Retention and Fluency run absurdly high. They read Concord, which almost no one else in the north can.

*They do not need to open your letter to know what it says from who sent it and how fast.*

### [Trait] Nobody Looks at the Clerk — The Tariff-Master's Cloak

* Clerks stand at the side of the room, and they are in the room for everything. It has no mechanism beyond that, and a stronger read would see them plain.
* Nothing is hidden from a Hallenfeld in the north for very long, because somebody had to carry it.

*The king who took the Seat lit his first fire in a small room the Hallenfeld clerks told him had been the Kōkan's own. He did not ask how they knew.*

### [Trait] Six Hours — The Road in the Legs

* In the courier line: Celerity, Economy and Reflex at a level that embarrasses swordsmen, spent only on arriving.
* The only people in the house who are any use in a fight, having spent their lives getting through other people's with a satchel.

*They have no interest in winning. It has made them very hard to stop.*

### [Trait] A Debt Is a Debt — No Forgiveness on File

* A debt on a Hallenfeld book does not trouble them, age or fade. Their Oath is simple: what is entered stands.
* They cannot be flattered out of it, which also means they cannot be generous without a line for it.

*A lord who has forgotten what he owes can expect a very courteous reminder at a very poor moment.*

## Doctrine and Arts

Reluctantly. House men carry boathooks and the weight of the law, and when there is real killing to be done the house pays for it, and the company in question is paid on the day, in full, in the most terrifying way possible, with a receipt. Their weapons are the chain, the halt and the courier captains. Brackets are fought on the chain bridges in midsummer in loud stripes, one fall into the river being a loss and two being a very cold swim.

## Culture

**Language.** Quick river Common with Dutch-sounding names, and Concord for the books. "Six hours" is the whole of the house's sense of humor, and it can be told as a joke by anyone at any time.

**Rites.**

* **The First Toll.** A child's first copper is paid at the junction, entered in the child's own hand, and kept on the books for life.
* **Calling the Rate.** Every morning from the lip of the falls, the reeve sounds the water and calls the day's figure up to the castle, after dropping a copper into the falls and entering it.
* **The Fresh Coat.** A toll house repainted every third spring, with fights between Limners and Daubers considered part of the expense.
* **The Boots On.** Under the counting-house stair is the room where couriers wait, and no one takes their boots off in it. There is a row of old boots on a shelf.
* **Closing the Page.** At a clerk's death their last page is ruled off by a colleague and the balance carried forward. The Waiting is kept standing up, like everything else.

**Marriage, feud and debt.** They marry other counting-houses, and the Hallenfeld Lamberts, cadets of the Lamberts of Sallowgate, sit among them. Thornwall is the long war, on account of the log-jam of 688: each year the rate on logs is negotiated as a continuation of it. Ashmore has the grain and they have the barges, and a wise person does not come between them or try to work out who is using whom. They had a tariff-master in a Moto cell for keeping honest books. The house wrote that down, and it was there in the ledger the morning the first bell rang at the Seat.

**What the commons get.** A rate, on the wall, in pictures, that applies to them exactly as it applies to a duke, and a receipt that means something. That is a rarer thing than you may think. Signal-post women get a hut, a basket and a wage, and in a hard year a bargee's family can borrow against a note witnessed by a sworn notary, which is a respectable trade and has been known to be knifed. And there is the Toll Bun, in the shape of a fist.

## Limitations and Soul-Costs

### [Curse] Exact Par — Never One Grain More

* The art cannot be bluffed from the inside. What is in the book is what there is, and every clerk knows the figure.
* A chain spent is a generation's work gone. The clerk who spends it will be told so at every family meal until she dies.

*Griet Hoogstraat's penny a week was docked, some years later, on review.*

### [Curse] The Honest Book — A Lie Parts the Link

* They cannot falsify a figure to save a life, a child's included. Clerks have died of this, and some of them were quite willing to be saved.
* It leaves them unable to tell a comforting story, a habit that follows them home.

*They were told that the Tenrai had broken Osbert Lambert. The house looked at the entries he had left, and they knew better.*

### [Curse] The Entry at the Bottom of the Stair — Owing

* Below the Fall Cellar, down a flooded stair behind the middle of the falls, is the Drowned Toll: a toll house far older than the house, its rate on the wall in a script no clerk can read, and a Well beneath it where Mourning Pike grow as long as a barge.
* A Hallenfeld clerk entered that rate as owing long ago. The chain at the head of that stair is the only one in the realm the house has never tried to collect on.
* Nobody in the counting-house will say which side of the bargain the house is on. Every clerk has done the sums on what a three-hundred-year debt comes to at par.

*Every other chain in the north is holding something back. A careful reader will ask what this one is keeping in.*

## How They Stand Against the Three Paths

**Body.** A hitter who comes to a chain finds out the balance, and one who walks around it finds a soft person with a ledger, and kills him, and pays for that for the rest of his life in late grain. **Spirit.** Ward is better than you would guess, and they are unusually hard to lie to, but a true Spirit fighter can read a clerk's balance off his Core and know exactly how much bluff he has. **Fate.** A Weaver can move a barge, a bridge pile or a river's flood. Hallenfeld regards Fate users as people who are extremely late with their paperwork. They have been outlasting them for three hundred years, on the principle that a pull costs what it costs and a toll comes in every day.

## Final Definition

House Hallenfeld is the most boring great power in the north and it would like that noted. They took the smallest honest thing a person can do, paying what was asked and getting what was promised, and they wrote it down, every time, for three hundred years, until it could stop nine hundred horses. They have no heroes, they read your mail by its weight, and they cannot tell a lie in their own books, and that means when one of them looks up from the page and tells you the rate, you should believe her and find your purse.

*"Six hours. I'll want that noted."*

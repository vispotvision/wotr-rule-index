Chainbridge has a contraption at its eastern pier for raising the middle span in a siege, and the guild-mark on it is the most boasted-about in the valley. On a blue October morning in Year 661, with the Hallen running green and four hundred yards of plank swaying in the sun, Aldous Rook's Long Company came down the east road, nine hundred lances in black and yellow, to take the junction by nightfall for a Midmarch king. The bridge's clerk that morning was Griet Hoogstraat, sixty-three, in a blue-and-ocher striped apron, and she sent a boy to raise the span. It jammed. So she picked up her stool and ledger, walked out past a toll house painted to the waterline with what happens to people who do not pay, and had two bargemen haul the old road-chain across behind her, at about the height of a horse's knee. Rook thought it was very funny.

She stood up as the first rank came into a canter, and everyone on both banks heard her.

**"NINE HUNDRED HORSE AT THREE PENCE THE HOOF! PAY OR BE CHAINED!"**

She stepped to one side. They did not pay. The lead horses struck the chain at full gallop and stopped, as though the bridge had been a cliff. There were eighty lancers in the river before the rest pulled up. Links as thick as a man's arm stood blue-white and humming over a pile of horses, with the air shivering around them. Two hundred and six years of carters, drovers, widows and pilgrims had each paid to come across that span, and what they gave up had gone into the book and stayed there. Griet Hoogstraat wrote something down, walked up to where Rook was picking himself out of the wreck with a broken collarbone, and showed him the page.

Eleven years of receipts had just gone out of that chain, and what was left would about stop a good-sized cart. She did not mention it. She said there were a great many hooves still on the east bank, and that the damage to the planks would be added. Rook looked at the chain, still humming, and at the old woman, and he paid, in full, for all nine hundred, and went home. She got a raise of a penny a week.

House Hallenfeld has no glamour and produces no legendary warriors. It produces quartermasters, road engineers, courier captains and the kind of administrator who treats six hours as a problem worth solving with the intensity other houses bring to swordwork. They hold the junction where three rivers and three roads meet, and with it the speed at which the north can respond to anything. They would prefer to be thanked.

## Origin of the House

Nothing floats past the triple falls of the Hallen. Every barge unloads and its cargo is hauled round under the castle wall, and everything hauled pays. Maarten the Hauler charged for that before there was a castle. He kept his accounts on a stair behind the falls, and it was his great-granddaughter, going further down than was sensible, who came to a flooded landing and a toll house far older than anything on the river, its rate still painted on the wall in a script no clerk has read. She came back up, put a chain across the head of the stair and started keeping better books. The house has never explained the connection.

They have receipts.

## The Paid Chain

**In a breath:** a toll is a small real bargain, and what you give up to cross goes into the chain at exactly its worth, so a chain with three hundred years of honest books behind it is as strong as everything anyone ever paid to pass it.

**How it works.** Oblation, the Wellspring of the offering, keeps one law: what you give up comes back as force, and never one grain more than you gave. It favors altars, old bargain-stones and toll bridges. When a carter pays three pence at a Hallenfeld span he gives something up, freely, for a known return, and that is a true Oblation, however small. The house's discovery was that the force need not come back to the carter. It can be left against the thing bargained for, the crossing. So the chain holds it.

Everything else about the Painted Rivers follows. **The rate has to be posted**, because a bargain nobody understood banks nothing, and that is why every toll house is painted with the rate in pictures. **The entry has to be made**, in a sworn hand, on the day. **And the book has to be true.** A false entry rots the balance, and the chain parts at that link. Osbert Lambert, of the Hallenfeld cadet line, read carts against their manifests for eleven years and would not change a figure when the Tenrai beat him for it. They thought they were dealing with a stubborn man. They were dealing with a man who knew what holds up a bridge.

### The forms

| Form | Who | What it does | What it costs |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **The Rate** | Any sworn clerk, at a post | The rate called aloud to someone who has not paid. The cart will not roll for a moment and boots grow heavy | Nothing |
| **The Chain** | A clerk of a span | Closes the chain against what is crossing unpaid, and meets it with force at exact par from the balance | Whatever it stopped, in receipts |
| **The Receipt** | Anyone who has paid | The other half of the bargain. A chain will not stop you, and a bargee with a good one has floated through a closed river in a siege | A copper |
| **THE HALT** | The Mistress, or a tariff-master | Every crossing closed in an hour by beacon. No grain or message moves, and an army a week away starves on the road. Mostly logistics, and all the more frightening | The house's whole income stops as well |
| **SIX HOURS** | Courier captains | The Relay. Riders sworn to one road are tied by Transference, the living exchange, so a rested rider's legs turn up under a tired one's. A satchel moves faster than any rider who carries it | A courier's body. Nobody under the stair expects to be good at forty |
| **THE TOLL IS DUE** | Domain, Stage VIII at least | Everyone inside pays reserve, posted and at par, before a working will fire, the clerk included. The only Domain on the north's books with a printed tariff | Falsify it once and it comes apart on you |

**What it costs.** A chain's balance is spent the moment it is used and comes back a copper at a time, so a clerk who stops an army has just cost the next twenty years of clerks, and will be told about it.

**Its limit.** Exact par, never one grain more, only at that chain and only against what is crossing. A clerk two hundred yards from a span is a soft person with spectacles. Monsters do not read posted rates, and a sump-toad in a mill-race is a matter for a miller with a hoist.

**Who beats it.** The first and best answer, which astonishingly few armies have thought of, is to pay. The old terms give a host passage and the house will hand it a receipt. Go around: the fords are real, and so are Hayato Amagiri's indigo roads. Spend the chain down, as Rook very nearly did. Then there is **Free Water**: a town that stops paying stops feeding the chain, and the Toll-Breakers, whose class names run from Chain-Thief to Free Water itself, are beloved by bargemen and hanged by Hallenfeld for exactly this reason. The Northern Road Company has lately found another way, so that a ferryman pays the toll in the morning and the Company in the afternoon, and the chain itself seems to have begun to wonder which is the law.

### The money under it

Ilse Hallenfeld, Mistress of the counting-house, does not fight. When the Seat had to fall, she told a council that its wards were kept by hand by Guild keepers, paid quarterly through her counting-house on credit the Seat had been running for eleven years. She bought the wardmasters, and at the first bell every Tenrai ward on the rock died. That was only paper. She wrote the ratification of the seven lines in her own hand and would not let a clerk touch the pen. *A debt is a debt,* she said. It is the largest entry the counting-house has ever made, and at least one Measurewright has wondered what, under the Oblation's law, it now holds up.

## Physical Appearance and Dress

**Stature and coloring.** Round-shouldered, indoors, soft in the belly, sandy and pale, often in small square spectacles. Couriers are narrow, sun-dried people who look packed for a journey. **Marks.** Ink on the second finger of the right hand that no amount of beating has got off. **Voice.** Exact and unsurprised.

**The look at fifty paces.** Blue and ocher stripes, loud enough for the far bank, on men with boathooks, short spears and the weight of the law. Toll houses are the true gear of the house: a fresh coat means solvent, and a peeling one means you should ask to see the books. The high end is restraint: Ilse's dark coat lined in wolf, a ledger under her arm and ink on the gloves. The river-reeve on the lip of the falls wears a long robe and carries a sounding-staff, and no holder of that office has been seen to fall, since every morning the reeve drops a copper over the edge and enters it, so the falls have been paid. Couriers spend real money on one thing, boots, Grade D and Uncommon, resoled monthly and worn to bed. A poor cousin has a single stripe on his sleeve and a great deal of knowledge about the price of barley.

## Lineage Traits

### [Trait] The Sworn Hand — What the Entry Does

* An entry by a sworn hand on the day carries a small stamp and cannot be altered without showing. Retention and Fluency run absurdly high, and they read Concord, which almost no one else in the north can.

*They do not need to open your letter to know what it says from who sent it and how fast.*

### [Trait] Six Hours — The Road in the Legs

* In the courier line: Celerity, Economy and Reflex at a level that embarrasses swordsmen, spent only on arriving. They are the only people in the house of any use in a fight, having spent their lives getting through other people's with a satchel.

*They have no interest in winning. It has made them very hard to stop.*

### [Trait] A Debt Is a Debt — No Forgiveness on File

* What is entered stands. They cannot be flattered out of it, and they cannot be generous without a line for it.

*A lord who has forgotten what he owes can expect a very courteous reminder at a very poor moment.*

## Doctrine and Arts

Reluctantly. When there is real killing to be done the house pays for it, and the company is paid on the day, in full, in the most terrifying way possible, with a receipt. Brackets are fought on the chain bridges in midsummer, one fall into the river being a loss and two a very cold swim.

## Culture

**Language.** Quick river Common and Concord for the books. "Six hours" is the whole of the house's sense of humor.

**Rites.**

* **The First Toll.** A child's first copper, paid at the junction, entered in the child's own hand.
* **Calling the Rate.** Every morning from the lip of the falls, up to the castle.
* **The Fresh Coat.** Every third spring, with fights between Limners and Daubers considered part of the expense.
* **The Boots On.** Nobody takes them off in the room under the stair. There is a row of old boots on a shelf.
* **Closing the Page.** At a clerk's death a colleague rules off the last page and carries the balance forward.

**Marriage, feud and debt.** They marry other counting-houses, and the Hallenfeld Lamberts, cadets of the Lamberts of Sallowgate, sit among them. Thornwall is the long war, and the rate on logs is negotiated each year as a continuation of it. Ashmore has the grain and they have the barges. They had a tariff-master in a Moto cell for keeping honest books, and the house wrote that down.

**What the commons get.** A rate, on the wall, in pictures, that applies to them exactly as to a duke, and a receipt that means something, which is rarer than you may think. Signal-post women get a hut, a basket and a wage. And there is the Toll Bun, in the shape of a fist.

## Limitations and Soul-Costs

### [Curse] Exact Par — Never One Grain More

* What is in the book is what there is, and every clerk knows the figure. A chain spent is a generation's work gone.

*Griet Hoogstraat's penny a week was docked, some years later, on review.*

### [Curse] The Honest Book — A Lie Parts the Link

* They cannot falsify a figure to save a life, a child's included, and it leaves them unable to tell a comforting story.

*They were told that the Tenrai had broken Osbert Lambert. The house looked at the entries he had left, and they knew better.*

### [Curse] The Entry at the Bottom of the Stair — Owing

* Below the Fall Cellar, down a flooded stair behind the middle of the falls, is the Drowned Toll: a toll house far older than the house, and a Well beneath it where Mourning Pike grow as long as a barge. A clerk entered its rate as owing long ago, and its chain is the only one in the realm the house has never tried to collect on.
* Nobody will say which side of the bargain the house is on. Every clerk has done the sums on what a three-hundred-year debt comes to at par.

*Every other chain in the north is holding something back. A careful reader will ask what this one is keeping in.*

## How They Stand Against the Three Paths

**Body.** A hitter who comes to a chain finds out the balance, and one who walks around it finds a soft person with a ledger, kills him, and pays for it in late grain. **Spirit.** A true Spirit fighter can read a clerk's balance off his Core and know exactly how much bluff he has. **Fate.** A Weaver can move a barge or a flood. Hallenfeld regards such people as extremely late with their paperwork, and outlasts them on the principle that a pull costs what it costs and a toll comes in every day.

## Final Definition

House Hallenfeld is the most boring great power in the north and it would like that noted. They took the smallest honest thing a person can do, paying what was asked and getting what was promised, and wrote it down every time for three hundred years, until it could stop nine hundred horses. When one of them looks up from the page and tells you the rate, believe her, and find your purse.

*"Six hours. I'll want that noted."*

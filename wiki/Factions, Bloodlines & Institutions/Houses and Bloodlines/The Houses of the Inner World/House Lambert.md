In the spring of 668 a duelist named Odo Vance, the Bright Champion of three kings in the Midmarch, rode up to the tariff-hall at Sallowgate in a coat of yellow silk and a very bad temper, because he had been asked to declare his horse. The clerk at the window was Perrin Lambert, sixty-one, a soft man with ink on his cuff, and he asked again. Vance was Stage IX, and the air around him had already gone tight enough to make the water clock shiver in its housing. Half the town came running to see the stupid thing about to happen. Vance drew and cut at him across the counter, and Perrin Lambert was standing a little to the left of where the sword went. He had not hurried. Vance cut again, and the clerk was somewhere else again, a hand's width, on his flat feet, with his pen still in his fingers, saying in a carrying voice, "That is two, sir. Assault upon an officer of the bench is two silver the attempt." Vance was fast enough that the people at the back could only see the shine of it. The clerk had no coat and no aura, and the assay rolls had him as a dead Crystal, thirty years filed. He went through seven more like a man finding his way across a familiar room in the dark, and at nine he stepped forward, because the shoulder had told him where the elbow would be, and rapped the Bright Champion on the wrist nerve with an oak ruler. The sword hit the flags. "Eleven silver, and the horse," said Perrin Lambert, and it was then that the mule behind him, frightened out of its wits by all of this, kicked out and broke his hip in two places. He had not seen it coming at all. They say he lay on the cobbles and wrote it down.

Vance paid. The whole South has told that story for fifty years, and most people tell it for the ruler. The Lamberts tell it for the mule, and for the other thing they know about it: Vance was angry, across a counter, using a tenth of what he had, and every cut said where it was going. Had he stopped meaning it for one breath, or spent one working, they would have buried the clerk that afternoon.

The Holy Sea weighs what people swear, on a stone that wets under a true oath, and an honest fool leaves lovely footprints. Somebody has to count what the oath is about. For as long as the Sea has had courts there has been a Lambert at the bench beside the cleric: tariffs at the gate, grain in the store, witnesses in the dock, the days between a lord's promise and a lord's delivery. They are the only people in the Sea allowed a sword by right, and they hold Sallowgate and the land marches. Their levies are small, drilled, and paid on the day. Their officers tell a general his numbers before battle, in front of his captains, and do not soften them. What they hold that everyone needs is a number that is right. What they want is for the count to come out true and for the Sea's courts to be heard north of the border, and in a world full of people who would pay for a different number, that is a much stranger ambition than it sounds.

## Origin of the House

The list begins with a woman. When two kings fought the Battle of Sorrel Water on a table at the top of the Stair, a tally-wife named **Perrine Lambert** was hired to keep the sutlers' account, and the house's whole claim to antiquity is that she sat through it with her thumb moving and told the first king's steward, before the soup, how many of his men would be alive at evening. She was off by none. The steward had her whipped, and then, the bench being what it was, had her hired. Her daughters kept the marches' grain, and her grandsons were at the gate. The Palatines came and went, and by the time the present line of them could count there was already a Lambert standing at each one's elbow who had counted first. Sallowgate came with the job, the land marches with Sallowgate, and the sword because a holy see needs somebody who may carry one.

Cadet branches went north on the Sea's business and stayed: the Hallenfeld Lamberts in the north, a steward whose thumb moved at the elbow of a northern king, clerks in every chapter house, and a diocesan administrator named Charles Lambert, forty-one and heavy in the stomach, whom a Primate of the Church had read for twenty-six years without noticing a thing, until the morning he watched that man step out of the way of a very great polearm four times running, soaked to the thigh, and then put a boot through the knee behind it.

## The House on the Crystal

A Soul Crystal is the soul itself, in three layers: the **Aether Shell**, where Aether (mana) comes in and force goes out; the **Essence Core**, where it becomes Essence (spiritual energy); and the **Attraction Layer**, a soul's own share of Fate. A rung on each layer's ladder measures its reach. Most Lamberts never see a rung.

| Layer | Plane | The Lambert form |
|---|---|---|
| **Aether Shell** | Physical | Sealed in a great many, for life. In a woken Lambert, thin and clean and very late |
| **Essence Core** | Soul | Every unwoken soul holds a trickle of Essence it cannot spend. A Lambert's goes wholly into the count, and that is why nothing else ever grows there |
| **Attraction Layer** | Fate | Nothing to report on the ladder. A lifetime's kept oath shows as a very hard Oath Sub-Stat, and sometimes as Thread I |

**Path.** A Path is the layer a soul leans on, recognized from how it lives. An unwoken soul has a Path too, though no instrument will tell it so, and the coil, reading only output, gets silence. The house's duelists tend to the Body Path, its benchmen to a long slow Spirit road, and a Lambert who walks either at all is unusual.

**No grip, no eye.** The family has neither. Their ladders open at Glory like anyone's, if they open, and on the coil they read as a very dull family. A Lambert's window has a Level, a health bar and a great many clerical skills at an alarming Grade.

| Stage or standing | Typical Lambert |

## The Computus

*Computus* is the Court's Latin for a reckoning, the same word its clerics use for working out the feast days. On the Stair they call it being early. In the taverns, they say a man has *got a Lambert in him* when he ducks before the bottle leaves the hand.

**What it is.** A read with no faculty behind it. Every Lambert child starts counting at about three, and something in the blood keeps the running total for the family. Shoulders, hips, hands, breaths, and which way the weight went. It comes up as a forecast, in the body, ahead of the blow.

**How it works, in plain words.** No blow starts in the hand. A shoulder commits first, a heel takes weight, a breath stops, and these come in an order that a body cannot hide, because it has to obey it. If you had watched ten thousand cuts you could say, with the first flicker, which of the ten thousand this one was. A good old master does that, and calls it experience. Scholars call it a forecast from priors: you lay what you see against what you have already counted, and the likeliest continuation is simply where to stand. The Lamberts are born with several generations of priors already filed. The trickle of Essence in the sealed Core has nothing else to do, and it never stops doing this. So the answer starts before the delivery it answers, by a margin so small that a spectator calls it luck and a practitioner calls it impossible. Since it comes from no working, there is nothing to sense. It has no glow, no color and no signature, and no one has ever detected it on purpose.

**The forms.**

| Form | Court word | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **The Thumb** | *Pollex* | The first stage, and it never leaves. The right thumb moving on the pad of the index finger, counting whatever there is. In the family this is breathing |
| **The Column** | *Columna* | One opponent, read through. The whole body starts to move early against one will |
| **The Sum** | *Summa* | A field, as far as the eye can go: men, horses, remounts, days of grain. A false return feels wrong in the hand like a cased weight. A woken master reads Forecast and Analysis a Grade high while he counts |
| **The Second Asking** | *Iterum* | The house's interrogation. Asked twice, with the weight of the list behind it, a man who lies the second time knows precisely what he is doing |

**What it costs.** Nothing, in reserve. It is the only art in the South that charges no Essence, and the bill arrives elsewhere.

It never turns off. They count stairs, wedding guests and how many breaths the priest took. They cannot be surprised at a party, cannot quite stop knowing how long the people they love have left at that rate of drinking, and in the family this is not considered interesting. It eats the soul's first room. A great many Lamberts never wake because the Core is busy, and what would have become a Crystal's first stirring has gone to the sum since before they could walk. The house has known this for centuries. It teaches its children to count anyway.

It also cannot be lied to, least of all by its owner. Priors are only worth what went into them. A Lambert who enters a false number has spoiled the column, and the error spreads. His read drifts out of true by a hair, then a finger, and one day a blow is not where he knew it would be. **That is the real reason for the Struck List.** They will tell you it is honor, and it is honor. It is also that the rest of the family's count runs through the same bench, and nobody wants to stand next to that man at the gate.

**Its limit.** It reads what has a will behind it and has been done before. A falling wall, a beast in a panic and a working that runs by itself give it nothing. It knows what a body is about to do, and that has never made anyone faster. Against a blow it can see coming and cannot get out of the way of, it tells a Lambert the hour. A clerk with no coat takes a hit like a clerk, and Charles Lambert had six weeks of bending as a project after one landed. It reaches as far as the eye and the reports go, and a field unseen is a hole in the sum.

**Who beats it.** Do something no one has done. Feint with the whole body so the intent is false, then strike with no thought at all, which is much easier to say. Send the mule. Stand out of sight. Raise your Stage until knowing does him no good. Or, if you must deal with one at a bench, give him a true number, which beats him every time, and is the one thing he has been trying to get you to do.

## Physical Appearance and Dress

They run tall in the Sallowgate line and every other size elsewhere, because they have married the bench for centuries with no thought for looks. There is some gold in the hair of the main branch. What marks them is a stillness in the face, and the thumb, and something that happens in the eyes when you walk into a room, where they check over your shoulder for how many came with you. Voices are courteous and unhurried, with a short true number in the middle of a long sentence. They do not say "about." They stand with their open hands kept low, and on wet ground they put their weight on the outside edge of the foot. You would not look twice, and the house has spent a long time making sure of that.

White and ink-black are the house colors, a balance on the field. Benchmen wear plain white wool with an inked cuff and an iron ring cut with the balance. The Warlord's office is the exception, and on purpose: the Bear Harness, gilded plate with a rearing Sallowgate bear in relief, Very Rare, under a scarlet cloak that makes him the easiest target on the field so his men can count him. Ask its price and he will give it to you to the copper, and the leaf by the ounce. The house sword is gray and plain, and every child at Sallowgate learns on a wooden copy of its balance. A poor cousin has a wooden tally on a string and a clerk's coat turned twice. He will know what your boots cost.

## Lineage Traits

### [Trait] One Before — The Inherited Margin

The Computus in the body, and it shows on the sheet as an Inherited Trait. A woken Lambert with this and the Reflex of his Stage is a horrible thing to fence.

- Against a blow with a will behind it, Reflex reads a Grade high in anyone woken, and in the unwoken the blade is merely elsewhere.
- Costs nothing and cannot be sensed.
- Slower against whatever has no intent in it.

*He is not fast. Nobody who has lost to one has ever been able to accept that.*

### [Trait] Furniture — The Blind Spot in Every Register

- The coil, the Refinement Spectrum and every other instrument that reads output report an unwoken Lambert as nothing at all.
- Pressure works on them as on any commoner, and they have had years to practice looking as if it did not.
- A woken Lambert gives this up on the morning he wakes. There is a real argument in the family about whether it is worth it.

*Fifty-three assessments of one man, by the Accord's own file, and the fifty-third agreed with the first.*

### [Trait] Open Hands — The Polearm Drill

- A taught habit that the Computus makes frightening: hands low and open to feel a shaft before the eye confirms it, hips shifting a degree or two every third of a second to keep a big enemy strung along one axis.
- Learned on wet decks and in tariff-halls, and taught to no one who would be expected to carry anything.

*The oldest answer to a long weapon, and it needs nothing but nerve and a lifetime's arithmetic.*

## Doctrine and Arts

**The answer.** Lambert fencing is a counter from first to last, and it needs to be asked. Nobody at Sallowgate is taught to attack. In the field they read the numbers aloud before the first blow, and in the Court's Latin when they feel like being unpleasant.

| Move | Tongue | Meaning | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **La Réponse!** | Sallowgate French | "The Answer!" | The Sallowgate Answer, taught to every child. The blade turns in the hand before yours leaves its line, takes it on the flat, and rides down into the wrist or throat |
| **Encore une fois** | | "One more time" | The Second Asking, in a duel. He gives you exactly the opening you offered before, and you are welcome to believe it |
| **Mains ouvertes** | | "Open hands" | The unarmed answer to anything long, four evasions and then the knee |

## Culture

They speak Common with Sallowgate French in the mouth, and Latin at Court, where each one is entered under a name he did not ask for.

- **The Reading of the List.** Once a year in the Sallowgate hall, the Struck List is read in full, name, figure and date. It is a real object, and anyone can ask to see it. The family stands. The list includes a man's cousin, whom he loved, and two hundred sacks of grain.
- **The Father's Count.** A Lambert who is dying gives the last figure he has. Someone is expected to be there to take it. A Warlord of the house died on the Grey Plain with the count of his own dead still in his mouth, and his son got there in time.
- **The Family Count.** One night each winter the house counts itself. The figure is entered in a book that has never left Sallowgate, and no outsider has seen it, a Palatine included. People have tried.
- **The Bench Marriage.** At his own wedding, a Lambert asks the other family how many of them there are. It is on the Stair's list of jokes because it is in the service.

**Who they marry, fight and owe.** They marry clerks, officers and, once, the Yukari: Lord Naiser is half Sallowgate, and the marriage is the house's one great political act. The Palatine they serve and do not flatter, and they have spent centuries quarreling with the clerics on their own benches. They audit Marrick companies and match tariff books with the Ryu. Sallowgate's commoners get the rarest thing in the South, which is pay on the day, a true weight at the market and a tariff that does not change for your cousin. Men queue for their levies. Those who cheat them find the water clock very accurate.

## Limitations and Soul-Costs

### [Curse] The Unwaking — The Count Takes the First Room

Most of the family will never have a coat, a Stage, or an aura, and they know why. Each generation spends itself on the sum its children will need. The woken who do come are late, with thin ladders, and the house argues every time about what they cost.

### [Curse] The Spoiled Column — A Lie Costs the Liar First

A false count puts the Lambert's own read out of true. It does not matter why he lied. The Computus judges the number and never the reason, and the man who shorted two hundred sacks to feed a village through a bad winter was as wrong in the shoulder afterward as any thief. He paid the same price, and so did the cousin who struck him, who bought that village's grain the same week and carries a fourth notch on his pommel.

### [Curse] The Mule — What Has No Will

They do not see the runaway cart, the falling lintel, or the frightened horse. A surprising number of Lamberts die of accidents, and their funerals have a certain tone.

## How They Stand Against the Three Paths

**Body.** Their favored meeting, as long as the enemy has a will and a Stage in reach. A big Stage gap breaks it by plain speed, and one blow lands on a body with nothing around it.

**Spirit.** Their deepest worry. A working that goes past the Shell does not move like a shoulder, and it has little to read.

**Fate.** They read it badly and hate it. A guard that slips has no shoulder in it. But a Fate fighter who has moved the odds has still not moved the true number, and Lamberts are unusually hard to deceive about what has actually happened. Report the strap that gave, and they will have the next strap checked.

## Final Definition

House Lambert is a family of frightening clerks, who stand behind the whole South's sworn word with a thumb moving against one finger. They hold a gate and the only true number in any room, and they have paid for it with their own waking. You may think you have never met one. You have certainly been counted by one. When a Lambert tells you how many of you there are, you should thank him, and then you should look behind you, because he has already allowed for the mule.

*Count True, and keep the list.*

He called the Ryu, who sent a superb letter. It is the best-known line of correspondence in the South, and you can buy it on a tea towel at Geumpo. On the first day of autumn in 602, while the rest of the Empire was failing to answer the Emperor Rokju from the square at Sum-gol, a Ryu courier in sea-green silk came up the Balcony stair with a lacquer tube, and read it aloud, as custom required. It took about as long as a good meal. It opens by recalling, with tears, the first voyage of the Vermilion fleet. It lists every ship, by name and tonnage, as a thing belonging absolutely to the Throne. It expresses a grief that no words could equal at the thought of the Emperor's present difficulties. It gives the state of the tides, which were unfavorable, and it says of the Thousand Moorings that they *remain, as they have always remained, in the hand of Your Majesty, which is at present at some distance.* Rokju Mahuo, who had just lost an empire and a stag, is said to have listened to the end with an expression of real gratitude, and to have asked whether the fleet would be coming, and it was only when the courier bowed and started down the stair that anyone at all noticed that the letter had not said.

Nobody has ever found a sentence in it that says no. Scholars have tried for a hundred years. The Ryu have sent another each autumn since, and the coast copies them out in a fair hand for the pleasure of the prose. The ships, the harbor dues and the salt fleet all stayed at Haeseong, with no one left to send the revenue to. The Ryu left the Mahuo's wealth exactly where it sat, and simply stopped forwarding it, and the South has never settled whether that is theft. Those are the family's own words, more or less, and they would like it noted that the Mahuo have never asked.

Everything that comes into the Inner World by water comes through this house, by their own account, and the account is close to the truth. They hold the harbor dues, the salt tolls and wreck-right on the whole coast. What they want is Sum-gol left empty, the salt road open, and the harbor dues uncontested. Admiral-Lord Ryu Ha-eun sat at the Cedar Enclave and heard Lord Naiser promise to restore the Mahuo royal line, and smiled, and asked a very good question about harbor dues. It is worth knowing why it was such a good question, and that is what the rest of this page is for.

## Origin of the House

The Mahou were valley people, and when they came down to the sea they needed somebody who knew which way it went. The family that told them were pilots and salt-carriers at the mouth of the Moorings, Ryu by name, and about the year 300 the first Emperor gave a Ryu Bo-gyeong the fleet he had just won, on terms that have been the whole of the house's law ever since. It reads, in the Admiralty's oldest book, that the ships are held for the Throne, that the Admiral shall keep them and use them as his own, and that on the Throne's asking they shall be rendered, *with the increase*. It was an ordinary form of words for a steward, and by the time anyone read it carefully the fleet had been ten generations in one family.

The Ryu did not leave at the Calling. They keep the Dynasty's old governor's palace on the next rise from their own fortress, swept and aired, with nobody in it. The counting-rooms keep the entire sum in a separate set of books under the heading *held for the Throne*, correct to the copper and growing, and the last small remittance, which paid the Post's eastern stations, stopped in the summer. They will show you the heading.

## The House on the Crystal

A Soul Crystal is the soul itself, in three layers: the **Aether Shell**, where Aether (mana) comes in and force goes out; the **Essence Core**, where it is refined into Essence (spiritual energy); and the **Attraction Layer**, a soul's own share of Fate.

**Path.** They lean to the Body Path: sailors, boarders and fighting-platform officers, with the Admiralty's clerks taking a slow second road on the Spirit side for the books. A Ryu who talks about Fate is usually selling something.

**The grip.** A small one, by long habit and the sea: **Aether** opens early. A Ryu child in the fleet reads Muridic, the first rung of the Shell's ladder, at Flourishing, Stage IV, a couple of Stages before anyone else's first reading at Glory. The Stage ceilings stand exactly as they do for everyone. The house has no eye, and Thread sits at Ø. Most Ryu climb the Aether ladder and stop at Harmonic, and that serves very well.

## The Holding — 보관 · Bogwan

**What it is.** The family's one great idea, and it works at every scale from a slap to a treasury. What comes in and is not claimed may be held. The holder gets the use of it while it is held. Then the tide turns and it goes out again, and it goes out double.

**How it works, in plain words.** Anybody can stop a blow, and it hurts. A Ryu learns on a heaving deck not to stop it. The force of a hit comes into the Shell and, instead of going to break a rib, is sent round: spread through the coat of reinforcement as a long slow swell, the way a shock goes into a bay and starts it rocking. Every bay has a natural rhythm, and scholars say that is why the tide runs so high in some narrow inlets: the water is sloshing at its own time, and each push from the sea comes exactly when it will add to the slosh. A held blow rocks in the Shell at the holder's own rhythm, a few breaths long in a young officer and much longer in an old one. She feels it as a tide in the chest. The copper at her throat, which every Ryu wears, sweats green along the edges where the Essence is running. It is the fight's best tell, and the old ones are proud of their verdigris.

On its turn, the swell goes out, through a fist, a boathook or the deck, and that is the part the Admiralty teaches last. A push in time with a swing adds to the swing. She adds her own Essence at the top of the swell and the whole thing comes out at twice what came in. **The tide pays twice, and the second payment comes out of her reserve.** Nothing is free. All she has bought is a blow she did not have to take now, and one that lands at the moment she picks, inside the limits of the tide.

**The forms.**

| Form | Korean | Meaning | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ripple** | 잔물결 · Janmulgyeol | "Small waves" | A single light blow held for a breath and paid back. Taught with a slap, at twelve |
| **Flood** | 밀물 · Milmul | "Flood tide" | One real strike, held through two or three breaths. Everyone in a boarding party can do this one |
| **Neap** | 조금 · Jogeum | "Neap tide" | A holding let out low and slow over minutes instead of all at once, at half the pay. It saves a life, and costs the fight |
| **Spring Tide** | 사리 · Sari | "Spring tide" | Several blows stacked on one swell and paid in one. The whole account comes due at once, and the holder had better have the reserve |
| **Tidal Wave** | 해일 · Haeil | "Tidal wave" | At the Luminous rung: not a blow but a ship's way, a wave, the weight of a ramming junk, held by an Admiral standing at the bow with a line of officers coat to coat behind her |

**The great form.** It works the same way on paper, and this is the part scholars argue about. Under Ryu law a thing that has come to them and has not been asked for may be entered as held, and as long as it is acknowledged every year in proper form, it is lawfully held and the holder has the use of it. The superb letter is the acknowledgment. It has to be sent, and it has to be true, and every Ryu word in it is a small vow, a line on the signer's own window (the soul's private report of its numbers). Every year the signer swears that all of this is the Throne's. And by the oldest clause in the Admiralty's first book, when the true owner comes and asks in person, it is rendered on that day, with the increase. The clerks have always read that as double.

So every fleet in those harbors is sitting on the largest unpaid account in the South. The money that should have gone to Sum-gol has mostly become ships. Now you see why the harbor dues were a very good question, and why a house that would sooner lose an arm than raise an army has been watching a young man in an olive coat with such close attention.

**What it costs.** A held account has to be paid. If the reserve is not there when the swell comes round, she defaults, and a default goes out through her: the whole sum, as it would have been, plus what it has gathered, inside the chest. It looks like a fighter who was winning falling over with her nose and ears running. Hold too much too often and the Shell stops settling. Old admirals shake on the turn of the tide, twice a day, and their sons take the wheel.

**Its limit.** It holds force and nothing else. It works on a blow, a wave or a ram. A slow crush has no impulse to send round, a grapple gives nothing to swing, and a working that goes past the Shell does not touch it. It has to be paid inside its tide, and she can only keep so much rocking at once. Below Spring Tide, one account is all most people can run. Above a certain blow it simply fails, and you have hit a sailor very hard.

**Who beats it.** Stop hitting her, as the best of the Ryu's enemies learn to do, and the swell comes round with nowhere useful to go, and she has spent the second payment on the air. Grapple. Use a Spirit art. Or do what the Bad Weather school of the outer Moorings teaches its children: **ask.** A blow stamped with Essence is still yours, and it knows your voice. Hit a Ryu, and shout for it back, *dollyeojwo!*, and the account falls due on the spot, out of her rhythm, at a bad moment. It pays you, certainly, but it pays her too, and on the wrong beat half of it stays inside. You have to survive being paid. Plenty of people have thought it over and decided to shout anyway.

## Physical Appearance and Dress

Harbor people, thick in the leg and easy in the knee, sunburned to the color of old teak, with salt-cracked hands and a roll to the walk they cannot quite lose on a paved road. Ha-eun is fifty-three, stout and merry, at sea since she was eleven, and the fleet would follow her onto the Sawbacks. They have loud, rolling voices, made to go down the length of a ship in wind, and they laugh with the whole chest. It is said a Haeseong child cannot sleep in the dark.

Sea-green and copper are the colors. Officers wear green silk on the quay and copper-scaled harness on the platform, each scale on its own rivet so it can move with the swell. Copper at the throat is the family's mark, a plain hammered collar in the poor branches and a worked gorget in the rich. It is left to go green on purpose. A clean bright collar means a new officer. A collar that is sea-green to the clasp means someone has held a great deal and paid it all, and that is the look at fifty paces, and it is the flex. A poor cousin has a loop of copper wire at the neck, tarry cotton and a cook's temper, and his wire is green too, since he works harder than you do.

## Lineage Traits

### [Trait] Sea Legs — The Shock Runs Through

- Taught, and early. Absorption and Economy run high: the deck moves and the Ryu stays with it.
- A Ryu knocked sprawling is a Ryu whose feet are in the right place when she gets up.
- Ashore on flat stone they lose a small part of it, and they will complain about the ground.

*Their first duel is on a barge. So is their last.*

### [Trait] Green at the Throat — A Readable Account

- A held blow shows in the collar, in the breath, and in how she stands.
- Other Ryu can read each other's accounts across a deck, and call out who has what, and when it falls due. A boarding party is a running argument in numbers.
- So can anyone who knows the signs, which is why so many of them have learned to bluff.

*A sharp opponent counts the green. A sharper one counts how much she wants you to.*

### [Trait] The Superb Letter — Courtesy as a Weapon

- Fluency and Command, trained on paper. Every Ryu child can write a letter of perfect obedience that gives nothing at all, and it holds up in court.
- It is a skill and not a working, and it has moved more silver than any fleet.

*"With the greatest respect" is the most dangerous phrase on the coast. The Tide Court has ruled on it twice.*

## Doctrine and Arts

**Windward, then rowers.** At sea, a Ryu line takes the wind, lets the current do the work and shoots the rowers first. Ashore, the salt road is held by toll-forts every thirty miles and they hire Marrick for anything larger, and they have never fought a land battle they could avoid by raising a price. Hand to hand, the doctrine is to invite a hit you can afford and then be standing in the right place at the turn.

## Culture

They speak the coast's Korean-registered tongue, with a family name first and given names of two parts. Harbor-mothers run the sampans, and thousands live on the water, paying mooring-fee by the lunar month.

- **The First Letter.** At twelve, a Ryu child writes a letter of apology to someone in the family for a thing they do not intend to give back. It is read at table and scored.
- **The Turn.** At each of the day's two tides the books close, in every house and on every ship, and the family says what is held and what is owing. Sailors do it in a sentence. Children do it with sweets.
- **Lantern Night.** That is every night, since 671, when Deok-sun the net-widow hung a paper lantern on a boathook. The Ryu fined the town for the use of the fishing line, and the town still pays the fine, in mackerel, every year on the day. Both sides enjoy it.
- **The Drowned Shrine.** Each village has one. A crew's wages left on the book are entered as held, and paid to the widow at double if she comes to the counting-room and asks in person. The Admiralty is quite strict about the in person. Widows come down on the tide in their hundreds, in good clothes, with a sister to hold the baby.

**Who they marry, fight and owe.** They marry captains, and now and then a Geumpo pilot, when the two guilds can spare a truce from their two-hundredth year of cold war. They dispute the salt toll with the Yan and everything else with Rimward, whose Lambert tariff books are the only ones in the South that match theirs. They owe the Throne. Sailors get a share of the voyage, a place on a good ship and a seat at the Tide Court, with a harbor-mother beside the bench to say who is lying, and what they get in the end is what they can come and ask for.

## Limitations and Soul-Costs

### [Curse] The Asking — Everything Held Can Be Called

The Holding is worthless if the owner never comes, and it is ruinous if the owner does. Anyone with a true claim can walk in, in person, and ask. In a fight that takes a shout, and in the counting-rooms a very long walk up the Long Mole, in olive. Every year the sum grows, and so does the double of it. No Ryu has ever been sure what happens to a fleet if the house defaults.

### [Curse] Default — The Whole Sum, Inside

A swell with no second payment behind it goes out through the holder. Fighters die this way in a good fight they were winning, in front of their crew, and the crew reads the collar afterward and can say to the copper what she was short.

### [Curse] Nothing Freely Given — A Family That Cannot Make a Gift

In every word of a Ryu's life, something is held and something is owed, and they are very bad at the other thing. Love letters from this coast are the most beautiful in the South, and no one receiving one has ever been sure of their position.

## How They Stand Against the Three Paths

**Body.** Their ground, as long as the enemy keeps hitting. A good Body fighter works this out in about three exchanges and starts grappling instead, and a Ryu in a clinch on dry land has a difficult afternoon.

**Spirit.** Their terror. A working that goes past the Shell finds nothing there to send round, and a Spirit fighter can stand at range and simply wait. They answer with crossbows, and by making sure they are on a ship.

**Fate.** It moves what a tide depends on: a slipped footing on the turn, a late breath. And a sufficiently bad-tempered Fate user can look at that first book, with that clause in it, and ask himself what holds it up.

## Final Definition

House Ryu is the most loyal family in the South, and they will show you the paperwork. They have kept an emperor's ships in perfect order for a century after the emperor, wearing the proof green at their throats, and every autumn they write to say so. The Mahuo have never asked. Everyone on the coast knows what they would be owed if they did, and the lanterns go up every night regardless, across the water, in a city that cannot sleep in the dark.

*The tide pays twice. It pays whoever is standing there.*

# The Yan Hall

## Overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Type** | Lineage hall in the old southern style · horse-masters of the Dynasty of Sum-gol · the Thousand Bloodlines |
| **Origin** | The Jade Plains, which the north's maps call the Grey Plain |

| **Seat** | Qingyuan |
| **Anchors** | The Nine Rows of tablets · the tall gray horse · the Stud Book |

The Duke of Tarn owed the Yan Hall for eleven hundred remounts. He had owed them for four years, through two very good letters, and in the summer of 677, at Cold Harrow in the Midmarch, he put every one of those horses into a single charge with his whole household on top of them, plate and plumes and painted lances, the grandest thing anybody on that field had ever seen, the gray backs rolling down the slope like a river going over a weir. Up on a knoll well out of bowshot sat a very small old woman in a jade sash, in a folding chair, with a boy holding a parasol over her, a ledger on her knees and a speaking-horn of lacquered ox-hide across the ledger. She was Elder Yan Meilan of the third row. She had come to see about the balance. She waited until the charge was committed, because she had a sense of occasion, and then she lifted the horn and read out one name. It was a stallion called Iron Rain who had been dead for nineteen years, and she gave his sire and his dam, and the sire and dam of each of those, in the bored clear voice of someone who has read a great many things aloud.

Eleven hundred gray horses, every one of them a grandchild of Iron Rain, turned their heads. They turned the rest of themselves a moment after. The charge wheeled in its own length, at the gallop, in perfect order, as lovely a piece of horsemanship as has ever been done, and went away to the south with the Duke's entire household still on board, sawing at the reins and bellowing, past the enemy's front rank, who stood up and cheered. They were still going at sunset. It is three weeks to Qingyuan at an easy pace, and they arrived in eighteen days, in good condition, most of them with a knight. The Hall stabled the knights at the ordinary rate. The Duke's heirs paid in the autumn, for horses, feed and knights, and the other side bought four hundred grays the next spring, with cash, and read the terms very carefully.

The Yan sell to everyone. The whole Inner World rides out of their pastures, and the Yan take orders, in both senses, and never a side. The Hall sold to all of them, and every one of those horses is in the book. What they hold that everyone needs is the only animal in the world a hand higher than anything the north can raise, dappled like river stones, and the written record of who has been buying how many. What they want is for every army to need horses, and for none of them to win too quickly.

## Origin of the House

The first tablet in the first row belongs to a man named Yan, who has no other name on it. The Hall's account is that he was a bankrupt salt-carter on the old plain before there were any empires on it to pay a toll to, that a spring blizzard caught him on the open grass with a dying mare and no idea which way anything was, and that he did the sensible thing and followed her. She walked for three days with her head low. She brought him to the cold spring at what is now Yuquan, where she had been foaled, and died there. He sat down beside her and, having nothing else, wrote her name on a piece of harness in charcoal, with her sire and her dam so far as he knew them, because he thought somebody ought to. *Old Horse Knows the Road* is the first line of every Yan child's first lesson, and the piece of harness is still in the Great Hall, in a jade box, and no two scholars agree on what the charcoal says.

His sons bred from her filly and their sons kept a book. The Hall paid the empire in grays for three centuries, recited its way through the last Calling of 602 so that no scholar can say if it answered, and still keeps the Emperor's stall swept at Qingyuan, entered every year as *held*.

## The House on the Crystal

The Hall is a clan ruled by its elders and its ancestral tablets, and a person enters it by birth, marriage, adoption or a good seat on a stolen horse. Its soul is therefore nothing like a bloodline's. A Soul Crystal is the soul itself, in three layers: the **Aether Shell**, where Aether (mana) comes in and force goes out; the **Essence Core**, where it is refined into Essence (spiritual energy); and the **Attraction Layer**, a soul's own share of Fate.

| Layer | Plane | The Yan form |
|---|---|---|
| **Aether Shell** | Physical | Ordinary at birth, and good by habit. The riders are clean and light, and they feel a horse through the seat |
| **Essence Core** | Soul | Trained on recitation. The Elders of Ledgers and Tablets read high here, and the name-reading lands through it |
| **Attraction Layer** | Fate | Nothing out of the way. A book entry is a small kept vow, and it spends the soul's own share and pulls no thread at all |

**Path.** Riders tend to the Body Path, with the arrow as their one concession to distance. The elders of the Ledgers tend to the Spirit Path. Nobody reaches anything like Fate's deep end on this plain, and a Yan will tell you that a true thing written down has never needed it.

**No eye, no grip, in the people.** The blood in the Hall's name of the Thousand Bloodlines is horse blood. A Yan's ladders open at Glory, Stage VI, like anyone's, and the Thread ladder stays at Ø for life. The lean is taught, in the saddle. The breed carries the rest.

| Rung and Stage | Typical Yan |
|---|---|
| Unwoken | Outer cousins, herders and a great many grooms, all of whom can recite. Their read horses still turn their heads |
| IV to VI | Stud archers, core disciples of a stud, first coats and first Releases on horseback |
| VII to IX · Aether II to III, or Essence II | The superb few: an elder of the lower rows, and captains who collect debts |
| X and up · Essence III or better | The upper rows. The Hall-Master, ninety, still rides every morning |

## The Stud Book — 馬冊 · Mǎcè

**What it is.** A set of ledgers older than the Holy Sea's, and a method. Sire, dam and grandsire for every animal the Yan have ever sold, with the name of the man who bought it. A gray of the Yan breed knows its own line, and answers to it from any distance a voice can carry, as you would answer if someone across a crowded market began saying your mother's name, and your grandmother's, and the name of the street you grew up on. A horse that hears this turns round. Given its head, it goes home.

**How it works, in plain words.** Every soul has its three layers, a beast's included, with the Shell sealed until something cracks it. A Yan foal is read its line morning and evening, by the same groom, from the hour it stands, at the same tone, for three years. It drinks at Yuquan's cold spring in those years, a small worked Core (a site where a Wellspring pools) that the Elder of Tablets keeps and will describe only as water. By the time it is sold, those words are laid down in its Core as deep as hunger. Scholars read it through the Wellspring Phreatis, memory as groundwater, which comes back to the well decades after it fell as rain. The horse does not obey anything. It remembers, all at once, from the bottom up, where it is from, and everything it has ever loved is in that direction. A Yan elder reading well is only telling it the truth in the right order. The working lands on a meaning, so it reads the reader's Essence rung, and there is no pull in it.

Because grandchildren carry the grandsire's name in their own line, you can read a whole get at once. That is why the third name is in the book.

**The forms.**

| Form | Tongue | Who or what it reaches |
|---|---|---|
| **Calling the Horse** | 呼馬 · Hū Mǎ | One horse, by its whole line, within earshot. Any core disciple can do it. It will walk through a market fence, as Rain-on-Slate did, with a professional thief still on it |
| **Calling the Get** | 呼系 · Hū Xì | A dead sire's name, and every living descendant who hears it. An elder of the upper rows, a good voice, a good horn, and a truly terrible afternoon for somebody |
| **Calling the Debt** | 呼債 · Hū Zhài | A buyer signs the ledger, and a signature is a small vow, a line on his own window (the soul's private report of its numbers). Read aloud in his hearing, the line comes up in front of his eyes, with the balance, and stays until he has looked at it. It compels nothing. Lords have paid a great deal to avoid hearing it in front of guests |
| **Calling the Cousin** | 呼族 · Hū Zú | Anyone with a tablet. Read your whole line, by your rung and your forebears, and you will feel the grass under your feet from four hundred miles off. It is a homesickness like vertigo, and it can be refused, by anyone prepared to feel like that indefinitely |

The window a mounted Yan sees, in a bad moment:

```
[Cloud-over-Slate · Tall Gray, first class · Level 88]
[Line: Rain-on-Slate × Long Evening · Slate Water]
[Bond: Yan Hua, rider]
[Reserve 41% · Near fore: bruised]
[Heard: his line, read, northwest]
```

**What it costs.** The reader pays in reserve at about what a real working costs, and in time, which is the true price. It has to be the whole line, spoken in the right order with no name skipped, and anything out of true is noise. A horse takes a breath or two, a get takes as long as the elder takes, and a Yan in a fight rarely has that long. The title-recitation every northerner laughs at is practice, and it is also necessary: it is how a person of the Hall keeps their own line laid down, and a Yan who has not recited in a season begins to feel as lost as they are. Everything called back is a stall, a groom and a line in the book, for good.

**Its limit.** It reaches only what has a home in it. A gray foaled off the plain, a horse with no true line, or a man who hates the place will hear only somebody talking. It needs to be heard, and it stops at what a voice can do. It moves no horse the horse's own legs would not move. It cannot be used on enemies, unless the enemy has been foolish enough to have bought from you, which is, admittedly, how the Hall arranged things. And since a line can only be read if it is known, a horse or man with "sire unknown" in the book cannot be read home at all. The joke of the Hall is that Lucky Bao, the best horse thief who ever lived, was the one man on the plain nobody could call in. He stayed thirty years.

**Who beats it.** Wax in the horses' ears, as a sensible cavalry master does before the charge, if he can bear the look on their faces, and a deaf horse is very bad at hearing a trumpet too. Crossbred remounts, which cost a third as much and run a third as well. A crossbow in the right hedge, which has killed more than one elder of the third row. Fire in the ledger-house, which has been tried four times by people who owed money, and is why every Yan child can recite a stud's whole column. And never buying a gray, which nobody who has seen one has managed.

## Physical Appearance and Dress

Many of the Hall's people are small, because small sits a horse better, and they are bowlegged from four years old with weather-cut eyes and a hard callus inside each knee. A rider's right thumb is hooked and thick from the ring. Voices run clear and level and rather bored, carrying well in the open, and every Yan speaks as if reading. The Hall-Master, at ninety, has inspected the teeth of most of the lords you have heard of, and tends to look at yours.

Gray and jade are the colors, with a running horse in silver. They dress in felted coats and quilted silk, cut to the hip for the saddle, and the jade is real. A jade bridle-boss from Yuquan is the Hall's way of saying what a horse is worth, the rider's sash says what row she sits in, by the number of knots, and every Yan keeps the small jade horse they were given at their naming on a cord, however poor. A stud archer's horn bow, lacquered black and sea-green, is Rare when the sinew is the Hall's own, and she will tell you whose fault it was. At fifty paces you will see a lean person on an enormous gray moving as if the two of them were poured. An outer cousin has a felt coat, a shovel and a jade horse, and is reminded of his fortune at every meal.

## Lineage Traits

### [Trait] The Rung — A Place in the Rows

Everyone in the Hall has a place, stated before business. A cousin who breeds a filly of the first class goes up three rows that evening, and the tablet-carver is sent for.

- Taught Dominion: an elder's presence builds with each title recited, because each is a real weight of ancestors and a real attention from everyone who knows what it means.
- The lineage halls say *wēiyā*, awe pressing down, and teach it as manners.
- It is slow, it obeys the Stage gap like any Pressure, and it does not work on anyone rude enough to interrupt.

*A Yan once introduced himself at a siege, and the siege was over when he finished. They tell that one themselves.*

### [Trait] Water Poured Downhill — The Seat

- Riders' coats extend through the saddle into the horse, and a good rider and gray move as one body, with the horse's power in her balance.
- Their shot needs no pause in the gallop: released between the fall of a hoof and the rise of the next, when the body is at rest.
- Off the horse they are small people with sore knees.

*She has not been on the ground for longer than an hour since she was four, and she walks like it.*

### [Trait] Read the Mouth — A Trade Skill Worth Paying For

- Any Yan can tell a horse's age, wind and soundness at a glance, and a man's likewise. Appraisal runs absurdly high in this family.
- In the ledger's senior elders this runs to Analysis of what a purchase means: a lord who orders forty remounts in spring is expecting to lose forty in summer.

*Lords pay well for that column, and pay better to have their own line of it left unread.*

## Doctrine and Arts

**Never stand.** The Hall's war doctrine is as simple as anything in the South. Their horsemen are few, and they ride around a force from a distance it cannot reach and take it apart from the edges, starting with whoever is shouting. They never charge. When the Yan ride out in force it is to make sure a buyer has paid, and they take a clerk.

| Move | Tongue | Meaning | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| **HUÍMǍ JIÀN!** | 回馬箭 | "Turning-Horse Arrow!" | Twisted in the stirrups, shot straight behind at the full gallop. You are chasing a person whose bow is looking at you |
| **BŌ BIĀN!** | 剝邊 | "Peel the Edge!" | A ring of riders at bowshot, shooting the outer men, moving always to the left. It eats a company slowly |
| **XIĀN SHÈ HǍN ZHĚ!** | 先射喊者 | "First Shoot the Shouter!" | Doctrine and a move both. Every arrow in the volley goes to one man, and it is whoever is giving orders |
| **SHUǏ LIÚ!** | 水流 | "Running Water!" | Coat through the saddle, and horse and rider together reach a speed neither has alone for a few hundred paces, leaving an afterimage in gray |
| **GUĪ CÈ!** | 歸冊 | "Back to the Book!" | The Reading, shouted as an order on the field. The last thing a horse thief wants to hear |
| **JIǓ LIÈ!** | 九列 | "Nine Rows!" | A whole line of the Hall, recited to the end by an elder under attack, with the Pressure of each row landing in turn. It takes several minutes. Nobody has yet found the nerve to let one finish |

## Culture

They speak the lineage halls' Chinese-registered tongue, and every word of address carries a rung. Bracket fights on the plain are run by stable-sects, each loyal to its stud: the Silver Bridles, the Black Girths, and the Yuquan Waders.

- **The Naming.** A child's first jade horse, and its first reading. The elder reads the line back as far as the first row, which can take a long while, and the child's name comes last.
- **The Line at Dawn.** Each groom reads each foal its line every morning, walking the stalls with a bowl of warm mash. Anyone can hear the Qingyuan paddocks from a mile off at sunrise, and it sounds like a very large and sleepy prayer.
- **Held.** Each New Year the Hall enters what has not come home: the Emperor's stall, the Ninth Paddock's stock, three parties sent to read it out, and one horse. The Elder of Ledgers closes the book and they eat.
- **The Last Reading.** When a Yan dies their tablet is cut and a descendant reads the whole line at the Great Hall, and the corpse's own horse is led in. If the gray turns its head, they say the dead has arrived. Nobody says what they do if it does not.

**Who they marry, fight and owe.** They dispute the salt toll with the Ryu annually, by reciting, and hold a line of boundary stones and an annual brawl at Mashi with the Marrick, the best neighbors they could ask for. The water comes from upstream, and they have started counting sick foals and showed the count to Maud Corwen. Herding families rent their stretch by the head of stock, get a share of the foals and can have a child taken up the rows on merit, which is why a Yan grandmother can reduce a horse-coper to tears from behind a tea stall by asking who his people were.

## Limitations and Soul-Costs

### [Curse] Entered Is Entered — The Book Holds Both Ways

Every Yan is in the book. The Hall can call any cousin home, from anywhere, at any hour, and a cousin who refuses gets to feel like that for the rest of their life. Nobody leaves the Yan. Some people go a very long way away and get used to it.

### [Curse] The Walking Home — Nothing You Sell Stays

A gray sold far off pines by about its eighth year. It will carry a beloved master through anything, and when that master is dead it will turn and walk home alone across half a continent, and the Hall will send the heirs a courteous note about the balance. Nobody who rides for the Yan can count on the horse staying once things have gone truly wrong.

### [Curse] The Water Upstream — One Spring Under All of It

The whole breed drinks from a very few sources, and the springs of the South have been going bad. The Hall has noticed. If whatever is wrong with the water gets into the line at Yuquan, it gets into all of the line, and it stays in the book as true.

## How They Stand Against the Three Paths

**Body.** A hitter has to catch them. Most never do, and they die at a distance, complaining. Those who can close the gap, by a movement art or a cliff at the Yan's back, find a small person on the ground with a bow and very little else to say.

**Spirit.** More dangerous, since a working thrown at range can meet them where they live, and a Spirit fighter can read an elder's line back at him. Memory workings terrify the Hall, and an elder who has lost a row of the Book has lost part of the Hall.

**Fate.** The strap gives and the horse goes down, and a whole charge can be lost to one bad hoof. But a sound horse with a clean line is a poor thing to move, and the Yan have fed, shod and inspected every animal on this plain for a thousand years. It is rare to find a strap to give.

## Final Definition

The Yan Hall is a very old family that writes down the truth and reads it out at the worst possible moment. They only know where everything comes from, in order, with no names skipped, and have found that in a world this far from home nothing else is needed. Every army in the Inner World rides out of their pastures, and every horse in every one of those armies knows the way back. Lords of the north hear that and laugh, and buy another gray. It is a very good horse.

*The Plain remembers the hoof. Bring a lunch.*

# The Articles of the Accord, and the Kinds of Polity

*The actual articles, the courts that hear them, the penalties, and the kinds of polity the Accord has to reach across.*
> *The Accord cannot hang you. It can make it impossible for you to sell anything, anywhere, ever again, and it prefers that.*

Jankin Pennyloaf, baker of Stowmarch, was amerced for a short loaf four hundred and twelve times across forty-one years, which is the town's record and, by my reading of the rolls, the Inner World's. When he died in Year 694 the market court adjourned for the first time anyone could remember, and all of Stowmarch turned out in its best colors to bury him: the bier hung with bread, the shawms and drums of the Bakers' company, the two sworn tasters at the head of the procession and already unsteady, as their office more or less requires, and a herald hired in from the next town to cry the total at every corner. At the graveside the Clerk of the Market took the last loaf from Jankin's oven off the coffin lid, set it on the beam, found it light by a quarter-ounce, and in front of the whole town struck his loaf-stamp across with the six-point (the punch that voids a false measure) and set the fine to his means. His widow paid it out of the funeral collection. The crowd cheered so loudly that the Clerk had to read the sum again.

Nobody in that churchyard wanted any other court. They had been eating Jankin's bread for forty years and knew to the crumb how short it ran, because any one of them could sound it at the beam at any hour without leave, and all of them had. Everything the Accord has ever been stands on that beam. The text follows.

## Part One: What the Accord Is

**A coalition with a law.** The Guild Accord is a coalition of guilds across many nations: adventurers' guilds, merchant guilds, craft guilds, alchemists, healers, couriers, free companies and more. A hall signs on for three things, shared rank, enforceable contracts and passage, and these Articles are how the second of them works and what the first and third rest on. They are why a mercer from one kingdom will sell to a hunter from another on sight.

**Where the Accord stands.** The text below is older than the coalition that now carries it. The formulary and its courts grew up over seven centuries in the Concord heartland, the [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Sealed Charter of the Concord|Sealed Charter]], and on the chartered routes; the Accord proper, the guilds and their crowns signing on together, is being made now, a hall at a time and a circle at a time. **Where a circle has signed, these Articles bind as law.** Where none has, they bind the members of signed halls and the parties who bring a case to the chancery's courts, and that older jurisdiction is the one described first.

**Not a sovereign.** The Accord holds no territory, keeps no standing army, and cannot execute anybody. (All three clauses are maintained with a straight face at Sealhall, in a city it bought, a mile from the Legion's drill plain, and it has never once been short of a crown willing to do the hanging.) What it has is a **jurisdiction**: a set of courts, along the trade routes and in the chartered markets, which merchants and locals alike bring their disputes to because those courts are faster, cheaper and more predictable than anything else available.

This is how the law merchant (the custom merchants carry from fair to fair) actually works, and the point is worth stating flatly because every minstrel gets it wrong. The law merchant is **not a universal code invented by merchants**. It refers to a jurisdiction and to certain procedural variances permitted in specific commercial courts. Ordinary local people use those courts too, under the same rules as the great houses.

**And enforcement runs on reputation and repeated dealing, with force a distant third.** The Accord's real sanction is exclusion: an unregistered man, an unproofed cask, a struck weight, a house whose instruments will not bind. A merchant who cannot get an attestation cannot get credit, and a merchant who cannot get credit is finished, and everybody in the trade knows it without anybody having to say it.

The formulary's four revisions, every one of them older than the first circle, are its whole legislative history until the last Title below, which the Sitting of Halls forced in when the guilds took the nine titles for themselves. What follows is the current text in summary, with the plain-tongue name beside the register name, because which one a character reaches for places him instantly.

## Part Two: The Articles

### Title I · Of Standards

| # | Register | Plain | The rule | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | *Unum pondus* | one weight | One weight, one measure, one reach, through all the land. | Amercement; the measure struck |
| II | *De verificatione* | the checking | Every weight and measure in lawful trade bears its stamps and is presented at the appointed window. | Trade suspended until presented |
| III | *De percussione* | the striking | A false weight is struck across its stamps with the six-point and destroyed. | Destruction; second offense, license forfeit |
| IV | *De soliditate* | solid or cased | No cased weight in trade. **Any person may sound a weight at any hour without leave.** | Amercement to the offender's means; the beam |
| V | *De mensura plana* | level measure | Grain sold by level measure, the board drawn once and evenly. | Amercement; forfeit of the sale |

### Title II · Of Instruments

| # | Register | Plain | The rule | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| VI | *De forma* | the wording | Only formulary wordings bind. A clause improved is a clause void. | The instrument fails |
| VII | *Coram testibus* | before witnesses | Attestation requires witnesses, and witnesses are compensated. | The instrument fails |
| VIII | *De die nullo* | the nameless | No instrument may be dated to Munahi. | Void, and it has been tested twice |
| IX | *Salvo iure tertii* | the third party | The saving clause stands in every instrument the chancery issues. | Cannot be contracted out of |
| X | *De registro* | entering | Registered within one turn, or voidable at the other party's election. | Voidable |

### Title III · Of the Trades

The register's words are older than anyone's memory of why. *The cutting* and *the writing* mean runes and tags, and *the pouring* means Drafts. These are skills anyone can level, and the Articles have never cared who can do them, only who is paid to.

| # | Register | Plain | The rule | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| XI | *De examine* | the examination | None practices the writing or the pouring **for hire** unregistered. | Amercement; tools forfeit; the work undone at his cost |
| XII | *De disciplina* | the indenture | Seven years for the cutting, indenture registered, a master limited in how many he may hold. | Indenture void; the boy freed; the master fined |
| XIII | *Probatum est* | the proof | Nothing sold as proofed that has not been tested. | The heaviest fine in the formulary |
| XIV | *De vase* | the vessel | Every charged vessel marked with maker and date of charge. Selling an uncharged vessel as charged is fraud, not error. | Reservation, and the Bench does not often forgive it |
| XV | *De voce* | the speaking | **Not licensed, and the Accord has never found a way to license it.** Liability for a spoken working falls on the person who paid for it. | Falls on the payer |

**Article XV is the interesting one.** A working that was spoken has no author in its own grammar. The invocation register has no first person, so nothing in the sentence identifies who said it, and the Bench spent four hundred years trying to place a mouth at a scene before giving up and legislating around the problem entirely. **The man who paid is liable.** This is why speakers are hired through intermediaries, why nobody hires one in front of a witness, and why the whole trade sits one step outside every other in the four quarters.

### Title IV · Of the Pool and the Round

| # | Register | Plain | The rule | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| XVI | *De foramine* | the bore | No ferrule (the fixed bronze ring at a Core's lip that limits how wide a sitter can pull) other than standard at any pool. | Barred from the pool; amercement; the fitting seized |
| XVII | *De tundisio* | the tundish | Every ring fitted with its tundish (the brass funnel that shows from the door the color of what passes) and unobstructed. **Covering a tundish is a separate offense from widening a bore**, and carries the heavier penalty, because it is done to conceal. | Barred from the pool; the heavier fine |
| XVIII | *De sigillo* | the seal | Breaking a Guild seal on a ring, a lamp stem or a lintel. | Struck from the round; a term |
| XIX | *De crure mortuo* | the dead leg | A stretch of a round given up (a dead leg) shall have its stems capped and be reported within one turn. | Amercement on the owner, whoever that now is |
| XX | *De arreragiis* | arrears | Striking a household from the round for arrears requires notice of one turn. **A death-house, a physician's house and a working forge may not be struck.** | — |

**Article XX is honored in the West and ignored everywhere the searcher is the same man as the collector**, which is most places. The exemption exists, is well known, and is claimed far more often than it is granted.

### Title V · Of Bread, Ale and the Common Sale

| # | Register | Plain | The rule | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| XXI | *De assisa panis* | the assize | The weight of the loaf is fixed against the price of grain, and moves with it. The price of ale is fixed. | Amercement, rising with repetition |
| XXII | *De gustatoribus* | the tasters | Sworn tasters chosen **annually**, unpaid, sworn to assay the ale, and bound to report defaulters at the next sitting. | Refusing the office is itself finable |
| XXIII | *De praeemptione* | forestalling | Buying goods on the road before they reach the market, to sell them dearer in it. | Amercement; goods forfeit |
| XXIV | *De remercatione* | regrating | Buying in a market to resell in the same market at a higher price. | Amercement; barred from the market |
| XXV | *De coemptione* | engrossing | Buying up the whole of a commodity to control its price. | The largest amercements the Accord has ever levied |

**Titles I and V between them are ninety percent of everything the Accord's courts actually do.** Grand commercial disputes are rare. Short loaves, weak ale, heaped measures and men who bought the carrots before the carts reached the gate are the daily business, and the rolls are full of the same bakers' names year after year, which tells you exactly how much the fines deter.

### Title VI · Of Persons and Carriage

| # | Register | Plain | The rule | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| XXVI | *De pede pulverulento* | the dusty foot | A traveler's plea is heard and determined **before the market closes.** | — |
| XXVII | *De custodia viae* | the road | The keeper of a stretch keeps it passable, and may take a toll only where he does. | Toll forfeit; the stretch reassigned |
| XXVIII | *De pignore* | the pledge | What may be taken in pledge, and what may not: tools of a man's craft, and the last beast. | Restitution |

**The dusty foot is the single most valuable procedural rule the Accord has**, and it is why merchants use its courts rather than anyone else's. A man passing through cannot wait three weeks for a sitting. A court that will hear him this afternoon, before he moves on, is a court he will bring his business to for thirty years. It is also why a crew of hunters three kingdoms from home will walk into a market court against a crowned head and come out laughing.

### Title VII · Of Standing and the Token

The newest Title, and the one the guilds wrote. Every other Title binds a person because a court says so. This one is why a stranger will hand you the goods.

| # | Register | Plain | The rule | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| XXIX | *De novem titulis* | the nine | Nine titles and no others in every signed hall: Initiate, Apprentice, Journeyman, Adept, Expert, Master, Grandmaster, Archmaster, Paragon. Each hall confers them by its own lights and answers for what it confers. | Hall amerced; title struck |
| XXX | *De fide artis* | the trust of the trade | A member deals, makes, carries or treats up to the title and no further, without a second signature from a token that covers it. | The sale voidable; amercement; the second offense, title struck |
| XXXI | *De signis et raritate* | stamp and rarity | A maker's title caps the Grade of what she may make, and a merchant's caps the Rarity of what he may sell. Every finished piece bears the Accord's stamp and its maker's mark. A false stamp is a false weight. | The piece struck across with the six-point; license forfeit |
| XXXII | *De tabula* | the board | Every Commission posted at its true Threat Tier, and taken up to the same Tier by the title of those who sign. A hall that posts a Tier light answers for the dead. | The bond forfeit; amercement to the hall's means; the hall under reservation |
| XXXIII | *De falso nummo* | the false token | **Whoever strikes, cuts, alters or knowingly carries a false token.** | Struck from every roll; reservation of all goods; delivered to the nearest crown, which often hangs him |

**Article XXXIII is the one every hall in the coalition agrees on.** A short loaf is a matter of a quarter-ounce. A false Journeyman on a T3 gets a crew killed, and a false token anywhere makes every honest token on that road worth a little less, and the whole coalition stands on those bits of silver. A good forger is also the one person the guilds will all chase together, in the only known instance of the Long Rope and the Red Caps riding out in the same week.

## Part Three: The Courts

**The market court** sits in the market, in the same building as the beam and the guardhouse. It hears debt, detinue and broken contract, and it absorbs whatever else the market throws at it: stall disputes, the assize, short measure. In a large seat it sits thrice a week. In a village with a charter it sits monthly, and by the fourth generation its rolls contain almost nothing but bakers.

**The Bench of Attribution** sits above it and exercises four powers: **entry, striking, attribution and reservation.** Entry puts a thing on the record. Striking takes it off. Attribution assigns an act to a person, which is the hardest of the four and the reason Article XV exists. Reservation withholds a standing pending determination, and a man under reservation is not condemned and cannot trade, which is worse.

**Sancta Lux** hears what the Accord will not: oath, status, legitimacy, and whether a thing was done in bad faith rather than merely done. The two jurisdictions overlap and neither will define the boundary, because both prefer being able to claim a case than being obliged to take one.

**Amercement** is the standard penalty and it is not a fixed fine. It is set by the court **to the offender's means**, which is why the same offense costs a laborer a week and a factor house an afternoon's profit, and why the factor house budgets for it.

**Arbitration** hears what the signed halls will not settle among themselves: feuds, demarcation, who rides eighth. It has no power to make a hall come. It has the roll, and a hall that will not answer finds its letters slow. **The Trials of Resonance**, under the Ribs at Sealhall, bind the sworn. The Lattice holds nothing of a signed member to convict by, so short of a prisoner held down under a Sealwright's hand, the market court will have to manage with witnesses.

## Part Four: The Kinds of Polity

The Accord has to reach across states that do not resemble each other at all, and this is the normal condition rather than an exotic one. **The characteristic state of the age is composite**: several territories under one ruler, governed as if they were separate kingdoms under their own laws and traditions, with high political, judicial and economic heterogeneity retained. That is the Concord. That is also why the Accord exists at all, because a merchant crossing three provinces of one crown crosses three legal systems, and why a coalition of guilds that all use one form is a thing crowns need and cannot afford to like.

### The seven forms

**Composite monarchy.** Several countries under one crown, each keeping its own law. The Concord, whose regalia sit in pledge to the chancery that lends them back for coronations. Chancery theorists hold that similarity of language and custom decides whether such a state holds together, which is precisely the argument the Accord makes for its own standards and its own tongue.

**Oligarchic republic.** Rule by a closed body of chartered houses, self-perpetuating, with formal offices and real elections among a tiny electorate. **Marrowhold.** It is rigidly oligarchical, and nobody in it pretends otherwise. (*Out of the world, for the table: the historical models are Venice and Genoa.*)

**Mandate.** Rule by standing decree, read aloud daily at every garrison, with no assembly and no appeal outside the decree archive. **Undaar-Keth.** Its strength is that everybody knows the law because everybody hears it every day. Its weakness is that the law is whatever was read this morning.

**Sealed moot.** An assembly whose rulings are issued in a register requiring interpreters, and whose interpreters are a hereditary profession. **The Keth-Gorrum.** Formally the most representative body in the four quarters and functionally readable by about two hundred people.

**Rotating crown.** An elective or rotating headship on a fixed cycle, designed so that no single will outlasts a season. **Iampu**, where the Crown passes at each solstice and equinox under the Treaty of Four Courts. (*Out of the world, for the table: real elective monarchies paired lifetime or fixed terms with senatorial oversight to prevent concentration.*) Such crowns characteristically drift toward capture by the electing body, which is a live risk in Iampu and is what the Frost Council actually is.

**Confederation.** No monarch, binding decisions requiring consensus across every represented house. **The Echo Elves' Longhouse Accord**, and behind it **Altherion's River Crown Council**. Confederations are ramshackle, slow, and remarkably durable, and the Echo Elf version is slower than any other by design.

**Calendrical principality.** Governance by appointed window rather than by session: nothing is decided outside its ceremonial time, and urgency is treated as a defect. **Eresse.** No other polity in the four quarters can negotiate with it at speed and none has stopped complaining about this in four Eons.

**And outside the typology entirely: Kharven.** A crown, a seat, a household structure, and a body of custom that has never been written and cannot be cited. The Accord never issued an instrument that bound there, and the seat never asked for one, until the first circle was signed in its own lower hall. The Articles now bind there under that seal, laid over a custom that still has never been written.

### What this means at the table

**A character crossing a border changes legal systems, not just flags.** The same act is an amercement in Altherion, a decree violation in Undaar-Keth, an unentered act in a hold, an untimely act in Eresse, and, until the first circle was signed, nothing at all in Kharven. A ferryman who works the line between two of them keeps two tally-sticks and two faces, and his children learn which to show on which bank before they learn to row.

**The Accord's courts and its silver are the only things that are the same everywhere**, which is the entire source of its power and the entire source of the resentment toward it. It did not conquer anybody. It made itself the only layer that works in all seven places, charged for it, and now has hundreds of guilds in hundreds of colors asking every crown on the map to sign. The crowns tolerate it, because they cannot sell anything without it, and they fear it, for the same reason.

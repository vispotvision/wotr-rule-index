# The Articles of the Accord, and the Kinds of Polity

*The actual articles, the courts that hear them, the penalties, and the kinds of polity the Accord has to reach across.*
> *The Accord cannot hang you. It can make it impossible for you to sell anything, anywhere, ever again, and it prefers that.*

### Part One: What the Accord Is

**Not a sovereign.** The Accord holds no territory, keeps no standing army, and cannot execute anybody. What it has is a **jurisdiction**: a set of courts, along the trade routes and in the chartered markets, which merchants and locals alike bring their disputes to because those courts are faster, cheaper and more predictable than anything else available.
This is exactly how the historical law merchant worked, and the point is worth stating flatly because it is usually got wrong in fiction. The law merchant was **not a universal code invented by merchants**. It referred to a jurisdiction and to certain procedural variances permitted in specific commercial courts. Ordinary local people used those courts too, under the same rules as the great houses.
**And enforcement runs on reputation and repeated dealing, not on force.** The Accord's real sanction is exclusion: an unregistered man, an unproofed cask, a struck weight, a house whose instruments will not bind. A merchant who cannot get an attestation cannot get credit, and a merchant who cannot get credit is finished, and everybody in the trade knows it without anybody having to say it.
The Accord's four revisions of the formulary are its whole legislative history. What follows is the current text in summary, with the plain-tongue name beside the register name, because which one a character reaches for places him instantly.

### Part Two: The Articles

#### Title I · Of Standards

| # | Register | Plain | The rule | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | *Unum pondus* | one weight | One weight, one measure, one reach, through all the land. | Amercement; the measure struck |
| II | *De verificatione* | the checking | Every weight and measure in lawful trade bears its stamps and is presented at the appointed window. | Trade suspended until presented |
| III | *De percussione* | the striking | A false weight is struck across its stamps with the six-point and destroyed. | Destruction; second offence, licence forfeit |
| IV | *De soliditate* | solid or cased | No cased weight in trade. **Any person may sound a weight at any hour without leave.** | Amercement to the offender's means; the beam |
| V | *De mensura plana* | level measure | Grain sold by level measure, the board drawn once and evenly. | Amercement; forfeit of the sale |

#### Title II · Of Instruments

| # | Register | Plain | The rule | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| VI | *De forma* | the wording | Only formulary wordings bind. A clause improved is a clause void. | The instrument fails |
| VII | *Coram testibus* | before witnesses | Attestation requires witnesses, and witnesses are compensated. | The instrument fails |
| VIII | *De die nullo* | the nameless | No instrument may be dated to Munahi. | Void, and it has been tested twice |
| IX | *Salvo iure tertii* | the third party | The saving clause stands in every instrument the chancery issues. | Cannot be contracted out of |
| X | *De registro* | entering | Registered within one turn, or voidable at the other party's election. | Voidable |

#### Title III · Of the Crafts

| # | Register | Plain | The rule | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| XI | *De examine* | the examination | None practises the writing or the pouring **for hire** unregistered. | Amercement; tools forfeit; the work undone at his cost |
| XII | *De disciplina* | the indenture | Seven years for the cutting, indenture registered, a master limited in how many he may hold. | Indenture void; the boy freed; the master fined |
| XIII | *Probatum est* | the proof | Nothing sold as proofed that has not been tested. | The heaviest fine in the formulary |
| XIV | *De vase* | the vessel | Every charged vessel marked with maker and date of charge. Selling an uncharged vessel as charged is fraud, not error. | Reservation, and the Bench does not often forgive it |
| XV | *De voce* | the speaking | **Not licensed, and the Accord has never found a way to license it.** Liability for a spoken working falls on the person who paid for it. | Falls on the payer |

**Article XV is the interesting one.** A working that was spoken has no author in its own grammar. The invocation register has no first person, so nothing in the sentence identifies who said it, and the Bench spent four hundred years trying to place a mouth at a scene before giving up and legislating around the problem entirely. **The man who paid is liable.** This is why speakers are hired through intermediaries, why nobody hires one in front of a witness, and why the whole trade sits one step outside every other craft in the four quarters.

#### Title IV · Of the Draw

| # | Register | Plain | The rule | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| XVI | *De foramine* | the bore | No ferrule other than standard on any service. | Cut off; amercement; the fitting seized |
| XVII | *De tundisio* | the tundish | Every housing fitted and unobstructed. **Covering a tundish is a separate offence from widening a bore**, and carries the heavier penalty, because it is done to conceal. | Cut off; the heavier fine |
| XVIII | *De sigillo* | the seal | Breaking a Guild seal on a conduit. | Cut off; a term |
| XIX | *De crure mortuo* | the dead leg | A cut conduit shall be capped and reported within one turn. | Amercement on the owner, whoever that now is |
| XX | *De arreragiis* | arrears | Cutting for arrears requires notice of one turn. **A death-house, a physician's house and a working forge may not be cut.** | — |

**Article XX is honoured in the West and ignored everywhere the inspector is the same man as the collector**, which is most places. The exemption exists, is well known, and is claimed far more often than it is granted.

#### Title V · Of Bread, Ale and the Common Sale

| # | Register | Plain | The rule | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| XXI | *De assisa panis* | the assize | The weight of the loaf is fixed against the price of grain, and moves with it. The price of ale is fixed. | Amercement, rising with repetition |
| XXII | *De gustatoribus* | the tasters | Sworn tasters chosen **annually**, unpaid, sworn to assay the ale, and bound to report defaulters at the next sitting. | Refusing the office is itself finable |
| XXIII | *De praeemptione* | forestalling | Buying goods on the road before they reach the market, to sell them dearer in it. | Amercement; goods forfeit |
| XXIV | *De remercatione* | regrating | Buying in a market to resell in the same market at a higher price. | Amercement; barred from the market |
| XXV | *De coemptione* | engrossing | Buying up the whole of a commodity to control its price. | The largest amercements the Accord has ever levied |

**Titles I and V between them are ninety percent of everything the Accord's courts actually do.** Grand commercial disputes are rare. Short loaves, weak ale, heaped measures and men who bought the carrots before the carts reached the gate are the daily business, and the rolls are full of the same bakers' names year after year, which tells you exactly how much the fines deter.

#### Title VI · Of Persons and Carriage

| # | Register | Plain | The rule | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| XXVI | *De pede pulverulento* | the dusty foot | A traveller's plea is heard and determined **before the market closes.** | — |
| XXVII | *De custodia viae* | the road | The keeper of a stretch keeps it passable, and may take a toll only where he does. | Toll forfeit; the stretch reassigned |
| XXVIII | *De pignore* | the pledge | What may be taken in pledge, and what may not: tools of a man's craft, and the last beast. | Restitution |

**The dusty foot is the single most valuable procedural rule the Accord has**, and it is why merchants use its courts rather than anyone else's. A man passing through cannot wait three weeks for a sitting. A court that will hear him this afternoon, before he moves on, is a court he will bring his business to for thirty years.

### Part Three: The Courts

**The market court** sits in the market, in the same building as the beam and the guardhouse. It hears debt, detinue and broken contract, and it absorbs whatever else the market throws at it: stall disputes, the assize, short measure. In a large seat it sits thrice a week. In a village with a charter it sits monthly, and by the fourth generation its rolls contain almost nothing but bakers.
**The Bench of Attribution** sits above it and exercises four powers: **entry, striking, attribution and reservation.** Entry puts a thing on the record. Striking takes it off. Attribution assigns an act to a person, which is the hardest of the four and the reason Article XV exists. Reservation withholds a standing pending determination, and a man under reservation is not condemned and cannot trade, which is worse.
**Sanctum Lux** hears what the Accord will not: oath, status, legitimacy, and whether a thing was done in bad faith rather than merely done. The two jurisdictions overlap and neither will define the boundary, because both prefer being able to claim a case than being obliged to take one.
**Amercement** is the standard penalty and it is not a fixed fine. It is set by the court **to the offender's means**, which is why the same offence costs a labourer a week and a factor house an afternoon's profit, and why the factor house budgets for it.

### Part Four: The Kinds of Polity

The Accord has to reach across states that do not resemble each other at all, and this is the normal condition rather than an exotic one. **The characteristic early modern state was composite**: several territories under one ruler, governed as if they were separate kingdoms under their own laws and traditions, with high political, judicial and economic heterogeneity retained. That is the Concord. That is also why the Accord exists at all, because a merchant crossing three provinces of one crown crosses three legal systems.

#### The seven forms

**Composite monarchy.** Several countries under one crown, each keeping its own law. The Concord. Contemporary theorists held that similarity of language and custom decided whether such a state held together, which is precisely the argument the Accord makes for its own standards and its own tongue.
**Oligarchic republic.** Rule by a closed body of chartered houses, self-perpetuating, with formal offices and real elections among a tiny electorate. **Stannvaard.** The historical models are Venice and Genoa and they were rigidly oligarchical, and nobody in them pretended otherwise.
**Mandate.** Rule by standing decree, read aloud daily at every garrison, with no assembly and no appeal outside the decree archive. **Undaar-Keth.** Its strength is that everybody knows the law because everybody hears it every day. Its weakness is that the law is whatever was read this morning.
**Sealed parliament.** An assembly whose rulings are issued in a register requiring interpreters, and whose interpreters are a hereditary profession. **The Keth-Gorrum.** Formally the most representative body in the four quarters and functionally readable by about two hundred people.
**Rotating crown.** An elective or rotating headship on a fixed cycle, designed so that no single will outlasts a season. **Iampu**, where the Crown passes at each solstice and equinox under the Treaty of Four Courts. Real elective monarchies paired lifetime or fixed terms with senatorial oversight to prevent concentration, and characteristically drifted toward capture by the electing body, which is a live risk in Iampu and is what the Frost Council actually is.
**Confederation.** No monarch, binding decisions requiring consensus across every represented house. **The Echo Elves' Longhouse Accord**, and behind it **Altherion's River Crown Council**. Historical confederations were ramshackle, slow, and remarkably durable, and the Echo Elf version is slower than any of them by design.
**Calendrical principality.** Governance by appointed window rather than by session: nothing is decided outside its ceremonial time, and urgency is treated as a defect. **Eresse.** No other polity in the four quarters can negotiate with it at speed and none has stopped complaining about this in four Eons.
**And outside the typology entirely: Kharven.** A crown, a seat, a household structure, and a body of custom that has never been written and cannot be cited. The Accord has no instrument that binds there and has never issued one, and the seat has never asked for one.

#### What this means at the table

**A character crossing a border changes legal systems, not just flags.** The same act is an amercement in Stannvaard, a decree violation in Undaar-Keth, an unentered act in a hold, an untimely act in Eresse, and nothing at all in Kharven.
**The Accord is the only thing that is the same everywhere**, which is the entire source of its power and the entire source of the resentment toward it. It did not conquer anybody. It just made itself the only layer that works in all seven places, and then charged for it.

# News, Rumour and the Speed of Information

*How far away a place is, measured in days rather than miles. Load-bearing for the Fronts.*
> *The wire follows the conduit. Where the draw stops, the world goes quiet.*

### The Governing Fact

There are three channels and they are not alternatives. They carry different things, cost different amounts, and are trusted differently, and a scene is often built out of the gap between them.
**The wire.** Near-instant, expensive, brief, read by the Guild, and it reaches only where the conduit reaches.
**The post.** Cheap, prepaid, reliable, and as fast as the fastest road or hull on the route.
**The mouth.** Free, universal, faster than the post over short distances, and wrong.
The Draw Age's defining change is that the first of those exists at all. Before it, everything moved at the speed of a horse or a hull, and the four quarters were four different presents. Now the capital knows a thing the same hour it happens, and a hamlet nine miles off the main learns it in a fortnight from a carter, and **that gap is the age's characteristic injustice.**

### I. The Wire

#### What it is

A signal line laid alongside the sealed conduit, Guild-owned, Guild-operated, running between gauge-houses. It exists because the conduit exists: the wayleaves were already bought, the trenches already cut, the stations already staffed. Nobody would have paid to build it on its own.
**The consequence, and it is the whole design.** The wire reaches exactly as far as the draw does and no further. A district on the main is on the wire. A district cut off is deaf. A Withering-era quarter losing density loses its signal net at the same rate, and towns that were an hour from the capital become a fortnight from it again, in living memory, while everyone watches. **The map of who knows things is the map of who still has pressure**, and both are shrinking.

#### What it costs and what that does to the message

Priced by the word, at a flat rate regardless of distance. A short message costs a labourer the better part of a day. This is not a detail; it is the reason every telegram in the world reads the way it does. **Cost forces compression, and compression is where meaning dies.**
The standard form is nine words, with a further charge for each word after. Names count. Punctuation is spoken as a word and paid for, which is why a stop is omitted whenever it can be, and why so many wires are ambiguous in exactly the place they should not be.

#### The press rate

Newspapers do not pay the public rate. The Guild carries press traffic at roughly a third, capped in length, and the concession is worth more than any subsidy the Accord has ever voted. In return the Guild sets the cap, and a Guild that dislikes a story can enforce brevity on it without ever refusing to carry it.
A paper wanting its own correspondent on the wire posts a deposit for each one, per town, in advance. That single requirement decides which papers have foreign news and which reprint it a week late, and it is why three houses in Stannvaard between them tell the four quarters what happened.
**Dedicated lines run to the papers at night and carry public traffic by day.** This is why a thing that happens in the evening is in the morning edition and a thing that happens at noon waits.

#### The distortion, which is mechanical and not moral

This is the part worth stealing whole, because it is how the real system failed and it is a gift to a table.
A correspondent compresses the story to fit the cap. **An operator who does not care about the content** sends it. A copywriter at the receiving end **elaborates it back out to length** for the page. The story that prints is therefore reconstructed from an abbreviation by somebody who was not there.
So the wire's errors are not lies. They are the specific errors of compression followed by expansion: the qualifier dropped, the number rounded, the name of the second man lost, the cause and the sequence swapped because the cap fell between them. **A Front can advance wrongly in the capital while being perfectly true on the ground**, and nobody in the chain did anything dishonest.

#### The boys

A wire arrives at a gauge-house and is carried the last stretch by hand, by boys, on foot, within about three miles of the office. Beyond that radius delivery is a further charge or is not offered. They are paid by the message rather than by the day, and a fast boy clears thirty in a shift.
They are the most underused characters in the setting. **A boy who runs wires knows, before anyone in the street does, who has had a death in the family, whose credit has failed, and which house has been summoned to the Bench.** He cannot read most of it. He does not need to. He can read a face at a door.

#### The hour

The least obvious consequence and the most far-reaching. A signal net cannot be run on local time, so the Guild pushes a single hour down the wire from Stannvaard and every gauge-house sets to it. **The four quarters are no longer governed by the sun.**
This lands as a political act everywhere it arrives.
- **Eresse.** The entire verb system marks whether an act fell inside its appointed window. A window is a calendar fact. A calendar fact set in Stannvaard means an Eressean rite is now timely or untimely by a foreign clock, and the Grand Principality has never formally accepted Guild hour and has quietly kept both.
- **The holds.** Mathematical Cadence times a Tally entry, and the Tally is the record. Northern clerks keep hold hour for entries and Guild hour for the wire, and the two-column ledgers this produces are a running administrative misery.
- **The Mandate.** Adopted it instantly and completely, because a decree read daily at every garrison at the same moment is a better decree.
- **Kharven.** Ignored it. Time there is a fire's length and a broth, and no wire has ever reached the seat.

### II. The Post

A uniform rate, prepaid by the sender, for a letter under a set weight, carried anywhere the Accord's writ runs. Prepayment is the change that mattered: before it, the recipient paid, which meant the poor refused letters at the door and correspondence was a thing only the comfortable had.
The post is cheap enough that ordinary people write, and this is a genuine break with the world before. **A century ago letters were an instrument of business and property. Now they are how a mother in a hold hears from a son in a garrison**, and the volume is enormous, and the Accord makes nothing on it and does it anyway because a population that writes is a population that can be counted.
It moves at the speed of the fastest thing on the route: hull, coach, or in the west the new lines. It does not depend on the conduit and therefore reaches everywhere the wire does not, which makes it the only channel that still serves a cut district.
**The post is also the only channel that carries a thing worth stealing.** A wire is nine words the Guild has already read. A letter has substance, and can be sealed, and can be opened, and the trade in opened letters is old, professional and entirely deniable.

### III. The Mouth

Criers, carters, pedlars, pilgrims, discharged soldiers, and the tap-room. Free, and over a short distance the fastest of the three, because a crier is in the square before a wire is copied out.
Its characteristic error is the opposite of the wire's. The wire loses detail. **The mouth adds it.** A rumour gains a name, a number and a motive on every retelling, and the additions are always the ones that make the story make sense to the person adding them.
So two contradictory accounts of the same Front are normal and neither teller is lying. The wire has stripped it to nine words. The tap-room has grown it back with the wrong material.

### IV. The Table

Use this to answer, on the spot, when a Front's news reaches a given place.
| Distance | Wire | Post | Mouth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same city, on the main | within the hour | same day | within the hour in the same quarter, next day across it |
| Same city, off the main | not available | same day | a day, distorted |
| A day's ride | within the hour if both ends are gauged | two days | two to three days, distorted |
| A week's road | within the hour if the line runs | six to eight days | two to three weeks, badly distorted |
| Across a quarter | same day if the line runs; otherwise as post | two to three weeks | a season, or never |
| Across the water | same day where cable exists, otherwise by hull | a month or more | rumour only, and usually a year out of date |
| Kharven seat | never | as the road allows, and the road does not allow much in the Thin Weeks | the only channel that has ever worked |

**Three standing rules for the table.**
**One.** Ask whether the destination is on the main *before* asking how far it is. Distance is secondary now, and that is the age's whole point.
**Two.** When a Front advances, decide the true version once, then decide separately what the wire carried, what the paper printed and what the tap-room says. They will differ, and a character's decisions should be made on whichever one reached him.
**Three.** The Guild reads everything. Anything a character genuinely needs kept is sent by hand, which costs days, and the choice between speed and secrecy is one of the few clean decisions the setting offers.

### V. Hooks Sitting Here Unused

**The deaf district.** A quarter loses pressure. The wire goes with it. Nobody in the capital notices for eleven days because the absence of news reads as the absence of events.
**The nine-word ambiguity.** A wire arrives correct in every word and wrong in its meaning, because the stop was omitted to save a charge. Somebody acts on it. The correspondent, the operator and the copywriter are all blameless and the man is still dead.
**The boy who reads faces.** A wire-boy is the only person in a street who knows which houses have had bad news this week, and somebody has worked out that this is worth money.
**Two hours in one ledger.** A hold clerk keeps Guild hour and hold hour in parallel columns, and a Tally entry falls on different sides of a boundary depending on the column, and a Cask-Oath's term turns on which one governs.
**The press deposit.** A story the Guild dislikes is not refused. It is simply capped shorter this week, and the paper that could have carried it in full cannot afford a second correspondent.

### Author Notes

**The wire** is built on the Victorian electric telegraph, patented in 1837 and commercially available around 1850. It ran alongside railways because the rights of way already existed, was priced per word at a simplified flat tariff, and forced brevity through cost. Press traffic was carried at roughly a third of the public rate and capped in length; newspapers wanting their own correspondents posted a substantial deposit per correspondent per town; dedicated press wires ran at night and carried public traffic by day. Delivery was by messenger boys within about a three-mile radius of an office, paid by piece at a penny a message, clearing around thirty a day. The telegraph was also used to distribute a standardised national time, which the railways required, ending government by the sun.
**The distortion mechanic** is taken directly from the documented failure of wire journalism: stories were compressed to fit the cap, transmitted by operators indifferent to the content, and then elaborated back out by copywriters at the receiving end, with accuracy frequently sacrificed in the process.
**The post** is built on the uniform Penny Post of 1840: a flat rate for a letter under half an ounce carried anywhere in the country, prepaid by the sender rather than paid on delivery, riding on a network of cross posts, mail coaches and, from the late 1840s, nationwide railway service. The shift to prepayment is the change that opened correspondence to people who had previously refused letters at the door.
The substitution of the sealed conduit for the railway as the thing the wire follows is originated, and everything downstream of it, including the Withering-era contraction of the signal net and the four peoples' different responses to Guild hour.

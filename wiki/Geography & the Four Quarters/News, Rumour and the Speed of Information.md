# News, Rumor and the Speed of Information

*How far away a place is, measured in days rather than miles. Load-bearing for the Fronts.*
> *The line goes as far as somebody will pay a warden to stand on a hill. Past that, ask a minstrel, and count your fingers afterward.*
>
> *Saying among beacon clerks*

Long Cuddy Ouzel of the Bluecap Yard died three times in the spring of Year 706, and he was present for the third. He had gone north to Marrowford to collect the bounty on a killer called the Tin Deacon, and he collected it on the ford stones in front of two thousand people, in a coat of aura the blue of a jay's wing, at the price of an ear, four ribs and most of his blood. The Yard's second ran to the nearest beacon-house with coin for the short form, and the clerk sent exactly what he was paid to send: OUZEL DOWN MARROWFORD KILLER TAKEN BODY NEEDS CARRIAGE HOME. Nine words, every one of them true. That was his first death. A copyist in Sealhall read them by the morning bell and spun them out to length for the herald, and by noon the squares had heard that Cuddy Ouzel was slain by treachery and his murderer in irons, and the bookies were paying out on the Deacon. Inside a fortnight there were three songs, in which he died on a bridge, on a wall and in the arms of a duchess he had never met, and that was his second.

The third was the funeral. The Bluecap Yard does nothing by halves. Four thousand people came down Cooper's Hill in blue scarves on the twelfth day, a band of trumpeters of bad reputation in front, and the whole Yard stood round an empty pyre with their coats up and lit it together from their bare hands, forty colors of flame climbing into a violet evening, while the rival yards sang his name from the rooftops opposite out of real respect and some relief. A carrier's wagon came off the kept road into the back of this, with a very thin man lying in it under a horse blanket. He sat up. He watched for some time. He ate a funeral cake that someone passed him, and then he asked who had won the money, and the riot that followed is the only one in the city's records with a song of its own. The letter he had sent by a merchants' rider, explaining everything, came in two days later.

Everything on this page is in that fortnight: a true message gone wrong by compression, a herald's house filling the gap, minstrels improving on it, odds moving on bad news, and a rider or a road arriving last with the facts. Ouzel is alive yet, nine years on, and complains to anyone buying that the duchess was the only version he would have paid to see.

## The Governing Fact

There are three channels and they are not alternatives. They carry different things, cost different amounts, and are trusted differently, and a scene is often built out of the gap between them.

**The beacon line.** Near-instant on a clear night, expensive, brief, read by the Guild, shut by fog, and it reaches only where the towers are lit, which is beside a few great roads.

**The bag.** A letter handed to whoever is going that way, paid for by the sender, cheap and fairly reliable, as fast as the fastest rider or hull you can afford on the route.

**The mouth.** Heralds and criers, minstrels, bounty bills, bookies' boards and the tap-room. Free or nearly free, universal, faster than the bag over short distances, and wrong.

The defining change of the age is that the first of those exists at all. Before it, everything moved at the speed of a horse or a hull, and the four quarters were four different presents, and for most people on most days they still are. But the capital can now know a thing the same hour it happens, while a hamlet nine miles off the line learns it in a fortnight from a carter, and **that gap is the age's characteristic injustice.**

## I. The Beacon Line

### What it is

A chain of hilltop fire-towers in sight of one another, Guild-owned, Guild-operated, running between beacon-houses, lit by wardens in shuttered colors, Fulguria (the electromagnetic family of Wellsprings) blue against Caloria (the heat family) orange, and read off by clerks whose Crystals have never woken. It stands beside the few kept roads (great old roads whose bridges and waystones are worked by hired hands) because the watch-hills on those roads were already cleared and the road-wardens' lodges were already fed. Nobody would have paid to build it on its own. Fog and heavy snow shut it, and when it opens again the Guild's own traffic goes first, then the Crown's, then whoever paid, so a public message may sit a day at the foot of the tower.

**The consequence, and it is the whole design.** The line reaches exactly as far as a warden can stand on a hill, filled and paid, and no further. A tower burns all night out of one person's reserve, and that person pulls Aether from the ground under her, so a tower on rich ground runs cheap and a tower on thin ground starves its warden and charges for it. A district past the last tower is deaf. A Withering-era quarter losing density loses its towers at the same rate, and towns that were an hour from the capital become a fortnight from it again, in living memory, while everyone watches. **The map of who knows things is the map of who still has rich ground**, and both are shrinking. The tower wardens may also walk off, like any warden, and when the Ridge towers struck in Year 698 over the winter ration, three kingdoms spent nine days believing nothing was happening anywhere.

### What it costs and what that does to the message

Priced by the word, at one rate regardless of distance: a silver for twenty words with the addresses counted, and a copper for each word after. A short message costs a laborer the better part of a day. That price is the reason every beacon message in the world reads the way it does. **Cost forces compression, and compression is where meaning dies.**

The addresses of sender, receiver and both houses eat eleven of the twenty on an ordinary day, so the standard form, the thing every clerk means when he says a message, is nine words, with a further charge for each word after. Names count. Punctuation is spelled out as a word and paid for, which is why a stop is omitted whenever it can be, and why so many messages are ambiguous in exactly the place they should not be.

### The heralds' rate

Heralds' houses do not pay the public rate. The Guild carries their traffic at roughly a third, capped in length, and the concession is worth more than any subsidy the Accord has ever voted. In return the Guild sets the cap, and a Guild that dislikes a story can enforce brevity on it without ever refusing to carry it.

A house wanting its own pursuivant on the line (a pursuivant being a junior herald sent to live in a town and report on it) posts a deposit for each one, per town, in advance. That single requirement decides which houses have foreign news and which recopy it a week late, and it is why three houses in Sealhall between them tell the four quarters what happened.

**The night watch on the line runs to the heralds, and the day watch carries public traffic.** This is why a thing that happens in the evening is cried at the morning bell and a thing that happens at noon waits.

### The distortion, which is mechanical and not moral

This is the part worth stealing whole, because it is how such systems really fail and it is a gift to a table.

A pursuivant compresses the story to fit the cap. **A beacon clerk who does not care about the content** sends it. A copyist at the receiving house **elaborates it back out to length** for the bill and the cry. The story the herald shouts is therefore reconstructed from an abbreviation by somebody who was not there.

So the line's errors are honest ones, the specific errors of compression followed by expansion: the qualifier dropped, the number rounded, the name of the second man lost, the cause and the sequence swapped because the cap fell between them. **A Front can advance wrongly in the capital while being perfectly true on the ground**, and nobody in the chain did anything dishonest.

### The boys

A message arrives at a beacon-house and is carried the last stretch by hand, by boys, on foot, within about three miles of the house. Beyond that radius delivery is a further charge or is not offered. They are paid by the message rather than by the day, and a fast boy clears thirty in a shift.

They are the most underused characters in the setting. **A boy who runs beacon messages knows, before anyone in the street does, who has had a death in the family, whose credit has failed, and which house has been summoned to the Bench.** He cannot read most of it. He does not need to. He can read a face at a door.

### The hour

The least obvious consequence and the most far-reaching. Two relay stations cannot agree on a change of horses by the sun, so the Guild keeps a single hour on the great water clock at Sealhall and sends it outward by relay rider, sealed sand-glass and the beacons, and every guild hall and beacon-house sets its own water clock to it, more or less. No two halls agree to the count, and a hard frost stops the clock. **But wherever a Guild hall's bell rings, the sun has a rival**, and in a cloth town the work bell has made the quarrel about wages as well as prayers.

This lands as a political act everywhere it arrives.
- **Eresse.** The entire verb system marks whether an act fell inside its appointed window. A window is a calendar fact. A calendar fact set in Sealhall means an Eressean rite is now timely or untimely by a foreign clock, and the Grand Principality has never formally accepted Guild hour and has quietly kept both.
- **The holds.** Mathematical Cadence times a Tally entry, and the Tally is the record. Northern clerks keep hold hour for entries and Guild hour for the relays, and the two-column ledgers this produces are a running administrative misery.
- **The Mandate.** Adopted it instantly and completely, because a decree read daily at every garrison at very nearly the same moment is a better decree.
- **Kharven.** Ignored it. Time there is a fire's length and a broth, and no beacon has ever reached the seat.

## II. The Bag

Nobody runs this. A letter goes across a city with a boy for a copper, and across a quarter with whichever carrier or merchants' rider is going that way for two to four, plus two coppers to the scrivener on the church steps if you cannot write it yourself. The sender pays before it leaves. That custom is the one that mattered: a letter that wanted paying at the far end was a letter the poor refused at the door, a carrier who had walked forty miles for nothing dropped the next one in a ditch, and correspondence stayed a thing only the comfortable had.

It is cheap enough that ordinary people do it, a few times in a life, and this is a genuine break with the world before. **A century ago letters were an instrument of business and property. Now they are how a mother in a hold hears from a son in a garrison**, twice in a good year, read out to her by whoever on the stair has letters, with the whole stair listening. The Accord makes nothing on it. Its clerks note who is sending what to where all the same, because a population that writes is a population that can be counted.

It moves at the speed of the fastest thing on the route that you can pay for: hull, barge, carrier's wagon, merchants' rider or relay rider. The relay riders are the pride of the road, in their yellow sashes, with a bell at the belt, a sealed bag, a rested horse every twenty to forty miles on the roads that can keep one and the right of way over anyone but a crown, and they are insufferable about it in every inn on the road. A great house in a real hurry can hire one to carry one bag and no other, at one to three gold the hundred miles, which beats everything else on the road by a day or two in the week and costs about what the horses do. The great merchant houses keep riders of their own, leaving when there is enough in the bag and arriving when they arrive. None of it depends on the towers, so the bag reaches everywhere the line does not, which makes it the only channel of the three that still serves a deaf district.

**The bag is also the only channel that carries a thing worth stealing.** A beacon message is nine words the Guild has already read. A letter has substance, and can be sealed, and can be opened, and the trade in opened letters is old, professional and entirely deniable.

## III. The Mouth

Heralds, criers, minstrels, bills on doors, bounty boards, bookies, carters, peddlers, pilgrims, discharged soldiers, and the tap-room. Over a short distance it is the fastest of the three, because a crier is in the square before a beacon message is copied out.

**Heralds and criers.** A herald wears a house's colors and cries at the bell, from the steps, in a trained voice that can be heard across a fish market, and a great lord's herald reads what the lord wants read. A crier is a herald's poor cousin, working a corner for coppers: he reads you the news off a hand-copied bill, sells you the bill for a copper, and adds whatever he thinks will sell the next one. A bill is about a dozen words, which is the only reason it is cheap, since a scrivener's boy can make forty in an hour; the Guild's day-rates copied onto a slip run three coppers, and a book costs what a book costs. The bills go up on doors overnight in bad letters, and every copy is a fresh chance for a slipped word to become a fact. People know this and believe them anyway, because it was cried and it is on the door.

**Minstrels.** The great carriers of fame, and the song is what lasts. A song moves at walking pace and gets everywhere, a season to cross a quarter, and it gets the details wrong in a way you can predict: the name changes to make the rhyme, the numbers go up by a factor of ten, the loser becomes uglier, and somebody acquires a sword. A minstrel can be paid, and a lord who has not paid one should expect to be a villain by harvest. Half the people alive know a famous fight only from a version in which the wrong person won, and they will correct you.

**Bounties.** A lord posts a bounty by bill and by herald, and that makes it news with a price on it. The figure tells everyone how frightened the lord is, and it brings hunters who had never heard of the quarrel. A bounty also travels a good deal faster than its cancellation. Nobody pays a crier to shout that a man has been pardoned, so a boy in a far market may be reading out a dead lord's grudge years after the ink dried, and people have been killed on it. The clerk at the bounty board will tell you this is a matter for the poster.

**Bookies.** They take odds on everything: a duel, a bracket, a siege, how long a stoppage holds. The board is the most honest news in town, because the bookie is out of pocket if he is wrong, and it is often the fastest, because the men who get a beacon message first go straight round and bet on it. A price that moves at noon with no news to explain it is the news, arriving ahead of the herald. Bookies keep a beacon boy on a retainer as a matter of course, and they read a crowd the way a warden reads weather.

**What fame does.** A name that has gone round changes prices, doors and who comes for you. An innkeeper gives the famous a free dinner or doubles the bill, according to the song. A gate opens, a guild master finds an hour, a rival school sends a challenge, and in due course some boy with a good coat and no name walks twenty days to find out if the song is true. An epithet (the name that goes ahead of a fighter) travels faster than its owner, and is often wrong or unflattering, and is seldom shaken off.

The characteristic error of all of this is the opposite of the line's. The line loses detail. **The mouth adds it.** A rumor gains a name, a number and a motive on every retelling, and the additions are always the ones that make the story make sense to the person adding them.

So two contradictory accounts of the same Front are normal and neither teller is lying. The line has stripped it to nine words. The tap-room has grown it back with the wrong material.

## IV. The Table

Use this to answer, on the spot, when a Front's news reaches a given place.

| Distance | Beacon line (fog and Guild traffic allowing) | Bag | Mouth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same city, on the line | within the hour | same day | within the hour in the same quarter, next day across it |
| Same city, off the line | not available | same day | a day, distorted |
| A day's ride | within the hour if both ends have beacon-houses | two days | two to three days, distorted |
| A week's road | within the hour if the line runs | six to eight days | two to three weeks, badly distorted |
| Across a quarter | same day if the line runs; otherwise as the bag | two to three weeks | a season, or never |
| Across the water | same day where island towers carry the line, otherwise by hull | a month or more | rumor only, and usually a year out of date |
| Kharven seat | never | as the road allows, and the road does not allow much in the Thin Weeks | the only channel that has ever worked |

**What each voice in the mouth does to it.**

| Voice | How it moves | How it goes wrong |
|---|---|---|
| A herald's cry | The next morning bell after the line brings it; as the bag where there is no line | Elaborated from an abbreviation, and leaning toward whoever pays the house |
| A crier and his bill | The same day through a city; onward by road with anyone carrying a copy | Copying slips, and a first line bigger than the facts |
| A minstrel's song | Walking pace; a season across a quarter | Names, numbers and winners, and it lasts longest |
| A bounty bill | As the bag, then by every hunter who wants the competition behind him | Correct when posted, and still up after it stops being true |
| A bookies' board | Within the hour where the line runs | The direction is usually right and the reason is never given |

**Three standing rules for the table.**

**One.** Ask whether the destination is on the line *before* asking how far it is. On the few roads with a line, distance is secondary now, and that is the age's whole quarrel. Everywhere else it is everything it ever was.

**Two.** When a Front advances, decide the true version once, then decide separately what the line carried, what the herald cried, what the song says and what the tap-room says. They will differ, and a character's decisions should be made on whichever one reached him.

**Three.** The Guild reads everything. Anything a character genuinely needs kept is sent by hand, which costs days, and the choice between speed and secrecy is one of the few clean decisions the setting offers.

## V. Hooks Sitting Here Unused

**The deaf district.** A quarter's ground thins, or its tower wardens walk. The line goes with it. Nobody in the capital notices for eleven days because the absence of news reads as the absence of events.

**The nine-word ambiguity.** A message arrives correct in every word and wrong in its meaning, because the stop was omitted to save a charge. Somebody acts on it. The pursuivant, the clerk and the copyist are all blameless and the man is still dead.

**The boy who reads faces.** A beacon boy is the only person in a street who knows which houses have had bad news this week, and somebody has worked out that this is worth money.

**Two hours in one ledger.** A hold clerk keeps Guild hour and hold hour in parallel columns, and a Tally entry falls on different sides of a boundary depending on the column, and a Cask-Oath's term turns on which one governs.

**The herald's deposit.** A story the Guild dislikes is never refused. It is simply capped shorter this week, and the house that could have cried it in full cannot afford a second pursuivant.

**The bill still on the door.** A bounty posted by a lord nine years dead is still paying out, in the sense that people keep trying to collect it, and the man it names has just come home.

**The price that moved.** The odds on a siege shift hard at noon with no news in town. Somebody knows something, the bookie will not say who, and half the street is already selling up.

**The song that will not die.** A minstrel's mistake has made a respectable family into murderers, in four hundred taverns, and the only person who can sing it right is the one who was paid to sing it wrong.

## Author Notes

**The beacon line** is built on real fire chains. The Byzantine beacon line of the ninth century, credited to Leo the Mathematician, ran some 450 miles across Asia Minor from the fortress of Loulon on the Cilician frontier to the palace lighthouse at Constantinople, and modern experiment suggests a signal could cross it in about an hour. Identical water clocks at the two ends assigned a different message to each of twelve hours, so the hour a fire was lit said what had happened. Later chroniclers claim Michael III shut it down so that news of an invasion would not spoil his chariot racing, which scholars usually treat as propaganda and this page treats as an idea for a hook. The Mamluk post in Syria and Egypt ran fire signals and carrier pigeons beside its relay horses, and Greek writers describe older hilltop chains. The real chains sent a few agreed signals. Sending words by shuttered color is this setting's own step past them, and the price per word, the heralds' rate, the deposit and the night watch all follow from that step.

**The distortion mechanic** is the plain arithmetic of any short signal sold by the word, and it has a cousin in the merchants' newsletters of the Italian towns, where news went from hand to hand and each writer summarized the last.

**The bag** is built on the relay systems: the Mongol yam, whose stations stood about 20 to 40 miles apart with spare horses, food and shelter, and the Persian royal road before it, for the riders; and for everything else the way medieval letters really moved, in the care of whoever was going that way, with the Florentine merchants' own courier service of the fourteenth century as the model for the merchant houses' riders.

The substitution of wardened fire-towers for the plain beacon is originated, and so is everything downstream of it, including the Withering-era contraction of the line and the four peoples' different responses to Guild hour.

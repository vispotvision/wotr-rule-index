# The Golden Hollow of Ashbury

**Say it** · ASH-bree

*House Ashmore · The Greenhollow · The Grain*

> *Perkin Oatley lit a little light,*
> *to see the mice were gone;*
> *the roof went up to say good night*
> *and came down Barleyford at dawn.*
>
> Children's skipping rhyme, the Greenhollow

The Great Tithe Barn at Sheafton is two hundred and forty feet long, with a roof of oak trusses a bishop would be proud of, and in the harvest of Year 678 it held every sack the upper Hollow had just been made to give. It also held mice, and the Ashmore steward hired a hedge-worker named Perkin Oatley to charm them out at four copper the day. Perkin was a cheerful, untidy man who could genuinely talk to mice and was, by all accounts, doing well. Toward evening, with the threshers shaking out the last sacks and the air inside gone thick and sweet and golden with barley dust, he lit a tallow dip to see into the far bays.

Fine flour in still air will burn faster than the eye can follow, and every miller knows it. Perkin Oatley was a hedge-worker. The flash ran the length of the barn, the great roof lifted off all together like a lid off a boiling pot, and the blast could be felt nine miles off. The sky over the upper Hollow filled with green roof-tiles, and for half an hour afterward it rained hot roasted barley on three villages, gently, in the sunset, with the whole country running out with aprons and baskets, shouting, children dancing in it, the smell like the best breakfast of your life. Eleven threshers died and Perkin was never found in any form that could be sat with, and the Hollow gave them a very handsome funeral. Then, the following week, the Ashmore clerks came round with tally-sticks and billed every household in three villages for what had fallen on their land, by the bushel, at the harvest rate. They paid. Everyone in the north always pays Ashmore in the end.

The Golden Hollow is the one warm valley in a cold country, whose owners know to the sack what you will need in January, and who have never once had to raise their voices about it.

## The Land and What It Looks Like

The Greenhollow is the southern lowland of the north: forested river valleys, deep soil and enough season for grain, livestock and timber, and the only territory in the old kingdom where agriculture is the primary occupation rather than a supplement to hunting and trade. Coming down into it off the plateau in late summer is one of the great experiences of a northern life. The road drops through the last of the spruce, the wind stops trying to kill you, and there below is a long green valley gone gold, barley and rye to the tree-line in both directions, red cattle in the water-meadows, the rivers bright between the alders. And standing over the wheat in every parish, like cathedrals to a religion of bread, are the **tithe barns**: oak and honey-colored stone, buttressed, under great sweeping tiered roofs of verdigris teal with carved finials, hundreds of feet long, with doors a loaded wagon can turn around in. The faithful are expected to fill them.

The Hollow is green from thaw to first frost and it is still cold by anyone else's measure, with a four-month growing season to work in. Visitors from the south find it charming and slightly small. Visitors from the north find themselves crying at a hedge.

### The major places

- **Ashbury.** The seat, on the water-meadows where the Kessen, the Marrow and the salt road from the south all meet. A storybook town of half-timbered halls under those same sweeping teal roofs (the biggest of them are tithe barns, and visitors keep taking them for churches), with golden trees in every yard, a clear stream under a stone bridge where the carts cross and small boats tie up, blue mountains behind it, and russet pennants on the gates. It has the Corn Hall where the allocation is read out at Stack-Count, and below the town at the crossroads the **junction stone**, a black upright older than the house, on which Ashmore oaths are sworn hand-flat, and which the Hollow believes holds them. A famous old woman at Ashbury licks a horseshoe and tells you whether the horse will see spring, and she has made a very good living.
- **Sheafton.** The barn town of the upper Hollow, with the Great Tithe Barn (rebuilt, with larger vents, and a regulation about tallow) and a Harvest Fair that draws bracket fighters from across the north, largely for the food.
- **Barleyford.** A malting and brewing town on a shallow crossing, smelling of sweet smoke in all seasons. It owns one green tile from the old roof, set in the church wall, and will show it to you. The best small beer north of the Center comes from here, and nearly all of it goes up the road to the Seat on Ashmore's terms.

**Roads.** North by the Marrow road to the Seat, through the last of the spruce, and upriver by the southern road to Threeways and the Painted Rivers. South of the valley the North stops and the marching country begins. From the junction below Ashbury the salt road goes to Halcrest in the Amber Cities, four days by relay rider, and on down the Scourge of Hell's east flank, and the Kessen road takes its ford for the Midmarch, five days to Kingsbridge, and on down the west flank.

## House and Rule

**House Ashmore** is the least martial house in a martial kingdom and the most quietly dangerous. Their power is grain. Their leverage is the specific kind that comes from controlling what everyone else needs to survive the winter, and they have never lost a negotiation with anyone who was hungry, which in the north means they have never lost a negotiation. They hold every sack between the Greenhollow and the treeline, and they govern through a steward's competence, soft voice and clean hands: an Ashmore knows exactly how many bushels each Banner House needs to survive December and is willing to adjust the allocation on political grounds. The Ashmores do not threaten. Threats are for houses whose leverage requires explanation, and Ashmore leverage explains itself every time the temperature drops.

The lord in Year 715 is **Edric Ashmore**, Lord of the Ashmore Marches and one of the Seven Warlords: forty, looks younger, a man who weighs before he speaks and who will look at an offer for exactly as long as it takes a cup of wine to go cold. For twenty years the Tenrai have held Ashmore's roads, so Ashmore takes Tenrai silver because Ashmore has no choice, and the Tenrai count him bought. In the taverns of Hollenmark they say a man who owns the grain in a famine does not need to fight anybody, and he will sell to whoever stands at the gate with silver when the snow comes and call it neutrality. Edric has heard this and thinks it is a fair summary.

## Wealth and Trade

Barley, rye, oats, malt, salted beef, hides, tallow, cheese, and every bit of the leverage those provide. The house runs its income in kind, through the **allocation**: each autumn the Corn Hall reads out how much grain goes to each house, town and garrison in the north, and the reading has ended more friendships than adultery. The salt road brings Nalūn salt bars up from the Center, and Ashbury is where salt, grain and river-barges change hands. Tolls on the three-way meeting bring in more than a duke's rents. The Ashmores buy big southern horses from the Yan of the Jade Plains, at Yan prices, and have a low opinion of the Yan as a result.

Every farmer in the Hollow owes a tenth to the barn, a day a week in plowing and harvest to the manor, and whatever he can talk his way out of to the reeve. Ashmore clerks count on notched tally-sticks split down the middle, one half for the barn and one for the farmer, so neither can cheat without the other's stick. Among the people who are never short of food, this is considered the height of civilization.

## Rent, Food and the Street

Land here is held in strips, by old custom, and the rent is a share of the crop. In Ashbury town rent is in coin and in a sack on Sack Day, the first market after harvest. This is the only part of the north where people eat fresh bread every day, where a laborer has an onion in his pocket, and where a plowman's wife can get fat, and no one from the rest of the north ever fails to mention it. Pease pottage, barley cakes with butter, dark rye, smoked eel from the water-meadows, plum brandy in a good year. Harvest Home runs a week, with dancing in the empty barns, sheaf-tossing, cloth-of-gold scarecrows carried on poles, and the local Stage brackets held on stubble fields under the moon, a dozen fighting schools in russet and yellow calling each other's mothers cows. It is considered bad luck to win on the first night.

Hedge-workers do steady business: talking the frost off a rye field, the mice out of a bay (under supervision), the blight from a pear. In the Hollow they charge per acre and they can and do refuse a lord who has shorted them, and in 684 the whole upper valley's hedge-workers walked out over a late payment and there were no pears for two years. People here call the aura coat the hide, as everyone in Kharven does, and add that a thick one is a sign of a good dinner. A great many households in the Greenhollow perform all fourteen Sōhai labors correctly, in order, every day of their lives, and would be insulted to hear they were doing anything other than keeping a house properly.

**What they joke about.** Thin people. The rest of the north, gently, from a position of having eaten. Perkin Oatley, always. Edric's wine, which is said to be the coldest in the kingdom. "Which sack?" is the punchline to about half the jokes in the valley, for reasons a stranger will never get, and the answer is always the one you already gave them.

## Law and How It Fights

**Law.** The manor court and the reeve for ordinary business, with the Corn Hall above them. A dispute over a bushel goes to the stick. A big promise goes on the junction stone, and it holds one oath at a time; two oaths on the same stone are known to grind, and lawyers have made fortunes finding out which one was first. Hollow people will not lie with a hand on stone, whatever else they do.

**The fighting.** The peasantry here fight with billhooks and a strong sense of grievance, and Ashmore's own fighters come in one form, the **Ashmore Line**: russet pennants and a long ragged charge of those big southern horses with lances couched, lords' sons in the saddle, very pretty, very expensive, and able to come down on a flank like a landslide of money. The fair answer is spears, set, on ground the horses cannot take at speed. Everything else the Hollow needs killed is arranged by contract, usually by not sending someone their grain.

## Monsters on the Roads

**The beasts.** Mice, in the quantities that have shaped the region's history, as above. Eels and herons work the ditches and red cattle the water-meadows. Everything that eats a mouse lives well here, and everything that eats a red cow will walk a long way to do it.

**The monsters.** Monsters move, and they live across the whole of the Inner World. The Hollow is the softest ground in the North, so everything hungry eventually comes to it. Reaching Briars (T4 on the Bestiary's Danger Tier, which runs from T1 to T9; Well-spawn hedges with a green knot over the root-crown) creep out of the woods and are burned by the parish. Greater Wolves come down in the Thin Weeks, and in a dry year a wyvern from the Ridge will take a red cow in front of the whole village. South of the valley the barns have arrow-slits, for what walks up from the Thresh as well as for armies.

### The Malt Deeps

The [[The Bestiary/Dungeons|dungeon]] (what a Wellspring Core makes around itself, or what grows up around one) is **the Malt Deeps** under Barleyford, old malting caves that went down too far and turned out to be a Verdantia Well (the Wellspring of growth). Hired Thornwall axes cleared them in the 540s at a cost Ashmore still disputes. It is a worked Core now, kept by brewers on an Ashmore license, its hours sold to bracket schools, and the air down there will sprout a dropped crust while you watch. The galleries glow a clear chlorophyll green and smell of cut grass over the sweet smoke, the old mash-tuns creak as their staves go on growing, and a brewer's apprentice spends her first year cutting the walls back with a hooked knife. The parish hedges that have to be burned are, by general belief, cousins of what lives down there, and the brewers deny this in writing. Barleyford also denies that any of it has to do with the small beer. Thornwall sends its bill for the clearing every spring, and Ashmore sends a very polite receipt. More on the [[The Bestiary/Dungeons|Dungeons]] shelf and in the [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Inner World — A Gazetteer|Gazetteer]].

### The Scourge and the Turn

No land of the Banner Houses lies closer to the [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Scourge of Hell|Scourge of Hell]] than the Hollow, with only the marching country and the salt basin between its last barn and the Thresh, the cutters' name for the dry country on the rim. In the herds' season, the late summer of a dry year, the wind on the southern parishes comes round with iron and pollen in it, right in among the smell of the harvest, and every reeve south of Ashbury goes up to look at the arrow-slits. Haze-kites (T4, membrane and hunger, the color of an old copper pot) have ridden that wind clean over the Salt Line and taken mules off the salt road in sight of a barn. The rim towns answer this sort of thing with bells on stakes and a great deal of paint. The Hollow answers it with a tally.

The Turn is the old rotation by which North, South and Center each spend nine years keeping the Scourge's monsters off everybody else, and the Ashmores have never lost one in their history, which is the plainest true thing anyone has said about them. Whoever holds the Turn may buy victuals at a fixed price, and every host that goes down either road buys at the junction below Ashbury at exactly that price, by the sack, and at this point the Hollow's favorite punchline applies. On the North's own Turn, Ashmore enters its grain in the Count (the running bill the three parties present to one another) as supplied at the fixed price and then allocates it to the other houses as usual, which has not improved feeling about the pie. The Ashmore Line itself has ridden down to the Hem on a Northern Turn, the Hem being the Scourge's outer belt, and it was very pretty, and it came home short a great many expensive horses. The Center has the Turn until 721, so for now the hosts at the junction are only the hungry ones going by, and a clerk with a copy of the Count has a desk in the Corn Hall.

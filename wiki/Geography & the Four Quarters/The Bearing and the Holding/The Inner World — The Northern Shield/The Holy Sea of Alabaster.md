# The Holy Sea of Alabaster

**Say it** · AL-uh-bas-ter

*The White Shelf · The Bench of the South · The Country That Counts*

The sale of Tobit Gurnard stopped at the second bid. It was the Feast of Saint Ebb in the spring of 683, the Rimward quay was hung end to end with blue and saffron pennons, sardines were frying on every bollard, and the wreck-merchant Gaspard Vauquelin had just sworn before the harbor bench that a storm put his galley, the *Good Intent*, and forty men on the watchtower rock, and that his sea-lenders therefore owed him eleven hundred silver. He was raising a little of it in the meantime by selling off the one oarsman who had crawled ashore. Tobit Gurnard, standing on the block with a rope on his neck and a gap where his front teeth had been, told the whole quay in a voice trained on a rowing bench that there had been no storm, that he had watched Vauquelin's man douse the tower lamp and hang a false one on the point, and that he would like to say so on the Walk.

The Archdeacon of Rimward, who was Vauquelin's brother, offered to swear the contrary. So the whole town went up the Stair behind them, fishwives and notaries and Serpentkin clerks and a Hobgoblin rope-seller with her stock still round her shoulders, and by noon the bookies had the Archdeacon at three to one. On the Sounding Walk, the long white pavement above the bay, both men took off their shoes. The Archdeacon swore in beautiful Latin and stepped back, and his soles left the limestone as dry as they found it. The oarsman swore in dockside Common with two words the clerk declined to enter, and under his bare feet the stone went dark and wet in the exact shape of his toes, as though he had that moment climbed out of a bath, and the prints stayed there till sundown with half of Alabaster filing past to look. Vauquelin hanged on the point where he had hung the lamp.

Then the auctioneer, who had been waiting patiently with his book, pointed out that Tobit Gurnard had no line in any register of the Holy Sea. His testimony had been witnessed and filed. His person had never been counted. The Manumission Doctrine protects the counted, and the sale would now resume. The roar they heard across the bay is on record. The sardine women passed their aprons, the quay and the Stair and a good part of the bench emptied their purses into them, and a Lambert clerk who happened to be going by counted thirty-seven silver aloud and corrected a bystander by two coppers. They bought him, walked him up to the Archive with a drum in front and had him written in, *Gurnard, Tobit, oarsman, counted*, before the lamps were lit. He spent his next twenty years as the most expensive professional witness in the south. They sell plaster casts of those footprints on the Walk today, and there are enough of them to pave it twice.

The Holy Sea is a white city that weighs what people swear on a stone that has never stopped listening, rules everyone through the registers it keeps, and keeps a family of counters at its gate to check the words against the sacks.

Foreigners expect the name to mean water. It has that too, and it will charge you for the view.

## The Land and the Look

Come in by the bay if you can afford the harbor dues. **Alabaster** stacks up out of blue water in tier after tier of pale wall, its roofs red and rust, its towers spired, the whole palace-city climbing toward clouds that pile up gray-blue and rose over a ring of misty mountains. An arched stone bridge leaps the inlet at its foot, and out in the water a lone watchtower stands on its rock with the gulls going round it. The roofs are red because the clay is local. Everything the Court itself owns is white and ink-black, the colors of the house that counts for it, and a white wall with a black balance painted on the door means that someone inside has a right to ask your name.

Below the city runs the **white shelf**: cliffs the color of milk, dropping into water so clear the fishing boats seem to hang in the air with their shadows lying on the sand beneath them. On either flank the bay is drowned country. Something struck this shelf about 310 IC, the low land on both sides went under the sea and the shelf held, and on a still morning the sponge divers can show you the tops of walls. Whose hand it was is argued; two Measurewrights file it as an unrelated coastal collapse, and the [[Factions, Bloodlines & Institutions/Houses and Bloodlines/The Archaic Bloodlines/The Moto Bloodline/The Long Reading — Moto Ancient History|Long Reading]] calls it something else.

Behind the shelf the ground climbs through lemon terraces, goat hill and thin chalky barley into the **land marches**, which run north to the edge of the [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Jade Plains of Qingyuan|Grey Plain]]. Higher still, inland and up, the shelf meets old hard rock and the frost hardly breaks. Up there stand broken walls, a well and a gatepost, the old Kōkan seat, where the residue lies so dense that a practitioner with the wrong kind of Soul Crystal cannot lay a hand on a wall without reading what happened against it. Pilgrims are advised not to. Pilgrims go anyway. The north's surveyors put the ground the Moto came from somewhere else entirely, west past the Slate Ridge, and that is one of the honest quarrels on the [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Inner World — A Gazetteer#Where the Maps Quarrel|Gazetteer's table]], since nobody able to read both places has been let near both.

All of this stands at the very bottom of the South, where the land finishes its long green steps down to the sea, with [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Copper Coast of Haeseong|the Copper Coast]] warm along the shore to the east and the Jade Plains and the Long Fields between the land marches and the Center. The country is poor in grain and rich in everything that can be written down.

## The Stone That Listens

The Holy Sea stands on the ground where the Moto once held the Long Reading, the seat where for four thousand years a contract sealed held anywhere because the ground itself witnessed it. Two kings once brought forty thousand spears to the foot of these cliffs and fought the Battle of Sorrel Water on a bench at the top of the Stair, which is the kind of thing that happened here. The Moto are gone from it. The bench is still there, and clerks eat their lunch on it.

What remains is the thing the Church built on. The Court calls the Aether under the shelf *Attestis*, a word found in its own Latin and on no Guild list of the Sixty, and every Measurewright who has sounded it gives a different account of which Wellspring it really is (a Wellspring being one of the sixty fixed types of Aether, the raw power practitioners pull from the world around them). The most respected reading is Thaloré, the Wellspring of Depth, under which emotional truth acquires a physical weight: testimony weighs density times depth, and the vessel it came from does not enter the sum. The Sōhai, the old Moto faith, hold that the whole of it is the residue of a Moto working that broke and never stopped running, that the Church discovered no Wellspring and built a doctrine on a scar, and that Sancta Lux is a tenant in someone else's house. The Church has heard this. It has filed it.

**How the Walk works.** A witness stands barefoot on the limestone of the Sounding Walk and swears aloud. The oath goes into the stone. A sworn account the swearer holds true, deep down and with their whole weight, draws water up through the rock and leaves two dark footprints that last till sundown; a clerk sets an assay fork (a tuned brass fork that rings to the Aether it touches) on the flag and reads how deep the oath went. A false oath leaves the stone dry. In the street, a liar is *dry-footed*, and a woman who wants to insult her sister's husband asks after the state of his socks.

**What it cannot do.** The Walk weighs what you hold true, and what you hold true may be a mistake, and that is the whole reason the Lamberts have a job. An honest fool leaves lovely footprints. The Sea measures testimony; the Lamberts measure everything the testimony is about. And a person whose name is in a mark the instrument cannot classify returns no reading at all, which the bench may not lawfully treat as true, false or anything else. See the Recension, below, and do not bring it up at dinner.

## Who Rules

**The Palatine** holds the Sea from the **Alabaster Court**, at the top of the Stair. The present one, Aurélien Cassard, is a small, courteous, nearly blind old man who has his registers read aloud to him while he eats and is said to catch more errors that way. Below him sit the Court's benches, each pairing a cleric with a Lambert: the cleric weighs the oath, the Lambert counts the facts, and the two of them have been quarreling over the same table for centuries. The Church of the Holy Sea keeps the saints and the Archive. Court Latin is its working tongue, and every lord who comes before it gets a Latin name he did not ask for.

**The Veil-Saints** do the work no one else wants. A continental working collapsed across this ground, the souls of tens of thousands died mid-oath on it, and the passage of the dead here has not gone right since. So the coast's holiest office is a trade: Veil-Saints perform their Sacraments over the dying so that the soul crosses without fragmenting, and you can hire one by the night and you will want to book ahead in plague years.

**The chapter hold** of [[Factions, Bloodlines & Institutions/Orders, Faiths and Cults/Sancta Lux|Sancta Lux]] stands inside the walls, reports past the Palatine to the Grand Church at Altherion, and politely declines to explain what it reports. A petition sent to the Sea is therefore read twice, once by a Court with reason to listen and once by an order with reason not to.

**House Lambert** are the hands that do the weighing and the only people in the Sea allowed a sword by right. They have held Sallowgate and the land marches for the Palatines since before the current line of Palatines could count. Their words are *Count True*, their colors white and ink-black with a balance on the field. A Lambert who gives a false count is struck from the house, the house keeps the list, and the list is a real object you can ask to see. The present Warlord of the Lamberts and Keeper of Sallowgate is **Léonard Lambert**, thirty-four, called Sword of the Bench, entered at Court as Leonardus Lambertus, Custos Portae Salicis. He is a tall man with wild gold curls and a clear green eye, in gilded harness under a scarlet cloak with the Sallowgate bear on his breast, and he came to the seat at twenty-nine when his father died on the Grey Plain with the count of his own dead still in his mouth. His father and grandfather fought with Crystals that never woke (a Soul Crystal being the organ of a practitioner's power), reading blows before they were thrown with no faculty to read them with, and were feared for it; many Lamberts are born that way, nobody outside the house knows how many, and the house knows. He has told three generals their numbers to their faces, and two of them listened. *"I do not doubt your courage, my lord. I am only telling you how many of you there are."*

## Cities and Towns

**Alabaster** is the capital, sixty thousand souls by the only census in the south anyone believes, and most of them are on a stair. The **Stair of Testimony** climbs from the bay to the Court in eleven hundred steps, with a shop on every landing, and the **Bridge of Notaries**, the great arched span, carries a double row of booths where you can have anything sworn, sealed or copied in nine languages while the tide goes under you. The **Sounding Walk** was laid after the shelf held, and four centuries of bare feet have worn the polish off its flags. The **Archive**, cut back into the shelf behind the Court, is the most complete register of births, deaths and contracts in the Inner World; its works are records, with no machinery anywhere, and the records outlast everything. Out on its rock stands the **Lone Clerk**, the watchtower, where a harbor scribe has counted every hull entering the bay for six hundred years and where, since the *Good Intent*, two men hold the lamp and watch each other.

**Rimward** is the harbor, down at the bay's western arm, all sail lofts, slave-blocks, sponge boats and galley sheds, a loud salt town that thinks the upper city is a pack of hypocrites and supplies it with fish. It is no relation of the Rimward among the free cities of Nalūn, and each will tell you the other stole the name. Half the tavern arguments in both concern whether the Sea's theology or the Yukari's arithmetic is the better reason for treating a Beastkin like a person, and these arguments end in the usual way.

**Sallowgate** is the landward gate, a fat walled town where the salt road's western branch comes in, with sallow willows thick along its ditch and a stone bear rearing over the arch that every passing soldier touches for luck. Here the tariffs are taken, the land-march levies drill, and every child of the house, woken or not, learns the Sallowgate Answer, the counter-cut that turns in the hand before your blade has left its line. The tariff-hall has the South's most accurate water clock, and the town's favorite spectator sport is watching a merchant discover what he actually declared.

## Wealth and Trade

The Sea sells certainty, by the page. Trade routes ran to Alabaster long before the Church, because the alternative was doing business without knowing what your counterparty was, and they run there still: registers of birth and title, sworn copies, sealed contracts, adjudications between foreigners who trust nobody else. Deposit-and-lending runs through the Archive's strongrooms too, which the free cities of Nalūn consider a sin and the Sea considers a service. The Sea has in turn maintained for four hundred years that Nalūn's distinction between lending and interest is a distinction without a difference, and it has maintained this while keeping its own money at Ironlink Hold, in the care of a chapel with one cleric in it whose primary function appears to be minding the money.

Alabaster also inherits jurisdiction over southern phosphate law, because the Marrowchalk barges (bone-rock that doubles a wheat yield and also raises the dead) all come through its lawyers. The Court has twice tried to establish that the two uses can be legally told apart at the point of sale, and has twice failed. Otherwise: lemons, salt fish, sponges, white cheese, good parchment and pilgrims. Grain comes in by sea and costs too much, and the north has been told so in writing.

## Rent, Food and the Street

Rent rises with the Stair. A landing near the Court costs a Guild journeyman's wage for a room the width of his arms, and the Laundry Steps at the bottom, wet and singing all day with washerwomen slapping linen on the stone, house six families to a floor at well under the [[Geography & the Four Quarters/Value, Coin and Trade/What Things Cost|city list]]. Bread is the price everyone watches. People eat sardines off a skewer, goat cheese with lemon salt, chickpea cakes, barley soup with fish heads, and on feast days a sweet white almond paste molded into the shape of a balance, which children eat from the pan that holds the lies.

The crowd is the most mixed in the Inner World, because the ground will not distinguish one race's oath from another's. Serpentkin scribes, Catkin sailors, Tinker Goblins and Eressean pilgrims jostle a Hobgoblin rope-seller on the same landing. Petty arts are everywhere and paid by the job: foot-washers on the Walk who scrub your soles for a copper so that the print reads clean, hedge-workers selling dry-foot charms that have never once worked, professional witnesses in good shoes, lamp-wardens who keep the worked lights on the Stair and walk off it at festival time for double pay. Two bracket schools run the city's fights like a pair of rival parishes, the Whites of the Upper Stair and the Inks of the Laundry Steps, and on bracket days a clerk's son in a white sash will cheerfully break a washerwoman's son's nose and then enter it in the proper form. Dockers call the aura coat the shine and Pressure the weight, and clerks say Reinforcement and enter the same broken nose under another heading.

## Law and How It Fights

**The bench.** Everything is decided by the sworn account and the true count, in public, with fair copies posted on the church doors. A tribunal of examiners also keeps a graded catalog of the corruptions a soul can fall into, down to the Corruption of Non-Commitment, which is innocence through distance, the man who says "it is complicated" while the preventable catastrophe rolls past him, and which the rest of the south finds hilarious until it is read out with their name on it.

**The Manumission Doctrine** extends legal protection through theology to populations the surrounding world considers disposable. It is the proudest thing in the Sea, and the anti-discrimination law behind it is a property of the ground rather than an ethical position anyone could revoke. It also reaches only what the Archive holds. The ground does not distinguish the testimony of a cleric from the testimony of a man being sold, and files both.

**The Recension** is the part that must be understood exactly. Some two to four generations before the present, the Church reformed its register, sincerely and competently, to correct accumulated drift and bring it into line with what the stone would witness. The clerics read their error rates aloud in open chapter. It improved the instrument and everyone involved was proud of it. It also drew a hard line where a soft one had stood, and the hinterland families of the old Kōkan remnant, whose names ran four hundred thousand years back in a mark no instrument could classify, fell outside it. An unclassifiable mark returns no reading, a person who returns no reading has not been witnessed, and a person who has not been witnessed has not been counted. Nobody in the Holy Sea decided to enslave anyone. Somebody decided the register was inaccurate.

**War.** The Sea's levies are small, drilled, and paid on the day, a rare enough thing in the south that good men queue for it. Lambert officers tell a general his own numbers before battle, out loud, in front of his captains, and do not soften them. Crossbow companies from the march towns hold the walls and the Stair, at a few coppers a bolt, and a Stair full of crossbows has discouraged more than one high-Stage visitor who could have flattened the gate. The Court fights its larger wars with paper: an excommunication from the registers, a refusal to witness, a line struck from a title. When it needs a wall of spears it hires one from [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Ochre Fields of Hammerford|Hammerford]], and audits it.

## What They Joke About

The Sea's people are funny the way bookkeepers at a wake are funny. They tell the one about the Palatine who died and was turned back at the gate of the next world for a discrepancy. They say that a Rimward man will swear to anything and a Stair man will charge you for it. They ask what a Lambert says at his own wedding ("how many of you are there?"). Of the Yan they say that a horse there has better papers than a duke, and of the Yukari that a crow will sell you your own secrets and enter the sale. Of northerners they say only that they write very short petitions.

## Monsters on the Roads

The parish has a form for most of it. Zombies (T2) get up in fields dressed too heavily with Marrowchalk, and the form has a box for what the thing was called when it was a farmer. Sirens (T4) work the rocks off the point, and two men hold the Lone Clerk's lamp and watch each other. Sea Serpents (T6) are a bad year's news. Beasts do the ordinary daily harm: gray wolves come down off the frost line into the land marches when the snow lies late, rock sharks follow the sponge boats along the drowned walls, and the gulls of the Stair will have a sardine out of your fingers, and a finger if you are slow.

**The dungeon.** The ground under the shelf is the great working site, which the Court calls *Attestis*, a word on no Guild list, and which Measurewrights read most respectably as Thaloré, the Wellspring of Depth. It is why a true oath wets the stone. The dungeon is **the Drowned Parishes**, the sunken low land on both sides of the bay since the blow of about 310 IC. The church bells are still in their towers, and in the autumn gales they swing, and Rimward's sponge boats stay home. Mourning Pike (T5, Well-spawn bred in Fluxia water, teal over the skull) hold the naves, the souls of tens of thousands died mid-oath on that ground, and the Veil-Saints will tell you why they charge what they do. Nobody has cleared a single parish, though the Archive still holds the deeds, and about once a generation some heir buys a sworn copy, hires a crew on the Rimward quay and goes down to collect the rent. More on the [[The Bestiary/Dungeons|Dungeons]] shelf and in the [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Inner World — A Gazetteer|Gazetteer]].

**The Scourge and the Turn.** The [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Scourge of Hell|Scourge of Hell]] lies far up at the other end of the South, past the Long Fields and the whole climb of the land, and nobody on the Stair has ever smelled its wind. The Sea pays for it anyway. Under [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Scourge of Hell#The Turn|the Turn]], the old wheel by which North, South and Center each spend nine years keeping the Scourge's monsters off the other two, the South's share falls on the old Dynasty's realms, this white shelf and the hired spears of Hammerford. The Sea fights its share with a pen: a Southern Turn runs by the season on subscription lists opened at Alabaster and posted on the church doors, every parish and guild with its sum beside its name, so that a stingy one can read about itself in a fair hand. Lambert officers go north with the companies to audit the spears and tell the captain-general his numbers each morning, and the Veil-Saints book out for a Turn as they do for a plague year. The South last kept the Line from 703 to 712, and the Archive is still adding it up.

Every Turn ends in a Count, audited at Sealhall and accepted by nobody. The Archive holds the South's copies, and the most complete register in the Inner World has looked for four centuries for the original of the arrangement without finding it, which it takes as a personal insult. Three clerks of three regions have now carried their three Counts up to the Sounding Walk and sworn to them barefoot. The three figures cannot all be right, and all three men left lovely wet feet, which the Court has asked people to stop bringing up.

## Neighbors and Grudges

**[[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Vermilion Dynasty of Sum-gol|Sum-gol]].** The Sea paid the old empire in records and never in soldiers, and it was a Lambert clerk who read the bill at the last Calling. It keeps the Dynasty's old rolls, and has had a very polite letter about them from the Post.

**[[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Silver Cedars of Yukari|The Silver Cedars of Yukari]].** Close by marriage: Senri Yukari took a daughter of House Lambert to wife, and Lord Naiser is half Sallowgate. The Sea arrives at equality through theology and the Yukari lands arrive at it through empiricism. The disagreement is genuine, respectful and permanent, because neither side can refute the other's reasoning without abandoning its own.

**The Kingdom of Kharven.** Distant, and doctrinally entangled in a way no trade ledger records, since its crown descends from the people who once held this stone, which makes every exchange an inheritance question wearing a diplomatic coat. Léonard Lambert has a standing grievance with the Palatine that nobody north of the Moto border will read a petition from the Sea, and his cousins at Hallenfeld write that nobody is sitting in the red chair to read one. *Later hand:* within a generation of this page the north is the Northern Empire of Whitemere, and the Archive's first response was to rule a new column.

**The free cities of Nalūn.** Money-keepers to the Sea and its favorite theological scandal, and the feeling is mutual; the chain-cities regard the reconciliation of the two positions as an achievement of ecclesiastical reasoning, with real professional respect.

**The Iron Mandate of Undaar-Keth.** The Manumission Doctrine and the Mandate's Ironblood Clause are two different answers to one problem, how to protect a population everyone else considers disposable. The clerics find the Mandate morally inadequate, the commanders find the Sea militarily irrelevant, and each is correct and incomplete.

**Sancta Lux.** The Sea measures testimony, the order measures everything else, and both hold that a measurement that does not drift is worth more than a judgment that does. Warm and distant, and neither party has left the other alone in a room since the chapter hold was seated.

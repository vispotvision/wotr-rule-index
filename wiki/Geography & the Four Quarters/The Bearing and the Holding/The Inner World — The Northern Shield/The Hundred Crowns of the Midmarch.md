# The Hundred Crowns of the Midmarch

*The Center's patchwork of petty kings, and the bridge that all the armies have to cross*
> *There have never been a hundred. The Guild survey found eighty-three in 710 and a hundred and seventeen in 712, and in the second count the surveyor was crowned at Pennycrown on his way home.*
>
> *Note in a Measurewright's circuit book*

King Gerent Tuppence the Third held the whole of his kingdom in nineteen arches of pale stone, from the pier at the south wharf to the pier at the north, and on the morning of the feast of Veyra in the year 662 he had his chair carried out to the crown of the eleventh arch, with the crown on his head and the cash-box on his knees, because two armies had sent word that they intended to cross him. From the south came six thousand Marrick spears in ochre and black. From the north came the host of the Banner Houses, red and gold and painted to the eyebrows, each bound for the far bank. Neither side had a quarrel with Kingsbridge, and each side's marshal arrived at his own end of the bridge, looked at the toll-board, and asked what a man would have to pay to keep the other one off it.

Gerent sold both of them the bridge. He sold the Marrick marshal the south nine arches and the northern marshal the north nine, with a sealed bill for each and the eleventh reserved to the Crown, and by noon the two armies had met head on in a roadway twelve paces wide, with the king's chair lifted out onto a mason's cradle over the water so as not to obstruct the proceedings. Every longship and barge on the Aldwater came to watch. The whole city climbed out along the wharves in its feast-day colors, the bookies went up and down the moored boats on planks, and a Marrick captain named Sella Drax, coated in a yellow you could read by, held the ninth arch alone for the time it took a bell to ring twice, with the dead going over the parapet on both sides of her. His Majesty's bailiffs fished them out and billed for it, under an old by-law, as refuse in the navigable channel. Two thousand men died on that bridge for the right to be on the other end of it. The eldest of Gerent's sons, who had gone out with the Standing Army to keep the eleventh arch clear, died there too, on his father's side of the line, with his crown-prince's circlet driven through his eyebrow, and the king did not get up from his chair until the fighting was over. Both armies were gone by harvest. He had taken in more that day than his ancestors had in a hundred years, and he rebuilt the eleventh arch with it, and his son's name is on every stone.

That is the Midmarch. A hundred-odd kings, each sure he is the rightful one, sitting across every road between the North and the South and charging the passing wars by the head. The bill is usually paid in sons. Its people are not much troubled by it and will tell you they have the best songs in the world.

---

## The Land and the Look

The Midmarch is the crowded country along both roads around [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Scourge of Hell|the Scourge of Hell]], thickest on the Kessen road down the west flank and thinner on the salt road up the east, river plain and low green hill, with more castles per mile than anywhere else in the world and nearly all of them small. You can stand on one keep's roof and count nine others, and every one of them flies something loud: scarlet bulls, blue-and-yellow checks, a gold goose on purple, three fish and a ladder. Herald-painters are a respected class and are paid before the masons. Between the keeps run hedgerows, water meadows and barley, and every lane has a chain across it with someone sitting beside the chain.

North of it are the salt basin and the Banner Houses' southern marches. South the two roads run on to Sum-gol and the Long Fields. Nobody lives in the middle and nobody goes there. The Aldwater runs down the middle of the crowded western half, broad and brown and full of traffic, and most of the country's quarrels are about who owns which reach of the river.

There is no capital and nobody wants one. There are three places everyone has heard of.

### Kingsbridge

Kingsbridge is the most painted sight in the Center, and its great stormy sky appears to be a local product. The bridge carries the Kessen road long across the Aldwater on huge arches, wide enough for a marching column, and behind it the pale stone quarters stack up the height one on top of another to a vast green-roofed minster, whose copper the rain has turned the color of an old coin. Longships and barges crowd the wharves three deep, and the wharves roar with cranes, fishwives and the men who get paid to shout about both.

**A king's whole kingdom is that bridge.** The city on the height and the wharves on the bank belong in law to their own town council and to a bishop, and the Tuppence crown ends at the last stone of each pier, as generations of bailiffs have discovered while chasing people. So the House of Tuppence collects, and it does not govern anything but traffic. Every morning the entire Standing Army, about two hundred men in red with red banners, marches the kingdom from end to end, with a drum, and is home for dinner. The minster is **the Scale Minster**, a Sancta Lux house, and its bishop hears crown claims from all over the Midmarch and has the busiest court in the country, since Sancta Lux hears legitimacy and the Midmarch produces it by the barrowload. The present king is Gerent's grandson, also a Tuppence, who is pleasant to everyone and has the tolls in writing.

### Tollgarth

Tollgarth is a castle-town shut across the one good pass in the Harl Hills, held for three generations by Tinker Goblin kings, and currently by Queen Tizz Copperwick the Fourth, three foot five in her boots, with one arm of her own and one of her own design. Most Tinker Goblins do not live past forty, the Essence running too hot for the frame, so a Tollgarth reign is short, and the coronation feast is usually paid for by the same bill as the last funeral. Every gate is a contraption. The Self-Minding Portcullis has killed four kings and one horse, the toll is collected by a machine that makes change only when it feels like it, and the whole town smells of burned vitriol and soldering copper. Merchants pay gladly, because the alternative road adds a week, and because watching a wagon go through is free. Her subjects are goblins, humans and a large contingent of people with burn scars who came for the wages.

### Pennycrown

Pennycrown is where you go to become a king. It is a town of goldsmiths, heralds and notaries, on the old fair-ground where, as the story goes, the heir to a lost crown bought a replacement for a penny and could not be shown to have done anything wrong. The law of the Midmarch, such as it is, says a crown is lawful if three neighboring crowns attend the coronation, or two and a Measurewright. Shops on Crown Row sell them at every price, from solid gold to a stamped copper ring for one penny, which by long custom is what a landless younger son wears until he has done something about it. **The Coat-Turning** is held here every tenth summer. It is a three-week fair at which the kings of the Midmarch, in full heraldry, announce whose side they are on for the next decade, and bookies from four quarters take odds on the order. The sides are mostly North and South. It settles nothing, and everyone comes.

## Who Rules

Everybody and nobody. A Midmarch crown may hold a county, a valley, a mill, a stretch of towpath or, in one case, a well. Human kings are the most common and the loudest, usually descended from a northern sergeant or a southern marshal's bastard who stayed. **Tinker Goblin kings** hold the workshops, the Harl passes and a surprising number of bridges, and their people have a saying that their reigns are shorter and their monarchs more thoroughly felt. **The Chimeric-Born** hold the Stitch Hills, a kingdom-in-many-pieces founded by people the Accord has no coherent policy on and therefore no way of evicting. King Ambrose Fivefold, who rules there now, has the Binding Scars that hold his body together gilded for court, and three neighbors have tried to get his coronation annulled on the grounds that he was assembled. He has so far won every case and every field. A few Beastkin hold crowns too, by the same right of nobody being able to stop them, and the Charter's clerks enter them as *nemo, rex*.

Kings owe one another fealty in chains that cross themselves. A man may be overlord of his overlord in a different county. Sancta Lux's Ember Magistrates ride circuit hearing who is whose. The Guild Accord reaches in patches: the stretches from the Brine crossing down to the marches of Sum-gol signed the first circle at Kharven-Seat, along with several kings who have since been deposed by others who say they never agreed. Past that line the Articles are a custom a merchant may take or leave, though the market court and the dusty foot are so handy that most towns keep them anyway.

---

## Wealth and Trade

Tolls first and always. Then what travels the Aldwater: wool, barley, Marrowhold's iron, Nalūn's salt, and wine going north. Then war itself, the largest local industry, which pays the Midmarch at both ends: victuals sold to the host coming up, plunder bought from it going home, and free companies hired out on the Thresh contracts to anyone with a monster problem. The great herds of the Scourge, the stilts and greywalkers and the things in the dry beds nobody has named, come up into Midmarch barley some years, and a bounty posted on a Midmarch door is cried for ten leagues.

**Heraldry** is the next trade. Painters, embroiderers, banner-makers, trumpeters, heralds and genealogists do better here than anywhere in the world, and a good pedigree is written to order. The minstrels of the Midmarch sell their songs up and down both roads, get the details wrong, and have done more for some kings than their armies have.

Nearly every crown owes the factor houses of Nalūn, and a third of the country's wars come from fighting over who inherits the debt.

---

## Rent, Bread and the Street

Rent goes to whoever's man turned up, and in a contested valley he may turn up twice. A Midmarch peasant keeps a chest of old tax receipts from dead kings against the day one of them is found to have been lawful. Coin is anybody's, and the money-changer at every crossroads will tell you what a Tollgarth mark or a Pennycrown penny is worth this week, which is how much metal it has in it, and he has a small hammer for finding out.

They eat better than the Charter does, since food is the thing armies pay for: barley bread and river eels, smoked ham from the hill kingdoms, big onions, apple beer. Market days run one town after another so nobody can miss one. Every town has a bracket ground, a sect in a loud shirt and a fierce opinion. The Sixth Arch Boys of Kingsbridge, in red and white, and the Harl Hill Scorchers of Tollgarth, in black and copper, have fought each other at every midsummer for ninety years over a bet about a goose, and when you meet a fighter from the Midmarch they will tell you whose, before they tell you their name.

The street's word for the aura coat is **colors** ("he's showing his colors," or, of a fighter nearly out of reserve, the Essence a fighter holds to spend, "his colors have run"). Pressure is **the crown**, and a strong fighter who walks into an inn is said to have put it on. Sensing is **the herald**, as in "my herald tells me you're lying about your Stage." Ordinary petty arts are everywhere and cheap: the woman who can make a chain across a lane too heavy for anyone but her to lift, the boy who knows by the smell which king's men are coming and sells it, the toll-sitter who cannot be lied to about what is in a cart until his fee has been paid. All of them can be bought, and all of them have been known to change sides by supper.

Greet someone with "Who's king today?" The answer is a joke unless it is not.

---

## Law and How It Fights

Every crown has its own law, and a farmer who crosses a ditch may cross a jurisdiction. Justice is mostly by toll, fine and the king's bad mood, and a traveler carries his plea to the nearest market court if he has any sense. A king can hang you in his kingdom, and if his kingdom is small you may be able to see the safe side from the gallows.

War between crowns is a sport with casualties. Levies turn out behind the banners for forty days and go home for harvest. Harness is mixed and much repainted. Spears do most of the work, crossbows do the killing, and a well-placed bolt has ended more Midmarch reigns than any Domain (the ground in which a high-Stage practitioner's own law holds), at about three copper apiece. Goblin kings bring contraptions that misfire in funny and terrible ways, and everyone stands well back from their own side. Kings who are also practitioners fight each other in person, with their titles shouted out in full first, and a small dispute may be settled in a tourney ring by champions with the whole county looking on. Yielding is allowed. Killing is forbidden, and it still happens.

When a real army comes through, the kings fall over themselves to join it. Then they sell its road to the other side.

---

## What They Joke About

That there are a hundred of them. That any of them is rightful. The Standing Army of Kingsbridge being home for dinner. "Long live whoever" is the loyal toast in about half the county, and in the other half they mean it. A man who has been promised something is said to have a Pennycrown deed. There are several hundred songs about a king whose kingdom is a mill, who went to war with the king downstream by stopping the water, and in all of them he wins in a different way. Northerners, they say, have one king and can't tell you why, and Southerners used to have one and can't get over it. They are terrified of the Moss Court and won't joke about that, since the last Midmarch host to go in as far as Rootgate is now mostly a very good stand of alder.

## Monsters on the Roads

The great herds south of the salt, the stilts and greywalkers and the things in the dry beds, come up into Midmarch barley some years, and a bounty on a Midmarch door is cried for ten leagues. A stilt stands on legs as long as a ship's mast, a greywalker comes after it in a file at dusk, and between them they have ruined more kings than the Coat-Turning. Barley and monsters are the country's two great local harvests. The beasts are those of any fat, fought-over plain: Greater Wolves follow armies, boar root up the dead, and every king's geese have been eaten by every other king's foxes. Manticores (T5 on the Bestiary's Danger Tier, which runs from T1 to T9 and prices every bounty) have been crowned in at least one valley, by a herald who needed the fee, and its tax receipts still trade.

The dungeon is **Nether Marl**, the only kingdom in the world that is wholly a dungeon: a labyrinth under a hill with a Minotaur (T5) at the Lower Workings, whose title King Hamo the Undefeated bought at Pennycrown along with a stamped copper crown. He has never gone below the Mouth. He sells licenses at the door, charges a toll going in and a much larger one coming out, and has legally been at war with whatever is down there for twenty-six years.

Nether Marl is only the loudest. **The Orchard Under Goosegrass** at Hollowhay, a Verdantia ground (the Wellspring of growth) whose nine galleries of apple trees stand in leaf and fruit in every month of the year, was won by thirty crossbows and a Sealwright and then lost to its own keepers, a family called Ambrey, over three seasons' back pay. They let the great gallery for weddings, and it is booked until 719. A crew found a Mouth (a dungeon's first band and its way down) under a tannery in Pennycrown, and the tanner discovered that he owned a dungeon. By the custom of the Center a clearing venture takes a third of the hours at the pool for nine years, the **clearers' third**, and by the law of the dead leg the fine on a lapsed gallery lands on its owner, "whoever that now is," which is why several kings of this country have turned out to own a catastrophe by marriage. ## Beside the Scourge

Everybody in the Midmarch lives with their back to it. [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Scourge of Hell|The Scourge of Hell]] fills the center of the continent, nobody marches through it, every army in history has used the same two roads around it, and the kings of the Midmarch are sitting on both. On the Kessen side the Hem (the Scourge's outermost belt, fifteen leagues of black-barked thornwood and pale dust) lies east of the last hedges, and on the eastern patches along the salt road it lies to the west. In the late summer of a dry year the wind off it comes hot and smelling of iron, the herds come out on that wind, and that is the week the barley goes. A petty king posts a bounty on his doors, hires a free company, and now and then loses a castle.

**Lastpenny**, on the Kessen road, is the edge town the whole country claims. At a quarter before noon on Midsummer Day of 676 the biggest earth wyrm anyone living had seen came up through its cheese stalls, in front of eleven thousand northern and southern spears who were waiting for noon to decide whose job it was, while a goblin king the size of a child beat on the northern marshal's stirrup with a ladle. The town has been rebuilt in stone and has kept the awning. The wyrm still comes up along that stretch every eleven years or so, wearing nine hundred spears, and everyone calls it Old Pincushion. At the Noon Fair each Midsummer the whole town stands in the square and turns its back on any soldiers present, and in the Midmarch a northerner or southerner who declines to help you has said *it isn't noon*, and you are entitled to spit. **The Turnstone**, a black pillar on the western rim off the same road, is where a commander handing over the Turn is supposed to hand his successor a broken spear. He generally sends it by courier.

[[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Scourge of Hell#The Turn|The Turn]] itself runs North, then South, then Center, nine years each. Whoever holds it keeps the Line of forts and beacon towers around the Hem, pays the door bounties, answers for every carter lost on the corridors, and may march through anyone's land to do it. So the host passing through on someone else's Turn eats Midmarch barley going up, the castles with the wall knocked in are Midmarch castles, and half the invasions in this country's history began as a Turn that lost its way, which has been shown on maps. The Center holds it now, from 712 to 721. Every king sends nine men and a very loud banner, free companies on Thresh contracts (monster bounties, paid by anyone with a monster problem) do most of the dying, and the betting in Kingsbridge is on who will be standing to hand it over.

Then there are the **sword-pullers**. Near the middle of the Scourge a colossal bronze-hilted sword stands point-down in the mountains, its blade a window on a violet field of stars, and you can make it out from the Hem on perhaps nine clear days in the year. Every few years a young Midmarch king with a good jaw sets out with a following, bookies alongside and a minstrel on retainer, to walk in and draw it. Half the taverns in the rim towns are called the Ordinary Sword and the other half the Other One, after the picture on the sign, a lone figure on a ridge with a plain blade, looking up. The record belongs to a Tinker Goblin named Nixie Sprocket, who got into the Shambles (the fourth belt in, all dead battlefields) with a mule-drawn engine of her own design and sent back a letter describing the bronze as warm. The bookies have never paid out. Pennycrown does a steady trade in crowns for the walk, and it does not give refunds.

## Neighbors and Grudges

**The North and the South** · The customers. The Marrick spears remember the bridge, and so do the Banner Houses, and all of them still use it. The Painted Rivers of Hallenfeld dispute every toll on the upper Aldwater, and the Dynasty of Sum-gol has maps showing the whole country as provinces that have been temporarily mislaid.

**The Sealed Charter of the Concord** · Sells them law at a markup and keeps the worst of the armies off its own roads by leaving them on Midmarch ones. Every king in the country resents this, and most of them send their sons to Sealhall to study it.

**The Amber Cities of Nalūn** · Hold the paper. The kings who pay least cheerfully are the ones with the most tokens, and a Nalūn scribe with a march contract is the most frightening thing a Midmarch army ever meets.

**The Central Warlords** · The Russet Crown takes in the Midmarch's runaways and sells it russet cloth and information. The Rose Coils remember every pedigree anyone has ever forged and will tell you, for a fee, which one of them is yours. The Avian sects of the Violet Peaks scout for whoever pays, and they find the hedgerows very funny from up there.

*Later hand, from the last winter before the turn, running past the year these shelves stand in:* when the Northern Empire of Whitemere reached the Aldwater it was sold the south nine arches. The clerk who wrote this note does not record what it did about that.

# The Scarlet Wood of Thornhaven

**Say it** · THORN-hay-ven

*House Thornwall · The Thornwood · Timber and Trade*

In the thaw of 688 IC the raft-boss Aud Rannsdottir of Thornhaven brought eleven thousand trunks down the Hallen in a single drive, her red-capped raftsmen dancing on the logs with their hook-poles, the river running green and brown and as loud as a market, and at the three-road junction the drive met the Hallenfeld bridge, the bridge's toll-men and a late plate of river ice all at once. The trunks stood up on end. By noon a dam of red timber forty feet high had the whole spring flood stacked behind it, the upper town stood knee-deep, and the Hallenfeld reeve was shouting across the water that she could pay the toll on every log before he would send one man to help. Aud Rannsdottir, who had a coat of aura the color of hot copper and a low opinion of reeves, walked out on the jam in front of both towns, found the one trunk holding all the others by kicking them in turn, bawled the only technique she ever troubled to name, FIRST OF THE MELT, and put her boot through it.

The jam went all at once. It took the bridge, the fish market, the reeve's counting-house and seventeen people who had come down to watch, and Thornwall and Hallenfeld fought over the bill with boar-spears in the road for the rest of that spring, while the loose trunks drifted south past Ashbury and warmed the grain-lords' stoves for a decade. Then winter came. Both towns were short of bread and roofs, and in January the men who had been killing each other in May were splitting wood at one pile, because the alternative was to be found in the spring by whoever was left. Thornhaven still has her boot, on a shelf in the Raftmen's Hall, and it can be viewed for a copper.

The Scarlet Wood is a red forest of trees as tall as a keep, floated downriver by the shipload by merchant-warriors who can tell you what you will run short of six months before you know you need it.

## The Land and What It Looks Like

The Thornwood runs across the hills east of Kharven-Seat and north along the head of the Ironwood March, and nobody forgets it. These are giant ironwoods, red-barked, the color of a fox at the crown and of old dried blood at the root, their trunks wider than a house and going up two hundred feet before the first branch, standing in snow as bright as linen. In the low sun the whole forest turns the color of an open furnace. Under the canopy the light comes down in long gold bars and the air smells of resin and woodsmoke, and every sound comes close until the raft-horns start and drown the lot. The dense stands are too cold for anything but moss and bear, and the Wood's bright little towns sit in the clearings along the rivers.

Those towns are the second thing you notice. Everyone here builds in the wood, because it is there and because it will outlast your grandchildren, and they carve it, with every gable, doorpost and beam-end a knot of beasts and leaves painted red and ocher and verdigris blue, so that a Thornwall hall looks like a chest somebody has spent forty winters decorating. The rivers are thick from thaw to midsummer with **log rafts**, hundreds of feet long, with a cook-shack and a family and a goat on each one.

Where the old road goes in, mist lies under the trees and scarlet leaves blow across everything, and at the head of a flight of worn steps stands a ruined archway of carved stone, older than the house and older than anyone's account of the house, an old clan gate with nothing left of the clan. Long strings of faded cloth flags run off from it between the trunks as far as you can see. They are the loggers' tally, one flag for each great tree taken, and every raft-boss has tied some of them. People call the place the Red Stair.

The forest also has **the long cuts**, narrow straight rides through the deep stands, walled in by trees so old and so close you cannot see the sky. Foresters of the house have kept them open for longer than the house will discuss, and when you ask why, they tell you it is for the timber.

### The major places

- **Thornhaven.** The seat, in the northern March where the Thornwood comes down to the water. A town of carved red halls, the Raftmen's Hall, the cargo-roll house, and on the hill the Thornwall hall itself with its black roof-tree. It is also the best place in the north for fire-enamel: the workshop of the Hale family has fired harness-fronts for lords who should have asked the price first.
- **Raftwick.** The great raft-town at the head of the river-drives, half of it afloat. It shifts its streets every spring and has no reliable map, and you tie up where you can. Raftsmen's brackets are fought on moving logs in spiked boots, and spectators bet from the bank.

**Roads.** By raft in the thaw, down to the Hallenfeld junction and beyond, with a toll fought over every spring. East runs the corridor, road and river-chain, to Collarbone in Marrowhold. North past the last spruce is the Aurora Reach, two weeks by sled from the Whalebone Gather in the hard months. South along the March are the Starks, in the habit of checking the stones.

## House and Rule

**House Thornwall** is merchant-warrior, comfortable with commerce in a way most northern houses find slightly undignified and the Thornwalls consider essential. Their wealth comes from the ironwood stands the Marrowkin import for tool handles and ward-frames, and their function as the house on the Marrowhold corridor gives them an outsized voice in the north's trade. They read cargo rolls the way other houses read battle reports, and consider the ability to predict a supply shortage six months out a more valuable military skill than the ability to hold a shield wall, on the grounds that the shield wall is useless if the men behind it are starving. They are almost always correct about trade, which is why the other houses find them slightly irritating.

The ruler in Year 715 is **Hisae Thornwall**, the Lady, about sixty and looking thirty-five. She married north forty years ago from the Shiraki hills to seal a peace nobody now remembers, and the rest of the north calls her the old woman while being careful where she can hear. She has sat in Thornhaven all that time under Tenrai taxes and Tenrai factors and Tenrai silver with a small dry smile, paying on the day. Her husband is not named on the record, and no one has ever got far by asking about him. The Tenrai count Thornwall bought. People who trade with her count their fingers after.

## Wealth and Trade

**Ironwood**, first and always. Dense, hard, slow to rot, wanted for tool handles and ward-frames and ships' keels, and floated down to anyone with money. **The corridor** next: the road and river-chain east to Collarbone in [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Ember Holds of Marrowhold|Marrowhold]], up which Thornwall sends timber and down which come the worked metal and the inscription stock ward-craft needs. Thornwall carriers move both and are paid for both. Then furs, pitch, tar, charcoal, resin, carved goods, and a small but well-loved line in tall painted drinking horns.

Thornwall reads a trading partner's behavior like a physician reading a pulse. When the Ore Council of Marrowhold cuts the grain it resells up the corridor and keeps sending inscription stock, the Thornwalls notice first, because they have tracked the holds' trade for generations and know the difference between a shortage and a message. Their standing reading: that kind of cut is political rather than logistical, because the holds are comfortable with an armed frontier buffer and uncomfortable with an independent one. They also tried once to take the whale-oil trade off the Keld by force, with a warband past the treeline, and the Keld still sing about it, and so does the wood, more quietly.

## Rent, Food and the Street

Foresters and raftsmen hold their posts by a house contract and are paid in a share of the drive, and it is not unusual for a raft-boss to own more than the lord she is delivering to. A room over the rope-walk in Thornhaven runs in copper, in split wood (everyone has wood, so it is priced badly), or a fortnight on the drive. Food is smoked river fish, venison and bear in a dark gravy, birch-sap beer in spring, flat barley bread bought dear from Ashmore. Every raft has a pot on it that has not been washed since the raft was built, and they will tell you this is why it tastes of something.

Thornwall's street life is carving. People whittle at table and in court, and a child gets a knife before shoes. Guild-marks are cut into every worked beam, and carvers brag about theirs and sue each other. Raftsmen wear red caps and sing dirty songs in rounds, and have a low-grade bracket scene between the red caps of Raftwick and the charcoal burners, the Black Hands, who are fairly sure the raftsmen are all thieves. Hedge-workers talk bark-beetle out of a stand and frost-crack out of a trunk, and they refuse to work at all on the far side of a long cut after dark, and give no reason for it.

**What they joke about.** Prices. A Thornwall will price the damage aloud while it is happening, and did so at his own wedding. Hallenfeld reeves. People who think a forest is scenery. "Six months" is the standing Thornwall punchline: told of anything that is about to go wrong, in a tone of mild regret.

## Law and How It Fights

**Law.** A contract cut in wood with two guild-marks on it is good from here to the Center. The Raftmen's Hall settles everything on the water, the cargo-roll house everything off it, and the Lady whatever is left, and the Lady is rarely troubled.

**The fighting.** House soldiers wear Thornwall gray felt and iron caps. The signature is the **Thornwall hedge**: long ash-and-iron spears, coming on at a walk, in step, to the slow beat of a drum, with the thorn-horn sounding behind them every hundred paces, then setting a hedge that horse will not go into. A Marrick captain would laugh at the length of those spears and has done, and the Thornwalls wrote his name down. It holds ground and takes walls, it is slow, and the fair answer is horse on open ground before the hedge is set. Woodsmen fight in the stands with axes and short bows, from behind things eight feet thick. Raft-crews fight with hook-poles and spiked boots, and they will drop a trunk on you. Technique names in the Wood tend to be named for the job, and nobody south of Ashbury should believe FIRST OF THE MELT was a joke.

## Monsters on the Roads

**The beasts** are the Wood's real landlords. Bear keeps the dense stands, where it is too dark for anything but moss and him. Marten, boar and red deer take the rest, and a boar in the stump fields has put more axe-men in bed than the Keld ever did. The beetle that a hedge-worker must talk out of a stand is about as long as your thumb, and over the years it has cost the house more than any war.

**The monsters.** Monsters move, and they live across the whole of the Inner World, and in a forest this old some of them have stopped bothering to. Sasquatch (T3 on the Bestiary's Danger Tier, which runs from T1 to T9) move through the deep stands masking their scent, and most foresters leave them food. Dryads (T3) guard the oldest groves and have started two wars with the axe crews. Reaching Briars take the edges of the stump fields. Hedge-workers refuse to work on the far side of a **long cut** (a narrow straight ride through the deep stands) after dark and give no reason, and I set that down without comment.

### The Red Stair and the Long Cuts

The [[The Bestiary/Dungeons|dungeon]] (what a Wellspring Core makes around itself, or what grows up around one) is **the Red Stair**: a ruined carved archway older than the house, at the head of worn steps, with long strings of faded cloth flags running off between the trees. The loggers' tally flags are tied there, one for each great tree taken. The steps go down among the roots into a Well nobody has reached the bottom of, and a raft-boss who has been down to the second landing drinks free in Raftwick.

The other thing crews talk about is **the Long Cuts** themselves. The Bestiary's shelf files them as said to be a *lying-down*, the crews' word for a dungeon laid flat with no roof: an edge you can cross without noticing, ground that thickens as you go on, and stock that changes with the ground. Nobody in the Wood admits they are anything. Crews have offered to walk one end to end, and the foresters have been very civil about refusing. It is for the timber, they will tell you, in a forest where nobody has taken a tree off one in living memory. More on the [[The Bestiary/Dungeons|Dungeons]] shelf and in the [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Inner World — A Gazetteer|Gazetteer]].

### The Scourge and the Turn

The [[Geography & the Four Quarters/The Bearing and the Holding/The Inner World — The Northern Shield/The Scourge of Hell|Scourge of Hell]] is a very long haul from the Wood, and the Wood has been selling to it for as long as the house has kept rolls. The Turn is the old rotation by which North, South and Center each spend nine years keeping its monsters off everybody else, and the party whose Turn it is keeps the Line, a chain of forts and beacon towers round the Hem, the Scourge's outer belt. Those forts want roof-trees and stakes that will not rot in country that is dry overhead and damp under the surface, and ironwood does not, so whoever holds the Turn ends up buying Thornwall trunks, floated down to Ashbury and carted on from there, at a rate the cargo-roll house quoted six months before they knew they needed it. The house has sold to all three parties in one year and regards this as the whole point of a rotation.

On a Northern Turn it also owes spears, and the Thornwall hedge has gone down the Kessen road at its walk behind its slow drum. A hedge is a fine thing against horse and an indifferent one against whatever comes up through the ground beneath it, as the surviving sergeants reported, with the cost per yard. On the last such Turn, which ended in 703, a Thornwall forester went in as far as the Black Roots on a bet, to see what tree those roots came from. He cut a slice with a hand-saw, smelled it, priced it at nothing and walked home, and that is the Wood's entire published opinion on the subject. The Center has the Turn until 721. The wheel then comes back to the North, and the cargo-roll house has already begun buying ash.

# The Scourge of Hell

**Say it** · the SKURJ of HELL · also Hell's Scourge

*The vast wasteland at the center of the Inner World: no ruler, no road through it, one colossal sword, and the worst monsters on the map*

> *Item, for the keeping of the Line, nine years: see attached. Item, for the dead: see attached. Item, owed to us by the other two: see attached.*
>
> *Cover sheet of the Count, the same in every copy, in three different hands*

At a quarter hour before noon on Midsummer Day of 676, in the market square of Lastpenny on the Kessen road, the biggest earth wyrm anyone living had seen came up through the cheese stalls. It came straight out of the ground in a fountain of paving and yellow rounds, a long bronze hill with scales the size of shields and a crest of stone spines, and it took the herald who was reading the speech. Around that square, under every banner they owned, stood eleven thousand soldiers in perfect order. Six thousand Banner spears of the North were drawn up on one side in Hollenmark blue and Stormfold slate. Five thousand of the Marrick companies stood on the other in ochre and black, with their drums. They had come for the handing-over of the Turn, the old arrangement by which the realms of the North, the South and the Center each take nine years keeping the Scourge of Hell off everybody else, and the Turn passed at noon, by the Accord's water clock, which stood on a trestle in the middle of the square under a green awning with a clerk beside it.

The northern marshal had formally stood his host down an hour before, with a very fine speech. Past that moment a Northern man who fought did so at his own cost, with no bounty and no pension for his widow. The southern captain-general's contract began at noon and not one drop earlier, and the Marrick companies do not fight off contract for anyone. So the two commanders looked at the water clock, and then at each other, and folded their arms. Eleven thousand spears stood and watched while the wyrm ate the Corn Hall, the reeve, and the better half of Butter Row. The whole town screamed at them in the Midmarch tongue, and a goblin king the size of a child beat on the marshal's stirrup with a ladle. Nine minutes in, someone tipped the water clock. The Center says it was a cheese-wife called Gytha Rowe, who had lost her stall and both her sons. The North says it was the wyrm's tail, the South says the clock was fast, and the clerk, who was sworn to read the instrument as it stood, looked into the bowl and called noon in a voice like a nail.

The Marrick drums came in before he finished the word. It took them until the lamps were lit to drive the thing back down, with four hundred dead and nine hundred spears left standing in its hide, and the South presented the North with the bill for all of it, as the North's last act of negligence. The wyrm still comes up every eleven years or so along that stretch of the road, still wearing the spears. Everyone calls it Old Pincushion, and the companies can tell you by the marks whose pay each one was. In the Center, to this day, a northerner or a southerner who declines to help you has said *it isn't noon*, and you are entitled to spit.

Here is the place in a breath: a wasteland as big as a kingdom at the center of the continent, filled with monsters so long ago that the oldest rolls already call it full, under a purple sky, with a sword in it. Nobody rules it and nobody marches through it, which is why the Center is shaped the way it is, why every army in history has used the same two roads, and why three great regions of the world hate one another with such careful bookkeeping.

## Where It Lies and How Far It Runs

The Inner World is one long continent pinched in the middle, and this is the pinch. The Guild gives the Scourge as some hundred and eighty leagues from north to south and a hundred and forty across, a league being about three miles and the figures being, by the admission of the man who drew them, a matter of walking round the outside and multiplying. No survey has crossed it. No road crosses it. All traffic between North and South goes around by two corridors, the **Kessen road** down the west flank and the **salt road** up the east, and every realm of the Center lives along those roads with its back to the thing.

## What It Looks Like

The first thing to say is that the name led me to expect something drab, and the name is a liar. It is the most violently colored country I have ever been frightened in. The stone is iron red and rust and in places a wet-looking purple. The haze goes copper at noon and rose at evening, and past the middle of it the sky itself turns the color of a bruised plum and stays there, day and night, with stars in it at midday if you are far enough in to be wrong about the time. Thorn-vines flower in vermilion after the spring rains. Lightning walks around the dry beds in green and violet with no storm behind it. A painter would be delighted, briefly.

### The Sword

Then there is the thing everybody came to see. Near the center of the Scourge, driven point-down into a range of mountains, stands a sword.

It stands about four leagues above the peaks by the best reckoning, or about three, those being the two surveys, taken from the Windstair and from Stoneglass Hollow, whose authors did not speak afterward. You can make it out from the Hem on perhaps nine clear days in the year, when the wind off the interior drops, a dark upright line on the sky. It has a bronze hilt with a guard as wide as a city, and in however many ages it has stood there the bronze has not gone green. **The blade has no metal in it that anyone can see.** Look at it and you are looking through a tall sword-shaped window at a starfield, deep violet and crowded, and as you walk along a ridge the stars shift against the blade's edges, near ones faster than far ones, the way the view through a real window shifts. Every star-clerk who has seen it agrees on two things: that the sky in there is no sky over this world, and that it should not be possible to agree about that from so far away.

Every edge town has the picture on a tavern sign, painted by every hand from masters to drunks: a lone figure on a ridge, under a purple sky, holding an ordinary sword and looking up. Half those taverns are called the Ordinary Sword. The other half are called the Other One.

## The Land by Depth

The Scourge gets worse as you go in, and it does so in belts, which is the first thing that made the Guild suspect it of being a [[The Bestiary/Dungeons|dungeon]] laid flat. The wind comes outward from the middle in every season, hot and dry, which is why the whole rim country smells of iron and pollen and why nobody has ever had the wind at their back going in.

| Belt | Width, roughly | Climate | Habitat | Ground (scholars' rung) |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Damp under the surface, always | Rill thickening to Freshet |
| **The Black Roots** | 20 leagues | Dry wind above, and warm and wet an arm's length down. Lime and violet fungus, thorn-groves, hollow ground. The ground stops feeling like ground and begins feeling like a lid | Freshet to Millrace |
| **The Haze** | 25 leagues | Haze that has never been recorded clearing, a fine dust of iron and pollen. The sun is a copper coin. Dry lightning, and very rare rain that does not reach the ground | Dead river courses, salt flats in pink and white, seas of stiff red grass. The rungs are the sites ladder (the register's scale of how strong you must be to stand on dense ground and stay yourself), and those past the Hem are estimates by people in a hurry. The Northern Shield as a whole reads Rill. Each rung up asks more, and a Millrace is survivable for an afternoon and not for a season, which every crew in the Haze is aware of and has decided to think about later.

**Ground as a lid.** People who have walked the Black Roots all say the same thing, and it is the thing to listen to. The roots hold up a thin crust over warm hollow dark, and the big things travel below it. A small party can feel it through the boot soles, a shudder running along under the mat. Edge towns have a petty trade in it: the lid-listeners, hired for a few coppers a night, usually old women with one small gift, who lie with an ear on a stake in the ground and tell a crew how many legs are coming.

## Why the Monsters Are Worse Here

Monsters move, and you will meet them on every road in the Inner World, and beasts are more widespread still. Every realm in the world has its own bad road and its own song about it. But this is the one area known for extremely dangerous monsters, and a Realm Hazard (T7 on the Commission's danger ladder, naming the rank of practitioner the Accord would have to send) gets a name and a ballad anywhere else on the map, and here it gets eaten. The Guild's accounts for this:

**Age.** It was full before the rolls begin, and no one has ever thinned it. Everywhere else a thing that grows dangerous gets a bounty on the doors, and a crew comes, eventually. Here it has had thousands of years of being left alone.

**Stacked Wells.** A Well (ground where one kind of Aether lies thick enough to rewrite what lives in it) elsewhere has its own country around it and its own stock. Here they lie on top of each other like dropped cards. A crew in the Haze may break masks in five colors before lunch.

**They eat each other.** Nothing native to the ground above an ordinary Well will touch a Well-spawn. In the Scourge, wyrms and worse have learned to, and masks have been cut out of stomachs here, and nowhere else on file. What survives a hundred thousand generations of that is very good at what it does.

**The Scourge-born.** Its great herds breed true and wear no masks, which the register's own rules say should not happen, since an animal warped by dense ground is supposed to come out short-lived and sterile. These have been at it so long that they stopped being warped and became a kind of animal. The entries sit under Natural Beasts, under protest. Beasts plus time plus Essence, as one old cutter put it, ecosystems with teeth. And unlike a shallow spawn they hold ground. Above a certain size a thing has a range, and it will not chase you out of it, since you are simply its until you leave or die.

**The wound.** The reading I favor, and no one can prove: the sword went into something, and all of this is what has been leaking out around it since.

**A wyrm core.** This is the fourth meaning of the word, and I apologize for it. Inside a big creature's chest the Essence Core (the seat of its nature) hardens over centuries into a fist of glassy stuff in the wyrm's own color, and a good one sells for a small fortune anywhere there is a crucible. Every body on the Scourge gets cut open for it before it is cold. Do not confuse this with the Core at a worked site, and be careful which one you promise a factor.

**The herds' season.** In late summer, in a dry year, the wind and the thirst push the herds outward, and what follows the herds comes with them. That is when the barley goes, and that is the season the whole arrangement below exists to meet.

## Dungeons and Cores

Dungeons, the Bestiary explains at length, are what a Wellspring site makes around itself, and the Scourge has more of them than it has surveyors. Cores (a Core being a site where one of the sixty kinds of Aether pools) mostly lie in dungeons and are also said to lie in vast territories, and the first thing anyone says about a vast territory is this one. There is a respectable school which holds that the whole Scourge is a single Core with no roof, so large it has weather. It has not been able to explain the other Wells, and has not stopped trying.

The ones crews talk about:

- **The Lamplit Stair**, in the Verath Expanse. An Aurevane stair going down from a thornwood gully, its walls a hard blue-white. It has been cleared twice and lost twice, and it is where half the Green Sigils in the Charter get their first bad night.
- **Nine Wells Gulch**, in the Black Roots. Nine separate Mouths inside a morning's walk, no two of the same Wellspring, and a mask market on the lip in good years with its own cook-tents and bookies. Crews have won it nine times by count. Nobody has held it through a second winter.
- **The Upside Abbey**, in the Haze. A whole abbey hanging from the underside of a rock arch, an Ascensio ground (a Wellspring of lift and weight), with its floors over your head and its bells hanging up. Relic hunters adore it. You go in by rope, and nobody agrees on which direction you fall when you stop.
- **The Dry Harbor**, in the Haze. A Thaloré ground (the Wellspring of crushing depth) in a dead river mouth. No water has been there in the memory of anyone, and yet men are found on bone-dry stone crushed flat as if from a great depth, with their ears burst and, in a few cases, a Mourning Pike's mask nearby in deep teal.
- **The Long Muster**, in the Shambles. A dead camp of the Great Spirit War on a slope, tents long since gone and the lines still straight, which takes the roll every dusk in a language no one has placed. It has more ghoststeel lying about than the rest of the shield together. Nobody who has answered to their name there has been of further use.
- **The Wound**, at the foot of the sword. Said to be a Core. Said by people who have not been there, mostly, to people who are buying them a drink. Bookies will take any bet on it, at any odds, on the sound principle that nobody will ever come back to collect.

## The Turn

The arrangement has no good name. The chancery calls it the *Rota*, the wheel. Everyone else calls it the Turn. **North, then South, then Center, nine years each, then round again**, so that a full wheel takes twenty-seven years and most adults have seen enough of it to hold firm views. Nobody can produce the original. The copy at Sealhall refers to an older copy, and that one refers to "the ancient custom."

**The three parties.** The North, the South and the Center each mean whoever can be made to pay. On the North's Turn that means Kharven and its Banner Houses. The South's means the old Dynasty's realms, the white shelf of Alabaster and the hired spears of Hammerford. The Center's means everybody who lives along the rim, which is a story in itself.

**What it costs.** The party whose Turn it is keeps **the Line**, a chain of forts and beacon towers around the Hem. It pays the bounties posted on doors for anything that comes out, fields a host every herd season, and answers for every carter lost on the corridors. It may march through anyone's land to do so, by right of passage, and buy victuals at a fixed price. The other two owe it nothing but a fixed subsidy that has been paid in full on no known occasion.

**The Count.** The other two watch their borders, send a clerk, and write it all down. Every Turn ends with each party presenting a Count of what the last nine years cost it and what it is therefore owed. The Accord's Arbitration division audits them at Sealhall, for a fee, and has never yet produced a figure that any party accepted. The accumulated difference is now larger than all the coin in the world. It is carried forward.

**The resentments.**

The North says the South hires its killing done, and walks off the field on the last day of the contract in the middle of whatever it was doing. This is accurate. A Northern Turn runs like a campaign, in long disciplined columns down the Kessen road, with icebear riders from the March going slowly mad from the heat. And it says the Ashmores of the Golden Hollow, who sell grain at the junction where the two roads meet, have never lost a Turn in their history, which is also accurate.

The South says the North only ever comes down to look at the Center's castles, and that half the invasions in the Center's history began as a Turn that lost its way. This has been shown on maps. A Southern Turn runs by the season, on contract, with subscription lists at Alabaster and keepers at the Wellheads charging what they like for the hours. It is well run and it stops at sundown on the last day.

The Center says the other two take turns. **The Center lives here.** The host passing through on someone else's Turn eats its barley going up, and its castles are the ones with the wall knocked in. When its own Turn comes round, the Charter pays by instrument, the Midmarch kings each send nine men and a very loud banner, Nalūn pays in salt and complains in writing, the Scalebond send surgeons at their posted price, the Violet Peaks pay in sightings and seniors, the Moss Court plants what it is sent, and the Unpapered, who are on no roll, fight regardless and bill nobody. Free companies on Thresh contracts do most of the dying. **The Center holds the Turn at present, from 712 to 721**, and the betting in Kingsbridge is on who will be standing to hand it over.

**The Salt Line.** Nalūn's one contribution everybody admires. A strip of salted ground, worked by hedge-workers with Chainsalt so that Aether will not pool on it, some sixty miles along the north rim and a hundred paces deep, renewed each spring. Whatever comes across it comes off its Well and comes weaker. It costs a fortune and it holds about as well as a fence, and on the far side of it the cutters of Halcrest will tell you which stretches were salted honestly.

## Who Goes In

**Bounty crews.** There is more bounty on the doors of the rim towns than anywhere else in the world, and a wyrm core on top of it. Crews come from every school in every realm, in every color they can afford, because a tally from the Scourge is worth three from anywhere else, and a song comes with it. Catkin out of the Russet Crown run some of the best-regarded outfits on the eastern side, and they are paid in actual money. The hobgoblins on the western ridges are the long-service men of the trade: outcasts and contract hands from the Mandate and the Isles, working the ridges above the Black Roots, stripping carcasses and hiring out as guides. A hobgoblin may see two centuries if the world allows it, so a ridge-man who says he has been doing this for a hundred and thirty years may well mean it.

**Relic hunters.** The Shambles are the richest dead battlefield known. Ghoststeel, the phasing metal nobody has ever reproduced in a crucible, lies there in quantities that would ruin the market if anybody could get it out, and so do old gear with names and legendary arts carved or bottled by the dead. People have killed one another over a good piece on the road home, three days short of the rim, within sight of the tavern.

**Sects.** Most of the fighting schools of the Center send their seniors to walk the Hem, and the proud ones send them to the Black Roots, with the school song and a banner. Coming back with a mask is how a Journeyman gets a name. The schools of the Violet Peaks keep an annual tally of kills per student and post it on the bridge, and a school that loses a season's seniors will recruit twice as hard for the glory of it.

**Sword-pullers.** There is one every few years. A young Midmarch king with a good jaw, a sect heir, a farm boy with a dream, who sets out with a following, bookies alongside and a minstrel on retainer, to walk to the middle and draw it. The Hundred Crowns have a particular weakness for this. The record is held by a Tinker Goblin named Nixie Sprocket, who got into the Shambles with a mule-drawn engine of her own design and sent back a letter describing the bronze as warm, and whether that means she reached it or wished to, the bookies have never paid out.

**Surveyors.** Measurewrights go in with forks to settle things, and porters go in with them.

## What Scholars Argue About the Sword

Most agree it is an old work of the Great Spirit War, from the days of the Physical Epochs when Bara, Ashura and Aegor walked in human form. Everything else is open, and a good Guild dinner can be ruined by asking.

- **Who drove it in.** Every list of the great combatants of that war has its partisans, every partisan has a passage, and the passages were written long afterward by people who already held the opinion. I have seen a knife drawn over this at a funeral.
- **What it was driven into.** A foe, say the romantic schools, and the mountains are what grew over the body. A rift, say the sober ones: a place like the Crevice in the Southern Pan, torn open in the war and stopped with the only plug to hand. If so, nobody should pull it, and every few years somebody sets out to.
- **Whether it is a sword.** A small and very pleased school of Measurewrights holds that the thing is a standing tear in the Veil, and that "hilt" and "blade" are what a human eye does with a shape it cannot read. The bronze is an awkward fact for this school, and it has a whole volume on why bronze is an illusion.
- **What the stars are.** One star-clerk at Sealhall has spent his career recording the field in the blade from borrowed reports, and he holds that it has moved twice in three centuries. Others hold that this proves the reports are bad.
- **What the neighbors think.** The Marrowkin look at the Ring's ribs, look at the sword, and suggest it is a butcher's pin marking a cut. The Moss Court says it is a stake holding up something that has not finished growing. The Scalebond remember what it was, they say, and they will tell you for a sum nobody has ever been able to raise. The Moto have a word for it that they do not use in front of guests.

## The Edge Towns

Everybody expects an edge town to be a sad little place of ash and widows. It has the widows. It is also the loudest, brightest, most expensive and most cheerful town you will ever try to get a night's sleep in, because every person in it either just came back alive or is about to find out.

- **Lastpenny.** On the Kessen road, in the Midmarch. Rebuilt in stone, and they have kept the awning. The Noon Fair every Midsummer sells a hard yellow cheese with a bite out of it, and at noon the whole town stands in the square and turns its back on any soldiers present.
- **Goodmorrow.** On the salt road, under the Charter's edge. A town of painted stilts over a sinkhole country, every bridge a different color so you can tell them apart through the haze. It has the best mask-clerks on the east side and the worst beer.
- **Halcrest.** Nalūn's gate, where the salt thins against the northern rim, the far side of the basin from the Thresh and the place every crew signs on, with cutters' taverns and a map trade. Everybody here will sell you a copy of a copy of the good map.
- **Rootgate.** The Moss Court's gate on the western flank, where the orcs will sell you a burial plot before you go, at a discount for paying in advance, and it is the only place on the rim where the prices fall as your chances do.

The houses all sit on stone footings with no cellars, which is the first thing you notice and the last thing they will joke about, and above the street run plank walks from roof to roof. Every wall is painted vermilion, yellow and blue, because it has to be seen through the haze, and every eave hangs with **ground-bells** on iron stakes that go a good way down, so that a shudder under the lid sets the whole street ringing. In the taverns the crews' tallies are painted over the door, and the board at the hall goes three bills deep. Outside the hall are the renderers, the core-buyers, the Scalebond surgeon who knows how the last man with your wound died, and the bookies on barrels with the depth odds chalked up for every crew that went out this week. Hedge-workers salt your doorstep for a copper and your bed for two.

Black-root tea is the local drink, bitter as a lawsuit, and every kind of meat in the stew is a matter of faith. A careful man pays for his own wake before he goes in, and the wake-houses keep the money in a marked jar. If he comes back he drinks it, with everyone he knows, and that is the great daily festival of an edge town: somebody at the door, burned and laughing and one ear short, shouting for his jar. The Widows' Bench outside the hall is an actual bench, and the women on it are among the best-informed investors on the rim, holding more shares in more crews than any factor. They will tell you to your face what you are worth.

At dusk the lamp-wardens go round and the whole town lights up in every color its keepers have, and out past the last wall the wind comes in off the Hem smelling of iron and flowers. On one of the nine clear days everybody stops what they are doing and goes up on the roof walks to look at it, the dark line standing up out of the end of the world, and then the bookies call the odds again and they all go back in.

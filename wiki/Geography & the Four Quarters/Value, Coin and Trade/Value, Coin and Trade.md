# Value, Coin and Trade

*An Account of What Is Worth Something, and Why*
> Second edition, revised. Compiled for the Research and Archives Division of the Guild Accord with the assistance of the Guild of Measurewrights. All dates conform to the Concordance of Ages, sealed Year 715 In Concordance, the twenty-fifth year of the Dark Ages. The first edition's era headings are withdrawn.

The carrack *Golden Purse* broke her back on the Gannet Bar in the autumn gales of Year 697, in full view of the harbor wall, and half the town had come out with hot pies to watch, because her bonded weather-worker, a Journeyman called Avis Reedmace, had been holding the sea off her for six hours in a coat of aura the green of a mallard's neck, with the spray standing up around the hull in a shining wall. When the mainmast went, the coat went with it. The ship opened along the keel in a burst of yellow cloth and painted crates, and at dawn Reedmace walked up the beach in one boot, spitting blood and asking loudly who owed her for the night.

What came ashore that week is the finest lesson in economics this archive knows of, and it was given free. Forty chests of coined silver went into twelve fathoms, and the salvors got nine of them up by midwinter. A bundle of Aethersteel billets washed in among the weed, and a gull-boy who took them for ballast sold them for the price of a pie. The thirteen bars of Nalūn salt in the cook's locker fed and paid the survivors for a month, in that order. A collar of Bloodsilver out of the owner's own cabin took three fingers off the bailiff who picked it up. The owner's bill on Ironlink Hold arrived in an oilskin tube inside the shirt of a drowned purser, and it was honored in full inside the month by men who had never heard of the ship.

The house that owned her went under that winter, and the Arbitration Division divided the estate in the sequence you will find below. Its book of Core hours (the Essence it had already contracted for, explained below) sold first, and for more than the fleet. Second came Reedmace herself, eleven years of bonded term entered in the same column as the ship, and she had one advantage over the ship, in that she could walk to the auction, where she threw the other boot at the auctioneer and fetched a record price. The Aethersteel went on the coil once the Division had found the gull-boy. The silver ranked fourth. The family's river estate came last, wheat and all, and paid out almost nothing. People in that port still argue about whether any of this was fair. This page will explain why every step of it was correct.

## The Two Ledgers

> **The Essence ledger** records the primary economic good. It is a ledger of **flows rather than stocks.** Nobody holds Essence but the person whose Soul Crystal refined it, out of Aether pulled from the ground they stand on; everybody else holds *somebody's hours*, for a term, at a rate: a keeper's hours at a Core, a crew's hours on the lip of it, and once in a long while a sealed flask on a mule.
>
> A pumping array (a standing runed working, here one that lifts water out of a mine) does not care what is in your vault. **It cares what arrives this week.**
>
> **The metal ledger** records the settlement of everything else. Land, grain, cloth, labor, ore, ships, debts, dowries, fines, ransoms.
>
> Metal is **the memory of the economy.** It is what allows a transaction to be completed between people who will never meet again.

Essence has all the properties of a terrible currency. It leaks from storage, cannot be assayed without an instrument, cannot be divided below a practical floor, is worthless to anyone who cannot use it, and **will not survive its owner.** It is nonetheless the primary good, because it is the thing everything else is made with.

The two ledgers meet at exactly one place, which is **the price of Essence in metal**, and that price is the single most watched number in the four quarters. In practice it is the price of one hour at a Core. It is published by the Logistics Division, cried from the steps of every guild hall on quarter-day, disputed by everyone, and enforced by nobody.

## How Essence Is Actually Bought

*Nobody in the four quarters has ever bought a pound of Essence, and the man offering to sell you one should be watched.*

Three things are for sale, and a working house buys them in this order.

**A keeper's hours.** A worked Core (a Wellspring site, a place where one kind of Aether pools) has keepers, who may be an order, a temple, a sect or one stubborn family, and what they sell is time on the lip of the pool, where a person fills far faster than on any ordinary ground. A warden topping up on her way to the round buys the **walk-up hour**, coin in hand at the gate, at whatever the keeper says this morning, and a stranger pays double. A serious buyer takes a **standing hour**: one named hour, at one named Core, for one body on the lip, every day for a year, sealed before witnesses with earnest money down and the balance due in silver marks and salt bars on the four quarter-days. That is the unit the Accord publishes, and it stands at forty silver marks the year. A house's whole parcel of them is its **book of hours**, which the older ledgers and every factor in the chain-cities still call a supply book, and the Holy See of Lurien has twice complained about the joke.

A keeper can close the gate, and a keeper can also sell the same hour twice. In Year 706 the Widow Pennifold, who kept the little Core at Ambry Spring, sold the dawn hour to three mining houses in three towns, and on the first morning of the term three crews in three colors of company sash arrived at her pool within the same quarter of an hour. They fought on the lip until the pool had been bled pale by everyone's coats at once, and the site gave nothing for a season. The Arbitration Division found that she had delivered exactly what she promised, which was an hour at the pool, and that nobody had thought to write down "alone". Every contract since has that word in it, and she is said to have retired to a very nice house.

**A crew's retainer.** An hour at the pool does nothing until a body stands in it. A house retains a bonded crew of array-tenders, Stage I to III and mostly of Apprentice standing, at six to twelve gold a head a month in coin. They stand on the lip, pull, refine, and carry it in their own Crystals to the works, which makes a crew the cheapest flask there is, since it walks and brings its own lunch. A great shaft with a Core in reach keeps eight tenders at the lip for each of the fourteen Guild hours, on two watches of seven, which comes to sixteen men and a hundred and twelve standing hours. On quarter-day the owner counts that as about three hundred and seventy gold a year to the keeper, against a hundred and twelve in Year 640, and, at a middling nine gold a head, about seventeen hundred and thirty a year to the crew. The men cost far more than the hours, and a house can hire men next week. Nobody can hire an hour the keeper has already sold.

**A sealed flask.** A carry-stone is a wine flask's worth of Aethersteel leaves and Chainsalt, thirty to sixty gold before the filling, hand-filled by the keepers at their hourly rate, and a rich mining house owns perhaps six ([[Materials, Alchemy & Trade/Work, Trade and Measure/The Apparatus of the Age — Equipment and Material Culture/The Apparatus of the Age — Equipment and Material Culture|the Apparatus]] has the object). They travel on mule-back in a Chainsalt-lined coffer, with two guards and a muleteer paid extra to ask nothing, to a shaft whose crew has fallen sick, a ward that is dying or a rite a month's ride from any Core. A flask holds about two and a half EU in every liter and loses a twentieth of its filling a day, so the mule is racing the flask. A shaft with a lapsed book has four days before the water reaches the working levels, and a train from the next valley's Core takes five and arrives a quarter spent. Nobody runs a shaft on flasks. They keep it breathing until the crew arrives.

**What actually changes hands.** Almost none of this is gold anyone sees. Earnest money is silver on the table at the sealing. Quarter-day is a strongbox on a mule and the keeper's whole family counting at the edge of the pool, in silver marks in the west and salt bars in the north, and a temple keeper may take a part of it in candles and masses. The crew is paid in silver by the month, the walk-up hour in copper at the gate, and the great books settle by bill on Ironlink Hold, between people who have never smelled a pool.

## What Is Actually Valuable

Stated highest first, **in the sequence the Arbitration Division pays creditors when a bankrupt estate is divided.** This ordering was arrived at by people with no interest in sentiment, and it is the most honest statement of value the Accord has ever produced.

| Rank | Asset |
|---|---|
| **First** | **Contracted Essence.** A secured right to a named keeper's hours at a named Core, with the crew bonded to stand on it, for a stated term. Nothing else is senior to it. A western house with a lapsed book of hours is a house whose great shafts will be underwater within four days |
| **Second** | **Attributable capability.** A certified person. Rank, Temperance Stage, coil-assayed output, clean contract record. *A guild-ranked practitioner is an income stream that walks*, and the Division treats a bonded practitioner's remaining term as an asset of the estate in the same column as a ship |
| **Third** | **Holding-grade material.** Aethersteel, Chainsalt, Clearbeam beryl, Stormfulgur, Blacktooth wolfram, Ashfast chromite. Priced on the coil (the brass assay instrument), not on the scale |
| **Fourth** | **Bullion and coin.** The only asset on this list that a peasant and a Trade-Prince both recognize on sight |
| **Fifth** | **Bearing-grade material and finished goods.** The visible economy, and the one that employs almost everybody |
| **Sixth** | **Land** |

> Land sits last, and the reason is the most misunderstood fact in the Accord's economy. **Land is priced on the Wellspring site beneath it, not on the soil above it.** (The Wellsprings are the sixty types of Aether, each affecting the Soul Crystal differently; a site is a place where one of them pools, and common speech calls a worked site a Core.) A barren upland estate sitting on a strong steady source will outprice a river valley of the finest wheat ground by a factor the Arbitration Division has stopped finding remarkable, because the wheat ground can be bought and **the source cannot be moved.**
>
> Half the border disputes in the four quarters that are conducted in the language of ancestral right are, on the deeds, arguments about saturation. This is also why the old nobility is poorer than it looks: a great house's wealth is in acres, and acres were valued on a schedule set before the arrays.

## The Four Metals

Every metal has two demands on it. The **monetary** demand wants it in a vault or a purse. The **thaumic** demand wants it melted, beaten, inscribed, or consecrated. These demands compete for the same physical stock, and the balance between them explains almost every monetary crisis in this history.

### Gold

**Resonance:** Benediction and Sublimatio.

Gold is the standard of value, the metal of the great settlements, and **the substrate of the oath.** That last is the fact everything else follows from. Gold carries blessing and elevation better than any material in common supply, which makes it the medium of ecclesiastical work, oath-binding, consecration, and every contract that must be sealed rather than merely written. Eresse's Sacred Gold is a plain description, however poetic it sounds: **gold that has been taken into the Verdantia rite and has stopped being money.**
> The consequence is a **permanent monetary sink.** Gold that enters an oath does not come back. It cannot be re-melted without breaking the working, and breaking the working is, in most of the four quarters, a capital matter. Every generation consecrates a portion of the world's gold and removes it from circulation forever, and **the more devout a polity is, the faster it drains its own money supply.**
>
> Chronic gold scarcity therefore has little to do with the mines. **It is a religion operating as a monetary policy that nobody voted for and nobody can repeal.**

In daily practice, most people will never touch gold. It settles accounts between houses, kingdoms, and guilds. *A laborer may see one gold piece in a lifetime, at a wedding, and will remember it.*

### Silver

**Resonance:** binding addressed to a person. Silver takes an inscription naming its bearer more cleanly than any other common metal, which is why Bloodsilver binds to bloodline and rejects a thief, and why Dawnsilver thread seals glyphs into a garment and can, in ceremonial stitching, bind a broken Name back into the soul.

Silver is the working currency of the four quarters and the accounting unit of almost every serious ledger.
> Its thaumic role produces the single most elegant institution in the economy. Because silver holds a personal inscription, **rank tokens are struck in silver.** A guild credential is a silver piece bearing the holder's certified rank, inscribed to that holder, verifiable on a coil, and worthless to anyone else. It is simultaneously a badge, a passport, a line of credit, and its own weight in bullion.
>
> An adventurer who has lost everything else still has a silver token that is, at the floor, money. And **an adventurer stripped of rank has watched an object turn from a credential into a lump of metal in front of witnesses**, which is why demotion ceremonies are conducted in public and why the Accord does them slowly.

### Copper

**Resonance:** conduction. Vellun copper out of the Senoth hills is a good spiritual conductor that melts far too easily, which describes copper's entire economic position.

Copper is the money of ordinary life: bread, beer, a night's board, a candle, a ferry crossing. For the overwhelming majority of people, copper is the only metal money they will ever hold, and **the price of copper is therefore the price of being alive.**

It is also the wire of the array trade. Every winding and every contact in every array in the world is copper pulled through a die and laid by hand, and one great array raised at one great shaft swallows the small change of a county.
> These two demands compete for the same stock, and **when a great array goes up, the small coin vanishes.** It is melted, and no government need be involved, only anyone with a crucible, because worked conductor is worth more per weight than struck coin and the arithmetic is available to a child.
>
> Every great array raised in the last two centuries has produced a copper famine in its quarter within three years, and **a copper famine falls entirely on people who have no other money.**
>
> The Accord has legislated against melting coin nine times. It has never once enforced it successfully, for the excellent reason that **a melted coin does not testify.**

### Bronze

**Resonance:** Runebronze, melt-forged with minor glyphs, was the first material in history designed to hold an inscription. **Bronze is old law.**

Barely money any more and enormously important anyway. Imperial bronze is still in circulation across all four quarters, worn smooth, faces illegible, valued purely by weight. *Nobody trusts the Empire. Everybody trusts the standard*, because the Empire's weights were honest for four centuries before they were not, and the surviving early issues are the most reliable physical measure of mass in the world. Assay houses still calibrate against them.

Bronze's living role is sacral and legal. Temple fittings, boundary markers, oath-plates, guild charters, the physical instruments of law. When something must be permanent, legible in five hundred years, and obviously not worth stealing for its metal, it is cast in bronze. **The Concord Codex's field copies are bronze plate.**

The problem is tin. New bronze is a luxury. *This is why bronze reads archaic: it is archaic, and increasingly it is the only kind there is.*

### The Metals That Are Not Money

**Iron** is too common, rusts, and is worth more as a tool than as a token. It is, however, extremely common as wages on the frontier, where a smith is paid in bar stock and settles his own accounts in nails.

**Salt.** Nalūn casts its rocksalt into standard bars of fixed weight and fineness, stamped by the cutting house, and those bars circulate as money across the whole northern quarter and well into the west. Salt is the only commodity in the ledger that is simultaneously **a food, a preservative, an isolation medium in every serious workshop, and a currency**, and Nalūn has never needed to lie about any of the four. A salt bar is worth a silver mark at the Accord rate, more in a bad winter, **and it can be eaten, which no other money can.**

**Aethersteel and Bloodsilver** are not currencies and are frequently treated as stores of value by people who should know better. Bloodsilver in particular is a poor asset, since it binds to bloodline and burns or corrodes an unworthy holder over time, which makes it **the only item in the Arbitration Division's schedules that can attack the bailiff.**

## The Oath, and Why Credit Exists

In a world of bad roads, worse law, and kingdoms that repudiate their debts on succession, the sensible merchant accepts metal and nothing else. And yet the great trade of the age is conducted in written instruments carried by unarmed men, and has been for four centuries, and the loss rate is low enough that it is insured cheaply.
> The answer is **Nalūn**, and culture has nothing to do with it.
>
> The Kingdom of Nalūn sits on sites of **Contract-aspected Wellsprings**, uniquely able to seal an agreement into metaphysical law. A Parun-bound contract (Parun being the tongue magical law is written in) executed under Nalūn authority is **a working** where elsewhere it would be a promise backed by a court, and breaking it has consequences that arrive without anyone needing to be convinced, subpoenaed, or bribed.

The Council of Links is sworn into office by such contracts. The High Chainlord is chosen by the **Trial of Ten Deals.** Every significant transaction in the chain-cities is sealed with linked tokens, and insulting a merchant's honor is a binding duel challenge because in Nalūn a merchant's honor is **a load-bearing financial instrument.**

What this produces is the one thing commerce anywhere else could not produce: **enforceable long-distance credit.** A bill drawn on Ironlink Hold is good in Kaetra and in the Ring smelters, and those places need feel no respect at all for Nalūn, **because the instrument enforces itself.**

| Consequence | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Specie stays put** | Metal does not need to travel if claims on metal can. This is why the great bullion movements of the era are so much smaller than the trade volumes, why most of the world's coin sits in guild strongboxes and money-changers' cellars, and why a robbery on the Nalūn road is a poor business proposition |
| **Interest is respectable** | Where enforcement is physical rather than moral, lending stops being a sin and becomes a schedule. The Holy See of Lurien maintains this is a distinction without a difference, and has maintained it for four hundred years **while keeping its strongboxes at Ironlink** |
| **The Accord's writ has a rival** | A Concord Writ requires the Enforcement Division to arrive. **A Parun-bound contract does not require anybody to arrive.** Arbitration and the Council of Links have a relationship both describe as cordial |
| **Nalūn cannot be conquered profitably** | Take the tunnels and you hold rock. The value is in the seals, the seals answer to the Wellsprings, and **the Wellsprings do not recognize occupation.** Every serious military assessment of the chain-cities in the last two centuries has ended in the same paragraph |

## The Art of Trade, Through the Ages

- **Antediluvian: you travel to the value**

  There is no long-distance trade worth the name in an era when a working cannot be carried away from the ground it was done on, because everything genuinely valuable is a **place**, and places do not ship. What moves is finished objects, precious stones, and people, and the last of those is the largest category by value.

  Antediluvian commerce is therefore pilgrimage economics. A person who needs a working performed goes to where the working can be performed, pays in whatever they carried, and goes home. The great Antediluvian sites are, read commercially, **toll booths on capabilities that could not be exported.**

  Barter throughout. No coin anywhere in the record.

  The single exception is **the trade in practitioners**, conducted at scale, in writing, with a price per head graded by coil reading. It is the earliest market in the record for which we possess anything resembling a price series, and it is a market in people. The Guild has been asked whether this belongs in a treatise on value and has answered that **a treatise on value which omits the largest priced category of its own period is not a treatise on value.**
- **Early Reconstruction: the first portable value**

  Notation makes an effect storable in an object, and an object can be carried. **This is the birth of trade as the age understands it.**

  Runebronze pieces, inscribed organic substrates, and Living Rune work move along the first genuine long routes. They are absurdly expensive, bought by kingdoms rather than by people, and they establish the pattern that has held ever since: **the high-value trade is Holding-grade, and the bulk trade is grain.**

  Money is weighed metal and standard commodities. A measure of grain, a bolt of cloth of stated width, and a bar of salt all appear in early Reconstruction reckonings, and all three still circulate.
- **Late Reconstruction: the invention of coin**

  The successor kingdoms minted, and **minting is the technology.**

  The metal was always there. Coin adds a guarantee of weight and fineness that saves every party the cost of assaying every transaction, and that saving, multiplied across a trading region, is enormous. The Kaetran issues were accepted **at count rather than by scale** across the whole of the tin road.

  Then they debased. Succession wars had to be paid for, and the fineness came down in steps small enough that each individual step was defensible. Within eighty years the coin was mostly base, prices had risen to a degree the surviving tablets record with visible bewilderment, and the merchants had gone back to weighing.

  The lesson was learned so thoroughly that no polity has debased since without immediate market punishment. The Guild observes that this is **the only instance in the record of a civilization learning an economic lesson once and retaining it**, and that wisdom played no part: the mechanism of retention was an instrument.
- **The Voyager Era: shared units, and the Great Inflation**

  Year Zero establishes shared units, the precondition for everything else. Then the routes open, **and the money breaks.**

  Eastern placer gold is won by hand, by anyone, with no permission required from anyone. That single fact is worth more than every other economic feature of the expansion combined, because it means bullion arrives in the western quarter in quantities **no mint controls and no crown can ration.**

  Prices roughly doubled within a generation. The western guilds spent thirty years blaming hoarding, foreign merchants, bad harvests, and moral decline before a theorist pointed out that when the quantity of money rises faster than the quantity of goods, the money is worth less, **which is not a moral fact and cannot be legislated against.**

  **Wage earners lost.** Wages are customary and adjust slowly; prices are not and do not. A generation of skilled men watched their pay stay nominally identical and halve in what it bought.

  **Fixed-rent landlords lost.** A lease struck in silver at founding rates, running ninety-nine years, became worthless in real terms. The single largest transfer of wealth away from the old nobility in the record, **and it happened without a single estate changing hands.**

  **Debtors won.** Every borrower repaid in cheap money what they had borrowed in dear. The great houses that expanded on credit in the first Voyager decades are the great houses of today, and that is the whole of the explanation for several fortunes usually explained by character.
- **The Long Reckoning: the contract becomes the market**

  A **contract for Essence** (a supply contract, in the old ledgers) is a commitment to deliver at a rate, for a term, from a named source. Delivery means hands: a keeper's Core, and a bonded crew of array-tenders who stand on it, pull, refine, and feed the works at the agreed rate. Among the counting-rooms of the great houses it is the most traded parchment in the four quarters. It is also, in every respect that matters, **a claim on future production**, which means it can be sold before delivery, discounted for early payment, pledged as security, and bet against.

  Nobody set out to invent a market in future Essence. The pattern was lying about in every upland abbey, where the monks had been selling wool off sheep not yet born to merchants for cash down, for generations, and going bankrupt at it. It came to Essence because a pumped shaft needs **certainty** of its hours more than it needs them cheap, and certainty is a thing that can be priced.

  By the close of the era, a western mining house's ledger is mostly contracts. The great houses are, functionally, **Essence traders that happen to own holes.**
- **The Withering: the price of certainty**

  No new instrument of credit has been invented in seventy years. The instruments are the same instruments. **What has changed is a single number underneath all of them.**

  When ambient density falls, a book of hours stops being a convenience and becomes *the* asset. A crew on thinning ground fills slower, spends more, and asks for more, and a keeper with a thinning pool has fewer hours to sell at any price. The spread between the walk-up hour and the standing hour, which sat inside a few points for five centuries, has widened in each of the last seventy years, and a contract written at Year 640 rates is now an inheritance. **Houses are being bought for their books of hours with the ore thrown in.**

  The second-order effect is the one the Arbitration Division watches. A market in claims on a falling quantity **rewards whoever is willing to promise delivery he cannot make**, because the penalty falls due later and the premium is paid now.

  Four houses have failed on delivery in the last decade. *Each failure was described at the time as a local matter.*

## Rank as Property

The Accord's certification is a meritocracy in the strict sense: it measures, it grades, and it does not ask who your father was. **It is also a market**, and understanding that explains most of what people find strange about it.

**Rank is collateral.** An Accord rank is a documented, verifiable claim on future contract income, and money-changers and factor houses maintain schedules of what each rank will support. This is the mechanism by which a landless person can raise capital, and it is the only such mechanism that exists. *A commoner with a rank can finance an expedition. A commoner without one cannot finance a cart.*

**Temperance is a wage schedule.** Because Essence output is measurable, labor that uses Essence is priced directly on the coil. An array-tender's pay is a function of certified output and nothing else. **No other labor market in history has possessed an objective productivity measure**, and the effect is exactly as brutal as it sounds, because it removes every argument a worker could make about their own worth that does not appear on the instrument.

**A person is an assayable asset.** If capability is measurable and future earnings predictable, then a person is a security, and bond contracts written against future output are ordinary instruments traded in ordinary markets. A young practitioner may sell a term of their future output to fund their training, which is how most practitioners who were not born wealthy became practitioners at all.
> The Measurewrights record, because somebody should, that the late Reconstruction kingdoms' enslaved Wellspring labor and a modern training bond **are the same instrument at different levels of refinement.** The differences are real and worth having: the modern bond has a term, a price, a court, and a theoretical buyout.
>
> The similarity is that in both cases **a person's output belongs to somebody else and the paperwork says so.**

**Rank travels; title does not.** A Baron's writ stops at his river. An Accord rank is honored in every member polity. For a great many people the Accord credential is the only portable legal identity they will ever possess, and it is issued on capability rather than on birth, **which is a genuinely revolutionary fact that the Accord has never once described in those terms.**

**Fame moves the schedule.** The instrument prices what a practitioner can do, and the crowd prices who they are. A fighter whose name the minstrels are singing draws a higher retainer than the coil would justify, a free dinner in one town and a doubled bill in the next, and a bounty hunter's interest everywhere. Houses pay for the epithet as well as the output, and they have been known to pay a minstrel to supply one. Bookies quote it. The Division declines to enter it in any schedule, and its valuers price it anyway, in their heads, with their eyes on the crowd.

> **And then there is the floor.** Someone with no rank, no Temperance, and no craft has only their body to sell, and the body is worth what a body is worth in a world where one ranked hand can lift what forty men lift, for a fee, and where in the great shafts the array does the pumping, the ventilating, and the lifting.
> 
> The arrays raised some and revalued everybody, and they revalued ordinary muscle downward, permanently, against a working that does not tire and does not eat. Every cloth town has a statute besides, fixing his day's wage at about what it was in his grandfather's time. The un-Tempered laborer of the Dark Ages is worse off in real terms than his Voyager-era ancestor, who at least was needed to turn the screw.
> 
> *A meritocracy is an excellent arrangement for people with merit that the instrument can see.*

## Crown and Coil

Almost every polity in the four quarters is a monarchy. The republics are the exceptions and they are recent, small, and mostly in the New World. So the world runs on **two systems of legitimacy occupying the same ground**, and neither can abolish the other.
> **The Crown holds** the land, the law, the levy, the tax, and the right to hang.
>
> Legitimate by descent. Requires nobody's certification. **The only authority that can compel a person to fight.**
>
> **The Accord holds** the assay, the rank, the credit, the writ, and the arrays.
>
> Legitimate by measurement. Recognizes no bloodline. It does not need to depose a king. **It needs only to stop pumping.**

*Neither side says this out loud. Both sides have costed it.*

The compromise everyone lives inside has three parts. **Crowns ennoble high rank**, because a Rank V commoner who is refused a place at court is a Rank V commoner available to a neighbor. **Guilds accept charters**, because a guild hall stands on somebody's land. And **marriage does the reconciling**, the standard instrument by which a lineage acquires certified capability and a certified line acquires standing. Every significant political marriage in the last century can be read in both directions at once, and both readings are correct, *and the parties involved know it while the vows are being spoken.*
> Where the arrangement breaks is **succession.** A crown passes to a body. Rank does not.
>
> A kingdom whose heir assays weak, in a century whose neighbors field certified Rank V commoners on contract, has a strategic problem that no amount of ancestry solves and that cannot be fixed at the speed a bloodline breeds. The responses available are all bad: regency, which invites a coup; marriage into capability, which imports a rival house into the succession; reliance on contracted guild talent, which mortgages the crown to the Accord; or **concealment of the assay, which works until it does not.**
>
> Four of the eleven succession crises recorded since Year 200 began with **a disputed coil reading on an heir**, and the Archives files all four under dynastic history.

## How the Poor Are Paid

*Everything above concerns perhaps one person in twenty. Any account of trade that omits the rest is describing a minority.*

**Most transactions involve no coin at all.** Barter is the default in the four quarters today, as it has been in every age, and coin is the exception, used at the boundary between strangers. Within a village, a valley, or a guild hall, obligations are **recorded rather than settled**: who owes whom a day's labor, a cartage, a share of the slaughter, a repair. Settlement happens annually, in kind.

**Wages are largely in kind.** Grain, salt, cloth, ale, board, fuel, and the use of ground. It is the sensible arrangement where coin is scarce and the thing being produced is the thing being consumed, and anyone who calls it charity or backwardness has never had to pay a harvest crew.

**Copper is the only metal most people touch**, which is why the copper famines that follow every great array are political events rather than monetary curiosities. When the small coin goes into the windings, the poor lose access to **the one form of money that connects them to anyone outside their valley.**

**Notched tallies serve where writing does not.** A split stick, both halves held, is a complete and forgery-resistant record of a debt between two people who cannot read, and it is legally admissible in six kingdoms.

**And in every kept city there is the bill.** A sealed household has never bought Essence or seen any. What it buys is **a grade of work**, monthly, which comes down to a named warden's thread of Essence in the lamp by the door at dusk and a named cutter's ward on the frame, under the charter of whichever company holds that street, and the lead seal on the lamp stem is the company's property rather than the householder's. A poor stair splits one lamp and one seal among six doors and fights about it, and its heat is its own hearth. The wardens can walk off the round and the street can stop paying, and both do ([[The Magic System/Practice and Institution/Who Pays for the Lamp The Living Crafts|Who Pays for the Lamp]] has the whole brawl).
> *This is the only relationship most people in the four quarters will ever have with the primary economic good, and it is the relationship of a debtor to the holder of a charter.* **The fee is set by the charter-holder, the grade of work delivered runs below what the fee implies in every poor quarter that has ever been surveyed, and the remedy is a magistrate the company's factor drinks with.**
>
> **The snuffer's cap costs nothing to fit and four seconds to lift**, which means the entire force behind a street's charter is one iron cap, one scribing iron for the wards, and a man with the right key. *He is the most despised trade in any city and the best paid of the four, for the same reason the executioner is.*
>
> **Mine scrip is the newest insult.** Lead tokens stamped by the shaft's owner and issued in place of coin, good only at the owner's stall at the pit-head, at prices the owner sets. Legal in the western coal foreland, legal in the Ring smelter towns, and defended by its operators on the reasonable-sounding ground that **there is not enough copper to pay in.** A clothier's version is to pay his spinners in pins and girdles, and that one is illegal in eleven towns and practiced in all of them.
>
> There is not, and the reason there is not is that the same companies melted it.

**And some wealth is not money at all.** The Tallow Clans measure a house by how many tighremts it can fill before the first frost, and record it in a **Ledger-Song** sung in front of every other clan, which is an accounting system with better fraud controls than most of the four quarters manages in writing. A poor high season is remembered for generations. No coin changes hands anywhere in the process, *and it would be a mistake to conclude that no accounting has occurred.*

## Unresolved Entries

**The Essence peg.** Settled. The contract unit is a **standing hour**, a standing myriad in the Accord's own books: one named hour for one body on the lip of a named Core, daily, for a term of one year. **Essence is quoted as a rate because Essence is a flow**, and every attempt to quote it as a quantity has produced a contract nobody could enforce. The Accord rate stands at forty silver marks the standing hour, against twelve at Year 640, **and the whole of the Withering's economic history is in the distance between those two figures.**
> *A house holding a book of hours written at Year 640 rates is holding an instrument worth more than three times its face and rising, which is why houses are now bought for their books with the ore thrown in.*

**Silver's resonance.** The attribution of person-addressed binding to silver is drawn from the behavior of Bloodsilver and Dawnsilver thread rather than from any direct statement in the substrate tables, which name basalt, quartz, iron, gold, and obsidian and are **silent on silver.** This is inference and is marked as such.

**Consecrated gold.** Whether gold taken into a rite is permanently removed or merely encumbered is doctrinally disputed between Lurien and Eresse, and the two positions have different monetary consequences **at a scale neither institution appears to have calculated.**

**The Empire's mint standard.** Assay houses calibrate against early Imperial bronze on the assumption that the early issues were honest. Nobody has independently verified this. **Every mass measurement in the four quarters rests on the good faith of a polity that later debased its silver by four fifths.**
> **Whether Nalūn's seals can fail.** No Parun-bound contract executed under Council authority has been recorded as failing. The Council of Links regards this as proof. The Measurewrights regard it as **an absence of evidence maintained by a body that would not publish the exception.**

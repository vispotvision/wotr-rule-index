# Master Works

*The one piece at the end of a lifetime that does what it has no right to: what they are, the famous ones in every trade, why the bench's great prize is the hardest of all, who has died trying, and what any of it has to do with being called a Master*

> *Pot, iron. Lid for same. Stamp: Marked. Rarity: Unique. Clerk's note: I have struck this entry out twice and written it in again.*
>
> *Assay roll of Pennycrown, the Hundred Crowns, for the year 672*

The siege of Pennycrown was in its ninth week when Ser Lucan Vaye came through the Bakers' Gate. He was the Comet of Tollgarth, [[The Magic System/The Soul and the Climb/The Sixteen Stages|Stage]] VIII, in plate enameled sunset orange, with a banner on his back and two hundred men shouting his name behind him, and he came in a full Release (the named, staged power-up a fighter calls aloud) with his Domain (the ground on which a high-Stage practitioner's own law holds) rolling out ahead of him like a burning carpet and a lance of lit air four yards long running out from his right fist. He called its name so they could hear it in the next kingdom. The whole of Bakers' Row had already run, apart from a Tinker Goblin of eighty-three called Mother Spragg. She had been making pots in that street for sixty-eight years, she had a stew on, and she had got up on a barrel in the middle of the road in her apron and a pair of smoked-glass goggles, holding up a pot lid in one small green hand.

"THE LAST LID!" she screamed, because craftsmen shout the names of their works, and this was the only time anyone ever heard her raise her voice about one. "FINEST GUILD WORK! SPRAGG'S OF PENNYCROWN! SIXTY-EIGHT YEARS NO COMPLAINTS!"

The Comet of Tollgarth hit it at the speed of a falling star. A strike at that Grade against that lid should have gone through it as if the lid were not there, and through Mother Spragg and the barrel and the next nine houses. It stopped. All of it stopped, four yards of lit air folding up flat against a circle of pig iron stamped Marked, the lowest stamp the Guild Accord (the great coalition of guilds across many nations) has, and everything he had put into it went back the only way left to go. It took his arm out at the shoulder in a great red fan across the bakery fronts, and it put him on his back in the road with his plate burst open and his Domain going out around him like a kicked fire. Two hundred men stopped shouting. Mother Spragg climbed down off the barrel, and she looked at him for a while, and then she told him he had got blood on her step. She went back in and put the lid on the stew.

It is an ordinary lid. Anyone can see that, and the coil says so: Grade E, plain iron out of a bad pot. What goes under that lid stays exactly as it was, and nothing that means it harm gets in. She died that winter, in her sleep, at the bench, with a run of nine new lids around her that were all perfectly good lids. It has covered a family stew in Bakers' Row for forty years, and nobody at that table has ever been poisoned, despite several sincere attempts, and the family will let you look at it for a copper. The Comet of Tollgarth lived, one-armed, to be a very thoughtful old man.

The trade has a term for such a thing, and I will now spend some time explaining why you will never make one.

## What One Is

**A Master Work is a piece that does what its Grade and material should not be able to, made once at the end of a lifetime, always Unique or Mythic, and named.**

Each of those clauses has been fought over in the assay rooms, so here they are one by one.

**What its Grade and material should not be able to.** The law of [[Materials, Alchemy & Trade/Work, Trade and Measure/The Apparatus of the Age — Equipment and Material Culture/The Item Tiers|Grade and Rarity]] holds everywhere else without exception: a material holds what it can hold, a maker cannot put in what she does not have, and a strike two Tiers over the proof passes through. A Master Work is the one standing exception. The coil (the brass assay instrument) reads it at its proper Grade, the material is the material, and it does the thing regardless. It usually does only one thing, the same one forever.

**Made once.** Nobody has made a second, and nobody has made one by trying harder. A maker who has done it can go on working, with sound hands and all her skill, and everything else she makes is merely excellent. Most say they felt something go out of them, and that they knew. Most die within the year. Mother Spragg lasted till the frost. The few who lived on tended to give up the trade and take up something like keeping bees.

**At the end of a lifetime.** Fifty years at one thing at the least, and most of them longer. A person's skill rises with use and shows as a Grade on the sheet (the window's list of a person's numbers, which only they can see), and fifty years at one thing can give an EX-Grade trade skill with no Stage to speak of. Such a master is frightening. They usually still lose a fight, and a Master Work is what they have instead. It takes place when it takes place: no maker has yet known in advance which piece was going to be the one.

**Always Unique or Mythic.** Rarity is scarcity, and it comes with the definition. Mythic means a handful in the world, and Unique means one. A small number of pieces get written Mythic in place of Unique because a trade insists they are one of a family, such as the bench's Fate Essence Drafts. A Master Work's other rating, its Grade, can be anything down to the bottom of the letters, and it often is.

**And named.** By the maker if they had the breath, and otherwise by whoever the thing first astonished. The name goes on the roll with the Grade, and the clerk generally writes something like *sic* beside it.

**Each area has its own.** Every trade has a list and brags about it, and every region is sure its own list is the only real one. Bonded gear grows with its owner, and other gear does not, and a Master Work stays exactly as it was made however long anybody holds it. It has no opinions, and that sets it apart from a true relic of the Great Spirit War or the Physical Epochs, which reads as question marks to every appraiser, which cannot be copied, and which may have views about who holds it. A Master Work reads perfectly well:

```
[Appraisal: A]
Pot lid. Grade E. Rarity: Unique.
Stamp: Marked. Mark: Spragg, Pennycrown. Reads true.
Does: ???
```

**On the bragging.** Craftsmen shout the names of their works and brag about their guild marks at all times, and a Master Work is the one thing every one of them will bring up in a tavern within the hour, as evidence that their trade is the serious one. A cooper of Raftwick will tell you with tears in his eyes that the barrel at Thornhaven is the finest thing ever done by human hands. It holds water. He will explain to you what is remarkable about that, and it will take him all evening.

## Why They Cannot Be Ordered

Everyone has tried. Great houses keep **old-maker's pensions**: a fine stipend and a warm room in the family's own grounds for any master past seventy-five, with every material they could want, a guard on the door and a clerk to write down anything they say. It looks like charity from outside, and the makers are all perfectly aware of what it is. Some of them have got ten very good years out of it and a number of excellent chairs, and at least two have spent their last winter making something deliberately appalling and leaving it on the steward's pillow.

**Why it happens** is the oldest argument in the Measurewrights' hall. The favored account at present runs like this. A soul that has worked one way all its life hardens along that way, woken or unwoken, in what the trade calls grain. Carry, in any rendered material, is a law something survived into existence and which came along into the stock. So a Master Work would be carry with no rendering: fifty or eighty years of one person's grain, the whole law of how they worked, gone out through the hands in one stroke into whatever was on the bench, and held there by what the thing is rather than by what it is made of. It explains the once, and it explains why they die. It does not explain the lid. The Guild's best instrument finds no Essence in the lid at all, and four Measurewrights have had it on their tables and gone home to think. *The account's great defender, Magister Rule of Sealhall, held that a person could therefore be assayed for one in advance, and he assayed some three hundred old craftsmen and published his predictions. None of them made anything. The woman who cleaned the instrument did.*

**What a forger does.** A fake Master Work cannot be disproved by assay, since the coil reads a real one as an ordinary thing. It can be disproved by trying it, and sellers are very good at explaining why you should not. A good forger beats a weak appraiser, and in this one corner of the market a good story beats a good appraiser. Every fair has a stall with the True Hammer of somebody on it.

**How one changes hands.** Rarely. A merchant's title caps the Rarity he may sell, so a lawful sale of a Mythic piece needs an Archmaster's token and a Unique one needs a Paragon's, and there are not many of those. So they go the other ways: inheritance, marriage, murder. A great many famous pieces are held by people who are wondering how to keep them, and there is a list of past owners on all of them that a sensible person reads to the end.

## The Fate Essence Draft

At the bench a Master Work is a Draft, and every alchemist alive dreams of pouring one kind. **A Fate Essence Draft** bottles a share of the Plane of Fate, the control of Attraction Force (the pull that makes a name, a bond or a vow take hold) and Obsession Force (that same pull when it will not let go), and through those two the workings of reality itself: the manipulation of probabilities to work outside what reality already knows. Real Fate Arts are rare and mostly by bloodline. For nearly everyone else alive, the only ways to touch Fate are a blessing (see [[The Magic System/Practice and Institution/Blessings and Curses|Blessings and Curses]]) or one of these. It lends the touch. It no more makes a [[The Magic System/The Soul and the Climb/The Three Paths Routing, Recognition and the Gate|Fate Path]] practitioner than a borrowed horse makes a cavalryman, and it attaches readily to an oath. There are a handful in the world at any time, and there has never been a price. Great families have spent a county to stand near one.

```
[Draft: unnamed lot, sealed]
[Appraisal: S]
[Grade: B. Rarity: Mythic.]
[Does: ???]
```

**The stock.** Nothing in a monster's stone will do it, because a stone is Core and nothing else. The Attraction Layer is the part of a soul that holds its share of fate, and the only Layer that has ever reached a bench was shaved off a dead practitioner's Crystal by a dresser and sold in a twist of waxed paper (see [[Materials, Alchemy & Trade/Alchemy/The Common Drafts#Stock, layer and lens|barber's curl]]). So the grandest bottle in the trade begins in the same back room as the Dead Man's Cordial. The masters who will talk about it say a great Spirit Beast's Layer would serve as well, and that they are still waiting for someone to bring one up the stair. No Alchemarch has entered the purchase in a day book.

### Why it is the hardest

Every other Master Work is a person doing the thing they have always done, one more time, and something happening. This one has five separate problems, and each of them has killed somebody.

**It has no [[The Magic System/The Foundations/The Sixty Wellsprings/The Sixty Wellsprings|Wellspring]].** Each of the seven stages of the Great Work takes its law from a Wellspring of its own, excepting Conjunction, the stage where two things that pull toward each other close the gap, which runs on Attraction alone. A Fate charge is a Conjunction with nothing to wed: the maker takes the charge up to the Wedding and has it marry the pull itself. So the bench has no Authorization to invoke and nothing that already knows the maker, and the discount a harmonized alchemist gets on her own Wellspring's Drafts does not apply.

**The boundary has nothing to hold.** A chain with no Boundary role is refused at the Bench outright, because a transformation without limits does not stop when the intended material is consumed. Odds have no edge. There is no glyph that says *here* to a probability, and the masters who have got anywhere have mostly done it by binding the working to a named person or an oath, which gives it somewhere to end. An unbounded lot is the bad day described below, and on a bad day it gets into the whole street.

**The maker is in the vessel.** The Essence Core (the seat of a soul's own nature) seeds whatever it is broadcasting into the material, so an alchemist's state is a working variable in any Draft. In this one it is the whole of the Draft. A maker who wants the thing to succeed has poured wanting. Wanting is Attraction with a grip on it, and that is the road to Obsession Force. The maker has to arrive at the last pour after a lifetime's ambition having stopped caring, and nobody has found a way of doing this on purpose, and the fact that you are trying is the entire difficulty.

**It takes a walk nobody takes.** The received account is that a master has to have walked Conjunction twice, once at Flourishing, Stage IV, in the first round of the Work and again at Dissonance, which is Stage XI, or else to have come as far in grain with no Crystal at all. That cuts the field to very few, and they are all very old, and each of them has been in a house's pension for some time.

**You cannot test it.** Any other Draft can be assayed. A Fate lot reads at whatever its energy says, usually something modest. The coil cannot see luck, a lucky man cannot prove it, and the only trial is to drink it, which consumes the sample. Nobody at the bench has ever known whether they had succeeded. Several of the most celebrated lots may be nothing. And anyone who pours one is, by the nature of the stock, surrounded by odds that have stopped behaving, in a room where everything now either goes unusually right or unusually wrong, for years.

### The ones that are known

**The Turned Coin**, by Alchemarch Hesper Ludd of Candle Wharf (an Alchemarch being the conferred honor of perhaps forty people in the four quarters, whose signature the Bench accepts without assay). She poured it in the last year of her life and sold it to a Thresh horse-coper named Oswin Gant for a horse. She was, by the account of her Luteboy (the apprentice who mixes the clay that seals a vessel), laughing at the time. The Coin is the only lot in the record whose effect is agreed.

**Nine Mornings**, by Alchemarch Ishbel Tarn of Vaultmere, was given as a dowry into a great house of the South two centuries ago and has never been unsealed. The family has prospered without a break in all that time, through three wars and more than one change of crown. Nobody knows whether possession is the effect, whether it is doing anything, or whether they are just very good at what they do, and nobody in the family will risk opening it to see. It has its own guard, its own chapel, and a steward who has gone slightly strange.

**The Even Hand**, by a master of the lineage halls named Wen Baozhi, after forty-one years of seclusion on a mountain. It was drunk by a magistrate in 590 who had asked to be fair. For the eleven years that remained to him, every judgment he gave came out exactly right, and he could not make any other kind. He sentenced his own son on the Thursday.

**Forged lots** are the most profitable counterfeit in the world, since nothing can prove them false. A buyer who has bad luck afterward is told it is working in a way he does not yet understand. There has been more than one lord who took a bottle of colored water, marched on a neighbor out of sheer confidence, and won.

## What the Title Has to Do With It

Almost nothing, and the assay rooms have been saying so for centuries, to no effect whatever. People hear *Master Work* and assume it is the piece that makes you a Master. The two words have only a spelling in common.

**The title.** Initiate, Apprentice, Journeyman, Adept, Expert, Master, Grandmaster, Archmaster, Paragon: the same nine in every guild, grading how far that guild trusts you in your trade (see [[Factions, Bloodlines & Institutions/Powers and Polities/The Guild Accord/Structure and Rank/The Tiered Path|The Tiered Path]]). **Master** is the sixth of them. You earn it by certified work and it lets you make up to a stated Grade. A title is other people's opinion, which is why it sits on a silver token at the belt and appears nowhere on your window.

**The rung.** A house's own ladder has a Master of the Circle on it, who scribes and seals and carries the liability. It is a job in one workshop, and it is a different thing again.

**The Work.** A Master Work is an event. It happens to the person who has the fifty years, and the token at their belt has no bearing. **Aldous Wray never rose past Journeyman.** He had a bad opinion of the Masons' examiners and declined to sit. Mother Spragg held an Apprentice's token in tin for sixty years, having failed the Journeyman's board twice for bad temper. Goodwife Lark had no token of any kind. A good many Grandmasters and Archmasters have lived out a magnificent career of sealed, certified, perfectly correct work at the top of their guild, and gone to their pyres with no Master Work behind them, knowing exactly who down the street had got one with a pot.

**The law, such as it is.** A smith's title caps the Grade she may lawfully make, and a merchant's caps the Rarity he may lawfully sell. A Master Work is the one thing no title caps, because a Grade E lid is well inside an Initiate's license, and whatever it does is nobody's license at all. The assay rooms have never agreed what to stamp on one. Usually the proper stamp for its Grade goes on, with Unique beside it, and the clerk's handwriting gets worse.

**Which means.** A guild can certify everything but this, so every guild has a very awkward relationship to it. They claim each one as theirs as soon as the maker is dead. Guild halls in every trade carry the name of some cantankerous old person whom they refused to promote, in gold letters, over the door.

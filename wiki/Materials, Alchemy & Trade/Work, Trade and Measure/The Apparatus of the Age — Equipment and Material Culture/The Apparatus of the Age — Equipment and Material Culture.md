# The Apparatus of the Age — Equipment and Material Culture

*A Register of Equipment, Craft and Material Culture Across the Four Quarters*

*Compiled for the Logistics and Supply Division · appendix to The Works and Days, The Bearing and the Holding, and The Four Ceilings*

> A soldier will tell you what his sword cost. His wife will tell you what the candles cost, and she will be nearer the truth about the war.
>
> *Marginal note in the Division's inventory of Marchbank, in a clerk's hand*

The Worshipful Chandlers of Tallow and the Worshipful Chandlers of Wax, in the town of Marchbank, had not exchanged a civil word in ninety years, and every one of those years they had fought through lawyers over whose candles went first in the Lamp Feast procession. In the ninety-first the Wax Company stopped hiring lawyers and hired Master Ivo Pettibone. He climbed the market cross in a saffron coat, with the whole town packed into the square under its banners, tallow blue and white on the east side, wax gold and crimson on the west, and he shouted the name of his masterpiece three times so that the back rows could get it right: THE PROCESSIONAL CANDELABRUM OF PETTIBONE, NINE HUNDRED LIGHTS AND NOT A HAND UPON THEM. It stood twenty feet high on an ox wagon, a brass tree hung with nine hundred tapers of the best beeswax, turned on its axle by an Ascensio-cut wheel (Ascensio being the Wellspring of the hoist and the long way up) with his guild mark on the base the size of a cart door. It turned beautifully for about a hundred yards. Then the glyph, which he had cut for a tree with no wax on it, ran away. The tree spun. The tapers leaned out flat on their springs, and molten wax went from it in a golden fan across the Tallow Company, the Tallow Company's banners and the Bishop of Marchbank, who was lit in the sleeve. The oxen, the only parties present with any sense, left for the river, taking the tree with them still spinning and still throwing fire at the striped awnings of Eel Row. The Tallow men cheered every awning until the row reached their own hall.

Seven people died, and an ox, and the town smelled of mutton and honey for a month. The award came forty-one years later, by which time everybody who filed the suit was dead, and it fixed the price of tallow at a copper bit for the dozen, which is still the price of tallow, and awarded Wax first place in the procession on condition that it walk. Pettibone lost his mark, and had it back inside the year in his wife's name.

The Division mentions the affair because it explains the order of this register. Every clerk who ever joined wanted to start with the swords. We start with the candle, because the candle is what the four quarters will actually go to law over, and we reach the swords in due course.

## The Common Table

**The majority of objects in the four quarters contain no Essence, hold no inscription, and are made from Bearing-grade material**: iron, wood, stone, leather, cloth, clay.

> *They are listed first because they are the most numerous, the most important, and the most likely to appear in a scene that does not involve practitioners killing each other.*

### Light

**The Tallow Candle** · The standard light of the four quarters, inside the wardens' rounds as well as beyond them, because a warden keeps the street and the door and nobody keeps a poor man's supper table, **and the object that appears in more scenes than any weapon.** Rendered from animal fat by the chandler, sold by weight. **Burns smoky, smells of the animal it came from, and provides enough light to eat by but not to read by unless you hold it close enough to singe the page.** Below it sits the rushlight, a peeled rush dipped in kitchen fat, which costs nothing and lasts about as long as an argument.

> *A household that is not wealthy burns tallow. A household that is wealthy burns beeswax, which is why the smell of a room tells you immediately how much money is in it.*

**The Oil Lamp** · Fish oil on the coast, seed oil inland, whale oil where the ships reach. **The standard lighting in any workshop where the work requires both hands.** *A smith works by lamp. An Engraver works by lamp and by loupe. An innkeeper works by whatever the guests have not yet stolen.*

> **The Essence Lantern** · The first object here that contains a working. A Clearbeam beryl lens over a Luminalis-inscribed Aethersteel plate (Luminalis being the Wellspring of light), made one at a time by a Guild craftsman with his mark on the stem: **steady, cool, odorless, no flicker, no smoke, no fuel.** You will find them on street corners, guild halls and temple porches, and at the doors of people who want it known they can afford one. Inside an ordinary house you will find tallow.
>
> Left alone it pulls what it needs from the ground it stands on, **which means it works indefinitely on rich ground and dims to nothing in a Withering-era district.** (The Withering is the slow failing of a place's Aether, the great thinning of the last seventy years, and every scholar has a different date for its start.)
>
> **That is the older pattern and it is now the rural one.** In any kept city the lantern is on a lamp-warden's round. She walks up at dusk with a boy and a pole, feeds it a thread of her own Essence, and it burns her color until dawn, so that a good street at night is amber, rose and kingfisher green, and does not care what the ground is doing. *The countryside burns what the ground gives it. The city burns what somebody pays a woman to keep alight, and she can walk past your door any evening she likes.*
>
> *A house with a working lantern at its door is a house where the local Wellspring site is still strong or the warden is still paid. A house that has gone back to tallow knows something the factor house has not posted yet.*

**Cost** · Tallow, a copper bit for a dozen. Oil, a copper per evening. Beeswax, a silver piece for a quality taper. Essence lantern, **three to eight silver marks plus the inscription fee, a gold mark and up, which is the expensive part.** *The lantern body lasts a lifetime. The inscription lasts as long as the local density does.*

### Heat and water

**The Hearth** · **The center of every household, from the shepherd's turf hut to the duke's great hall, where there are six of them and a wood bill that would buy your street.** It heats, cooks, lights and gathers. *Fuel is wood, peat in the uplands, charcoal where wood is scarce and coin is not, and dried dung on the frontier, which burns hot and smells exactly as advertised.*

**The Ember Box** · A clay-lined container holding slow charcoal or a single **Ember Flint.** *The Flint is the single most widely distributed Essence-active object in the eastern quarter by simple count, and in the west it is the field practitioner's survival stock:* **a reliable fire-starter that works in wet conditions where flint and steel will not.**

**The Tile Stove** · A mason's work and the great luxury of the cold countries: a tower of green-glazed tile built into the wall of a rich burgher's best room and fired from the passage outside, so that the smoke and the servant both stay out of sight. **It warms one room and takes a month to build.** *An upland merchant will tell you what his stove cost before he tells you his wife's name.*

**The Warm Stone** · A fist of cut stone a warden has filled with a day's slow heat, wrapped in flannel and carried to a sickbed, a lying-in or a rich man's cold feet. **Hired by the night from whoever keeps one, and in a hard winter it costs what its keeper says it costs.** The poor have the parish stone, the waist-high iron-bound working point in the road where a warden keeps a little heat, where you bring a pot to boil and a child to thaw, and where there is always a quarrel about whose turn it is.

**The Common Oven** · Most city rooms have no oven and many have no hearth worth the name. The dough and the pie go down the street on a board to the baker, who stamps them and charges a copper, and the cookshop does the rest. *More families in Marchbank have gone to law over a swapped pie than over a swapped child.*

**The Well** · **The most important thing in any settlement.** In a warded settlement an inscription at the foundation stone monitors contamination, *which makes the ward-carver (the mason-practitioner who cuts a settlement's great wards) the person standing between the community and the specific death that arrives when the water turns bad.*

**The Phreatis Filter** · A stone cut by the ward-carver and set in the well's spout, one to a village if the village is lucky. **It purifies nothing.** It changes temperature when the water passing through carries biological load. *A diagnostic rather than a treatment, and the limitation is that knowing your water is bad and being able to do anything about it are two different problems connected by the distance to the nearest clean source.*

> **The chain-cities' obsession with drainage has a plain cause.** *Standing water dissolves salt, and a chain-city is carved from salt, and the sentence writes itself.*

### The kept house

*A kept city keeps its doors by hand. What a household gets from the people who keep it is a visit, and two small objects to prove the visit happened, and neither object belongs to the household.*

**The Seal** · On the lamp stem or pressed into the lintel's wax, a lump of stamped lead saying who did the work, what was paid and until when. **The seal is the wardens' company's property rather than the householder's**, which is why breaking one is a specific offense with its own fine and is prosecuted more reliably than assault. *A house on a good street is described in a lease by whose wards are on its door before it is described by its rooms.*

**The Snuffer's Cap** · A blank iron cap the snuffer (the fee-collector's bailiff, the most despised trade in any city) drops over the lamp stem when the fee goes unpaid, after he has scratched through the wards on the frame. **It mends nothing and guards nothing, and it lifts in four seconds for anyone with the right key and for nobody else.**

> **The commonest illegal object in any city is a scrap of parchment with a glyph copied on it.** A botch, a lamp fed or a lintel re-cut by a stranger after dark, is theft from a lawful company, which is a magistrate's business rather than the Accord's, **and is completely unrelated in law to Mechanica (forbidden working that takes power without lawful consent) and completely indistinguishable from it to the man in the room upstairs.**
>
> *A botch is done by somebody who knows what they are doing and paid in something other than coin. Every quarter of every kept city has two or three of them, and everybody local knows which two or three, and nobody says.*

### The larder and the well

*What a household eats this week depends on what it can salt, smoke, pot and pickle, and on how far it has to carry it home.*

**Salt, smoke and fat** · Every larder in the four quarters stands on these. Herring in the barrel, stockfish on the pole (cod dried hard in the north wind, and beaten with a mallet before anyone can cook it), pork in brine, ham in the chimney, goose potted under its own fat in a sealed crock, plums in honey, cabbage gone sour on purpose, hard cheese with a red rind you can see across a market. **Salt is the price behind all of it**, so a bad salt year is a hungry winter eight months later, and every cook in the quarter can tell you which year that was.

**The Ration Barrel** · The company, the ship, the expedition and the miner's dinner all keep on the cooper's work: salt pork and herring in oak, hard biscuit in a sack, dried pease, a cheese in a net. **A barrel goes wherever a ration has to keep**, and the cooper's mark on the head says who to blame. *The salt barrel has done more to lengthen a campaign than any working in the Codex, and the pork wants a day's soaking that no sergeant has ever once allowed it.*

**Snow and the Ice-House** · A great lord's pleasure: a brick-lined pit on the north side of a hill, packed with winter ice and straw, and opened for sherbet, a banquet or a physician's fever. A few mule trains a year bring northern lake ice south over the passes in sawdust and sacking, **and what is sold at the far end is about half of what set out.** *The cold is free and the hauling is not, and a winter that fails to freeze the lakes is a summer when a duke drinks warm wine and his physician has nothing for the fever.* **It is the north's proudest luxury and the one the north itself never uses.**

**Master Frake's Everlasting Winter Cupboard** · Every generation of Guild masters promises a cold cupboard for every kitchen, and this is the one that was built. It stands chained in the undercroft at Marchbank: a lined oak cabinet with a Cinerion plate set in the back (Cinerion being the Wellspring that carries heat off), made for one bishop's wine by Master Lambkin Frake, who shouted its name from the cathedral steps. **The cold in it is made by carrying heat out of the box and into whatever stands behind it**, and every step of that climb has to be fed by a warden. Frake understood all of this and stood it against the wall of the pastry kitchen. On its first afternoon it froze the Bishop's wine, split the cask and set fire to a pastry cook's apron through eleven inches of stone. It has since worn out three wardens, and the Bishop's successors will neither use it nor let anyone else have it.

**Water and waste** · Water is carried. **A great abbey or a duke's house may have a conduit in Kirkyard lead, run from a spring by a plumber (a lead-worker) who is paid like a sculptor, and a garderobe shaft over a moat or a stream.** A rich town has one public conduit-head, a carved stone fountain with a daily brawl at it. The middle have a well in the yard and a privy shared by the stair. **The poor have the parish well, the pit and the gong-farmer's cart**, the gong-farmer being the man who digs the pits out by night and is allowed to live only in certain streets, and they queue at the well in the morning for the day's water or pay the water-carrier a copper for two buckets up the stair.

> **The poor buy food by the day.** There is nowhere to keep it overnight and nothing to keep it from the rats, so it is salt herring at dawn and the cookshop at dusk. *A household's diet is a map of its purse, and the map can be read off any table in the city.*

### Dress

**Common dress** · Wool or linen, undyed unless the wearer can afford the dyer, **in which case the color itself is the class marker.** *Blue from woad is common, red from madder is dearer, and anything that holds its color after six months of wearing and washing cost more than the garment.*

**The Practitioner's Coat** · **No standard pattern.** *A Corporis fighter wears what moves with the body and survives being hit. An Engraver wears something with pockets. An Alchemist wears something that has already survived several chemical accidents and shows it.* Sect people add the colors of their school, as loud as the school can afford, and will fight you about them.

> **The single universal element is the title token**, a silver disc at throat, belt or wrist, inscribed to the bearer with guild and title (one of the nine, Initiate through Paragon) and verifiable on a coil. Sect people wear theirs beside the colors, since a sect rank says where you stand in that school and a title says what you are trusted with, and both matter.
>
> **Simultaneously a badge, a passport, a line of credit, and its own weight in bullion**, *and losing it is a catastrophe the bearer will spend the next three months resolving, in person, at a series of halls.*

### Station in cloth

*A city street is dressed across three generations at once, and which one a person wears is the first thing anybody reads.*

**The rich** · Dress to this year's fashion and to the capital. **The short parti-colored doublet, the dagged sleeve and the long-toed shoe for men; the fitted gown, the tight lacing and then the loose open surcoat, the tall horned headdress for women**, from a tailor who keeps their measurements on a notched stick. *A rich house can be dated by its daughters' headdresses, and in the capitals the date is always this year.*

**The middle** · Dress as their parents dressed at their age. **The long gown, the stiff hood and the tall felt hat on a Guild day; the full skirt, the wimple and the good belt.** *Respectable runs a generation behind fashionable, knows it, and counts this a virtue.*

**The poor** · Wear what work has always worn. **Fustian, frieze and russet, a cap, a shawl, a canvas apron, nailed boots**, bought third-hand or made over. *Work clothes do not change because the work does not, and a laborer from the first years of the age would pass unremarked on a building site in the last.*

> **The alchemical dye-vats have bent the old color order without quite breaking it.** Now and then an alchemist-dyer coaxes out of one vat a mauve or a magenta no plant ever gave, cannot do it twice, and is sued by the Dyers' Company whichever way it goes. Between those vats and the rag-sellers, the rule above, that color is money, still holds in the provinces and is already a joke in the capitals, where a fishwife may go to market in a shade a duchess wore last spring, bought fourth-hand from a fripperer (a dealer in old clothes). *The rich answered first with sumptuary rolls (town laws saying who may wear what), which nobody obeys, and then by moving the marker from color to cut, which no fripperer can alter.*
>
> **Every people bends all three through its own Inventory.** The cut, the color and the object that must be on the body are theirs. The layering by class is the age's.

### The hour

*Two hours run in every Guild town, and a man is late for one of them.*

**The Guild Hour** · Twenty-four equal hours of sixty minutes, kept on the great water clock at Sealhall and carried out by relay rider, sealed sand-glass and the beacons to every assay post and guild hall the Guild runs, where a water clock of their own holds it more or less until the next rider. **Where a Guild hall stands, its bell rings the Guild's hour.** No two halls agree to the count, a hard frost stops the clock and the boy is blamed for it, and the arguments keep a good many Measurewrights in wine.

> **The work bell is what made the Guild hour matter.** A cloth town's masters hang a bell to start and stop the day for everyone on time wages, and a bell rung late on a long summer evening is wages stolen in front of the whole town. In Year 694 Jonet Teasel, a journeyman fuller of Cardmere (a fuller being someone who pounds woven wool thick), climbed the hall tower on the eve of the wool fair with her arms blue to the elbow and cut the rope. The masters hung a new rope and passed a new law: sixty silver to anyone who rang that bell to call the town out. Three nights later the fullers, weavers and carders of Cardmere pushed a wheelbarrow to the hall steps, tipped sixty silver in coppers onto the masters' table, and rang it until dawn. *Relay riders need the hour too, because two stations cannot agree on a change of horses by the sun, but it was the wheelbarrow that made it law.*

**The Quernby Bar** · One object, and the Guild would like you to stop asking to see it. A bar of cut stone inscribed with Basilithe (the Wild Wellspring that fixes a body's resonance) and struck, which rings at one pitch and holds that pitch against anything that tries to drive it off. Measurewright Aldith Quernby spent thirty-one years cutting it, and the Guild's assay forks and reference coils are tuned against it once a year, in a room with no windows, by people in felt slippers.

> **Its limit is the one its Wellspring carries.** A frequency that cannot be shifted cannot be detuned away from a forcing that matches it, so the Bar was cut off the pitch of the local hum (the faint note every kept street gives off from its wards and lamps), and **a sound held at its own pitch will shatter it.** *The pitch is written in a sealed book. The Guild has hanged two choirmasters on suspicion of having looked.*

**The Sand-Glass** · A blown glass and a measure of fine sand or ground eggshell in a wooden frame, good for half an hour or a whole Guild hour, on a ship's deck, in a preacher's pulpit, in a physician's bag and on a lawyer's table. **It tells you how long it has been and has no idea what time it is.** A boy turns it, and the boy can be bribed.

**The Pocket Dial** · A folding sundial of boxwood or ivory, or a brass ring with a pinhole in it, small enough for a purse, with a thread for a shadow and the saints' days cut into the lid. **The clerk's sign of office and the rich man's toy.** Boxwood for the foreman who has to prove he was on time, brass for the clerk, ivory and silver for his employer. *It needs the sun, so it is useless at night, in the rain, indoors and down a shaft, which is to say everywhere a clerk actually works, and every clerk carries one. A man who owns a dial has joined the Guild hour in spirit. A man who does not still lives by the light, and arrives at about the same time.*

**Bells and the sun** · In the country and the north the working day still runs from first light to last, longer in summer, rung from the church or the fort. **A traveler coming out of a Guild town into the provinces sets his day by the bell and puts his dial away.**

## The Soldier's Kit

> *The quality of a soldier's equipment is the single most reliable indicator of how much the employer values his life, which is usually less than the recruitment speeches suggested.*

### The levy soldier

**Gambeson as baseline wherever the levy marches**, in the north, the hill provinces and the frontier companies, and most other places besides. Eighteen to thirty quilted layers, worn by every levy man regardless of what goes over it. **Genuinely protective**: stops glancing cuts, absorbs blunt impact, resists arrows at range.

> **The correction that matters: riveted mail vastly outperforms butted mail.**
>
> A bodkin point **forces a butted ring apart and is resisted by a riveted one**, *which makes construction a plot-relevant distinction, and cheap mail a death sentence dressed as equipment.*

**Helmet** · **The most important piece after the gambeson, because head wounds kill and everything else merely disables.** *A soldier without a helmet is a soldier his officer has given up on.*

**Weapon** · Arming sword or spear, and a crossbow where the armory had one to spare. **Stillband iron: good Bearing, poor Holding.** *A line soldier's weapon holds no inscription because it does not need one and the inscription would cost more than the sword.*

**Pack** · Bedroll, spare shirt, mending kit, wooden bowl, spoon, belt flask, three days' rations, whetstone. *The Ember Flint if he is lucky enough to own one. And a prayer, if the soldier is the praying kind, and soldiers on campaign become the praying kind faster than any other demographic.*

### The sworn company and the guild-muster

*The levy above is the older soldier and still the commoner one. The sworn company, a band of professionals under a captain in a lord's or a city's pay by the season, is the soldier a Crown hires when it means to win, and the guild-muster is who it meets at the ford.*

**The guild-muster** · Every craft of a walled town turns out under its own banner and its own saint, weavers with the weavers and butchers with the butchers, **in bright tabards by ward and trade, with a spear, a kettle hat and a grudge against the next banner along.** Lords' horse comes against them in lace and plume, lancers and light horse, with the charge as the decision, and weavers with long spears have been surprising it for as long as there have been weavers. *Bright color was the point: a man had to find his own banner through the dust of a charge, and a man in a red tabard was a man who could not desert quietly.*

**The pavise company** · **The painted pavise (a tall shield with a prop) on a back-strap, the soft arming cap, a spade, a windlass arbalest and a quiver of steel-headed bolts.** *Every pavise is painted as loud as the town that pays for it, lions and saints and a cheerful hanged man, because for the whole of a long afternoon it is the only part of you the enemy will get to see.* A bolt beats a charge only if the charge can be slowed, so the sharpened stake and the horse-pit follow the pavise, and the spade becomes the second weapon of the company. Every veteran sews his first company's color inside his collar, and no sergeant has ever managed to stop it.

> **Practitioners change everything anyway.** A titled practitioner on a line is worth a siege train and is paid like one. An Onceglass volley answers a stake-line; a Petralon working answers a charge; a Judicium hand (Judicium being the Wellspring of the lightning's line) blinds a beacon tower. **Every army of the age is two armies**: the line that fights the other line, and the handful of titled people who decide where a line can stand at all.
>
> *Custom and the captain's lungs govern the first. Nothing governs the second except the Accord's schedules and the depth of the Crown's purse, and a deep enough purse can buy its own, a Stage at a time, out of a bottle.*

### Bolt and proof

*The reason the harness above is still worth a lord's rents, stated once and then assumed everywhere.*

**A bolt is cheap and a working is not.** A bolt costs about three coppers; a practitioner's expenditure is pulled against a reserve that took years to build. **The economics of an engagement between the two have never favored the practitioner**, which is why lines exist, why ranks exist, and why nobody sensible has ever stopped wearing armor.

**The Crossbow** · Slow to span, simple to teach, and it does not care what Stage the man in front of it reached. A hand-spanned crossbow is five to ten silver and throws an ordinary bolt of about 100 J; a steel-bowed, lever-spanned war crossbow is three to four gold; a heavy windlass arbalest, six to ten gold where one can be had at all, throws about 440 J. Street people call the whole family the poor man's Domain (a Domain being the world made to obey one fighter's art). *A fighter at Speed Grade D can read a crossbow bolt loosed across a room and slip it, if they see the loose. At Grade B they can stand in a volley of bolts and arrows without significant reactive disadvantage. A volley is a volley.* The full law is at [[Materials, Alchemy & Trade/Work, Trade and Measure/The Apparatus of the Age — Equipment and Material Culture/The Bolt and the Enhanced Weapon|The Bolt and the Enhanced Weapon]].

**The Belt-Bow** · The rarest bow on the rack: a hand crossbow with a short steel lath, spanned from a hook on the belt and fired one-handed, made by perhaps a dozen bowyers alive, each of whom will tell you the other eleven are frauds, at eight to fifteen gold where one will sell at all. Short range and a weak bolt. Vain captains, a few paid killers and anyone with money who expects the fight to happen inside a room carry one spanned under a cloak, and the steel tires of being kept that way. **What has killed the most practitioners above Stage V is its cheap cousin, the hand-spanned crossbow**, because it comes out at a distance where reaction is not the deciding variable.

> **Proofed harness and the mark.** Armor is tested by loosing at it and stamped with the result, and **the proof-mark is the whole trade**: the house that tested it, the bow, and the range. Harness proofed against a hand-spanned bow at twenty paces carries a different mark from harness proofed against a windlass arbalest at fifty.
>
> **The marks are forged constantly**, and every soldier who buys his own harness knows it. *A proof-mark is a promise about a specific bow at a specific range, made by a workshop that may no longer exist, on a piece of steel that has since been repaired twice.* **They buy anyway, because the alternative is buying nothing.**

*Aethersteel takes a proof-mark like any other harness, and the Impact Damping Lattice inscribed along the sigil lines is tested separately and marked separately, and the two marks are read together by anyone competent and read as one mark by everyone else.*

### The armored professional

**The field harness: twenty to twenty-five kilograms distributed over the whole body**, mail under a coat of plates or a breastplate, and a fit fighter can mount unaided, run, and roll. *The crane-and-immobility myth is from tournament armor rather than field equipment.*

> **The field harness is engineering.** Every surface angled to deflect, every joint articulated, **and the whole assembly fitted to the individual, because harness that does not fit is harness that kills its wearer by restricting movement at the moment movement matters.**

*Aethersteel harness carries Impact Damping Lattices inscribed along the sigil lines, turning it into a resonant structure that distributes incoming shock across the whole surface.*

**A sword does not go through a breastplate. Half-swording into gaps and grappling are the answer.**

**Mail voiders** · Armpits, inner elbows, groin, the back of the knee. ***These are where the killing happens***, and the voiders are the last defense between the gap and the flesh. **The most expensive per-unit-weight item in the harness.**

### Bow, pole, blade and stone

*The crossbow takes the headlines. These take most of the casualties.*

**The War Bow** · Yew or layered horn, and a draw weight that takes ten years to build into a man's back. An archer's skeleton can be told from anybody else's by a renderer at a glance. A crossbowman can be trained in a fortnight, and that fortnight is the crossbow's whole advantage, because a good archer looses several arrows for one bolt and has never once let anyone forget it.

**Polearms** · Spear, bill, glaive, halberd and the long-hafted axe. **The spear is the commonest weapon on any field, because it is a cheap head on a cheap stick and it keeps the other man away.** The rest are the spear's answers to armor: a hook to pull, a beak to punch, an edge to chop. A company's polearm is the first thing a stranger recognizes, and each region has its own and its own jokes about everyone else's.

**Swords** · Six to ten silver for a trooper's saber; four to seven gold for an officer's blade. A sword that carries a working is priced on the coil and not by the smith. **A sword is the sidearm that tells people who you are**, which is most of why anyone pays for one when a bill would do the job better.

**Thrown things** · Javelin, sling stone, throwing axe, weighted dart. All are read by Speed Grade like any projectile, and all cost next to nothing. A sling in a shepherd's hand is the most underrated weapon in the four quarters, and shepherds prefer it that way.

**The Great Engines** · Trebuchet, mangonel, ballista, springald (a small torsion bow on a frame), ram and siege tower; the full schedule sits with the Order of Battle. The **rampart arbalest** is the ballista's small and frightening cousin, a runed siege piece bolted to a city wall: a bow whose limbs a Guild master has cut with Ascensio glyphs one at a time, throwing a dart at 400 m/s and up, which no plain steel has ever managed, and it is the fastest thing most fighters will ever have to read. A rich city owns two or three. Engines have names, usually after a relative, and those names are the first thing a garrison learns about a siege. *The trebuchet Mother Gravel, at her first shot before the walls of Ostrey, threw her stone straight up. It came down on the master of engines. She took the town anyway, some weeks later, and is still on the Crown's books. When the skein slips on the Sixth Marchers' springald, she throws her dart anywhere in a half-circle, which is why Aunt Griselda is said to have killed more of the Sixth than of anybody else, and why they would not part with her for a duchy.*

### Names on the rack

**Gear has a reputation before it has an owner.** Plain weapons get nicknames in a barracks and lose them by the next war; a good piece keeps its name, adds past owners, and travels ahead of whoever carries it, under the two ratings set out at [[Materials, Alchemy & Trade/Work, Trade and Measure/The Apparatus of the Age — Equipment and Material Culture/The Item Tiers|Grade and Rarity]]. A cheap crossbow that once killed someone famous sells for more than a fine one that has killed nobody, and pawnshops near a bracket ground keep a whole wall of swords that belonged, on the owner's oath, to Sigvard Wenlock, the Hill That Walks, who owned one sword in his life and was buried with it.

**Hide, bone and scale** · **The best gear going comes off something that tried to eat the last owner.** A crew brings up a beast's plates with its core, a tanner or an armorer with the title for it cuts them into a brigandine or a shield-face, and the piece fights on the same Grade law as steel with its old owner's temper faintly attached. Plain folk see it as the mark of a crew that has been somewhere: a deep crew's captain in a coat of Tolling Crab, the color of old amber, gets served first in any tavern on the Kessen road. Very few of them can afford to wear what they kill, because the same plates sell for a year's rent.

### The crew's kit

*The soldier above is paid by the season. The crew below is paid by what it brings up, and packs accordingly.*

**On the rope** · Knotted line, hammers, wedges, a bait lantern, smoked glass, a sack of salt, a one-hour glass for the caller, the assay fork for the band, and ward-chalk for the night. **Bolts by the pouch, because a crossbow does not care what Stage the thing in the gallery reached either.** *The rest of a crew's gear is whatever came off whichever crew went down last. Every delver's coat has somebody else's darning on it.*

**The Core Box** · A hand-sized Chainsalt-lined box with a lead hasp, on the same principle as the Sealed Coffer, so that a monster's core carried up a stair reads like a lump of salt to anybody with Gnosis and a grudge. **A crew that cannot afford one walks home through every bandit and hedge-renderer in the district, shining.** *It also keeps the core's owner from opening the lid at the camp, looking at it, and having a very bad idea about swallowing it.*

### The practitioner-combatant

| Kit | What it is and why |
|---|---|
| **The Kineticist** | Brigandine or reinforced gambeson, **leaving hands and arms mobile.** Gauntlets are a compromise; **many forgo them entirely and accept the trade: faster casting, vulnerable hands.** *A Kineticist's dominant hand is scarred, calloused, and visibly different from the off-hand, because the hand that channels daily develops the wear patterns of a tool that is used too hard and too often* |
| **The Corporis fighter** | **Minimal armor. The body is the conduit, and metal between the skin and the world is metal between the conduit and the target.** Wraps at hands and wrists, reinforced clothing, boots that grip. *The Moto tradition adds Fractura-attuned handwraps inscribed on the binding cloth, amplifying impact transfer through the first three knuckle joints, the Corporis practitioner's equivalent of a mage's staff* |
| **The Thaumaturge** | A ritual blade **(a precision cutting instrument for glyph-work in hostile conditions, useless as a weapon)**, consecrated chalk, a substrate pouch, and a staff functioning as a resonance anchor. ***The staff is a tuning fork the size of a walking stick, and the practitioner who plants it and works from it is the practitioner whose circle holds*** |
| **The Alchemist** | A bandolier of finished Drafts: incendiaries, corrosives, healing compounds, smoke agents, **and the class of offensive Draft the Accord classifies as munitions and the Alchemist classifies as applied chemistry.** *The leather is treated to resist the contents of its own flasks, and it fails at this roughly once per campaign, which is why Alchemists' coats carry the specific pattern of chemical staining that identifies the discipline to anyone who has seen it before* |
| **The Mancer** | **Almost nothing visible.** A Clearbeam loupe, a notebook, a small field coil, reference standards, a stylus. *A Mancer in the field looks like a clerk who wandered into the wrong camp, which is exactly how a competent Mancer prefers to look, because a Mancer's value is intelligence, and a Mancer who looks valuable is a Mancer who gets targeted* |

## The Guild's Tools

> **The Holding Coil** · **The most important instrument in the economy of the age.** A wound conductor of copper and brass in a glass case, answering measurably to the Essence character of whatever is set inside it, producing a reading that is **simultaneously a quality assessment, a provenance check, and a price.**
>
> ***The Accord's legitimacy rests on the fact that two coils in two kingdoms produce the same number for the same object.***
>
> *A Measurewright calibrates the reference coils annually, and the calibration is the closest thing to a religious ceremony the guild system has.*

> **The Appraiser's Bag** · A loupe, a tuning fork, a small field coil and a very low opinion of everyone. The coil gives Grade, the fork gives density, and the Appraisal skill on the bearer's own sheet does the rest, or does not: **a good forger beats a weak appraiser**, and the whole of [[Materials, Alchemy & Trade/Work, Trade and Measure/The Apparatus of the Age — Equipment and Material Culture/The Item Tiers|Grade and Rarity]] is about what follows from that sentence.

**The Title Token** · *The Accord's most brilliant invention: a credential that is simultaneously a badge, a passport, a line of credit, and money, and the reason a stranger from another kingdom will hand you the goods.* It says Grandmaster on a merchant who may not be able to throw a punch, and it says the same word on a fighter who can level a street, and nobody in either trade is confused about which they are dealing with. **A forged one is a hanging in every realm.**

> Somebody stripped of a title **watches the inscription go inert in front of witnesses**, and the object that was a credential becomes a lump of metal, *and the ceremony is conducted slowly, because every guild in the coalition understands that humiliation has a pedagogical function.*

**The Seal-Token** · Nalūn's contribution. **A merchant without one is a merchant without a history, and a merchant without a history is a merchant without credit.**

**The Engraver's Loupe** · Carried in a padded case, never lent, **and insured at a figure that exceeds the Engraver's own bond value**, *which tells you everything about the relative importance of the person and the tool.*

**The Ward-Stone** · The fundamental unit of a settlement's protection. *A settlement's perimeter is its ward-stones connected by the field they collectively generate, and the gap between two stones is the vulnerability every attacking force looks for first.*

**The Field Crucible** · Ceramic lined with **Blackcap alloy**, which keeps its temper inside a working field where ordinary steel goes soft.

> *A crucible whose reagent rack is organized and whose seals are fresh belongs to a professional. A crucible whose rack is chaos and whose seals are cracked belongs to someone who is either a genius or dead within the year, and the two categories overlap more often than the guild admits.*

## The Road's Requirements

> **The Weight Ablation Harness** · Ascensio glyphwork halving the effective load. **It lifts nothing and lies to the rope about the weight**, *and when the lie fails it fails all at once, which is why every winding house has a bell and every carrier checks the inscription every morning.*
>
> Each one is cut by hand and costs roughly **four months' wages** for an independent carrier. *Every carrier who can afford one owns one, and the divide between carriers who have it and carriers who do not is the single most visible divide on any trade road in the four quarters.*

**The Sealed Coffer** · Chainsalt-lined, **so a coffer full of Aethersteel blanks looks, to anyone reading the ambient field, like a box full of salt.** Great houses move their Draft stock this way, and the wagons carrying the top shelf travel with more guards than the crown does. *The lock answers to the owner's Essence Signature and nothing else. It can only be forced by destroying the liner, which contaminates the contents, which defeats the purpose.*

**The Factor's Kit** · Seal-token, a ledger bound in **Sunkwood boards (Essence-quiet, so entries are not contaminated by proximity to Holding-grade cargo)**, portable coil, loupe, sealed reference weights, and enough silver for three days.

> *The commercial equivalent of the soldier's pack: everything you need for seventy-two hours without support, and nothing you cannot afford to lose to a bandit and replace at the next town.*

**Way-Station Stock** · Water and fodder for two days, salt, fuel, a medical chest, **two spare Weight Ablation harnesses rented by the day to carriers whose own have failed**, and Ember Flints at a markup the merchants complain about and pay without exception.

> *The keeper also stocks information, which is not listed in the inventory and is worth more than everything that is.*

## The Wheel and the Road

*The Road's Requirements above are what a carrier needs. This section is what the road and the river are, and what turns the mills beside them, and the Guild craftsmen will tell you all about it whether you asked or not.*

### The wheel

**The Mill** · **Water and wind do the heavy work of the age, and they have been doing it for a thousand years.** An undershot wheel on a slow river, an overshot wheel on a hill stream, a tide mill on a salt creek, a post mill on a bare ridge that the miller turns into the wind by putting his shoulder to it. They grind grain, full cloth, saw planks and lift the trip-hammer at a river forge. A mill belongs to whoever holds the water, **and a lord's tenants must grind at the lord's mill and leave him a share of the flour.** *Abbot Odo of Sallowford once seized every hand-quern in his valley and paved his parlor with them, and the valley's women came up in their feast clothes every year for three generations to spit on that floor.*

**The Essence Wheel** · **No trade in these exists.** There is one wheel at one mill, then another a long way off, each the life's work of a single master who will not explain it, and the Division can count nine in the four quarters. An Aethersteel rotor cut with an Ascensio glyph and fed by hand. The glyph turns Essence into a push and the push turns the rotor. Nothing burns, nothing boils, nothing smokes. It hums at the pitch of whoever keeps it, and its keeper, called a wheel-man wherever there is one to call, is a practitioner on wages, feeding it out of his own reserve as he pulls from the ground underfoot, or else buying hours at a Core (a worked Wellspring site, where one kind of Aether pools).

> **The best known is Old Contrary, at Sallowford.** Master Bevis Oakapple built it into a fulling mill whose river had wandered off in a bad decade, and on a good day it pounds cloth as though the river had never left, for the abbey's profit and to the valley's disgust. On a bad day it runs backward. In the last year of Abbot Odo's life it ran backward at full load, threw its upper stone across the mill-race and through the abbey wall, and came to rest on the parlor floor, among the querns. The valley held a dance. *The forge-array took a thousand years to change the four quarters. No wheel has changed anything except the shape of that parlor, and every master who has seen one goes home sure he can do better.*

**What it costs** · A wheel-man's wages, or Core hours at trade rates or bonded. **A wheel the size of a traveling trunk pulls about four thousandths of an AU/s at full load; the one great wheel at Harle Weir pulls a whole AU/s**, and the keeper's body knows the difference to the last thread. Harle Weir has gone through two keepers.

**What it leaves** · **Residue, and no smoke at all.** A wheel run hard and long leaves its field in the ground it stands on, and the men who stand beside it carry the rest. *This is the unsealed field of the Master Material Ledger's hazard register, and the reason a renderer can pick a long-service wheel-man off the table by his Shell alone.*

**Its limit and its tell** · **A wheel is exactly as strong as what feeds it.** Thin ground runs it slow, and a Silence Realm (ground where no lawful working can be authorized) stops it for good. *It falls quiet the moment its keeper walks out or the Core closes, and the whole valley hears the difference.*

> **The shout and the mark.** Every contraption has a maker, every maker has a guild mark, and every mark has a man standing in front of it in a loud coat shouting its full name to a crowd. Contraptions misfire, and the trade counts that as advertising. *Master Haddock's Self-Spanning Bow, Never Again A Tired Arm, spanned itself in its case on the road to its first demonstration and put a bolt through the purchasing officer's mule. Repaired at Haddock's expense, it put the second through the purchasing officer. The Crown ordered forty, for use against people standing in front of it, and in eleven years Haddock has delivered three.*

### The road

**The Carrier's Wagon** · **The goods of the age move behind oxen.** A four-wheeled wagon and six or eight oxen under the yoke make ten or twelve miles on a good day and stop to graze; a mule train makes more, carries less and goes over the passes. The carrier leaves when he has a load and the weather holds, you find him by asking at the inn, and nothing on his wagon arrives on a day anyone could promise. For a place on the load you haggle.

**Getting Across a City** · **A city is as wide as a walk, and its walls say so.** The rich go by litter, mule or their own barge, a ferryman at every river stair will take you over for a copper and a complaint, and everyone else goes on foot, through the mud, past the pigs, and shouting.

**The Kept Road** · **Old work, and nobody living could lay a mile of it.** Tuned waystones lie sunk in the bed of a few great roads at every span, set there in the Great Spirit War or the days of the Physical Epochs depending on which Measurewright you buy a drink, and road-wardens fill them now as their mothers did, each with her stretch, her lodge and her toll-bar. A heavy wain rides over them with a twin stone slung beneath its bed, a hand's breadth above the road and cut to the same pitch, easing an Ascensio-cut back axle so that two horses draw what eight would on plain ground. **The twin never touches the waystone. It couples across the gap by Transference (the Wellspring that passes energy between tuned things without contact), and the coupling falls away so sharply with distance that a twin lifted one span has lost almost all of it.**

> **Nothing travels farther than that hand's breadth.** The road is kept like any other work, cut, sealed, paid and guarded, and the Transmission ceiling stands. *Every stretch has a toll-bar across it, and the toll is whatever that warden has decided about your load, your face and the weather. A wain goes when its team is ready and gets there when it gets there.*
>
> **The limits every carter knows.** A wain off the stones is a plain wain and a tired team. A flooded cutting is a dead stretch. A stretch can be shut from the road-warden's lodge the way a lamp is capped at its stem, and a wain running onto it slows to a stand. **And the coupling runs both ways**: a wain carrying a filled stone over a dead stretch empties it into the road instead of pulling from it, which is why no carter crosses a dead stretch with his twin slung.
>
> *The waystones sing before a wain arrives. It is the first tell every child on a roadside street learns, and the last thing a man asleep on the road hears.*

**The Carry-Stone** · Leaves of Aethersteel separated by Chainsalt in a case the size of a wine flask and about the weight of a full bucket, hand-filled at a Core by its keepers, who charge by the hour of filling. **A carry-stone holds about two and a half EU in every liter**, more than four times an Apprentice's whole reserve, and it is carried the way a physician carries poppy. It takes one Core's Aether to a rite, a dying ward or a deathbed a month's ride from any Core. At thirty to sixty gold before the filling, few people have ever seen one, and nobody has ever been fool enough to run a wagon on it.

> **A carry-stone leaks.** Essence does not keep, and a stone standing idle loses about a twentieth of its filling a day. *A stone filled five days before it is needed is a quarter spent, and nobody refunds the quarter.*

### River and sea

*Where there is water, nobody who can help it uses a road.*

**The River Barge** · Flat, tarred and painted at the prow, floated down on the current and hauled back up by men or horses on the towpath. **A barge carries what a dozen ox-wagons carry, and pays no road-warden.** The bargemen's guild is older than most of the towns on the river. *A river with a mill on it has a war on it, because the miller wants the water held and the bargeman wants it let go, and both of them own axes.*

**The Great Yard at Saltash** · The one entry in this register where the Division will use the word enormous, and the shipwrights' own schedules sit with the Admiralty. A walled basin owned by the city, with its own oak forest inland and a rope walk a quarter of a mile long, where two thousand sworn shipwrights, caulkers and oar-makers build galleys by hand, plank on frame, under their own saint and on free wine. **It launches a galley in a season and a bad temper.** Saltash will kill you for drawing the place, and every other port on the coast has tried to hire away its master caulkers, with roughly that result.

## The Beacon, the Scrivener and the Limner

*The age talks to itself by fire, by horse, by mouth and by hand, and the Guild owns the fire.*

**The Beacon Line** · Hilltop fire-towers in sight of one another, lit by wardens in shuttered colors beside the few great roads the Guild runs, and a beacon-house in every town the line touches. **Guild line, Guild price, read by a clerk and carried from the house to the door by a beacon-boy**, at a silver for twenty words with the addresses counted. Fog shuts it, a snowstorm shuts it and a tower-warden's toothache shuts it, and when it opens Guild business goes first, then Crown business, then whoever paid. Nine words is the common length, because words are charged, and a message arriving at a house is read on the doorstep in the boy's face before it is read on the paper. *The beacon does not reach where the Guild does not. A district is deaf the moment its tower-wardens close the shutters.*

**Relay Riders** · A rested horse every twenty to forty miles along the roads that can keep one, a yellow sash, a bell at the belt and a sealed bag. **Nothing with four legs carries a letter faster**, and the riders are insufferable about it in every inn on the road. A great house in a real hurry can hire one to carry one bag and no other, and pays about what the horses do.

**Heralds and Criers** · Everyone else's news. Heralds carry a lord's word in his colors, criers sell it by the corner, relay riders carry what must arrive whole, and minstrels carry what must arrive memorable and get the details wrong. **Fame moves at the speed of the song**, and a fighter's price, welcome and list of enemies move with it.

**The Scrivener and the Stationer** · A scrivener keeps a bench by the church door and will write your letter, your will or your lie for a couple of coppers. A crier's bill is cheap for one reason, which is that it is a dozen words long: a scrivener's boy can make forty in an hour, **and the bill costs one copper, the Guild's day-rates copied onto a slip cost three, and a book costs what a book costs.** A book is a flock of sheep's skins and a year of one copyist's eyes, and a stationer in a university town will rent you an exemplar a quire at a time so that you can ruin your own. The folding almanac on a physician's belt, the romance in a good hand and the psalter with gold in it all sit with the books, priced like a horse. *A scandal reaches the provinces at the speed of whoever cannot keep his mouth shut, and that is still faster than the man it concerns can finish his breakfast and ride.*

> **The door and the shopfront.** Every church door and market cross in a town wears bills: a bounty, a lost horse, a widow's goods for sale, nailed one over another by evening. Every shop on a good street hangs a painted sign, because most of the street cannot read, and a boot, a bush for wine or a gilded pestle says it all, and the best of them hire a warden's lamp after dark. *The bill-sticker works faster than the magistrate who fines him.*

**The Limner's Miniature** · The limner (a painter of small likenesses), his squirrel-hair brush and his shell-gold, an oval of vellum or ivory the size of a plum, three weeks' sittings, for a betrothal or a dead son, at two to eight gold. **Nobody's likeness is cheap.** A watch hunts a man by a written list of his scars, his squint and his accent, a bounty bill says the same in twelve words, and half the men hanged on one looked nothing like him. The street's version is the badge, a lead-cast Hill That Walks on a pin for a copper, looking like a thumb. *Limners cannot get an aura right, in ways the guild argues about and cannot fix, and half the sect heroes on the market are wearing the wrong color.*

**Lantern Shows and Singing Shells** · **Fairground wonders, which arrive with the traveling show and leave with it.** A lantern show in a hired barn, where a Luminalis-worker throws painted glass onto a sheet and walks it across, and the first show most towns ever saw was a dragon coming over their own gate, after which the barn needed a new back wall. A Harmonia-cut shell holds one voice that outlives the singer, and there may be forty in the world. *The showman will charge you a copper to hold it to your ear, and he will tell you it is a dead queen.*

## The Stuff of the Age

*The materials the age is built from are mostly the old ones, dug, hewn and hauled by hand, and a few that no earlier age had the nerve to touch.*

**Steel and iron** · **Iron is everywhere and steel is dear.** A bloomery and a charcoal burner's whole wood go into a few pounds of it, the water-driven trip-hammer at a river forge spares the smith's arm and little else, and plowshare tip, axe edge, bow-steel, chain, nail and needle each come off somebody's anvil. Stillband iron cramps the stones of every bridge in the western quarter; Forge-Iron arms the line. *Whole villages make nails at the hearth, and a family moving house has been known to burn the old one for them. A steel bow is three gold because a man spent a month on it, and it has still shortened the careers of a great many important people.*

**Wool and cloth** · The richest trade there is and the angriest. A clothier buys the wool, puts it out to spinners in a hundred cottages and weavers in a hundred rooms, **pays by the piece, and owns the cloth at every step** without ever touching it. Fullers, dyers and carders stand at the bottom, and the carders belong to no guild at all. *Every cloth town has a date its weavers took the hall, and most have several. The unguilded carders of Cardmere held the whole city government for three and a half years, and the masters still lower their voices about it.*

**Gum and gutta-percha** · Tapped from trees in the groves of the far south and east and carried west by the chest, as a curiosity. **Rubber waterproofs a cloak for someone who can pay, and makes a ball that rich children lose down wells; gutta-percha sets hard around a knife hilt or a seal-case.** *The sailors who bring it home will tell you what it costs at the far end, and neither end of the voyage has any idea what the other is doing with it.*

**Horn and resin** · **Horn is the Horners' Company's business**: boiled, split and pressed flat for combs, spoons, gaming pieces, dice and the see-through panes of a lantern. An alchemist's bench-cured resin does the same for buttons and seal-cases, bright, never cheap, and inclined to burn. *The alchemical houses price it accordingly, and the Horners have been suing them for it since before anyone can remember.*

**The Ledger's materials** · The age leans on these as much as on steel, by their own names. **Chainsalt** for the isolation collars of every array and the separator leaves of a carry-stone. **Kirkyard Lead** for abbey conduits, cathedral roofs and the shielding of every assay room. **Greaves Tin** for the array contact, the bronze bell and the pewter on the table alike. **Cloudmetal** for the siege-tower frame and the great bow's stock, from works that exist in four places. **Stormfulgur** for the long runs of a great array, gathered as glass from the nitre pans after a storm. **Clearbeam Beryl** for the lantern's lens. **Aethersteel** for the great blade and the rare wheel rotor. *The Master Material Ledger lists each by tier and hazard; this register lists them by where a clerk will meet them.*

## The Works Beneath

*The great works nobody sees until they stop. Each one has a keeper, and a keeper can strike.*

**The Pumping Array** · The work that broke the Water Ceiling, in the few great shafts rich enough to keep one. Everywhere else it is the drainage adit, the horse gin and a rag-and-chain pump with a bad temper. **A deep shaft is as deep as its array can pump, and no deeper.** *A drowned working is a working whose array stopped, and the Old World is a graveyard of shafts that were profitable on the day the water came back.*

**The Ventilation Array** · Turns a deep working into a lung. *Where it falters, the old method returns within the hour: a man with a wet hood and a candle on a pole, sent in to test whether the air will sustain life.*

**The Forge Array** · Holds temperatures charcoal cannot reach, steady for days. **Aethersteel exists because of this**, *and the four locations where the arrays run at full capacity are the four locations where the four quarters' high metallurgy actually happens.*

> **The Hexagonal Grid** · The Measurewrights' masterwork. Sigil-frameworks cut by hand in the bedrock beneath a few great cities, over generations and at the price of a cathedral, distributing Essence stress.
>
> **Invisible, silent, and the single most important work in any city large enough to have one**, because a city of practitioners without a grid is a city **whose ambient field is being distorted by every working performed inside it.**
>
> *The distortions compound. The compound distortions produce what the Accord classifies as a Realm-quake and what the people living above it classify as the ground opening.*

**The Concord Gate** · The Accord's road through nowhere. *Access is restricted, monitored and priced, because a Gate is a hole in distance, and a hole in distance is a strategic asset the Accord does not share with anyone who has not signed the Codex.*

**The Ward-Perimeter** · Maintained by the local ward-carver where one exists **and by nobody where one does not**, *which is a significant number of frontier settlements, and the reason frontier ward failure is the ordinary northern death.*

## The Disposable Workings

> **The four quarters' most democratic working is the one that breaks after a single use.**

**Onceglass Charges** · Obsidian quenched inside a live Wellspring field, inscribed once, **and sold to anyone who can pay the asking. One casting, and then it is gravel.**

> *Cannot be repaired, recharged, or traced back to its Engraver, which is why Enforcement hates them and why the eastern arc's entire militia doctrine is built around them.* **An eastern militia carries Onceglass the way a western militia carries arrows: expendable, quenched by the basket in hill villages on piece rate, and lethal in volume.**
>
> *A Pyrokinetic burst costs roughly what a good knife costs. A Gravikinetic pulse costs roughly what a horse costs. The price tracks the complexity of the inscription rather than the material, because the material is cheap and the Engraver's time is not.*

**Ember Flint** · **The cheapest Essence-active object in the four quarters by unit cost.** *Carried by anyone who has been cold and wet and alone and has discovered that the ability to start a fire without dry tinder is worth the copper piece the flint cost.*

**Healing Salve** · A Verdantia Draft in rendered fat, beeswax and herbs. Accelerates clotting and granulation **for four to six hours, after which the Essence is exhausted and the salve reverts to an ordinary unguent.** *Sold occasionally by herbalists who have learned enough to know that they are selling above their title and who price accordingly.*

**Ward-Chalk** · Chainsalt ground and bound, holding four to eight hours before the powder has absorbed enough ambient Essence to lose its dielectric properties. **The field practitioner's tar and twine: temporary, imperfect, and the difference between sleeping safely and not sleeping at all.**

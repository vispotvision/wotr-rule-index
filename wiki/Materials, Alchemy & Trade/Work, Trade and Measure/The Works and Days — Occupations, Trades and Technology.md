# The Works and Days — Occupations, Trades and Technology

*A Register of Occupations, Trades, and Technology Across the Four Quarters*

*Compiled for the Research and Archives Division, with the assistance of the Logistics and Supply Division*

> *"He asked what I did. I told him I kept the array running. He asked what magic I used. I told him I used a mallet. He did not know what to do with me after that, and I have found that this is the general experience."*
>
> *Brida Voss, Senior Tender, Greyshaft Twelve, testimony to the Logistics Division*

Ranulf Scarrow, called the Anvil-Eater, grandmaster of the Scarlet Rams of Harrowgate, bit a corner off an anvil every midsummer for thirty years in front of anyone who would pay to watch, and at the feast for his seventieth year he got up on the high table in his scarlet and gold, with three thousand people in the yard and the school's drummers going, and told them that a fist of Master standing owed nothing to any pair of hands in the four quarters, and that the bakers, ditch-diggers and washerwomen of Harrowgate were welcome to kiss the part of him that sat down. The yard loved it. The bakers kept on baking. The layer-outs of Harrowgate, thirty-one old women who wash the town's dead for a few silver and are not asked to dinner, said nothing whatever, and one of them wrote it down.

Nine years later a herring bone killed him at supper. Harrowgate gave him the funeral of the age: Ram banners the length of the Long Market, twelve-foot candles, core disciples in red weeping aloud on a catafalque built over the school's fountain, and pie-sellers working the crowd from both ends. No layer-out came. A woman of Master standing does not stop when she stops, and neither does a man; the Crystal goes on producing Essence for a good while after the heart has quit, and the woman who knows how long, and what to do to the body until then, is the woman you sent for. On the first day the aura stood up off the bier in a scarlet column as tall as a house and the crowd took it for glory and sang. On the second day every candle in the hall burned scarlet, the funeral cakes turned to charcoal on the trestles, and the eleven core disciples who rolled up their sleeves to wash their grandmaster themselves were carried out with their forearms cooked. On the third, in the heat of a Harrowgate midsummer, the red ran down the gutters and into the dyers' vats, the school's own rams broke their pen and left town, and the gravediggers, asked to open the ground, explained with some pleasure what a Master laid in at the wrong depth does to a churchyard for the next four hundred years. A hundred disciples dug it themselves and dug it wrong, and had to stop when the spades began to warm.

At dusk Elder Hawise Bretton, who was said to be able to put her open hand through a town gate, walked down to Tanners' Row, downstream and downwind and outside the wall, and went onto both knees in the fish-guts in front of Gammer Thirza Lubbock, who was shelling peas on her step. It is recorded that the Gammer finished the bowl. It is recorded that the fee was paid three times over and that the Elder was made to say a sentence out loud, in front of the street, about whose hands a Ram needed. The thirty-one came up the hill at nightfall with salt, vinegar, and a long wooden spoon nobody has ever explained, and by morning the gutters ran clear. The rival Blue Herons of Lower Harrowgate have sung "Who'll Wash the Ram?" at every bracket since, children sing it in every lane from here to the coast, and a Ram disciple who hears it in a tavern is expected to fight or buy, and generally does both.

This register exists because of that bowl of peas, in the sense that it concerns the people who were in the room when the grand thing stopped working. It lists what they do and what they cost, and it begins, as the Gammer would have wished, from the bottom.

## The Governing Question

**The coil classifies people by what their Soul Crystal can do. The economy classifies them by what their hands can do.**

> ***These two systems overlap in approximately one person in twelve. This register concerns the other eleven.***

(The Soul Crystal is the soul itself, which holds power and sends it out. The Accord reads what it puts out with the brass coil, the instrument that gives every practitioner a [[The Magic System/The Soul and the Climb/The Sixteen Stages|Stage]], one step on a ladder of sixteen, and the word "instrument" in this register always means that coil and the people who stand behind it. A [[The Magic System/The Foundations/The Sixty Wellsprings/The Sixty Wellsprings|Wellspring]], which this register mentions constantly, is one of the sixty governing ideas of magic, an alchemical and spiritual transformation that changes how a soul uses Aether, the world's medium of power. A practitioner pulls Aether from the ground around them and refines it in the Crystal into Essence, the power they hold and spend, and a place where one Wellspring's kind of Aether pools is a Core.)

Every occupation below exists because something needs doing **that has not yet been done by an array (a standing working cut into stone and metal), a glyph, or a practitioner.** The day any of those three replace it, the occupation vanishes, and the person who held it discovers what *Value, Coin and Trade* describes with perfect accuracy and no sympathy at all: **that a meritocracy is an excellent arrangement for people with merit that the instrument can see.**

## The Three Floors

*Almost every argument about dignity, wages, and what a person is worth can be resolved by asking which floor they stand on.*

| Floor | Standing |
|---|---|
| **First** *people the instrument sees* | Titled practitioners carrying silver tokens that are **simultaneously badges, passports, lines of credit, and bullion.** In the Arbitration Division's language, **attributable capability**, sitting second on the creditor schedule behind only contracted Essence (bonded work and Core hours already sold). *Roughly one in twelve in the west, fewer in the east, and falling in the Withering era because Welling-stage children are fewer and the instrument's floor has not moved* |
| **Second** *people the instrument needs* | **The people who work with Essence without touching it.** Someone builds the array, maintains it, supplies its substrate, calibrates its inscriptions, mines the ore, smelts the metal, weaves the cloth the Engraver wears, bakes the bread the Engraver eats, **and buries the Engraver when the occupation kills him.** *A practitioner can do extraordinary things. A practitioner cannot do extraordinary things without a conduit somebody forged, a substrate somebody mined, a lens somebody ground, and a meal somebody cooked.* Plenty of them carry a token too, a Master cooper or a Grandmaster merchant, on the strength of their work and their guild's word, and the coil has no opinion of either. The floor the economy pretends does not exist, and the one that stops existing for a single day and the economy notices by the second hour |
| **Third** *people the instrument does not see* | **Everyone else.** Farmers, herders, fishermen, porters, washerwomen, ditch-diggers, rag-pickers, nightsoil collectors, **and the vast category of people who do whatever needs doing that day and are paid in whatever is available.** *Invisible to the Accord because they hold no title, invisible to the factor houses because they hold no contract, and invisible to the archives because nobody is paying a scribe to write down what a field hand did this morning* |

> *This register begins with the Third Floor, because they are the majority, and any account of the world's work that begins with the practitioners is describing the exception and calling it the rule.*

A careful reader will have seen one in ten elsewhere in these shelves, that being the share of people who wake a Crystal at all, and will want the difference accounted for. The market stall has it. Somebody who wakes a Crystal, learns one trick and never pays for a silver token is on nobody's roll, and the section on the Small Powers below is about her. The other way onto the First Floor has a price: a Waking Draft, very dear, which often kills or warps a child. Most of the rich buy one for their children, and commoners sell themselves into guild debt for the chance.

### What the instrument is actually missing

*The floors are usually explained as a difference in capability. They are a difference in what one machine can read.*

A coil reads the output of a woken Crystal. **A Trait (a standing law carried in the soul) is not output.** It runs in people whose Crystal is Dormant and always will be, and awakening arms what was already there rather than granting anything new. **So the eleven in twelve have laws of their own, and nothing in the Accord is built to detect them.**

And they have sheets. The Soul Crystal is the soul and the Continuum measures it, so a window, the soul reporting its numbers to its owner in their own tongue, comes to everyone, the unwoken included, and nobody else can read it. Skills rise with use and show as Grades. A baker has a Baking skill and a little sheet. Gammer Thirza Lubbock, asked by this compiler what hers said and having decided she disliked him, reported the following and shut the door:

```
[Laying-Out: A]
[Shelling: C]
[Patience with Rams: F]
```

What a Third Floor soul undergoes instead of Temperance (the remaking a practitioner's Crystal goes through to climb) is **the Weathering**: refinement under sustained strain with no melt and no recasting, the soul hardening in place along the direction it was already being used. The trade word is **grain**, borrowed from the joiners, and a man is said to have taken his set clean or to have set crooked.

> **Every occupation in this register is a grain-forming occupation, and the register has been describing the process throughout without a word for it.**
>
> *The mason who reads stone by hammer-tap after a lifetime touching one material. The fisher family's taxonomy of catch-by-Wellspring-exposure that outperforms the Bestiary. The midwife who knows the local births the way a farmer knows the local weather. The tender who can hear that a Phreatis pump sounds different on Tuesday than it did on Monday.*
>
> **The window calls some of that a skill and gives it a Grade, and it is one. Under the skill lies something the window has no line for: a law, hardened along the axis the work pushed it**, real, measurable if anyone measured it, and **useless in any other direction for the rest of that person's life.** The tender who walks into a city carries a genuine and specific capability that the urban economy cannot recognize. His own soul reports it to him every morning, and the reason nobody else knows is that the instrument was never pointed at him.
>
> *A meritocracy is an excellent arrangement for people with merit that the instrument can see. People tend to read that sentence as bitter, and it also happens to be a technical description.*

## The Ground Trades

**Agriculture employs the majority of the four quarters, feeds the entirety of it, and appears in the Accord's classification apparatus exactly nowhere.**

> *The omission is deliberate. A field does not have a Soul Crystal, and the Accord's instruments do not assess dirt.*

### Grain and the table

The western staple is **spelt wheat**, ground on stone querns at home or at the lord's mill, for his share of the flour. **The nutritional distinction is texture. The social distinction is vast:** *mill-ground flour, sifted through a bolting cloth, is white, fine and urban; hand-ground flour is brown, gritty, and what the person eating it could afford.*

> A western peasant eats dark bread, pulse porridge, whatever grows in the kitchen plot, whatever was salted or smoked in autumn, and the occasional egg. **Meat is a feast-day occurrence for most households and an ordinary meal for none.**

*The Marrowkin grow nothing underground and import everything at prices that make every other kingdom wince*, which is why their cuisine is built around what can be preserved, concentrated and stored in bulk, **and why the sour barley beer of the lower Ring levels is described by every non-Marrowkin visitor as an acquired taste and by every Marrowkin as beer.**

| Trade | The work | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **The Farmer** | Plow, sow, weed, harvest, thresh, winnow, **and pray that the weather, the insects and the local Wellspring density cooperate in that order.** *On a prosperous farm the plow may be weight-ablated (glyph-cut so the load bears lighter), which does not pull it but halves the labor of the team. The farmer who owns this has never called it magic. To him it is the harness that cost two seasons' surplus, and he sleeps with it inside the house* | The specific musculoskeletal damage of forty years behind a plow, **and the levy, which takes the young men at planting and returns them at harvest if it returns them at all.** *Withering-era agriculture in formerly high-density districts is a slow catastrophe: the soil remembers the yield it used to produce, and the farmer is working ground that has been taught to expect more than the local Wellspring can now deliver* |
| **The Herder** | *A herder in a low-density valley manages animals. A herder on the Thornback March manages animals that can occasionally see through walls and object to being moved.* A beast grazed on Wellspring-consistent pasture produces leather that takes an inscription. **One grazed on variable pasture takes it badly,** and the premium has doubled in a generation | Marginal standing. *Settled communities treat herders the way settled communities have always treated people who move: as necessary, unreliable, and probably stealing something* |
| **The Fisher** | Net, trap and line. In the Lake Districts of the Crow's Reach, **Phreatis-aligned waters produce fish that are larger, more regular, and occasionally capable of behaviors freshwater fish should not exhibit.** *The fisher families' practical taxonomy of fish-by-Wellspring-exposure bears no resemblance to the Bestiary's classification and is considerably more useful for predicting where the catch will be* | **Coastal fishing is the most dangerous common occupation in the four quarters.** *The mortality rate exceeds surface mining by a factor the Accord tracks and the fishermen do not want published, because published numbers attract regulation, and regulation attracts people who have never been on a boat* |
| **The Forester** | Fell, coppice, burn charcoal for every smith and bloomery in the four quarters, since the forge arrays stand in four places and everyone else has a fire. **It takes an appalling quantity of wood to make a little iron**, and the charcoal-burner is *the man standing between the forest and the forge, reducing one to feed the other* | The charcoal-burner's cough. *And in high-density forest: a Verdantia-touched wood grows back faster than it should, which sounds like a gift until the forest decides the clearing you made last season was a wound and closes it* |

## The Material Trades

### Mining

> **Before the arrays, no shaft went deeper than sixty fathoms below the adit line. After the arrays, in the few great shafts rich enough to keep one, a shaft is as deep as the Essence somebody will pay to pour into its pumps.** Everywhere else it is still the adit, the horse gin and the rag-and-chain.
>
> ***The technology changed. The danger did not.***

**The Miner** · The cutting has not changed in five centuries: **a man with a pick, a man with a shovel, a candle, and a prayer.** *The array pumps the water and moves the air. The miner cuts the face.* **Between the array and the face is a distance measured in fathoms, and every fathom of it belongs to the miner and the rock and nothing else.**

> Weight Ablation glyphwork on the cage halves the load on the winding rope. **When the glyph fails, it fails all at once, and every winding house has a bell for that.**

*Paid piece-rate by volume of ore broken, not by the hour. A miner who is sick, slow or injured earns less, and the shortfall appears as his problem on his ledger, not as the company's problem on theirs.*

> **The Array-Tender.** *A tender does not cast spells.* They replace worn packing, clean conduit junctions, test inscription integrity with a coil-calibrated assay fork, and report degradation before it becomes failure.
>
> **The distinction between a tender and a practitioner is the distinction between the master who drew the conduit and the person who goes down to it in the dead of night with a lamp and a bad knee.**
>
> Most are Stage I–III: **enough sensitivity to feel when the array is running wrong, not enough to do anything about it except report it.**
>
> **The Tender's Shake.** Prolonged proximity produces tremor, sleep disruption, **and a narrowing of affect that continues past the point where the professional can choose to stop.**
>
> *The Accord has known for a century. There is no pension for it, because paying one would mean pricing the cost of every great shaft in the four quarters at a figure the owners have already reckoned and already decided against. The miners' brotherhood keeps a box at the pit-head, and the box buys the coffin.*
>
> *A tender who walks into a city is a person with no legible credential, and the specific expertise of knowing that a Phreatis pump-array sounds different on Tuesday than it did on Monday, and what the difference means, does not translate into anything the urban economy recognizes.*

### The pool trades

*Every worked Core (a Wellspring site, a place where one kind of Aether pools, in the common word, and as a rule a dungeon somebody cleared and now holds) has its keepers, and under the keepers it has the people who do the wet part. The pool and the city's night-pits share a good many of those people, since a man who will do one will generally do the other.*

**The Core-Tender** · Works the lip of the site in pairs, with an assay fork and a lamp. Checks seals, clears residue from the channels, bleeds the pool when it runs too thick, and reports density drift before the site goes dry. **Wet, cramped, poorly paid, and performed at the edge of a pool at densities that would be logged as an Active Concentration if a surveyor found the same reading in open country.**

> *No baseline is taken before a man goes down, because no baseline exists for a soul whose Crystal never woke. Which means nothing anywhere establishes what twenty years at the pool does to a man, and the trade knows exactly what it does, and has a word for the late stage that appears in no company document.*

**The Seal-Clerk** · Presses and records. Literate, indoor, resented by the men below and by the householders above in roughly equal measure.

**The Fee-Walker** · Walks. **The only representative of the entire magical apparatus that most people in a city will ever meet**, and the reason the apparatus is understood by most people as a bill.

> **The Snuffer** · Attends when a household stops paying, strips its lamps, scratches through its wards and drops his iron cap over the lamp stem. **The most despised of the four and the best paid**, for the same reason the executioner is: somebody has to, everybody knows it, and nobody wants to be adjacent to the person who did.
>
> *A snuffer works down a list drawn up by a company clerk who has never seen the room. The silence arrives before the dark does, and the snuffer is still on the stair when it does.*

The lamp-wardens, ward-cutters, hedge-workers and keepers who stand above these four have their own chapter, [[The Magic System/Practice and Institution/Who Pays for the Lamp The Living Crafts|Who Pays for the Lamp]], which the reader should take as read.

### Smithing

**The Blacksmith** · The most important tradesman in any community too small for a guild hall. *His forge is the warmest building in winter, which makes it the social center by default and the information exchange by accident.*

> The anvil is **the single most expensive object most smiths own**, often inherited, frequently older than the building it sits in.
>
> *Standing is high: a smith sits at the intersection of every other trade and is trusted by proximity. People bring their broken things to a person they trust, and the smith sees every broken thing in the village.*

**The Aethersmith** · Forges Holding-grade material: metal designed to accept, retain and discharge inscriptions. *The relationship to the Engraver is that of papermaker to scribe: the Aethersmith makes the surface, the Engraver makes the meaning.*

> **Stage III minimum**, because the Aethersmith must sense the Essence properties of the metal during the pour and the quench, and her title in the smiths' guild caps the Grade she may make ([[Materials, Alchemy & Trade/Work, Trade and Measure/The Apparatus of the Age — Equipment and Material Culture/The Item Tiers|Grade and Rarity]]). A Journeyman stamps Tempered and no higher, whatever came out of the quench.
>
> **This is done by hand and by ear. An Aethersmith listens to the metal cool, and the pitch of the cooling tells them what the coil will confirm after the fact.**

*Products: Aethersteel, whose crystalline structure accepts inscription at a depth cold-forged iron cannot match; Bloodsilver, alloyed under Anima Spirare conditions to bind the metal to a single bloodline; the substrate bars and blanks the inscription trades begin with; and, when a crew comes in with something on a cart, scale and bone worked up as gear.* A good Aethersmith shouts the name of a finished blade across the yard and stamps a guild mark on it the size of a thumbnail, and will fight you about that mark in any tavern you care to name.

### Cloth, hide, and stone

**The Spinner** · *Spinning is the most widely distributed craft in the four quarters, and the worst paid.* Every household that cannot afford bought cloth makes its own, a clothier's mule comes up the lane with wool for the ones who need the coppers, one loom keeps half a dozen spinners busy, **and spinning is the task that fills every hand not otherwise occupied, in every season, at every hour of the day.**

> ***A distaff is more common than a sword, more essential than a plow, and less discussed than either.***

**The Dyer** · Ancient, essential, **and unspeakably foul.** *A dye-works smells of stale urine, rotting plant matter and heated metal salts in a combination no amount of familiarity makes tolerable*, **which is why dyers occupy the same district as tanners: downstream, downwind, and outside the wall.** The result of all that stink is the reason a city street is the color it is: madder reds, weld yellows, the blues a whole quarter goes to war over.

> **Aetheric Residue (the trace a working leaves in a place) alters dye chemistry.** Wellspring-touched dyes produce a depth and luminance ordinary chemistry cannot replicate, *and the most valued dyes in the world exist where a specific Wellspring's Residue meets a specific mordant, guarded as trade secrets by the families that found them.*

**The Tanner** · Months of soaking, liming, unhairing, fleshing, **bating in a warm slurry of dog or bird dung whose enzymes break down proteins**, then oak bark in pits of increasing strength.

> **Leather from stable-Wellspring pasture takes an inscription.** The tanner who can produce Holding-grade leather from a consistent herd **commands a price the ordinary tanner cannot touch.**
>
> *Which produces the specific class inversion where the most despised trade in the settlement is also, occasionally, the wealthiest, and the settlement processes this by continuing to despise the tanner while borrowing money from him.*

**The Mason** · A mason's skill is **reading the stone**: where it wants to split, where it will carry weight, where it will fail, *by eye, by hammer-tap, and by the tactile knowledge of a lifetime touching one material.*

> **Greystone fortification uses stone selected for minimal Essence conductivity. A wall that does not conduct magic is a wall a practitioner cannot work through.**
>
> *The mason needs no Temperance. The mason needs to lay a wall a Stage XII practitioner cannot crack, and the fact that this is possible is the single most reassuring fact in military architecture.*

**The Ward-Carver** · A mason with Gnosis (the perceiving faculty that reads Essence). **Second Floor by training, First Floor by output.** Stage III–V, *enough to verify the inscription is seating, because a ward-carver who cannot feel the glyph seating is a mason who makes expensive mistakes in expensive stone.* The ward-cutter on your street corner is her poor cousin, who does lintels, and she will tell you so.

> **The highest standing of any artisan trade.** *A ward-carver who maintains a settlement's perimeter is the person standing between the community and whatever the perimeter is keeping out, and the community knows it.*

## The Inscription Trades

> **The Reconstruction invented writing Essence down. Everything since has been the consequence.**

**The Engraver** · Cuts Parunic syntax (the grammar of the glyphs) into prepared substrate. **The scribe of the material economy, the person who turns inert matter into working goods.** Stage IV minimum; Stage VII and above for recursive arrays, living runes, or anything that must interact with another soul's architecture.

> *The requirement comes from the work and owes nothing to politics. An Engraver whose Gnosis cannot read the substrate at inscription depth is carving blind, and a blind carving in a material that holds Essence is an object waiting to fail.*

**The loupe** · Ground from Clearbeam beryl, letting the Engraver see the pattern **at inscription depth** and verify the glyph is seating into the crystalline structure **rather than sitting on the surface.** *The single most valuable object an Engraver owns and the one most likely to be stolen, which is why Engravers' guild halls have better locks than the counting-houses.*

> **First Floor by classification, and resented for it by every other artisan trade**, because the Engraver **does not mine the metal, does not smelt it, does not forge it into shape, and earns more than all three combined for the final step of cutting a pattern into what other hands made.**

**The Glyph-Scribe** · Drafts the pattern the Engraver cuts. *The Scribe is the draftsman, the Engraver is the builder.* **Seven years minimum, and the Journeyman's examination is the most failed in any guild of the Accord,** because an error produces a template an Engraver will follow into a substrate **that will hold the error permanently and discharge it at the worst possible moment.**

**The Calibrator** · Puts the finished product on a coil, measures output against specification, **and signs the certificate that says it works. Or does not sign it, in which case the Engraver starts over.** *The calibration log is the legal record standing between the Engraver and liability if the product fails in the field.*

## The Small Powers

*Nobody compiled this section for the Logistics Division. It was added by the Archives, because the compiler's own laundry came back dry in the rain.*

Walk any market in the Old World on a working morning and count the people doing something impossible for money. About one person in ten wakes a Crystal, very few of those climb far, and a Crystal that will never see Stage III can still do one small thing thirty times a day for forty years. These people pull a thread of Aether from the ground under their stall, refine it, and spend it on the only trick they own. On fat ground (rich ground, in a scholar's mouth) they work till dark. On lean ground they are done by noon and charge double. They wear whatever their aura happens to be, so a fish market in full cry is a smear of little colors at wrist height, and nobody looks up.

Each of them is a tradesman first. Each of them costs money, has bad days, and can tell you to come back tomorrow, and the street treats that as it treats a baker's temper. Each has the trick on their own window as a skill, at a Grade they will tell you about over a second drink. The hall in any signed town has a rule against taking pay for a working without a token, and the fee-walkers have long since learned which stalls to walk past.

| Trade | The small power | What it pays | The bad day, and the refusal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Cold-Wife** | Lays a palm on a basket of herring and it stays sea-cold till evening | 2 copper the basket | Done by noon on lean ground. *She has declined the Fishmongers' steward since the day he called her "girl," and his fish know it by the second afternoon* |
| **The Link-Child** | Walks you home after dark by the light of her own open hand, in whatever color she woke | 1 copper the street | Children tire, and they stop where they tire. *She will also set you down at the corner of any street where someone once kicked her* |
| **The Knot-Wife** | Ties a knot that will not slip until she says so. Sailors, hangmen and brides | 3 copper a knot; 1 silver for a ship's lashing | She can die. *Every harbor has one rope nobody can undo, on a dead woman's say-so* |
| **The Bone-Thumb** | Hot thumbs that find a break through the skin and ease a joint home | 2 copper, or a chicken | Wrong about one break in ten. *Will not touch a man who has hit his wife, and the street knows who has been turned away* |
| **The Sour-Stopper** | Keeps milk, ale and cheese from turning, by the touch | 1 copper the churn | *When she has quarreled with a neighbor the whole stair's milk turns by morning, which has settled more feuds than the magistrates* |
| **The Mule-Talker** | Persuades any draft beast to go one mile further than it meant to | 4 copper the mile | *The mule has to be persuaded. On several recorded occasions the mule has persuaded her* |
| **The Whetter** | Strokes a blade with two fingers and it keeps its edge the month | 1 copper the knife; 2 silver for a sword she respects | *She can see what the sword has been doing, and she charges by her opinion of it* |
| **The Hush-Nurse** | Two fingers on the breastbone and a screaming infant goes quiet | 1 copper the night | *Her own children have never once slept* |

A Measurewright (one of the Guild's scholars of the art) once wrote out in three copied folios why a Cold-Wife's palm could not lawfully chill a basket below the temperature of the market, on grounds of heat having to go somewhere. He was correct about the heat. It goes up her arm, which is why they all wear short sleeves in the dead of winter. Whether a small power of this kind is a learned technique or a grain that happened to wake with its owner has been argued in the Guild halls for two hundred years, and the Cold-Wives have not been consulted.

**Guild craftsmen** stand one rung up from the stalls and let you know about it. They shout the names of their works across the square and fight over their marks, and what they build is medieval in everything but its ambitions: glyph-cut gearing, wheels with opinions, contraptions that misfire in funny and terrible ways. Dame Mabel Madder's Cradle of Perpetual Slumber, Warranted Against All Colic, has rocked three infants clear over the foot-rail and carries two guild marks, one of them in approval.

## The Yard and the Token

*A title is a job. Nobody on the street has ever been confused about this, and only the holders of titles have ever needed it explained.*

Practitioners are common, and their ranks are as well known as the grades of a butcher's cut. A carter can tell you what an inner disciple is good for and what one costs. A boy's family has been Heron for four generations, he wears the blue rag on his arm to haul sacks, and he will explain to you which Heron core disciple ought to be dropped from the bracket and why. Any city of size has a dozen fighting schools with their colors out, and a school is an employer of the ordinary sort, with a kitchen, a landlord, a roof that leaks and wages that are usually late.

| Rank | What the morning looks like | What it pays |
|---|---|---|
| **Outer disciple** | Hauls water, scrubs the yard, cooks, runs errands, drills at dawn, and hires out as the fifth guard on somebody's caravan with the school taking its share | A bed, board, the first forms. Entry itself costs fees, a seven-year indenture, or an oath sworn before the first lesson |
| **Inner disciple** | Teaches the outer ranks, runs missions, minds the school's gate and its stall at the fair | The next forms, a better room, Drafts (bottled workings that feed growth), hours at the school's Core, and the rites for its Wellspring |
| **Core disciple** | Fights when the school is challenged, wins in public, marries where the house says, and is sung about in taverns by people who have never met them | The inner manuals, the road to a Release, the first call on everything above, and a price on the head in somebody's ledger |
| **Elder** | Keeps the books, argues with the landlord, gives the word that lifts a disciple who lost or holds back one who won | Whatever the school can spare, and it always says it can spare very little |
| **Grandmaster** | Over all of them. Bites the anvil, if that is the custom | Commissions, bonds, gifts, and the bill for the funeral |

That is the school's ladder, and what it teaches is the part money cannot buy: Releases, Domains, techniques, skills and the rites for its Wellspring. A rich boy with a bought Stage still has to come and be hit by somebody's elder for these, and he pays the fees like anyone else, and it does him good.

**The token is the other ladder**, and the two are separate and both matter. A sect rank says where you stand in that school, and a title says what you are trusted with. The Guild Accord is a coalition of many kinds of guild across many nations, and every hall that signs on uses the same nine titles, grown out of the old guild words for the seven-year term and the masterpiece, each grading trust in that hall's trade. Guild people sneer at fighters, and fighters return the favor at volume.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Initiate** | Marked work, Grade E | Common goods | T1 commissions | Murmuring |
| **Apprentice** | Proofed, D | Common | T2 | Welling |
| **Journeyman** | Tempered, C | Uncommon | T3 | Ascension |
| **Adept** | Instrument, B | Rare | T4 | Flourishing |
| **Expert** | Hallowed, A | Rare | T5 | Splintering, Glory, Refraction |
| **Master** | Storied, S to SS | Very Rare | T6 | Transcendence, Invocation, Realization |

Read the right-hand column last, because it applies to one kind of hall. A fighter's token says how dangerous they are. Everyone else's says how much of your property they may be left alone with, so **a Grandmaster merchant may be handed a duke's regalia on a nod and may not be able to throw a punch**, and one of the commoner ways to die in a port town is to take the second fact as permission. The whole arrangement rests on a stranger being able to read the silver, which is why a forged token is the great crime of every trade and is hanged in every realm that has a rope.

The soul keeps a ladder of its own. At thresholds it offers a woken person a named Class fitting how they have lived, and a fair number of those names turn out to be trade names, offered to people who were not expecting to be told what they are. A steward does not get to see it unless the holder chooses to say. In a Guild roll the coat a fighter wears is **reinforcement**, the weight of a strong soul is **Pressure** and the trained read is **sensing**. A hiring steward in Common asks whether you have the shine and the nose, a Marrowkin foreman asks how long you can stay on shift under the load, and a Moto household asks nothing and watches how you hold your *matoi* at the door.

## The Crew and the Commission

*Nobody's mother wants this for them, and half the children in any Mouth town want nothing else.*

The Merry Thimbles of Pennycrown came up from the Upper Workings one wet spring evening with a Tolling Crab on a sledge, all six of them alive, and fell to fighting in the tavern yard before the mask-runner had her boots off. Level moves by danger overcome, so a kill on something far above you moves it more than a hundred on something beneath you, and the soul reports the gain to whoever it judges overcame the thing. Five of the Thimbles had spent an hour taking that crab apart with hammers and a tuning bar. The sixth was Nan Hobday, their cook, who had come down to bring them soup and had hit it with the pot as it fell over, and it was Nan's window that lit up. Their captain demanded to see it, which cannot be done. Nan described it, with feeling, for the whole yard. They were still arguing at closing, they broke two benches and a nose, and the Thimbles have gone down ever since with a written rule about the soup, and with Nan, who is a better fighter now than the captain and goes first.

**Adventuring is a trade**, conducted through some of the loudest halls in the Accord, and a hall is a guild like any other, with a green door, a board, a grudge against the hall across the square, and the same nine titles. Hunting halls post commissions rated by Threat Tier, T1 to T9, the same tiers monsters themselves are graded by, and a title lets you take them up to its own tier and no further. Monsters live everywhere, on the roads and in the barley and under the mill, so the work is not all stairs. Most of it is smaller and meaner than the songs, a T1 at a henhouse for a few silver and a Green Sigil's worth of hedge-pests along a toll road. The depth, the crew's posts and how a clearing goes are at [[The Bestiary/Dungeons|Dungeons]], and this register will confine itself to the money.

**An adventurer is somebody who ventures capital.** A big job is raised with subscribers and shares, so a crew holds a piece of its own luck and draws no wage, which is why it will go one more gallery than is wise. Six to twelve is usual. A real crew bond, the earned kind, shows as a line on each member's window, shares out a little of the Level gain, and shows the others' bars in a fight, and betrayal breaks it in front of everyone concerned. Every captain will tell you the bond is worth more than the shares, and has generally just finished arguing about the shares.

**What comes up the stair** is the whole economy of the thing. The hall pays on the mask or the bounty. **The core is what the crew came for**: in a way the monster's Soul Crystal, worth more the stronger the beast, a few silver at T1, most of a laborer's year at T4, and a sum that changes a family's history at T6 ([[Geography & the Four Quarters/Value, Coin and Trade/What Things Cost|What Things Cost]] has the rows). **Hide, bone and scale** go to the armorers. Anything left behind by the last crew goes to whoever can carry it past the next crew. A commoner who comes up holding a core has walked out of a hole with the one thing the great houses cannot simply refuse to sell her, and every factor in the valley is at the top of the stair with a very good dinner. A lucky crew is the nearest thing to a revolution most of the four quarters will ever see, and the houses have noticed, and they buy early, in several senses.

| Trade | The work | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **The Delver** | Signs on with a captain, goes down, breaks what the caller says to break, and comes up with a share. Takes a title if the hall will sit them, and works without one if it will not. **Mostly young, because of the arithmetic** | A leg, a hand, a coat that has changed color. *Crews go down six and come up four, and the two are entered in the book before the share is struck* |
| **The Porter** | Carries everything. Usually unwoken, with a little window that says *Carrying: C* and nothing to set off on bad ground. **First to say this is wrong, and generally right.** Paid a flat fee where the delvers hold shares | Being last in line. *In the Center a licensed labor-stock porter holds no share and no name on the instrument, and the roll writes him down with the rope* |
| **The Renderer** | Takes a core or a carcass at the door and turns it into something an alchemist can pour. Weighs on the assay pound, reads on the coil, pays on the day, and keeps the sharpest knives in town. **The honest ones keep a book of who sold what** | Contamination in the hands, a smell the neighbors petition about, and being offered, about once a year, a "monster core" with a shred of somebody's linen stuck to it. *Saying yes pays for a house. It is the worst crime in every realm, and it is why the honest ones keep the book* |
| **The Core-Runner** | Carries a core from the stair to the buyer, fast, in a Chainsalt box, before it loses grade and before the news gets ahead of it | Bandits, hedge-renderers, and her own crew's second thoughts |
| **The Bounty-Hunter** | Works the board for one name, man or beast. A famous monster has an epithet, a bounty and a following by the time it has eaten enough, and there is usually a queue. **Works alone because the shares come out better** | Finding out that the epithet was accurate. *Getting on a ranking list makes you a target too, and several of the people working the board are also on it* |
| **The Stair-Sutler** | Sells rope, bolts, salt, hot pies and fresh advice at the head of any dungeon that has a street on it. Buys the gear of the dead at a fair price from people too tired to argue. **The only trade at the stair that has never once lost money** | Being asked, by someone whose sister did not come up, why that coat is on the rail |

**Old delvers** are a sight in every Mouth town: a table of them under the painted name of a deep crew, missing the expected parts, drinking on a tally that gets longer every year. A few of them hold more of the town than the lord does. The rest are the reason that the sons of good families still get told about what happened to their uncle, usually to no effect.

## The Works and the Mill

> **The arrays made the Old World deep. Cloth is making it crowded.** A wheel on a fast river pounds the wool thick, a bell on a hall tower starts and stops the day, and the hands that do everything in between are coming in from the country faster than the town can roof them.
>
> ***This is the new floor of the age, and no register can count it, because it is never all in one room.***

Four years after Jonet Teasel's wheelbarrow of coppers (the [[Materials, Alchemy & Trade/Work, Trade and Measure/The Apparatus of the Age — Equipment and Material Culture/The Apparatus of the Age — Equipment and Material Culture|Apparatus]] has it, under The Hour), in the hot summer of Year 699, the masters of Cardmere quadrupled the fee for entering the Weavers' Hall, on the stated ground that the wrong sort of people had begun saving for it. The carders of that town, the people who drag raw wool between spiked boards until it will spin, belonged to no guild, were forbidden by statute to meet in any number above ten, and had been meeting in their hundreds for a generation under cover of a burial club. They came up Tenter Hill at dusk with teasels in their hats, grease to the elbow and a banner somebody's aunt had sewn out of the masters' own scarlet. The dyers and shearers fell in behind them in every color the vats could throw, and by midnight the wool hall stood open, the debt rolls were burning in the square, and a comber named Dickon Burr was sitting in the Provost's chair, in the Provost's gold chain, over a smock you could smell from the door. Inside the week Burr's people had made three new guilds, stopped all debts under twelve silver, and ordered the work bell rung at a decent hour.

Then they governed, and that is the part the masters lower their voices about. For three and a half years the poorest people in the richest town in the quarter ran its courts, its walls, its grain and its accounts, and ran them about as well as anyone ever has. The valley lord's horse came against them twice, in plumes, and the carders' muster met it at the weirs with long spears and wet ground. The gilt spurs are still nailed up in the carders' chapel, several hundred pair, and the chaplain will show you for a copper.

What finished them came with no sword at all. The clothiers went to their country houses and stopped putting out wool. A town can be seized, and a wool clip can be sold to the next town down the river. By the fourth winter there was nothing to card, and Cardmere's newest guild was three thousand hungry people with an excellent charter. The masters came home at the thaw and struck the three guilds from the roll. Burr, who had kept the accounts honest, got a pension and a house outside the walls on condition that he never again come through the gate. The Division has not decided whether that was mercy. The carders have.

What follows is how such a town comes to be possible, and it is how most of the hands in the four quarters that are not on a field actually earn.

**The Thirty Hands** · A bolt of Cardmere scarlet passes through about thirty pairs of hands between the sheep and the duke, and most of those hands never meet: sorter, beater, washer, carder, comber, spinner, warper, weaver, burler (who picks out the knots), fuller (who pounds the woven wool thick), tenterer, shearer, dyer, presser and the rest, in lofts, cellars, cottages and yards from the town wall to the hill farms. No one of the thirty can make the cloth, and each is paid only for the step they do. *A thing cut into thirty steps needs no craftsman after the first, only thirty people who cannot each sell the whole, and the clothiers found that out before the guilds did.*

**The Clothier** · This is the putting-out system, and it is the great invention of the age's money. A clothier (a draper, in the old towns) buys the wool, carries it out to a hundred cottages to be spun, collects the yarn, carries that out to a hundred rooms to be woven, **pays by the piece, and owns the cloth at every step** without ever touching it. He keeps a counting-room, a mule and a list. When trade goes slack he stops coming, and a hundred rooms find out on the same morning. *The loom is the weaver's own, and so are the rent, the debt on the loom and the bad year.*

**The Fulling Mill and the Dye-Hall** · **The only roofs that gather the hands together: timber-framed, loud at the near end and warm at the far one, and raising no chimney.** A fulling mill is a long shed with a wheel at one end and a shaft overhead, the shaft turning cams that lift great oak stocks and drop them on wet wool from first bell to last. A great dye-hall is the same shed with vats in it, and half of what goes on there is magic from end to end, done under trade work by thirty people who have never woken, each performing one licensed step. *Visitors from the provinces expect smoke and find a hum, and cannot understand why the people inside look so tired.* **The grime of the age is in the conditions.** The dye and cloth trades hang their work from the rafters to dry and peg it out on tenter frames (long racks that stretch wet cloth square) across the nearest hillside, so the tiredest people in the city labor under a ceiling of crimson and saffron, and the slope above Cardmere can be read from ten miles off, in stripes.

**What it makes** · **What a household cannot make for itself and a stranger will pay for.** A household still spins, bakes, brews and mends. The town sells it the rest: cloth by the bolt, each one sealed in lead at the cloth hall, and by the same arrangement of list and mule, nails by the keg, pins, needles and pewter. Boots are still a cordwainer's, one pair at a time, at the price in *What Things Cost*.

**The Crown's bolts** · Crossbow bolts come from a castle. A Crown keeps its quarrel forge in charcoal country, one master and a dozen hands at their own anvils, thirty thousand heads a year cut to a pattern gauge, so that a head goes onto any shaft of the same mark, at about three coppers the bolt, barreled and carted to the armory by the wagon. *The master's name is on the Crown's rolls and the gauge is the only true one in the quarter, and he sleeps on it.*

**Who owns it** · **Chartered houses, great houses and the factor houses that fund both**, with the clothiers' own hall sitting in front of them. The raw stock comes in under charter from the eastern arc and the south, the fine reds and the alum (the salt that fixes a dye to a cloth) above all, the finished bolts go back out along the same routes at the price the charter sets, and the margin stays in the west. *The age calls this trade. The places at the far end of the charter have other words for it.*

| Trade | The work | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **The Bench Hand** | Everyone in the cloth with no guild to stand behind: carder, comber, washer, beater, tenter-man. Holds a place at somebody else's bench from first bell to last, seven hours, half the Guild day, twelve days in every turn. **Women and children at the combs and spools, men at the stocks and shears, anyone at all at the washing troughs.** *Third Floor by classification and unskilled by definition, because nobody has ever paid a scribe to write down what a carder's hands know* | Fingers in the stocks, deafness by forty, fines for lateness by the hall bell, a statute against meeting in any number above ten, and **the Residue in the ground under the dye-hall**, which no clothier surveys and every hand eventually feels as a heaviness |
| **The Journeyman** | Has served the seven-year term, owns his tools, owes nobody, and works for a master by the day or the piece. Under the old rule he saved for the fee, made his masterpiece and opened a shop. **In any cloth town now the fee rises faster than the wage, a master's son enters for the price of a dinner, and most journeymen will die journeymen.** *He sleeps under the master's roof until he marries, and in a good many towns he may not marry until he is a master* | His brotherhood's dues, and the road. A brotherhood that finds a shop has cheated a man declares it foul, and no journeyman of that craft will work there, in any town a tramping man can reach. *The shearers of Cardmere once walked three miles out to a hill with their shears on their backs and sat on it for nine weeks, singing, while the cloth piled up unshorn. It cost them the harvest wages and it got them the rate* |
| **The Cloth-Searcher** | A craft's sworn officer, with a measuring rod and a bag of lead seals. Stretches each bolt at the cloth hall, counts its threads, stamps what passes, and burns what does not in the market in front of the man who made it. **The seal is what a stranger three kingdoms off is buying** | Bribes, offered daily by people who know where he lives. *Two have been found in vats, a color that took some identifying* |
| **The Miller** | Holds the water, with the lord's right behind him. Grinds, fulls or saws at the lord's mill, and takes his share of what comes through by the scoop. **Every tenant in the valley must come to him and is sure he is being cheated**, generally with cause | Flood, fire, flour in the lungs, and the bargemen, who want the water he is holding and own axes |
| **The Mill-Warden** | Keeps one of the nine Essence wheels in the four quarters, where a master's one-off stands in for a river that wandered, and is called a wheel-man on the floor: reads the assay fork, trims the load, listens for the note going wrong, and stops the wheel before the shaft does something worse. **Most are Stage I to III, like the array-tender, and paid like one** | The Shell clouding (damage to the Crystal's outer skin) the renderers find on the table, **and the knowledge that the wheel will be stopped before he is told why** |
| **The Overlooker** | Walks the tenter ground, the mill shed and the dye-hall for the clothier, sets the pace, keeps the time and fines the hands. **Risen from the bench, and the bench never forgives him for it** | Hated below and distrusted above. *The only man in the shop who knows both what the owner wants and what the benches can actually give. Cardmere's went into the madder vats on the first night of Year 699's rising, and came out the color of the town's best cloth* |
| **The Yard Hand** | Shipwright, caulker, oar-maker or rope-walker at the Great Yard at Saltash, one of two thousand sworn to the city, building its galleys by hand on the city's bell, under his own saint and on free wine. **The Yard men are also the city's fire company and its guard of honor, and they know it.** He may carry home at last bell a bundle of oak offcuts as big as he can lift on one shoulder | Crushed hands, pitch burns, and never leaving. *A caulker who takes a foreign wage gets a visit. The size of a bundle has been in dispute for two hundred years, the bundles have grown, and several houses in the Yard quarter are built of nothing else* |

### The guilds at war

*The register records these because every one of them is a lawsuit, and several have been a battle.*

**The Handloom Weaver** · **Once the aristocrat of the cottage.** Now working longer hours for a third of the town rate, for a clothier who carried the wool out past the town's three-league line because a cottage pays no guild, and teaching the trade to children who will never earn by it. The town weavers call him an interloper and hold a charter that says so, and every generation or so they march out in full muster to break his looms. In the Loom War of Year 671 the weavers of Cardmere went twelve miles in scarlet, with drums and a wagon of axes, to Nether Wadding, where the village women had carried forty looms up the church tower in pieces and were standing on the stair with distaffs. The expedition came home with one loom, a broken nose and a lawsuit that is still open.

**The Cordwainer and the Cobbler** · A cordwainer makes shoes of new leather, a cobbler mends them with old, and each company holds a charter against the other. **The whole dispute comes down to how much new leather may go on an old shoe before it becomes a new one**, and the searchers of both companies walk the town with rulers.

**The Nailer and the Chainmaker** · Whole villages at the hearth, the family's hands making nails by the thousand. A nail-monger puts out the rod iron by the bundle at the start of a turn, takes back the nails by weight at the end of it, weighs them on his own scale and pays in whatever he has. **Pins, girdles and bad ale are the usual coin**, and there is a statute against that, which he can show you.

**The Carter and the Carrier** · **Beaten wherever a river runs** (a barge carries what a dozen ox-wagons carry and pays no road-warden), tolled wherever a kept road runs (one of the few great old roads whose sunk waystones, tended by hired road-wardens, ease a heavy wain across a hand's breadth of air), and pushed out to the roads nothing else can reach, which are the roads that break axles. *The Weight Ablation harness that cost two seasons' surplus is worth half its price the day a bargeman ties up at the same wharf.*

**The Lamplighter** · The man with a ladder and a can of oil. The good corners are lost to the wardens with their boys and poles. **He keeps every alley, inn-yard and back stair that cannot pay for a warden's color**, and the Oil-Men's Company has been at law with the wardens' companies for eighty years over which corner is which.

> **The fee-bound poor are the age's own invention.** The hand who came in from the country for the cloth lives in one room on a stair, on sealed household work (a warden's lamp and a cutter's ward, split among six doors) at a quality below what the fee implies, pays the round-fee before bread, and is snuffed the month the clothier stops coming. *He came to the city because the lamps were there. He is now the person the lamps cost most.*

## The City the Cloth Built

*The great city of the age is timber, brick and stone, four to six stories where the walls will not let it spread, each story leaning a little farther over the street than the one beneath, and its newest quarters were mostly built inside one long lifetime.*

**The Street** · **Rows for the clerk, rents for the hand (a rent being a tall house let by the room), walled houses with a tower for the owner, and a market cross in the middle for all of them to shout at.** Brick in the west, gray stone in the north, painted plaster on the fashionable squares. Stone bridges cramped and banded in Stillband iron over every river, mud and pigs underfoot and a gutter down the middle. *The good streets are Essence-lit in every color their wardens happen to burn and are never quite dark, the rest go black at curfew, and the country-born do not sleep for the first month on either.*

**The Cloth Hall** · A covered street of stalls a bowshot long under a vaulted roof, with colored glass in its clerestory and the town's belfry on top of it, lit on market nights, warmed in winter by a warden's stones, patrolled by beadles who keep out the poor. Every bolt in town is sealed here and every town built it before it built anything else. **The age's cathedral, and it charges no tithe until you buy something.**

| Trade | The work | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **The Builder** | Mason, bricklayer, hod-carrier, joiner, plasterer, slater, glazier. **The city is going up faster than it has ever gone up wherever the cloth money is**, one master and his lodge at a time, and the men putting it up live in the parts that went up worst | Falls, lime-burns and the seasonal lay-off. *A builder is out of work every winter and on the parish every hard one* |
| **The Counter Hand** | Stands behind a stall in the cloth hall or the let-down shutter of a shop from the opening bell to curfew, in some year of a seven-year term, sleeps under the counter, and is cuffed for sitting down. **The journeyman watches the floor and the customer at once** | Varicose veins, a loft shared with four others, and the indenture's rule against marrying before the term is out |
| **The Bill-Sticker** | Nails hand-copied bills to church doors and market crosses at night, ahead of the magistrate's man who fines him in the morning. **Every door on a market road is somebody's advertisement** | Fines, which whoever hired him pays and docks from his wage |
| **The Street Trades** | Costermonger, raker (who shifts the muck), tinder-seller, shoe-greaser, hackney man with his horse, the boy who runs for anyone with a copper. **The city's Third Floor on its feet** | Weather, the watch, and the license each trade now needs and none of them can afford |

## The Service Trades

**The Innkeeper** · **A social institution first and a bed second:** the place where news arrives before it is announced, where prices are set by conversation before they are set by the factor house, and where the innkeeper's opinion of a guest determines **whether the guest's horse is stabled where it can be stolen.**

> *In practitioner-heavy districts: a high-Stage practitioner sleeping in a thin-walled room leaves Residue the next guest can feel. Good innkeepers install dampening inscriptions in their best rooms, which cost more than the furniture, and charge accordingly.*
>
> **The innkeeper knows everything. The innkeeper is trusted by nobody.**

**The Merchant** · Carries a seal-token representing their oldest active contract. *Torven Greis is the template: eleven way-stations on the Iron-to-Salt Corridor, metal one way and salt the other, making money on the spread and on the station fees and on knowing, better than anyone in a factor house ever will, what a week on the road actually costs.*

**The Carrier** · Does not own the goods. **Paid in weight and distance, the oldest arithmetic in commerce.** *Weight Ablation harness halves the effective load, which means a carrier without one is worth half as much, so every carrier who can afford the harness buys it, and every one who cannot is aware of his own obsolescence.*

**The Way-Station Keeper** · *An innkeeper stripped to essentials and stationed where nobody would live by choice but everyone needs someone to be.* **Quality runs from the Corridor stations, well-stocked because Greis-house money runs through them, down to** a roof, a well, and the specific hospitality of a person who has been alone for three months and is willing to feed you in exchange for conversation.

## The Healing Trades

**The Physician** · **In a Guild city the physician examines, puts the patient under with the sleeping sponge (poppy, mandrake and henbane, or a hired hand from the healing trades), and scrubs in vinegar and spirits of wine before anyone cuts.** Past the Guild cities the older school still does not touch the patient. **It reads**: urine, pulse, complexion, tongue, the character of the breath, and where Gnosis-trained, the Essence Signature. *A two-tier system results, and the two tiers disagree about everything except that the patient is their problem.*

**The Surgeon** · Cuts. **Below the physician, above the barber, and angry about both.** *The jurisdictional dispute is always the same: the physician claims diagnostic authority, the surgeon claims procedural authority, and the patient claims the right to see whoever will actually help.*

> **The Midwife** · The most experienced practical clinician most people in the four quarters will ever be touched by. *She knows the local births the way a farmer knows the local weather: from no book, and from a lifetime of being in the room when it happens.*
>
> **A birth is the formation of a Crystal**, and thin-Veil districts produce complications **the midwife recognizes and the physician has never studied.**
>
> *Her practical taxonomy of birth-by-district is the closest thing that exists to a medical account of what Wellspring density does to the formation of a new soul, and no physician has ever asked for it.*

**The Herbalist** · Knowledge arrived at by **giving people things and seeing what happens**, *which is exactly how every pharmacopoeia in history was built, and exactly how every college describes it in terms designed to obscure the similarity.*

## The Death Trades

> **Indispensable and untouchable.** Their touch pollutes. Their presence at a feast pollutes the feast. *Their money spends, and nobody asks where the coin was before the gravedigger's hand held it, because the question pollutes the asking.*

**The Layer-Out** · Always a woman. **The person who stands between the family and the physical reality of what a body does when it stops being occupied.**

> *A dead practitioner's Crystal does not cease producing Essence immediately. A body that is still radiating is a body the ambient Continuum has not yet acknowledged as gone.* **The layer-out in a practitioner community knows how long the Residue lasts** by Stage, by the Wellspring the dead one tempered on longest, by Path (a Body fighter leaves the most of it in the flesh, which is what renderers race for; a Spirit hand haunts; a Fate hand leaves vows still binding), and by whether the death was peaceful or violent, **and knows it** by having been in the room enough times to count. The Scarlet Rams can tell you what it costs to forget this.

**The Gravedigger** · **Burial depth matters metaphysically.** A body interred within Residue decay range of the surface bleeds Essence into the topsoil, and the accumulation over generations produces **Heavy Earth**: *soil that reads to Gnosis as remembering being alive.*

> Burial grounds in old settlements are measurably different from surrounding terrain. **Crops grow differently on them. Animals avoid them.**
>
> *The gravedigger who works them is standing in the accumulated memory of every person he has buried, and the occupation's reputation for strangeness is simply an accurate observation that got into the folklore.*

**The Executioner** · Usually hereditary, always polluted, **and the only person in most communities who possesses genuine expertise in how bodies fail.**

> **A practitioner's execution is a difficult piece of work.** The Crystal's death-response varies by Stage, by Path and lens, and by the conditions of the death, *and an executioner who does not understand what happens to a Crystal at the moment of severance is an executioner who will be killed by the discharge.*
>
> **The lowest standing in the four quarters.** The family does not marry outside the trade. His money is spent at a specific window. His children attend no school.
>
> **And his expertise in Essence-at-death is the most practically valuable body of knowledge in the death trades**, transmitted entirely within the family, **wanted by the Research Division, which has asked three times and been refused three times, because publishing it would require acknowledging where it came from.**

## The Paper Trades

> **The Accord is a coalition that runs on paper.** Outside its own Sealed Charter it holds little ground and raises no levy, and what binds a mining hall in one kingdom to a merchants' hall in another is a contract each of them can enforce. *A thing that has not been entered has not happened, and a thing that has been entered in three copies has happened three times.*

A hall sells three things, shared title, enforceable contracts and passage, and every one of them comes out of an inkwell. **A commission on the board, a venture's share-list, a hall's charter, a token's entry, a pass for the Concord Gate, a letter of passage over a border, a stamp on every contract and a stamp on the stamp.** Each is made out in triplicate by hand, one copy for the party, one for the hall and one for the Archive, *and the Archive's copy is the one that survives.* Crowns tolerate all this and are afraid of it, for good reason, since a kingdom can hang a hall's clerk and still find that nobody in it can get a shipment of tin across the river.

| Trade | The work | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **The Clerk** | Copies, files, enters and totals, on a high stool, by the Guild clock. **The largest respectable trade of the age**, and the one every family off the land wants for its cleverest son | Short sight, a stoop, a wage paid quarterly in arrears, and the knowledge that a bench of cheaper copyists is coming for the copying half of the work |
| **The Copyist** | Works the copy-bench: reed pen, ruled sheet, a boy to mix the ink, and three fair copies by nightfall for a hard-pressed hand. **The one office bench a woman can commonly sit at**, because a scrivener's widow or daughter has always held the pen in the back room, and there are more of them every year than the men in the next room find comfortable | Paid less than the clerk for more copies. *Half the offices of the age call the bench "the ink," and half of those call the women at it the same* |
| **The Scrivener and the Stationer** | The scrivener keeps a bench by the church door and will write your letter, your will or your lie for a couple of coppers, and his boy can make forty dozen-word bills in an hour. The stationer, in a university town, rents out a book's exemplar a quire at a time to whoever means to copy it. **The only literate person most people will ever hire** | Ink-galled fingers, the contents of every will in the parish, and the people who would like to know them |
| **The Beacon Clerk** | Reads and sends on the Guild's beacon line (hilltop fire-towers in sight of one another beside the few great roads the Guild runs, lit by wardens in shuttered colors and shut by any fog), counts the words, charges by the count, a silver for twenty with the addresses counted and a copper for each word after, and reads every message that passes. **Sworn to the Guild's secrecy, and the best-informed person in any town that has a tower** | Night duty, smoke, and a reputation for knowing what the neighbors paid to say |
| **The Hall Caller** | Stands on the great stair of a Guild hall in the big cities and bawls the name of whoever has a message waiting, as it comes up from the towers and the relay riders, until somebody claims it. **Chosen for a clear voice, and paid a little extra by certain people to mispronounce certain names** | A ruined throat by thirty, and a steward on the stair behind, counting |
| **The Hall Desk** | Stamps, countersigns, returns forms for the wrong color of ink, and is the face of the Accord to anyone who wants anything from a hall. **The seal-clerk's cousin, and as resented** | *Nobody has ever thanked a hall desk, and the hall desk has long stopped expecting it* |

## The Evening Trades

*The feast calendar leaves the age some ninety days with no work bell on them, and the age keeps a trade ready to fill each one.*

**The Pageant and the Tavern Hall** · **The pageant plays to the whole town and the tavern hall plays to whoever is still standing.** On a great feast every craft rolls out its own play on its own painted wagon and does it at each corner in turn, shipwrights a flood, bakers a famine broken, butchers a martyrdom with real props. The tavern hall has a host, a bar, a comic singer, a strong man and a turn that closes the night. Wagon-wrights, scene-painters, dressers, the hired warden who throws colored light from a rooftop. *A comic song from a tavern hall reaches every street in the city within days and every province within the year, and nobody can say who wrote it.*

**Sport and Spectacle** · **Racing through the streets and down the meadow on feast days, ward against ward in their colors, with the bookmaker at the rope and the whole city betting by runner. Prize-fighting, half-legal, under a bridge arch or in a tavern yard. Football on a feast afternoon, parish against parish, which is the same war as the schools' with fewer rules.** And the spectacle nobody else has: **the Stage bracket**, with colors, songs and a bookie on every bench. The great tournaments are the world's sport: realms send champions, schools play for colors and Cores, and a win can be worth a bought Stage. Beside the bracket stands **the licensed practitioner exhibition**, where a titled hand shows before a paying crowd what a Stage can do, under Accord license and on Core hours somebody has paid for.

> *Ordinary people regard a titled practitioner as half a saint and half a racehorse, and the exhibition sells benches on it.* **The license caps the working at exhibition grade and requires a warden on the floor**, and every promoter knows that the crowd came for the moment the warden might be needed.

| Trade | The work | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **The Herald and the Crier** | Cries the news at the cross and the bracket results at the gate, by the cry and by the corner. **All the news most people will ever get, at a volume you can hear across a fish market** | A ruined throat by fifty, and a beating from whichever side the news went against |
| **The Minstrel** | Sells songs about people who did things, and gets the details wrong in the direction that sells. **A fighter's name travels at the speed of a minstrel who needs supper** | Being found by the person the song was about. *Several have been asked to correct a verse, and at least one did it with a broken jaw* |
| **The Bookie** | Takes odds on brackets, duels, sieges, how long a stoppage will last, and whether the grandmaster will really eat the anvil. **Keeps the only figures anyone trusts** | Broken fingers, on a regular basis, and the debts of people who outrank him |
| **The Bounty-Clerk** | Keeps the board where lords, sects and houses post what they will pay and for whom, chalks up the changes, and takes a fee for posting and a fee for striking off. **Knows what everyone is worth, to the copper, and to whom** | Having his own name go up, which happens |
| **The List-Keeper** | Maintains one of the rankings: the Accord's roll, a herald's list, a realm's, a sect's, a bookie's. **They all disagree**, and each sells copies to the people on the others | Being visited by whoever came in ninth. *Getting on a list makes you a target, and so does keeping one* |
| **The Fixer** | Arranges for a bracket to go the way somebody has already paid for. A word to a second, a swapped vial, a sick mother. **Fixing fights is a major crime and a major business**, and every tournament town has one who dines with the magistrate | The rope, the river, or an honest fighter who was not told |

**The Tea-Stall, the Drinking-Hall and the Confraternity** · **The stall for talk, the guild's drinking-hall for men of one craft with nowhere else to sit, and the confraternity for everybody else.** A confraternity is a burial club with a saint, a banner, a feast and a box of dues, and any carder may join one, which is why the statutes against meeting have never once stopped a meeting. Guild halls keep a reading room with the rate sheets and the registers chained open to any member, and the confraternities keep the crier's bills and a preacher every turn. *More plans are made on confraternity benches than in council, and the Guild has noticed.*

**The Feast Garden and the Fair** · **Lit walks, hired trumpets, a hired hand throwing colored fire over the water and a rope-walker on an Ascensio line, when a great house opens its park on a feast day for the respectable; the fair for everyone**: the traveling show with its menagerie, its strong woman, its shadow-play booth worked by a girl with a light in her hands, and its fortune-teller who will read your Crystal for a copper, wrongly. The park at Merrow Hythe has a bridge that drops its guests in the lake and a stone ape that spits at ladies, both by a Guild master long dead, and neither can be turned off. *The fair arrives in the provinces with the only lantern show the town will see that year, and leaves with a year's pocket money.*

## What the Trades Are Paid

*Compiled, under protest, from* [[Geography & the Four Quarters/Value, Coin and Trade/What Things Cost|What Things Cost]]*, for readers who skipped the rest. One gold mark is twelve silver marks, and one silver mark is twenty-four copper. Past the last warden's round most of this is paid in kind.*

| Work | Pay | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **A laborer, by the day** | 12 to 24 copper | Half a silver in a slack season, a silver in a good one. Often paid part in board or kind |
| **A soldier, by the month** | 12 to 15 silver and his keep | Less stoppages for kit, which take a third of it |
| **A clerk, by the month** | 4 to 8 gold | Paid quarterly, in arrears. The copyist gets less for more copies |
| **A searcher, by the month** | 10 to 18 gold | And the gifts nobody records. Searchers inspect for the companies, with a seal press and an assay fork |
| **A miner** | Piece-rate, by volume of ore broken | Sick, slow or injured means less, on his ledger |
| **A carrier** | By weight and distance | Halved in practice if he lacks the harness |
| **A tender or mill-warden** | Paid like each other, and Stage I to III | No pension for the Shake |
| **The snuffer** | Best paid of the pool trades | The executioner's reasoning |
| **Lamp-wardens, ward-cutters, hedge-workers** | Round-fee; by the job and the glyph; in kind, usually | They can walk off the round, leave the last stroke uncut, or live a long way from you |
| **Petty folk arts** | A copper here and there | See The Small Powers, above |
| **A titled practitioner** | By title, 3 gold a month and up | See The Yard and the Token, above |
| **A crew** | By share, and by what comes up the stair | See The Crew and the Commission, above. The porter gets a flat fee |

## The Age by Place

> **There is no year in which the age arrived. It arrives by distance from rich ground and a Guild hall.** A traveler on this continent moves through time as well as across it, and the register cannot give a date for any object without first asking where it stands.
>
> *The wardens' rounds go out a few streets further every year, the chartered houses push further into the eastern arc every season, and every mile either of them gains is a mile of somebody's fee.*

**The Accord and the Guild cities** · **The age at its newest, and ahead of it where money allows.** An old kept road on the few great routes that have one and a beacon on the hills beside it, lamps of every color at the rich curb, a water clock in every hall and the Guild hour on every bell, Guild surcoats in the barracks, clerks and copyists in every office. The sleeping sponge and vinegar in the hospitals. *A clerk here carries a pocket dial that is no use to him indoors, and thinks of working by the light as something his grandmother did.*

**The provinces** · **Decades behind, and in no hurry.** The road reaches the county town in ruts; the inn is where the letters wait, and the carrier's wagon is the news; the fulling mill stands beside a street where the country looms are still working and the town muster has not yet come to break them. Horses on every road, the militia in bright tabards, tallow in most houses and an Essence lantern on the bishop's porch, and the spital and the barber for medicine. *A provincial town keeps two times, the Guild's and the church's, and lives by the second.*

**The north** · **The age at its oldest, and in Kharven nothing later.** No warden's round north of the arrays, no kept road past the last waystation, no beacon past the last Guild post. Tallow and rushlight, peat and dung on the fire, letters by rider, the levy in gambeson with a crossbow if the armory had one, the light and the bell, and medicine from the barber and the old women. Almost nobody reads. *The north cuts the lake ice a southern duke drinks his wine over and has never once wanted an ice-house. It sends the age its cold and shares in none of its comforts.*

**The east and the south under charter** · **Every decade of the age at once.** A Cloudmetal works, one of four in the world, stands a day's walk from villages the age has not reached, and the chartered grove, the chartered wharf and the Guild post are the age at its newest set down in the age at its oldest. *The charter brings the works, and the works bring the wage, the fee and the snuffer, in that order.*

> **A journey from an Accord capital to Kharven-Seat runs the whole age backwards.** A carrier's wain over the old stones to the edge of the fat ground, the same wain off the stones behind a tired team to the county town, an ox wagon to where the road is paved, a hired hack to where it stops being paved, the mule to the pass, and the last of it on the road the first cart made.
>
> *The traveler who makes it folds away his pocket dial at the last Guild bell, changes his coat at the last waystone and his manners at the pass, and arrives in a country where his dial is a curiosity and his title token is the only thing about him anyone understands.*

## What the Division Has Not Surveyed

| Polity | What it builds, and what that says |
|---|---|
| **Marrowhold** | **Extraction technology. The Marrowkin invented nothing that does not come out of a hole.** Pumping, ventilation, winding and forge arrays, and the Aethersteel process that exists because Ignivale kilns hold temperatures charcoal cannot reach. *Between a miner's pick and an Engraver's burin lie roughly forty named occupations that the Accord classifies as Second Floor and the Marrowkin classify as the reason they eat* |
| **Nalūn** | **Trust, where Marrowhold builds extraction.** The Parun-bound instruments, the seal-tokens, the bills that present for payment across three jurisdictions because **the bill enforces itself.** ***Nalūn makes no things at all. Nalūn makes the agreements that allow other people to make things at a distance from each other***, the physical expression of a culture that discovered **the most valuable thing in the world is a promise you can verify** |
| **The Silver Cedars** | **Perceptual.** Ward-perimeters, Boundary-layer monitoring, the Yukari contribution of perceptual governance. **Technologies of watching, reading and knowing, with very little of making in them.** *The Reach builds walls out of awareness rather than stone, and treats maintenance as a civic sacrament and novelty as a risk to be assessed before it is adopted* |
| **Kharven** *(redirected from the struck Sātūlagi entry)* | **Martial.** The Moto Fractura attunement gives a structural advantage in fortification assessment, demolition, **and the military engineering that involves looking at a wall and knowing where it will fail.** *Kharven builds for war the way Marrowhold builds for extraction: with no elegance whatever and the specific brutal competence of people who have been doing one thing for a very long time and have stopped pretending it is anything other than what it is* |
| **The Holy Sea of Alabaster** | **Testimonial.** The Wellspring, the most complete archive of births, deaths and contracts in the Inner World, **and the deposit-and-lending operations Nalūn considers a sin and the Holy Sea considers a service.** ***Its works are records, with no machinery anywhere, and the records outlast everything*** |
| **The Eastern Arc** | **What happens when a polity commits array capacity to something other than pumping water.** Cloudmetal reduced by Judicium arrays at a scale that exists in four places in the world. Onceglass charges sold as consumable munitions. Ascensio-augmented looms the west cannot match. *It demonstrates the thesis of* The Four Ceilings *in real time: technology owes more to geological accident and institutional willingness than to genius, and a people sitting on different rock with different priorities will build a different world* |

> The Logistics and Supply Division commissioned this register to establish **what work exists and who does it**, and it has done that. It has touched entertainment only where entertainment is a trade, and it has not touched **food and cuisine, ground-level justice, domestic life and courtship, or funerary custom**, and the omission is consistent rather than accidental.
>
> *Every one of those is something the Third Floor does when it is not working. The Division surveys labor because labor is what the Division buys.* **A register compiled by a purchaser will describe what is for sale and will be silent on everything a person is when the day ends.**
>
> The Archives has proposed a companion survey on four occasions. **Each proposal has been referred to the Division that would have to fund it.**

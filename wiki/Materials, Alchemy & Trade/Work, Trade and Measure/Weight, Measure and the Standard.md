# Weight, Measure and the Standard

*What a pound is, who decided, and who has never accepted it. The same political act as standardizing the tongue and the hour, performed by the same institution, resisted by the same people.*

> *One weight, one measure, one reach, through all the land.*
>
> *The Accord's formula, repeated in four successive revisions of the formulary, each of which was necessary because the previous one had not worked*

On the last morning of the Barley Fair at Saltby the grain cog *Fat Margery* warped off the town quay with her red sail going up, painted eyes on her bow, a band on her foredeck and five hundred loads of barley in her, and half the fair came down to wave, because her master owed money to most of them. She had been filled by the Saltby measure, heaped, and her master had signed for five hundred loads by the Accord's measure, level. The difference between those two words is about a quarter, so she was carrying a hundred and twenty-five loads nobody had written down, and she rode so low that the boys on the quay were laying odds. A furrow out, a seam opened. Dry barley that takes on water swells with patient and enormous force, and the hold of a cog is a closed box. *Fat Margery* let go at every plank from the keel up, in full view of the fair, and five thousand measures of barley went out across the green harbor in one long gold slick with the band still in it, and every gull on that coast arrived to see.

Her owners sued the quay-factor for the cargo. The factor sued the master for the signature. The master, in a wet red coat, sued the town for its measure, and the town, wanting somebody smaller, sued the strike-board man who had filled her, Rolling Ned Tolland, who came before the Bench in his best hat and demonstrated his stroke on a sack of peas, slowly, three times, to the delight of the gallery, while two clerks tried and failed to describe it. The court sent out for the town's standard measure, which was brought in on a cart with some ceremony and turned out to carry a six-pointed strike, filed halfway smooth. The suit ran thirty-four years, outlived three judges and every party but Ned, and produced one finding anyone remembers: that nobody in Saltby, then or since, could be made to say on oath how big a measure is. The barley came up that spring on the harbor mud in a long green stripe. It is called Margery's Acre, and the question of who owns it is still before the Bench.

What follows is everything Saltby could not say on oath, set out by an archivist who has checked his own rule against the wall twice this week.

## Why This Is Political

Three institutions already built cannot function without standard weights: **the assay**, which determines fineness by comparing masses; **the proof-mark**, which certifies that a thing was tested; and **the Bench of Attribution**, whose four powers are entry, striking, attribution and reservation, and two of those are exercised on weights.

Somebody had to make the four quarters agree what a pound is. The Marrowkin did not want to. **Nobody wanted to**, because a local measure is a local advantage, and the man who knows the district bushel is a heaping bushel is the man who buys in that district. The Accord is a coalition of guilds across many nations, so it could not simply decree the thing. It made the standard a term of signing on: a hall that wants the shared titles, the enforceable contracts and the passage takes the pound along with them, then goes home and keeps the old bushel in the back.

The pattern is unambiguous, here as in every history that has ever tried it: **central authorities issue uniformity statutes over and over across centuries, and the fact that they keep issuing them is the evidence they are not working.** The Accord's formulary has been revised four times. Every revision says one weight and one measure through all the land. Every revision was needed.

## I. The Grain

Everything is built on a seed.

The base unit of both length and weight is **the grain**, a single counted seed of standard barley, dried. Three laid end to end give a **thumb**. Seven thousand by mass give a **pound**. Nobody designed this system and nobody would call it elegant. You arrive at it when the only thing available in every village in the four quarters that is reliably the same size is a seed.

**Which is also the flaw.** Grain from different soils is not the same size, and the Accord's grain is Western grain, and northern clerks have been saying so for six hundred years and have never once been able to prove it in a room.

## II. Length

| Unit | Equal to | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **grain** | — | one seed, end to end |
| **thumb** | 3 grains | the joint of a thumb, near enough |
| **span** | 12 thumbs | the working unit; a mason's rule is a span long |
| **reach** | 3 spans | fingertip to fingertip is close; the Accord's standard reach is a bronze bar |
| **rod** | 5½ reaches | awkward, universally complained about, never changed |
| **furrow** | 40 rods | what a team plows before it turns |
| **walk** | 8 furrows | an hour's walking on level road, which is why distance and time are the same word in most of the four quarters |

**The Accord wanted fourteen and did not get it.** Fourteen is the doctrinally correct number and the chancery proposed it in the second revision: fourteen thumbs to the span, on the reasoning that the day divides by fourteen and so should everything else. Every trade in the four quarters refused, in unison, for a reason that has nothing to do with theology. **You cannot halve fourteen twice.** A carpenter needs the half, the quarter and the eighth, and twelve gives him all three, and fourteen gives him one and then a fraction he has to think about.

The Sohai retains a fourteen-count for rite measurement only. It is the one place the Accord's number lost to a carpenter's, and the chancery has never referred to the episode again.

## III. Weight, in Two Systems

Two parallel systems, exactly as the real world ran troy against avoirdupois, and for the same reason: what you weigh gold with should not be what you weigh coal with.

**Market weight.** Sixteen ounces to the pound. Bulk goods, food, fodder, ore in the raw, timber, cloth, people. The pound of seven thousand grains.

| Unit | Equal to |
|---|---|
| dram | — |
| ounce | 16 drams |
| pound | 16 ounces |
| stone | 14 pounds |
| hundredweight | 8 stone |
| ton | 20 hundredweight |

**Assay weight.** Twelve ounces to the pound, with finer subdivisions, and it is the only system the Bench recognizes. Precious metal, gemstone, Draft ingredients, monster cores, physic, and anything a proof-mark will be struck on. **Nothing is ever priced in both.** A merchant quoting assay weight for a commodity has said something either technical or dishonest and the room will decide which.

The **assay pound** is deliberately scaled so that a bead's mass reads directly as fineness against the sample taken, and the assayer does no arithmetic at all. This is the single cleverest thing in the Accord's metrology and was borrowed wholesale from a trade that had worked it out centuries earlier.

**The stone is fourteen pounds** and the Accord did not choose that either. It arrived from the wool trade, where it had been fourteen for so long that the second revision simply wrote down what was already happening. It is the only place where the doctrinal number turns up in commerce, and it got there by accident, and the Sohai has never made anything of it, which the Sohai considers a discipline.

## IV. Capacity, and the Oldest Argument in the Four Quarters

**The measure**, for grain, with a quarter-measure below it and a load of eight above.

The entire dispute is one word: **level or heaped.**

A level measure is struck flat with a strike-board across the rim. A heaped measure is filled to the cone. The difference is roughly a quarter, which is a fortune across a harvest, and every statute in the Accord's formulary insists on level, and heaping is universal, and everybody knows it is universal.

**The strike-board is therefore the most political object in any market.** Who holds it, how fast they draw it, and whether they roll it or drag it, decides the trade. A slow, rolling strike leaves more in the measure. There are men who are known for it and are hired for it, and there is a word for the practice in every dialect on the Common page, and it is never the same word twice.

## V. The Beam

**The public weighing beam** stands in the same building as the toll office, the customs table, the council chamber, the courtroom and the guardhouse. It stands in with them, closer than any neighbor, because they are all the same function: the place where the polity touches a person and takes a reading.

The standards are physically fixed to the outside of that building. The bronze reach is set into the wall at hand height, worn smooth, and anybody may check a rule against it at any hour without asking, and people do.

The building hires out its own small powers, too. A **true-hand** is a market's petty folk art (one small working and one small living): somebody with a woken Crystal who can heft a weight and call it to the grain, at a copper the call, with a Heft skill on her own window that she will tell you about at length. Nobody has ever beaten a good one fairly. Everybody knows what a true-hand costs to be wrong, and on a busy morning she may decide she is tired and go home, which has closed markets.

**This one arrangement tells a stranger more about a town than any amount of description.** A man taken up in the street is taken into the building where his grain is weighed and his rule is checked, by people he has seen weigh grain and check rules.

## VI. Verification, Striking, and the Cased Weight

### The stamp

Every weight and measure in lawful trade carries **verification marks** struck into it: the maker's mark, the town's mark, the system it belongs to, and the searcher's stamp from its last check. A rim of small marks around a bronze measure is a history of every time it has been tested and passed.

### The striking

A weight found false is **struck**: cancelled with a deep six-pointed mark cut across the verification stamps. A struck weight is unfit for trade, must be destroyed, and frequently is not, because bronze is expensive and a struck weight still weighs something.

**Struck weights in circulation are one of the most reliable indicators of how far the Accord's reach actually extends.** In a Western market you will not see one. In a district the searcher has not reached in nine years you will see nothing else, and the six-pointed marks will have been filed halfway smooth, and everyone trading there knows exactly what they are looking at.

### The cased weight

The classic fraud, taken whole from the real trade because it cannot be improved on.

A **solid** weight is bronze all through. A **cased** weight is a bronze shell filled with lead, which is heavier for its bulk, so a cased weight of the correct external size weighs over, and a hollowed one weighs under, and either can be made to look perfect.

**And it is detectable by ear.** A solid weight rings when struck. A cased one gives a dull note. Every market searcher in the four quarters carries a small hammer and taps before he does anything else, and every child raised near a market knows the two sounds, and the sound of a dull weight in a quiet market is the sound of something about to happen.

## VII. The Draught

The keepers of a worked Core (in the common word a Wellspring site, a place where one kind of Aether pools, and in most cases a dungeon somebody cleared and now holds) sell time at the pool, and a practitioner pulls Aether from the ground around them, fast on rich ground and slowly on thin, which means what the keeper sells had to be measured. The Guild solved the problem the way trades always do: **it measured the aperture and the time, and left the substance alone.**

The **standard ferrule** is a bronze ring of fixed bore, glyph-cut and set into the stone at the lip of the pool, and a practitioner sitting at it can pull no wider than the ring allows. A **draught** is that bore, held open on ground of the site's stated grade, for one Guild hour by the water clock. (The keepers hold to the old spelling on purpose, so that nobody will mistake it for a Draft, the bottled working, and everybody does anyway.) Nothing about the metaphysics enters into it. The keeper's book counts apertures and hours, the bill is written in draughts, and neither the fitter nor the clerk nor the sitter has any opinion about what is actually passing through the ring.

**Consequences, all of them mundane and all of them useful.**

A Core on falling density bills the same for less, because the book counts time and bore rather than delivery, and the lamp-wardens who top up there pass the loss on to every street they keep, and this is the single largest source of Withering-era grievance that no minstrel has made a song about. A sitter who widens a ferrule is stealing and can be caught by a searcher with a rule. The tundish, a small brass funnel of a fitting set on the ring so that the color of what passes can be seen from the door, exists so that the widening cannot be hidden. And **the Guild bills in Guild hours**, which is the second reason the equal hour won: a draught measured in unequal stations would be worth more in summer.

## VIII. Who Uses What

| People | Position |
|---|---|
| **The Accord and the Guild** | Their own standard, in every hall that has signed, in all things, and the formulary revised four times to say so |
| **Concord towns** | The Accord standard in the market and the old local measure in the back of the shop, for regulars |
| **The holds** | Both, in parallel, the same as the two-column hour ledgers. Hold weight for the Tally, Accord weight for the beacon line. A Marrowkin will not enter an Accord figure without converting it, and the conversion is itself an entry |
| **Eresse** | Accepts the Accord's weight and rejects its authority to set it, on the grounds that a standard nobody witnessed is a standard held contingently |
| **The Mandate** | Adopted instantly, as with the hour. A decree specifying a quantity is a better decree when everyone means the same quantity |
| **Kharven** | The ration, the bowl, the stack, the fire's length. The scale is inspected publicly at Ice-out and that inspection is a rite, not an audit |

## IX. Where the Weighing Is Also Worship

**Urion's Labor is the Weighing:** one thing weighed on a true balance every day. It requires no priest, no building and no scale but the household's own, and it is performed by more people in the four quarters than any organized rite.

So the same physical act carries three meanings at once and the actors are usually unaware of two of them.
- **The Accord** weighs to establish a fact and charge for it.
- **Sancta Lux** weighs to establish worth, and holds the establishing to be sacred.
- **The Sohai** weighs to acknowledge Balance, expects no answer, and considers the other two to have confused a Labor with a transaction.
- **Kharven** weighs the ration and inspects the scale in public at Ice-out, and would tell you it is doing none of the above and is simply making sure nobody starves.
A scene in which a man weighs something in front of another man is never only about the weight.

## X. Hooks

**The dull note.** A searcher taps a weight in a market where he is outnumbered, and it does not ring.

**The filed strike.** Every weight in a district carries a six-pointed mark filed halfway out. The district has been trading on struck weights for nine years. Somebody has just been sent to inspect it.

**Falling density, same bill.** A quarter is billed for draughts it did not receive, because the keeper's book counts bore and hours. The arithmetic is correct. The Guild's position is that the arithmetic is correct.

**Western grain.** A northern clerk can demonstrate that the Accord's grain is a Western seed and that every weight in the four quarters is therefore calibrated to one soil. He is right. He has no forum, and the demonstration takes two days and a set of scales nobody will lend him.

**The strike-board.** A slow roller is hired for a season's buying. The difference across the harvest is a fortune. It is not illegal and cannot be made illegal without describing a hand movement in a statute.

## Preliminary Note · Urbis, the Division's Desk

The Division keeps its Urbis desk in the lower floors of the Archivum, under the Monastery, by the Heralds' leave. I went down on Maelor's day with the crown's warrant still creased from the clerks' press. Two clerks. A file-press, and a gauge on the wall with the Board's seal on its glass, and one desk set to face the stair, so that whoever sits at it sees who comes down before they see her. The main hums louder down there.
Halveth sat at that desk. Description of record: a woman past sixty, spare, her grey hair pinned flat to the skull; half-spectacles on a black ribbon; the right middle finger ink-stained to the second joint; fingerless mittens of grey wool against the cold of the lower floors; a Division clerk's grey, with sleeve-guards over the cuffs. No silver that I could see. At a Division desk that means none, or a pocket.
She rose, set the file on the table in front of me, and stood until I picked it up. She said nothing then. She has said nothing to me since, and she writes.
The letter of access lay on top. Its first line read, "The Searcher will keep a field journal." She had underlined the word field and circled the word journal, and in the margin, in a small precise hand, she had added the rest.
> Margin, H.: not a report. Actual observations. Including the ones you don't want to have.
A report is a filing. A filing goes to the bulletin office, which prints what it is given on the cheapest rag it can buy for anyone to read, and field notes under the Division's seal are printed nowhere. I did not ask whether that was her reason. It is the one I have written down.
The chapter secretary told me the Division had asked for someone who reads nothing. Halveth gave no reason. My guess is that a woman who reads nothing has no reading to be argued with, and I set it down as a guess.
The order covers what I would rather leave out, so: I was screened at seven, in the screening season, and nothing stirred, so no roll took me. The chapter bench read me when I joined the Society and entered me Class Ø. Every reading in these notes belongs to an instrument or to somebody who can read, and I name which. On my left wrist is the regulation bracelet, brass bands wound with grey wire, which pulls toward anyone holding his weight down. It is the only warning I get.
I walked an Urbis walk for eight years, the slow-main streets east of the high street. I have carried the crown's warrant for twelve. A cut district that went on humming after its plate was dropped. A relic lot at the river stairs whose Carry ran heavier than its papers. A man whose file put him at Stage VI, who believed Judicium had begun to speak to him in particular.
A name in a Society file is a line somebody can follow. A walker's name heads every insert he files, and whoever reads the inserts knows where to find him. This file names the subjects of the warrant, the witnesses I hear and the officers who handle it; everyone else goes in by post or by exhibit. The two volumes stay at the desk for the same reason, read and not copied.
Exhibit C came to the Archivum after the volumes were withdrawn, and the Division's desk holds papers touching a sealed deposit. Nobody has opened it. Its wax is the evidence of the mark, and I will not break it either.
Exhibit D is the reason there is a warrant. Neros, whose readings Exhibit B reports, found the Weight moving again this autumn, to the same side as before. The Measurewrights' instrument chair at Nalūn confirmed it. The confirmation went to the crown's clerks, and the warrant came to the Society inside the same turn, before anyone could return it as weather.
The Weight has moved. Not by much. Enough.
I am to establish whether it follows the commission in Exhibit B; whether other workings of the kind have been done or are being done, and at what scale; and what the crown should know. Halveth added a line against the last.
> Margin, H.: and find Gisli Draycott.
The Guild's field protocols say to baseline the unawakened members of a party before departure, by any means available, even if it is only a note on how they were. I am the unawakened member of this party, so from the first entry I baseline myself.
I took the file up the stair under my arm.

## Entry the First · Castlefall, the Lower Room

**Baseline.** Irath's day, early winter, Castlefall on arrival, eleventh division. Slept four divisions the night before. Hands dry and even, the left no warmer than the right. Appetite ordinary. Temper even, which Halveth may annotate.
The line runs up the valley by day. The conduit hum came up through the bench into my back teeth, a note under the tram's, until the foot of the valley, where the carriage came off the main onto the station spur. Castlefall's platform is cedar. It is ten winters old and still weeps sap onto the carters' boots.
The station carver met the train and asked me who keeps my posts. I told him I had none, which in Ketsuen counts as an answer, and he put me on the station's record on my own word: name, Society, chapter, business on the crown's warrant, lodging. Anyone may read it. Ketsuen has no crown and governs by the Proving, so here the warrant is paper, and I can ask and cannot compel.
> Margin, H.: You are on their record now.
The pressure came on behind my temples inside the first division off the train, a low weight laid there and left. Ketsuen gives it to everyone.
Castlefall smells of lime first. In the morning the leat below the tannery ran grey with it. Next door a carver's mallet was going on a practice stone, every stroke on its beat, and the tanner says the boy who used to land every third stroke late keeps half the street's posts now.
The flat above the tanner's is let again to a dyer's family, and the lower room at the back of the yard, the old drying-room, to a distiller. Gauge, the foot of the outside stair, seventh division: seven and two marks. The survey tables put that at the high end of Ambient Saturation, and the ladder calls ground that reads high and steady Rill. It harms nobody.
Calla, distiller, of Castlefall. Description of record: a woman near fifty, short and broad through the shoulders, her hair cropped close under a kerchief the colour of old rust; a square weathered face, spectacles pushed up onto the kerchief; forearms scarred with the small white burns a still gives; a leather apron over grey wool worn shiny at the hip where the bench edge meets it. Concord-born, from down the line. Her certificate hangs framed by the door: bench-certified, pure alchemy. A copper sat on the sill. She aired the room three days when she took it, as her mother taught her, and left the coin against the ill luck a working's leavings bring.
She had covered the circle with a rug. She read the warrant through, which few people do, and pulled the rug back herself.
The circle takes up most of the floor. Exhibit B says its ink drank the lamplight at the edge of the flame and gave none back. I held the lamp to it. It does. The line looks cut into the boards.
"You'll know what it is, Searcher," she said.
I told her it was a Boundary, which is what Exhibit B calls it and as far as the Codex goes.
She looked at it a while. "It feels like a room that hasn't finished with something."
I wrote that down.
Twenty years at a still gives a woman something in the hands that the trade calls grain, and no instrument in the Accord reads grain. At nineteen I was tally-clerk on a Guild survey party, and the man who first said the ground was wrong was the one carrying the tent poles. The survey lead listened to him. So do I. By her certificate, she and I were the two in that room with nothing awake in us.
Coil, over the circle, five stands, eighth division. The needle came to a quiet reading at the first stand and did not move from it at the other four. The scale card calls that residue at its floor: what an ordinary working leaves once it has closed, thinned as far as it will thin. It holds there and does not climb.
I took a scraping of the circle's ink into a Fixatio tin and sealed it standing on the boards, under chapter wax. By the wall, where she said the old bench had stood, there was a drop of a second ink, darker, the size of a thumbnail. It drank the lamplight too. I took it into a second tin under the same wax.
I have seen an ink that takes the light that way once before, on a Mortalis-adjacent piece in a relic lot at the river stairs. I am drawing no conclusion from the ink.
The Codex is a scholar's account. I am the one who comes after the scholar and counts what he left on the floor. I put the rug back and thanked her.
I bought the coffee Exhibit B calls terrible at the stall by the tanner's, one copper. On the platform, leaning on a post that wept sap onto my glove, I read again the treatise's second residue, as the Codex reports it.
> The Sympathetic Bond Trace is the resonance-thread that runs between a Soul Crystal and the people it loved, a bond of the Attraction Layer. At death it attenuates slowly and never severs entirely, and its living end stays in the mourner's own Crystal.
My reasoning. If the commission worked as the Codex describes, the working that stood on that floor reached through a bond still living in a mourner in the eastern lowlands. The residue under the rug is the near end of that reach. The man who closed the circle is the third point, and one thread runs through all three, thinned.
Traces thin. The floor holds.
I finished my coffee. I boarded the down train.

## Entry the Second · The Eastern Lowlands, a House with No Name on the Gate

**Baseline.** Maelor's day, midwinter, sixth division, an inn on the river road. Slept badly on the line, well at the inn. Hands cold, both alike. The pressure at the temples lifted somewhere past the last ward-post, and I did not mark where.
The crown's registry in Urbis gave me the names from the land records without much trouble. The house and its land stand in the name of Cassian Ault. His wife, Madeleine Ault, is entered dead of the lung-sickness in the winter of 694, in a clerk's round hand: In registro relatum. Exhibit B leaves them unnamed.
Here the warrant runs, and nobody at the gate needed to read it twice.
The ground is lower than Castlefall's and wetter, with a slow river near that I heard before I found it. By the door a meter under the Board's seal ticked once while I waited. The house keeps the kind of quiet money buys. Cassian Ault received me in a study lined with books whose spines have never been broken.
Description of record: a man of about sixty, tall, heavy in the frame and gone slack at the middle; white hair, thick, brushed straight back from a high forehead; clean-shaven, the jaw heavy; a black coat of good cloth cut for a heavier year and taken in at the seams by someone careful; a gold ring on the left hand that he turned with his thumb. He poured two glasses of wine and did not touch his. "Have you come about my wife, Searcher?" It was a prepared sentence.
Gisli Draycott, I told him.
His face went still. My own thought, marked as mine: he had been waiting for whoever would come.
I told him he had broken no law. He paid for one working, unentered, done by a Master of the Circle and sealed by that Master's own hand, and the breach is the seller's, who by Exhibit B has named it.
He drank his wine then, the whole glass.
His account in brief follows; the whole is sealed separately as Ault's statement.
She spoke, he said. She knew the names: his, the housekeeper's, a dog they had when they were first married. On the first morning she coughed as she had coughed at the end, and he sent for the physician before he remembered. She held his hand and told him not to grieve for her. She asked after her books. She believed herself whole.
"It was my wife," he said. "In every particular I was able to test."
On the eleventh day she began to do things his wife had never done. He chose his word for it with care and gave it to me twice: with intent. Before the eleventh day was out she asked him for the key to the room he had shut on the day she died, which had been her workroom. He opened it. On the bench, which she had cleared a little at a time over the last turns of her illness, lay a cloth, and under the cloth a paper, folded and sealed.
Then she ended. "In one breath," he said. "Like a lamp when the main is cut."
He sat in that room for some hours. Then he read the paper.
I call what he received the likeness. Exhibit B quotes the treatise's footnote, "Never give it the name," and as the Codex reports, the word matters. I do not use her name for it.
The Concord sits nine days with its dead, in shifts, because after the ninth nothing is left in the body to call. The likeness came two years after her ninth day, poured, by Exhibit B's account, from what the places and the people had kept.
The younger physician, he said, had gone home before the end. The old man with the stiff knee stayed through all eleven days and wrote things down, and on the last day asked to be in the room and stood in the doorway when he was refused. Inside the turn that followed, a fair-haired man came to the house, sat at her bench, read the paper there, and did not take it away.
He keeps the empty vessel in the shut room, on the cleared bench. He let me see it.
Dark heavy glass, sloped at the shoulder, with a lute of grey wax over the stopper, cut and pressed back. The grade was stamped low on the side, and I read it with the loupe: Class II, Field Substrate; fidelity Sound; Carry Marked. The grade is the stamp's. My coil gave residue at its floor inside the glass and nothing to set against the stamp. The maker's mark in the glass answers to Furveus on the roll. Exhibit B has him pour it. In the wax over the lute was a ring crossed by three spokes, the lowest longest, with registry letters beneath. It is the mark on Exhibit C. On a contracted lot the consignor's wax goes over the lute, and I have read a hundred such at the river stairs.
I asked what the paper said.
"I will not tell you what she wrote. It was written to me."
He brought the cover sheet from a drawer and held it out by its edges, the broken wax toward me. The same ring and spokes, the same letters under the loupe. He called it her seal. It is a registered craft mark on the roll, and it is also on a letter to a man he has never met.
"It was a letter to me," he said. "Concerning the two of us."
A letter to a husband is seldom sealed under a stranger's registered mark. Either he does not know whose mark it is, or he would rather I thought so. Entered, and not pressed.
My reasoning. Exhibit B has Strom telling the lower room that the ground up in the passes would keep a thing like this a hundred years. This ground is low and wet. Whatever she left in it had a shorter time, and the commission came inside that time.
When I left he was still holding it by the edges.

## Entry the Third · On the Line, and at the University

**Baseline.** Auren's day, late winter, the line toward Urbis, ninth division. Slept three divisions. Hands cold. Appetite poor, which I put down to the inn.
Gauge-housing on a post, Guild seal on its door. The hum dropped a note.
The paper was on her bench before the vessel was poured. She left it there. The commission found it.
Exhibit A sets down the old debt doctrine, the Aphorism of Mortalis: whoever raises the dead borrows the current and cannot step back out of it without a cost to his own Crystal. Exhibit B gives Kwon Mu-jin's gloss on it, and the gloss is his: whoever draws on Mortalis to rebuild what has come apart summons himself, arranged in their image, and calls it resurrection. The commission did what the gloss says. What Furveus built was an impression, and what Cassian Ault received wore his wife's tone and answered to her name.
And the paper was real. So was the wax on it. Draycott's mark sits over the lute of the vessel and on her paper both, and he brokered the commission, so he knew what she meant to do with the eleven days.
The grief was the postal route, and the commission the courier. Exhibit A has Kwon telling Draycott at the café that Mortalis is a gate and carries nobody anywhere. I have found nothing in this case to make him wrong.
What happened in that house let the impression close an account its maker had left open when she stopped. On the evidence of this file only two people knew the paper was there: the woman, and the man whose wax is on it.
Furveus has rooms at the Heralds' Medical University while it examines his results.
Description of record, set against Exhibit A's: broad in the shoulder and soft at the middle; hair the colour of wet rope, greyer at the temples; a physician's black gone brown at the cuffs. His head nodded, fine and fast, and the jaw followed it once he had been talking a while. Grey nail-beds, and pale bands across the nails.
He laughed, and then said, "Everyone who comes through that door reads me before they say good morning. You said good morning. Sit down."
By Exhibit B he has not liked being looked at for some years. With me he was easy, since looking is all I can do.
He gave me the fee without my asking for it: three hundred gold marks, half before the pour and half after. The vessel was poured to hold a turn at the outside. He stayed the first days and came home before the end.
"Ivor stayed," he said. "Ivor always stays."
I asked him what he owed, and he set it out in order, as Exhibit B says he set it out to Kwon.
"I sold a formula I may hold and may not sell. That's the breach. And I'm a Master of the Circle, so whatever my circle closed is mine to answer for. That's the liability."
> Margin, H.: Seen; not referred.
I asked where Draycott could be found.
"No," he said, pleasantly. "Gisli finds people. Let him find you."
When I stood he looked at his hands, front and back.
The scholars call such work necromantic. The law's word is narrower. By the law the Crossing gives it, a necromantic construct holds a body's parting products past their nine days, and nothing in this case held any of them.

I liked him.

## Entry the Fourth · Genesio, the Lower Stacks

**Baseline.** Veyra's day, spring, after the thaw, the Genesio waystation, sixth division. Slept four divisions. Hands even. The pressure at the temples came on inside the first division off the train, harder than Castlefall's.
A day up the line from Castlefall. The waystation's regulator is a bar of Basilithe-cut stone on a post, which rings at one pitch when the clerk strikes it on the hour. I asked to see the freight book, and the clerk said, "On the record," and turned it round for me.
Through three winters, consignments under a Castlefall address to a pass-house above the waystation: render stock, attested, from a chartered house down the line, and ink, reserved stock, under a rendering house's seal. I copied the address.
Two days by hired mule up the passes. The Archivum stands at the top of the archive-town, squat and weathered, with Maelor's edict cut over its door: Nothing is lost, only waiting to be remembered. I copied it into this book and let it stand.
Gauge, the reading floor, eighth division: nine and five marks. The top of Ambient. The floor is cold enough to show breath.
Archivist Dessa Mael has kept the lower stacks longer than I have been alive. Description of record: small, straight-backed, past seventy; white hair scraped into a knot and pinned with a steel pen; a narrow pale face, lined fine round the eyes; the Heralds' grey under a knitted shawl; ink on both forefingers. She brings tea when she judges that a person needs a moment before the next question, and she brought it before the first.
The Necrocursica, she told me, is one of four works of its kind below the gate. Two are generations old. The fourth is about six years older than the Necrocursica, deposited without a name from a postal address in Castlefall.
She checked the address for me. It was registered to Ivor Strom, and it was the address in the freight book.
"He reads in the older hands when he comes in," she said. "He has not come in since the thaw."
The Strom Submission has sat below the gate for about twelve years, reviewed once, by a senior archivist since retired. Dessa Mael read me the note.
> Submission describes a residue of the dead not in the catalogue. It keeps an intent. Recommend escalation to the Research Division for further study.
"Was it escalated?"
Her eyes tightened a very little at the corners, and she let a pause stand. "The recommendation was logged. I cannot confirm it was actioned."
Exhibit B transcribes Draycott's first letter to Kwon, where the fourth residue is "described nowhere I have found in the Heralds' tradition or in the Genesio Archivum." Strom's paper had been in the Genesio Archivum some eight years when that sentence was written, and the letter's postscript carries a message from Strom. Entered.
Ninth division. Ninth division.
> Margin, H.: Twice.
I tried to take it in a useful order. First, log it. Second, acquire clearance. Third, read it. Fourth, show Halveth. Dessa Mael put in for the Strom paper under the same authorisation as the treatise and brought more tea, which I took to mean that the clearance would be granted, slowly.
I also asked, through the Archivum's post, for the treatise's author. The answer came back to the reading floor on my third day, in a clean, even hand.
> The author of a text kept under seal answers for its contents to those whom the seal admits. The seal does not admit you. File me by the title. I did not choose it, but it is exact, and your file will want exactness.
> 
> Scribe to the Genesio Archivum, by order.
Filed by its signature, as asked, and by the file's own rule.
Dessa Mael set the Castlefall scrapings against the Archivum's reference sheets under her own coil. The chapter bench in Urbis had given me Family and Class on both and could not name either ink. She named both.
"Gravetide," she said, of the circle. Of the drop by the bench: "Gravemark."
The reference sheet under her coil gives Gravetide Ink as the Boundary made material: it states where a working reaches and when it ends, in a medium a residual field can be assayed against. It gives Gravemark Ink as the Sealing, which inscribes a name the Continuum registers. The sheet enters Gravemark at the Adept rank of the Index and reserves it at the Expert gate, and notes beside the row that its lawful sources are three and it uses more than the three produce. Two roles of one working, on one floor, in two inks. Whether the hand that used the second stood at the Expert gate, I cannot answer.
Over the sheets she told me one thing more: a record read coarsely is less of a record afterward, and every archivist learns it on the first page she wears through.
The passage below the town is closed to ordinary traffic. At my asking, Dessa Mael stopped three flights up its stair. The protocols put one of a pair outside the radius: the one inside reports, the one outside confirms or contradicts, and the contradiction is the diagnosis.
Gauge, the gate at the foot of the stair, tenth division: twenty-one, climbing while I held it. That is inside an Active Concentration, at its edge. The ladder calls it Freshet. Exhibit A puts the hall at the passage's far end on Riptide, ground where the Veil has begun to give. I did not go down to it. The Archivum's log, read at the same gate once a turn, gives ten at midsummer, eleven in the autumn, fifteen at midwinter, nineteen at the thaw. Ground that is drawn on should fall through a season. This has climbed through three.
Coil and gauge disagreed, the same way at every stand. The coil read a field and the gauge a density, and the two would not reconcile. The Accord's text on Mechanica says the discrepancy is the size of what is being taken, and that finding it requires two instruments and someone who can be bothered to compare them. I compared them at every stand.
Dessa Mael said the brick-stand lamps in the corridor beyond, which Exhibit A says pulse slowly, stopped at midwinter. I did not see them.
My left hand had warmed. It has run warm in dense ground since I was nineteen. Nobody ever calibrated it. I stayed.
A taste came up at the back of my throat, metal and something sweet. Then Dessa Mael was saying Searcher from three flights up, twice.
"You stopped talking," she said. "Fifty breaths. I counted."
I had been reading her the gauge, and I have no memory of stopping.
The protocols count withdrawal as a step of the procedure. I took it. I came up the stair and marked the perimeter from the top, where the gauge gave nine and five marks again.
That night at the inn I wrote a line and struck it.
~~I should finish the letter before I sleep.~~
I have begun no letter.
> Margin, H.: Keep this.
I left the archive-town a day early, and took the mule down in rain with the tins inside my coat and the left glove off.

## Entry the Fifth · Urbis, a Room I Was Not Invited To

**Baseline.** Maelor's day, early summer, Urbis, seventh division. Slept three divisions. The left hand warm; it has not cooled since the stair. Temper short.
The Urbis office keeps the Division's anomaly returns for the Alftian country: practitioners' reports of ground behaving wrongly, each with the Bureau's classification under it. Since the summer they cluster at Genesio and along its supply road. Castlefall and the lowlands show only residue at its floor.
Every Genesio report came back from the Bureau the same way: Wellspring bleed, Anamnesis, no operator indicated. Laid out by place, they stop at the Archivum's lower stair. The Society's rule for it: a bleed has no edge, and a boundary means somebody chose one.
The returns log what practitioners report. Nobody who reads nothing has been asked what the passage stair does to a porter, and until the Fourth entry I had not asked it of myself.
Neros lodges near the Monastery's platform, with his split object-glass on the sill and the Board's seal on the meter by his door. He had not asked me up. His landlady let me in on the warrant, and he did not send me out. Description of record: thin, dark, in a coat a size too large; the left eye turned out a little; ink on the right thumb. Exhibit B says he answers late. He did.
"It moves again," he said. "The same side. Faster since the thaw."
I asked what moved it. He turned his rule end for end on the chart, as Exhibit B has him do with a figure that will not come clean, and took a long time.
"I cannot identify the source."
Correspondence is his word for it in Exhibit B, and Kwon's. Entered as theirs. I laid the Genesio returns beside his column, date against date. Each step in his column comes some turns after a cluster at Genesio. None comes before one.
"Late," Neros said. He did not explain it, and I have not either.
The registry's roll of marks, of which the Division keeps a copy here, confirms the ring and three spokes as Gisli Draycott's. Rank as the roll records it, Expert. His last registered address is three years stale.
My reasoning. Exhibit B's Appendix does not know whether a record of that kind can be woken. At Genesio somebody is waking them, as many as he can reach, with render stock for the working and reserved ink for the seals: Strom, by the freight book and by the address on his paper. A record read coarsely is less of a record afterward, as Dessa Mael has it. The dead's open accounts may be closing by the score. Below, the ground climbs where drawn ground should fall, and above it the Weight steps after, late: one regional field pressed again and again, and I have no instrument for what lies between.
The law has a word for part of it. Output taken from a Crystal that is not the operator's is extraction, and the donor may be dead. That is Mechanica, and by Statute XXVI nothing closes an unsealed boundary. The Enforcement Division prosecutes extraction with a ferocity nobody doubts, and I have marked the extraction for it. For carrying a dead woman's business to its end the law has no instrument yet.
> Margin, H.: Not yet.
I stopped writing at this point for some time. Exhibit B lay open at the page where Kwon writes, "I was clear about what I saw. I had simply decided not to be responsible for it." Then I wrote: Find Gisli Draycott.
Then I went down to Halveth.
She read these notes standing, while I stood across the desk from her, and her face gave me nothing to write down. At the foot of the last page she wrote a line, and after a pause another.
> Margin, H.: Good. Now write the report.
> 
> Margin, H., below, in a slightly different hand: He answered the letter.
I did not know of any letter. Entered.
She pointed at her desk. On it lay a letter folded twice and sealed in grey wax: the ring, the three spokes, the lowest longest. It was addressed to me, care of the Division.

## Exhibit E · A Letter from Gisli Draycott, Transcribed in Full

> To Orin Farrant, Searcher of the Night Watch Society, care of the Research and Archives Division, Urbis.
> 
> Farrant,
> 
> I expected the crown's people some years ago. That they have come at last, by way of a station record in Castlefall that anyone may read, flatters my discretion and says less for how the crown spends its watchers.
> 
> I write because a letter can be read twice. Kwon shares the preference and practises it more thoroughly: he has answered neither of mine.
> 
> The Trace is real. The finding was hers before it was mine. Ivor found a crude half of it on his own, and the Archivum keeps it in a drawer at Genesio. The name is Kwon's. The framing is mine, and I will answer for it.
> 
> Madeleine died holding a decision. She wrote it down, and asked me to seal the paper at her bedside and not to read it while she lived. I did both. She told me she trusted Cassian to open the room. What I built was the means for her impression to finish what she had been doing when she stopped. The seal closes on Return when the sentence is finished, which is why she ended in one breath. Once finished, it is spent; the ground does not keep it twice.
> 
> Let me be exact about the ground. What it keeps are records, as it keeps every other record, and there is no soul among them. Anamnesis reads them, and a careful reading can wake one. What wakes is the last word of a sentence the person did not finish. What Ivor is doing is finishing sentences.
> 
> I have struggled with the ethics of this for six years, since a café in Castlefall where a boy corrected me about a gate. I intended one application, and it was hers. I did not intend Ivor to understand what he watched in that house, write it down, and carry it up the passes. I did not intend the ground at Genesio to begin behaving as a queue.
> 
> I want the work done correctly. Ivor has my curiosity without my care. His activations are coarse, a coarse reading takes what it reads, and the ground up there is giving to a man who does not ask it. I would like him stopped before it gives out, by somebody who understands what he is stopping.
> 
> I know where Ivor is. I know what he has finished. I know which sentence the ground at Genesio has held longest, and I will tell you in person.
> 
> The coffee stall on the Castlefall platform, at the second bell, on the morning after your train comes in from Urbis. I will be the one drinking the terrible coffee.
> 
> G. Draycott
> 
> P.S. Tell him the second was not rhetorical. In his second volume he names Separation Perverted, the Corruption of Non-Commitment, and gives it a spectator soul, "clear-sighted, exact in speech, and excused by its own analysis." I would add that it ends when a man reads himself from outside, and someone should be waiting when he does. I have been waiting.

## Entry the Sixth · Castlefall Platform, Second Bell

**Baseline.** Irath's day, late summer, Castlefall, the morning after the Urbis train. Slept two divisions. The left hand warm, the right cold. Stomach steady. The pressure at the temples since last evening.
Noticed on the line: the postscript to Exhibit E quotes Exhibit B, which the Division withdrew under seal in the season it was deposited. Exhibit B records a fair copy sent out before the seal. Somebody let him read one or the other. Halveth's margin, He answered the letter, sits on the page before it. His letter also says the seal closes on Return when the sentence is finished. I have no instrument that reads a seal. I take it as his.
The chapter's coil-carrier came up with me and took the far end of the platform, the coil at her chest, outside any radius I could guess at. Fog on the cedar and on the rails. Sap had come through the post at the stair head and set in amber beads. At the next stall two carters were arguing whether a drake hatched this year would be Weight-bred, with the Weight out of its station. One said the worst in a generation. The other said no.
The second bell went.
Description of record. A silver token first, on a chain at his collar, the disc turned outward. Then the man: about forty-six, lean; the fair hair greyer than Exhibit A has it and going thin at the crown; a long face shaved close, the skin fine at the eyes; a dead front tooth, grey among the white, which he shows often; a signet on the smallest finger, worn smoother; good grey wool, a coat cut close and brushed. He was already drinking a cup of the terrible coffee.
The bracelet on my left wrist pulled toward him from the top of the platform stair, and it did not stop pulling while I was there.
"Farrant." He looked pleased. "You'll take coffee."
I paid for my own, one copper.
He laid his token face up on the counter between our cups, as the Concord does when it means to be believed, a token being said to darken beside a lie. I asked him how he had my name.
"The station record. Anyone may read it."
The token lay where he had put it and did not change colour. I set down both.
He told me where Ivor Strom is: in the hollow below the archive-town at night, and by day in a pass-house above the Genesio waystation. He told me that works have been finished.
"Real ones. Things people died meaning to do, and they're done, and they're in the world because of him." He turned the signet on his finger. "That's the difficulty, Farrant. He's doing good. Ivor woke things badly for ten years before he ever saw it done well. It took him three winters to find enough ink."
"And the Weight?"
"Ivor calls it tolerable deviation."
I asked what the ground at Genesio has held longest, which his letter promised me in person.
"I'll tell it to the one it's for."
Set against the letter: he promises in writing and hedges at the counter.
He asked whether Kwon had read his letter. I did not answer. It was Kwon's to answer.
"Tell him the second wasn't rhetorical."
"I'll carry the paper," I said. "The man can carry himself."
"Say it again. Slowly."
I did not.
He smiled into the cup. "You're restful to stand beside, Farrant. There's nothing of you in the air."
I asked him about the treatise's author, the one his letter leaves out.
The bracelet slackened. Dread came first, with nothing in front of me to fear. I was on one knee on the cedar before I knew the leg had gone. The boards were wet through the cloth. Sweat stood at my hairline. My stomach turned over, and again. Down the platform a carter sat down on his crate as though somebody had pushed him, and a child began to cry.
The bracelet pulled again, hard. The weight went off me. I could stand.
"I beg your pardon," Draycott said. He had not moved, and his hand lay flat on the counter beside the token.
I enter the slip and nothing about its reason. I have none to enter.
The coil-carrier came down the platform. Her needle had gone over toward the stall at the count I went down, and come back when I stood. The protocols say the contradiction is the diagnosis. There was none. Her coil and my knee agree.
He took a packet from inside his coat, sealed under his mark, and put it into my hand. I did not open it on the platform. It is filed under the Division's seal as Volitional Trace · Genesio · active.
The up train for the waystation came in at the third bell, and he finished his coffee, took his token off the counter, and boarded it.
**After.** Hands cold, both. The taste at the back of the throat, with the coffee over it. Knees steady.
I took the down train.

## Entry the Seventh · Urbis, a Door

**Baseline.** Auren's day, late autumn, Urbis, sixth division. Slept three divisions. Left hand warm. Appetite ordinary.
The report is written and filed under the Division's seal. These notes are no part of it.
Word came to the Archivum inside the turn that Kwon Mu-jin had been brought to Urbis from the Ashgate road on one lung, and lodged in a room with an east window above the square. Halveth released Exhibit C to its addressee the same morning.
> Margin, H.: By hand. Not by post.
I had reached that already, by Exhibit B. He copied Draycott's first letter into his own manuscript and left it there, by his own count, "unanswered through two midwinters with the third coming on." A letter posted to him would sit on his desk. A letter carried to him might sit there too, and he would know whose hand had put it down.
The tram took me across the square for a copper, drawn along the conduit on its low hum. A meter by his street door carried the Board's seal. His stair is narrow and steep.
The bracelet went over toward him hard as the door opened, harder than on the platform, and it stayed there.
Description of record. At his collar, a ranked man's silver token. Then the man: young, twenty-four by the Codex's own count; white-blond hair grown out long over the ears and uncut; a round lens on a thin chain over the left eye, and behind the lens the eye gone white from lid to lid; a cloth binding over the right; the right arm hanging still in its sleeve, the hand not moving; his breath moving one side of his chest and leaving the other still; three fingers of the left hand bound together, dark at the tips, and ink in the creases of the same hand. He held the door frame.
A tired man was holding a great weight down, as if to let a stranger stand in his doorway. I have that from the bracelet and from the platform at Castlefall, and from nothing else.
I held out the letter. "Kwon Mu-jin."
He took it between the thumb and forefinger of the left hand and turned the wax to the window. He held it there a long while: the ring, the three spokes, the lowest longest. He did not open it.
"Who held it?"
"The Division, under the seal on your volumes."
"I did not know they were sealed."
He set the letter on the desk beside a closed book, square to the edge.
I had Draycott's postscript in my mouth. I stood in the doorway with it a moment and did not say it. I had carried the paper. The man can carry himself.
On his stair I wrote the time: the seventh division.

# Who Pays for the Lamp: The Living Crafts

> A working is a thing a person does, and a person wants paying, and may on any given morning decline. Everything else in this chapter is detail.
>
> *Note on the flyleaf of the Southbank rolls, in an archivist's hand*

On the eve of the Midsummer Fair the lamp-wardens of Southbank walked off their rounds over a single copper. The company had taken one more copper in the silver out of the round-fee, and Nan Tallowgate, who had kept Kettle Bridge alight for twenty-six years and whose aura had settled to the deep yellow of a good egg yolk, climbed onto the bridge rail at dusk with three hundred wardens behind her in their soot-blue hoods, sang the first line of the Wardens' Round very badly, and closed her fist. The lamp over her head went out. Then the next one did, and the next, down both banks of the river, the amber and rose and kingfisher green of eleven hundred lamps folding back into the hands that kept them, street after street, a darkness running away from the bridge faster than a horse, and the whole fairground, mouths full, cheered it as though it had been laid on for them.

The Provost had an answer ready, and his answer was Magister Lucan Hale, a man of Master standing whose coat you could read a letter by at forty paces. Hale walked out onto Kettle Bridge in violet silk, raised both arms, and lit all eleven hundred at a stroke.

Southbank went the color of a cathedral window. Nan Tallowgate sat down on the rail, took out a heel of bread and an onion, and waited, because she knew what he had never had to learn. Lighting a lamp is nothing. Keeping one till dawn is the trade. A warden feeds each flame a filament no thicker than a hair, out of ground she has walked since she was twelve, and she knows which lamps on Cooper's Row burn greedy in the wet. Hale was holding eleven hundred filaments out of a single reserve, on thin river ground that could not fill him as fast as he spent. By the second bell of the night his hands shook and the violet had paled to lilac. By the third there was blood down the silk. A little after midnight he went over on his face on Kettle Bridge, and every lamp in the quarter went out together, and in that dark a cart went off Tanner's Stair into the river and a dropped tallow dip took the ropewalk. Nine people were dead by morning, and both sides have been blaming the other for them ever since. The company gave back the copper and added another. The minstrels put the wardens at three thousand and give Nan Tallowgate a sword. Hale's heirs still pay double on that side of the river, and they have stopped asking why.

Hold on to that night, because the whole of this chapter is in it. Magic in a city or a village is done by living hands. Every lamp, every ward and every warm stone has a price, a keeper, an argument, and a person you can hate, and any one of those people may refuse you.

## I. The Trades

Aether was always in the ground and the air, thick in some places and thin in others. **The Long Reckoning discovered nothing here and priced everything.** The cities were worked first and most thoroughly, and their lights, their heat, their clean water and their warded doors are kept by hired people on regular rounds; past the last warden's round the trades thin out, the towns and the wilds still burn oil, and when something goes wrong somebody sends a boy for the hedge-worker.

Ground is graded by the density it carries (Aetheric Density, in the scholar's term), because a warden on rich ground fills fast and works cheap while a warden on thin ground starves and charges for it. The grade is stamped at intervals on the corner-stones in Accord Chancery Hand, so that any searcher in any kingdom reads the same mark, and a map of a city's rents is very nearly a map of its ground.

| Trade | What they do | How they are paid | How they refuse |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lamp-wardens** | Keep street and door lamps, by the round, dusk to dawn | Round-fee from a company or parish; a household by the month | Walk off the round, or simply pass your door |
| **Ward-cutters** | Cut and re-cut wards into lintels, thresholds and strongrooms | By the job, and by the glyph | Leave the last stroke uncut until the balance is settled |
| **Hedge-workers** | Everything in the country: frost off the orchard, a charm on the well, a fever talked down | In kind, usually | It is a long walk to the next one |
| **Core keepers** | Hold a worked Core, which is a dungeon somebody cleared, and sell hours at it | By the hour, the season, or an old obligation | Close it, or stop bleeding the pool |
| **Guild craftsmen** | Smiths, dyers, renderers and engineers of contraptions, under guild mark, up to what their title lets them make | By the piece | Decline the commission, loudly, in front of your rivals |
| **Petty folk arts** | One small power and one small living | A copper here and there | Shut the stall |

**Lamp-wardens.** A street lamp is a glass and a glyph-cut stem, and without a warden it is an ornament. An Essence lantern costs three to eight silver, and the inscription fee on top is the expensive part. Wardens walk at dusk with a boy and a pole, and each lamp burns the color of whoever keeps it, because what it burns is a hair's worth of that person's own Essence, so that an old resident can tell you whose round she is on with her eyes half shut.

**Ward-cutters.** Chisel, chalk and a good eye. A ward is a working inscribed in material, it wears like any other cut, and the tap of a cutter's chisel on a lintel is the most reassuring sound in a poor street and the most alarming one in a rich street, where it means somebody has been robbed. The cutter's mark goes under the ward, and every trace examiner can read it.

**Hedge-workers.** They work by voice and hand in the country beyond any company's bounds, one for every few villages, and most of them have never seen a primer. The schedules forbid taking pay for a working without a license. They have never defined an egg.

**Core keepers.** A Wellspring is a governing idea, an alchemical and spiritual transformation that changes how a soul uses Aether, and in the scholars' account it is one of sixty types of Aether that affect the Soul Crystal differently. A place where one of them pools is a Core in common speech, and left to itself it does not stay a place. **Most Cores lie in [[The Bestiary/Dungeons|dungeons]]**, which grow down in floors around the Core with something guarding the heart, and the rest are said to lie out in vast territories nobody has finished walking. **A worked Core is a dungeon somebody cleared and now holds.** Under every respectable pool in the city is a stair, a seat of old stone, and somebody's great-grandmother's account of what it cost. A worked Core recharges by the season if it is not bled, and it has keepers, who may be an order, a sect, a temple or one stubborn family, and they sell hours at the pool, to anyone who can pay to pull there or to take a Wellspring from it. Half the wardens in a city top up at a Core they do not own. **The keeper can close it**, and when one does, every working in the district that depends on those hours goes thin within the week. A keeper can also simply stop bleeding it, and the place remembers what it was, as the Ferrants of Hollowhay learned at a wedding. The old soldier's version of the same thought: to beat a Core-fed enemy, take the fight off rich ground and onto salted, bled or thin ground, and let him find out what he had been standing on.

**Parish stones** rise at street level: iron-bound, waist-high, warm, and unlovely. A cutter sets one and a warden keeps it charged, and it is the public working point of the street, where you bring a pot to boil and a child to thaw. In a good district it stands in a railed enclosure and is opened by a company man. In a bad one it stands in the road and has been opened by everybody.

**Assay posts** stand wherever one fee-band meets another, each holding a brass fork in a glass case, the fork ringing truer as the ground thickens and the reading going down in a book. A post is brass, slate-backed, locked, and searched quarterly, because a street's fee hangs on its grade. **The lock is the point. The fork is honest, and the case is where the argument about what it said takes place.**

**Guild craftsmen** shout the names of their works and brag about their marks in the street, and their contraptions misfire in funny and terrible ways. Master Hobb's Self-Turning Spit, Perpetual In All Weathers, has twice roasted a cat and once a bailiff, and there is a waiting list.

**Petty folk arts** are everywhere, because about one person in ten wakes a Crystal and very few of those ever climb far. The noodle seller's knife that has never dulled, the laundress who dries a sheet between her palms, the rat-charmer of Pudding Lane who is at war with the rat-charmer of Pudding Lane's other end. Each one costs money, each one has a bad day, and each one can tell you to come back tomorrow.

## II. The Hum

A kept street is audible. The sound is faint and hardly sound at all, but every ward and lamp carries the note of its own Wellspring, and a district thick with them has a floor under it that everybody in it stops hearing by the second week and hears immediately when it stops.

**The hum is the single most reliable orientation instrument a city person has.** They navigate by it without knowing. They sleep in it. Children raised on a kept street find quiet countryside physically difficult and are widely reported to be poor sleepers away from home, which the companies cite as evidence of nothing and the physicians cite as evidence of something.
> **When the wardens walk, the silence arrives before the dark does.** People come to doorways. The companies have known for eighty years that a stoppage produces crowds within four minutes and schedule their own repairs accordingly, which is to say between three and five in the morning. The wardens have known it for eighty years too, and they walk at dusk.

## III. The Fee and the Seal

Every lawful working for hire carries a seal, and the seal is the whole of the trade in one object.

It is a lump of stamped lead on the lamp stem or a mark pressed into the lintel's wax. It says who did the work, what was paid and until when, and it is the property of the company rather than of the householder. **Breaking a seal is a specific offense with its own fine, prosecuted more reliably than assault**, because the seal is the instrument by which the company exists.

**Grades of work:**
- **Sealed household.** Heat, light, warded doorframes, a working stove. Paid by the month, capped, cheap, and adulterated in the sense that the work delivered is below what the fee implies and everyone knows it. Three to five silver a month for one room; eight to twelve for a clerk's house with every doorframe done. It is the largest line in a poor household after rent, and it comes due whether the family has coin that month or not.
- **Trade work.** Stronger, dearer, and searched twice a year: two to five gold a month. Any workshop that cuts, pours or renders hires this.
- **Bonded work.** Uncapped, individually warranted, and issued against a name. Practitioners of standing, Accord premises, and three of the great houses. **A bonded contract is a legal fact about a person before it is a service.**
- **Unsealed.** Illegal, universal, and effected with a stolen glyph and a nerve. The companies call it foreign work. Everyone else calls it **a botch**.
> *A botch stands well clear of Mechanica (forbidden working that takes power without lawful consent). What it robs is a lawful company of its fee, which makes it a magistrate's business. The distinction matters enormously in law and not at all to the man freezing in the room upstairs.*

## IV. The Companies

The Accord, a coalition of guilds across many nations, runs none of this directly. The trades are run by chartered **companies**, one to a trade in each city or quarter, signed on with the Accord for shared rank, enforceable contracts and passage, licensed under its seal, and owned by whoever bought the charter.

They are monopolies. They set fees, they hold the Core rights, they employ the searchers, and they answer to a licensing body that depends on their fees. **The Accord regulates the companies and is funded by them, and this has been raised in council eleven times.**

**The searcher** is the characteristic figure of the whole arrangement: a man with a seal press, an assay fork, a book, and the authority to strike a household from the round, on ten to eighteen gold a month and the gifts nobody records. He is seldom much of a practitioner. He is frequently not liked. He is the only representative of the entire magical apparatus to whom most people will ever have to answer.

## V. Who Can Refuse

Everything on this page is done by hands, so all of it can stop, and everybody in a city lives with that fact the way a sailor lives with weather.

**The stoppage.** Illegal under most charters and common under all of them. Wardens walk a street at a time or a quarter at a time, ward-cutters down chisels with half a gate cut, and a keeper's whole family sits on the steps of the pool eating lunch. Bills go up on doors overnight, hand-copied in bad letters, and criers sell the news of it by the corner. Bookies take odds on how long the company lasts.

**The passed door.** Quieter and meaner. A warden who has been cheated, insulted or merely bored by a house walks past it, and no law requires otherwise until the searcher has been fetched, and the searcher drinks with the wardens. A street can go a year with one dark door and everybody on it knowing why.

**The overcharge.** In a hard winter a warm stone costs what its keeper says it costs. Ask anyone who has paid it.

**The fee strike.** The refusal runs the other way as well. A whole street stops paying, the company sends the snuffers, and the street stands in the road with its sleeves rolled. The great fee strikes have been won by the street exactly as often as the company has been willing to lose its snuffers.

**The answer.** A company on the wrong end of a stoppage has three replies: bring in strangers from another town, who do not know the lamps; pay; or hire somebody grand. The Southbank rolls record what the third one costs, and yet some provost tries it every decade, because the grand somebody always swears he can hold it.

## VI. Bad Work

Work delivered below the fee is the ordinary condition of poor districts, and it has consequences that are physiological rather than merely commercial.

**Residue accumulates where the work is cheap and the ground is thin.** Every working leaves Aetheric Residue (the trace left in a place by what was done there), and residue thins on a schedule set by local density, so thin ground clears slowest of all. A crowded quarter relit every night by the cheapest hands available, at poor η (the share of an expenditure that arrives as effect) and with the waste going off hot into the walls, never finishes clearing one night's leavings before the next night's arrive. Residue is a persistent alteration to the field of the place it sits in. The affected streets read wrong on any instrument and read wrong to the people living in them, who describe it as a heaviness and are told it is damp.

**Over a generation it goes like this.** For ten years, nothing anyone would swear to. Then an apprentice's ward cut over an old ward cut over an older one starts failing in two years instead of ten, so it is cut again, cheaper, on top. The lamps on the stair burn a shade off their keeper's color. Milk turns early, dogs will not settle, and the street's old people start saying the street has gone heavy. Thirty years on the grandchildren are waking at the wrong ages or not at all, one family on one staircase has something strange it passes down, and rent has fallen far enough that only the poorest come, who can only afford the cheapest work.

**The documented effects** are unspectacular and cumulative: poor sleep, low-grade Gnosis (perception) noise in anyone sensitive, Crystal awakening (the soul itself opening) at abnormal ages in children, and a Trait (a standing law carried in the soul) incidence in long-settled families that the companies have never commissioned a study of. **The Physicians' College has requested one four times.**
> **The Southbank finding, and what happened to it.** A company surveyor established in a single quarter that residue concentration tracked tenement density and inversely tracked fee band, and submitted it.
>
> *The report was accepted, the surveyor was promoted, and the recommendation was referred to a committee that has met twice.* **Nobody suppressed the finding. It is available. It has simply never been the business of anybody with the authority to act on it.**

Two Measurewrights of my acquaintance dispute whether the heaviness comes from the residue at all or from a lifetime of being told it is damp. They have been disputing it for nineteen years, in the same tavern, on a thin-ground street, and neither sleeps well.

## VII. Bolt and Proof

The crossbow is ordinary and old, and it is the reason nobody sensible has ever stopped wearing armor.

**A bolt is cheap. A working is not.** A bolt costs three coppers at the fletcher's and a practitioner's expenditure is pulled against a reserve that took years to build, so the economics of an engagement between the two never favored the practitioner and still do not. It is read by Speed Grade like any projectile. A fighter at Grade D "can read a crossbow bolt loosed across a room and slip it, if they see the loose", and that last clause has buried more proud people than any Domain.

**Proofed harness** is armor tested by loosing at it and marked with the result. **The proof-mark is the whole trade**: a stamp naming the house, the bow tested, and the range. Harness proofed against a hand-spanned bow at twenty paces carries a different mark from harness proofed against a windlass arbalest at fifty, and the marks are forged constantly.
> *A proof-mark is a promise about a specific bow at a specific range, made by a workshop that may no longer exist, on a piece of steel that has since been repaired twice. Every soldier who has bought his own harness knows this and buys anyway, because the alternative is buying nothing.*

## VIII. Road, Beacon and the Rest

**The kept road** is a street that does not stop. Ward-cutters do its bridges, road-wardens its waystones, and a waystation's stones are cut in tuned pairs: the warden fills one in the station, and its twin under a carrier's cart-bed takes the heat across a hand's breadth of air by Transference (a Wellspring of Fate, whose law binds two tuned things so that energy passes between them without contact) and burns nothing. **The road is the reason the companies matter beyond their own walls**: every town it reaches is a town on somebody's fee, and a company with a road through its country negotiates with every waystation on it.

**The beacon line.** Hilltop fire-towers in sight of one another, lit by wardens in shuttered colors, Judicium's arc-blue against Pyreveil's ember-orange, and read by clerks whose Crystals have never woken. **The beacon line is the first apparatus in the world's history that lets an ordinary person send something faster than a horse**, and its cultural effect substantially exceeds its technical one. It is a Guild line at a Guild price, a silver for twenty words with the addresses counted, carrying information as a licensed commodity, and the Accord guards it in the tone of an institution that knows exactly what it would lose. Everybody else has relay riders, ships, heralds, minstrels who get the details wrong, and hand-copied bills posted on doors.

**Workshops** run on trade work, raise no chimney, and employ a great many people who have never woken to operate processes that are magic from end to end. *A dye works uses Ignivale at workshop scale by having thirty men each perform one licensed step badly.*

## IX. The People Who Tend It

**Core-tenders** work the lip of a site, in pairs, with an assay fork and a lamp, clearing and bleeding the pool, in what used to be a dungeon and would like to be one again. The work is wet, cramped, and pays badly, and the residue exposure is continuous and undocumented. The ones who live longest learn to mention to someone when a gallery they know has moved.

**Seal-clerks** press and record. **Fee-walkers** walk. **Snuffers** attend when a household stops paying, strip its lamps and scratch through its wards, and are the most despised trade in any city and the best paid of the four.
> **None of these people are screened.** Core-tenders spend careers at the edge of a worked pool, at densities that would be recorded as an Active Concentration (a density that alters bodies over time) if a surveyor found the same reading in open country, and no baseline is taken because no baseline exists for the unawakened.
>
> *The trade knows what it does to a man over twenty years. It has a word for the late stage, the word is in no company document, and a physician who has never worked a pool will diagnose it as something else with perfect confidence.*

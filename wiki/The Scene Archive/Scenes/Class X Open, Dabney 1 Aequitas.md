*Aetherion Academy. The Crucible preparation chamber, the first morning of the Class X Open Tournament.*

The whetstone goes down the edge of Aequitas in one long pull from the heel to the point, and the sound it makes is the sound a gull makes a long way out over water, thin and rising and then nothing. Dabney lifts the stone, wets it from the tin cup between his feet and sets it back at the heel. Same angle. Same weight behind it. This is the seventh pass on the left side. He will make nine on each side, because nine is the count Darius gave him at eight and he has never found a reason good enough to change it.

He has the end of the bench nearest the wall, under a lamp bracket nobody has filled, where the stone is cold enough through the seat of his trousers to keep him awake. His boots are under the bench with the laces tucked into them. Nobody is looking at this end of the room, and so his feet are bare and flat on the floor, and every time the ward-cycle comes up through the stone he feels it arrive in his heels before he hears it: a dull push, like a bell struck in the next street, going up through his shins and into the bench and out of the top of his head. The chamber smells of liniment and lamp oil and the white chalk the wardens use to draw boundary lines, and of wool that has been sweated in since dawn. People are tightening wraps that are already tight. Somewhere behind him a mother is redoing a braid for the third time and saying the same four words each time she starts. By the far gate a boy he does not know is being sick very neatly into a fire bucket, and a proctor in a grey Academy coat stands beside him holding the rim of the bucket with two fingers and reading a slate in the other hand.

Dabney has nothing but his preparation. He knows to the ounce how much that is. In a room like this it is worth everything or nothing, and which one depends entirely on who is standing across the floor when the gate lifts.

Of everyone in Class X he is the one nobody knows. Nobody on the benches has heard his name. No house stands behind it that anyone here could recite, no banner, no father in the tiers. He settled seven days ago on fighting at half, because half will look like a talented thirteen-year-old with good instruction behind him, and nobody will start the other conversation. The plan is sound. He has checked it from every side. It still sits under his breastbone like a swallowed stone.

He has long suspected that the rest of him was hidden, and that it was his uncle who hid it. He has never once put his finger on it. It lives in the shape of what Edward left unsaid. Eleven times in four years Edward asked him about a thing he could not remember, each time a different way, as though a different knock might open the same door, and after every one of those questions Edward wore the same look: the look of a man who has added a column twice, got the same figure twice, and does not intend to write it down. Edward Lambert counts everything. Dabney has watched him count grain and men and the days between a lord's promise and a lord's delivery. He has never heard Edward count him out loud.

*He knows the sum,* Dabney thinks, and brings the stone down again. *He won't show me the working.*

The stone catches near the point. A burr, small enough to feel and not to see. He turns the blade a hair toward the light, and it is not the light of this room that he sees on the steel.

---

He was seven, and it was early, and the gulls were already arguing on the roof ridge of the chapter house.

The yard behind it was paved in the white stone the whole Palatinate is built from, worn to a shine along the lines where four hundred years of novices had walked their forms, and in the middle of it stood an old stone basin with a hand's depth of rainwater in it and one plane leaf turning on the water in slow circles. Darius had told him to stop the leaf without touching it, and Dabney had not needed the second half explained. He knelt at the lip of the basin with his chin almost on the stone and found the leaf the other way, the way he had always been able to find things, by the strands that ran out of it into everything else. Into the water. Into the wind coming over the wall. Into the basin, the yard, the gull that had just lifted off the ridge. He found the strand that was turning the leaf, fine and taut and humming under his attention like a plucked hair, and he took hold of it with the part of him that had always known how.

The leaf stopped, and the water kept the turning for it. It came up the side of the basin in one solid slab and went over the lip and soaked him from collar to knees, and on the roof every gull went up at once, screaming, and inside the chapter house a door that had been bolted for the night came open on its own and banged against the wall. When the water settled, the leaf sat dead still in the middle of a basin that was nearly empty.

Darius did not hurry across the yard. He never hurried anywhere. He came with the slight forward lean of a man whose lower back had held opinions since before the boy was born, white hair cropped as short as a novice's, one shoulder riding a little lower than the other and staying there. The morning around him went whiter at its edges as he came, the way it always did, as though the yard were being lit for him a moment before he reached it. He looked at the basin. He looked at Dabney, who was dripping.

"No," Darius said.

Dabney wiped his face with a wet sleeve, which did nothing. "It stopped."

"It stopped. You emptied the basin to stop it, you put up every bird in the quarter, and in about a minute somebody is going to come into this yard wanting to know who opened the bursar's door." He lowered himself into a crouch, one hand on his knee to take the weight, until his eyes were level with the boy's. They were pale, those eyes, and very patient, and they did not blink much. "You are trying to control the flow of things instead of letting it flow through you."

"I could see it, though. The one that was turning it."

"I know you could. You see it better than I ever will." He said it the way he said everything, flatly, like a line entered in a book. "Fate is a web, Dabney, and you are going to spend your life with your hands in it. Pull one strand and three others answer. Push one and something a street away leans to take the weight. You took hold of the strand that was turning the leaf and you held it still, and everything that strand had been carrying still had to go somewhere. So it went into the water, and the water went into you." He tipped his chin at the soaked wool. "The art is to learn what each strand is doing before you touch it, and then to work with what it is already doing. That is Hataraki. The Works. It is labour first and power after. Do not let anyone teach you those two words in the other order."

Dabney looked at the leaf. It had started, very slowly, to turn again.

"There is a second thing." Darius's voice did not change, but he leaned in a little, and the white at the edges of the morning leaned with him. "You were born holding your Judgement. You look at a thing and you know what it weighs, the way other children know a tune after one hearing, and you never have to think about it at all. You will lean on that, because it has never once failed you. And one day you will meet a man who cannot weigh anything at all, who has spent thirty years learning nothing but how to swing a bill-hook, and he will cut you in half while you are still reading him." He let that sit long enough for the boy to see it. "You will never be strong if Judgement is all you have. You must build skills beyond it. Forms. Feet. The edge. You must find a balance in all things, in everything you approach, from the way you breathe to the way you act."

"The way I breathe," Dabney repeated.

"Breathe in."

He did.

"You took more than you needed, because you were cold and you wanted the cold gone. Now out." Dabney let it go. "And you gave it all back at once, because holding it was uncomfortable. There. The whole lesson, in one mouthful of air." Darius almost smiled. On him it looked like a man remembering where he had left something. "Neutrality is the true path of the Judger. It is the true way of power. Lean too far into control and you fall into obsession: the hand that cannot open, the strand you will not let go of because you have decided it belongs to you. Lean too far into attraction and you grow dependent on what you are bound to, until the thing you love is the thing that moves you, and anyone who takes hold of it has taken hold of you."

He stood. It took him a moment, and his breath went out short through his nose at the top of it. He went to the rack by the wall and came back with two wooden practice swords, and on the wet stone in front of the boy he leaned them together tip to tip so that they made a peak, and took his hands away. They stood.

"A man once put me in the ground up to my ankles to teach me this," he said, "and I argued with him for the better part of a month, because I was sixty years old and very clever. What is holding those up?"

Dabney studied them with his whole face. "Each other."

"Each of them is falling. Neither can fall, because the other is in its road. That is the whole of an arch, and every cathedral in this city is standing on it." He touched the place where the two tips met with one finger, very lightly. "The meeting place is no stronger than either sword. It lets both of them push through it, and so it stands. A man who steps between two things with his hands out and holds them apart is doing something else. Men do that for years, and everyone calls it wisdom. Then one day their arms come down." He took the finger away, and the swords stayed up on the wet stone as though nobody had touched them. "You must become the scale."

Dabney looked from the swords to his teacher and back. "Which side do I stand on?"

"Neither."

"Then where."

"Where they meet," Darius said. Across the yard the door to the chapter house opened properly, and a man in a bursar's black came out into the morning with his keys in his fist, and Darius turned to go and tell him it had been the wind.

---

A young Dabney had no idea what his master meant. The one sitting on this bench at thirteen, with a sword across his knees and his bare feet on a floor that thrums every half-minute, still does not.

He has turned it over for six years the way he turns the whetstone, looking for the face that will cut. Become the scale. A scale holds two things in its pans and tells you exactly what each one weighs and does not mind in the least which way it tips. It weighs a loaf. It weighs a murder. It hands you the figure and has no opinion. To be master of all things, set in the middle of everything, judging each thing for what it is, good or evil, without leaning: he has tried to picture a person who could stand there, and every time the picture comes out as something he would not want to sit at a table with. *Is that even a person,* he thinks. *Or is it a thing you hang in a doorway.*

He has read enough of Darius's books to know the Work of Balance has a rot in it. The old commentaries call it fairness gone to stone: the scale that will not tip because tipping would be choosing, the arbiter who lets a man drown because pulling him out would mean taking the side of the man over the river. Paralysis that calls itself justice. He thinks of the two practice swords leaning on the wet stone, and of what Darius said about arms coming down, and he cannot make the two pictures agree. A scale that never tips is only a man standing still with his hands out.

At five he asked whether there was a way to be more careful, and they told him to slow down, and he told them that was not what he had asked. Nobody had an answer then. He has believed ever since that care is a place a person reaches by trying harder. Becoming the scale sounds like the opposite of trying. It sounds like getting out of the way.

He works the burr off the point with three short strokes, the stone held almost flat. It hisses. Across the chamber the proctor with the slate has given the boy back his bucket and is reading names now in a voice pitched to carry over the noise, two at a time, and the families nearest the far gate go quiet a little before each pair as though quiet could change the order. Dabney listens for his own name with one ear and keeps the other on the edge.

It is not his name. He turns the blade over and starts the right side. One.

---

They forged Aequitas in his second year, at the long bench in the back of Darius's workroom where the old man did his calcining. Dabney remembers the smell of that room better than the work: hot ash and alum, the iron tang of a crucible that has been used for forty years, and Darius's coat hanging on the door with the sea still in its wool.

There was no ingot. Darius had cleared the bench to bare wood and set a single iron nail in the middle of it.

"Find your own," he had said. "The one that comes out of your chest and goes on into the morning. You have looked at everyone else's. Look at that one."

It was harder than the leaf. Every strand in the world was easier to see than the one he stood on, the way your own nose is the one thing in your face you never look at. When he found it at last it was warm, and it went out of him and away through the wall into a distance that did not end, and when he closed his hand on it the whole of him closed with it, so that for a moment he could not tell which of them he was holding. Darius told him to draw it, and he drew. A length of it came out of the air the way a hair comes out of a comb, and it hurt in a place he had no name for, low under the breastbone, an ache like being homesick for a room he was standing in.

Darius laid both hands flat on the bench on either side of it. The light went white at its edges. Dabney felt the old man's working take hold, a weight settling into the wood like a man sitting down in a chair he means to stay in, and the strand stopped trembling. Fixatio, Darius told him afterward: anchor and seal, the first thing he was ever good at. It gave the thread a place to be. The body was Dabney's to give.

He pushed himself into it until there was nothing left in his hands to push. He remembers the thread gathering, the way frost comes onto a cold pane out of nothing you can see, onto the one place cold enough to take it. Silver first, a fine down of it along the strand. Then thicker. Then a line that was a ridge, and a ridge that was an edge, and by the time the lamp needed trimming there was a longsword lying on Darius's bench in the space where a single thread had been. Dabney slept through the rest of that day and all of the night after it. When he woke, Darius was asleep upright in the chair beside his bed with the sword across his knees.

It takes the stone like steel. It rusts like steel if he is careless with it, and the armourer at the Academy gate turned it over in his hands on the first day and found nothing to say about it but the balance. But it runs a little warmer than the room. When Dabney closes his hand on the grip, the faint grain that runs down the fuller lies flat, the way Astro's fur lies down under a palm. When he is frightened, the whetstone's note goes up. It is going up now.

Darius had named it the morning he woke. He had been holding it up to the window, turning it so the grey light ran along the edge.

"Aequitas," he said.

"What does it mean?"

"Aequus is the old word for level ground. A field flat enough that a ball set down on it stays exactly where you put it." Darius lowered the blade. "Aequitas is the condition of being level. The courts took the word later and made it mean fairness, and fairness is a word for a feeling. Make it mean the first thing." He turned the sword and held it out hilt first. "Level scales. A balance at rest. A true ledger, every debit with its credit and nothing on the page that is not in the vault. Your uncle will tell you that is the only kind of book worth keeping, and on that subject your uncle is right."

Dabney took the grip. It was too big for his hand that year. It fits now.

He understands one part of the name better than the rest. The blade was drawn out of his own thread, and the thread still runs in it, on past the point and out of the steel the way it runs out of him, away into the morning, into a distance that holds what he is and also everything he might yet be. Darius called that the true shape of a thing: the shape of all its possibilities at once, held level, with nothing yet tipped. He has always suspected that was the part Darius meant most.

---

Nine on the right. He lays the stone down on the bench and dries the edge with the cloth from his belt.

There is a grey hair on his sleeve, one of Astro's, long and fine, left over from this morning when the cat sat on his folded kit and refused to be moved. He picks it off, lays it across the edge near the point and draws the blade a finger's width toward him. The hair parts without a sound. The two halves drift down and land beside his bare foot.

*Half,* he thinks. *A talented boy with good instruction.* And under it, in Darius's voice, the line the old man gave him on the training floor and in the corridor and over the supper table until it stopped being a lesson and became the shape of how he sits in a chair: *Never show anyone your true power.*

He slides Aequitas into the scabbard. The grain lies flat under his palm. The ward-cycle comes up through the floor into his heels, and the proctor reads two more names, and neither of them is his.

Dabney puts his hands on his knee, one over the other, and waits.

*Aetherion Academy. The Crucible Field, the opening match of the Class X Open Tournament. The third exchange.*

The chain rings.

It rings at the first joint, faint and clear, the struck bell under water, and Dabney feels it the way a fisherman feels a tug come up a line into his palm. Then it rings again at the second. And the third. Each face of the box sounds once as the light reaches it, and each sound is a small death: he feels the light end, cleanly, exactly as his ground requires, and in the same instant feels something identical begin on the far side of the joint with nothing having crossed between. The ringing walks down the chain toward him link by link, a stammer of violet flashes moving through the silver like a bead being passed hand to hand along a rope.

*Every ending can be an origin.*

He hears it go into every tier at the same polite weight as his own voice, and something in him that is not fear, that is very close to delight, sits up. It is a lawful answer. He can find no flaw in it to put on a scale. The other boy did not fight the Ruin. He let the box do its work and stood an origin behind every ending. Darius would have tapped the brick and said nothing for a long time, which was the highest praise the old man had.

Then the figure at the mouth of the box comes apart.

He sees it go from the feet upward, a perfect boy dissolving into pale violet motes, and he feels the arena's recognition lurch as one Taesyn Valorin stops existing without ever having died. For half a heartbeat the field has no Taesyn in it at all. In the next half, it has one again, from an angle he was not watching, and this one is crossing his ground.

He feels it the way you feel a stone dropped in a still pond from the other side of the pond. A body is in the box. It is moving through the joints without a single borrowed thread on it: no road drawn from outside, no floor nailed to the arena, no frame hung from anywhere but itself. Every joint it crosses ripples. He can feel the small wrongness each crossing makes, the drop in the stomach his ground imposes on plain matter, repeated, repeated, at a running pace. The boy has stripped himself down to the one thing Chains of Heaven lets through, flesh carrying its own cohesion, and poured it through the cells the way water goes through a sieve: no shape to catch on, no edge to break, only a body flowing toward him through broken space.

It is an interesting way to counter him. It is the first time in his life anyone has read his law back to him and used it.

Dabney's eyes go hot.

---

He did not ask them to. That is the first thing he knows, and it frightens him more than the boy in the box. The heat starts at the back of each eye, at the place where the eye meets the dark behind it, and comes forward through the colour from the inner ring outward, the way warmth comes through a cup when you pour into it. Brown goes to amber. Amber goes to gold. Inside the gold, in each iris, a finer ring begins to turn, slow, steady, like the second hand of a clock seen through water.

He has felt this four times before in his life that he remembers, and Edward has asked him about it more than four times, and nobody has ever told him what it is. He has a name for it only because he gave it one, alone, at eleven, out of a book of Darius's about the stars: **the Meridian Eye.** In the old almanacs the Balancer's sign in the sky is a pair of stars turning round one another in perfect harmony, one golden and one blue, and astronomers call such a pair a meridian pair because they cross the sky's high line together. His eyes go gold. They have never gone blue. He has wondered, privately, more often than he would admit, where the other star is.

It runs whether he wants it to or not. He has learned that much about it. It is written somewhere under the part of him that makes decisions, and when the ground under him turns serious enough, it opens. It is opening now, in front of every face in the dark ring. There goes the half, he thinks, very clearly, and then there is no room left to think it.

The world goes onto a graph.

That is the only way he has ever been able to say it. Every possibility in front of him stands up out of the air as a point of light, a dot, the way the surveyors' tables mark their measurements on squared paper: here, and here, and here, scattered. A thousand dots, ten thousand, each one a thing that could happen in the next breath, each one hung at the height of how likely it is and how soon. They are a mess. They look like the stars on a clear night look to someone who has never been shown the constellations.

Then they smooth.

He has watched the Academy's measurewrights do it on a slate with chalk, the trick the Concord geodesists use to find the true shape of a coastline out of a hundred bad surveys: put down every measurement, every error, every disagreement, and draw the one line that passes nearest to all of them at once, so that the sum of every point's distance from it is as small as it can be made. The line of best fit. It belongs to no single measurement; it is what all of them, together, mean. In his gold eyes the dots of possibility pull toward their line the same way, the outliers fading, the scatter thinning, until out of the whole star-field of what might happen there runs one bright line of what will.

Light does the same thing, and so does everything that moves. Every point a wave touches sends out its own small wave in every direction, and nearly all of them cancel one another out, and the only place they all agree is along the single path the light actually takes. The Concord opticians have had the rule two hundred years. The old Order mathematicians had a grander version: a thing that moves takes the path along which the cost of moving is as still as a pond, the place where every small change to the road costs nothing extra. Nature lays down every possible road at once and walks the one where all the others agree.

His eye shows him the agreement.

Everything goes surreal. The sound of the stadium draws away to the far end of a long corridor. The lamp-light over the Crucible thickens to the colour of honey held up to a window. The ringing of the chain slows to one note every age, each ring hanging in the air long enough for him to count its overtones. His own heart, which he would have sworn a moment ago was going like a bird's, beats once, deep and slow and loud, like somebody knocking on a cellar door.

The graph is not only the boy. It is everything, all at once, and for one long breath he cannot keep the rest of it out. Up on the east stair the almond-vendor's tray has a line running off its edge where a twist of sugared almonds is about to tip and fall, and it will fall; he sees it fall before it moves. The referee at the silver-red line has a fan of lines out of his right hand, most of them dim, one of them brighter than the rest, the one where the hand comes up to stop the match if a body goes down. Somebody in the third tier is about to sneeze. The whole Crucible is a field of scatter, ten thousand small futures each finding its line, and none of them knows it is being watched. It is the most beautiful thing he has ever seen, and he has seen it four times, and every time it frightens him a little more, because a boy who can see all this is a boy who will one day be asked what he means to do about it.

He pulls his eyes in, the way Darius taught him to pull in a wandering point, until only the ground in front of him is lit.

And the boy in the box comes up on the graph.

His eyes cannot find a body among the glitter of the joints, the thousand thin silver edges throwing the lamp-light back in pieces and breaking every shape behind them into fragments. The boy is using that. He is using the box as cover the way a deer uses a birch wood. But the graph does not need eyes. On the graph, the boy is a dense knot of points moving toward the box's mouth at an angle, low, and out of the knot runs a line, the line of what that body is about to do with its right hand.

It is a punch.

The line is plain, almost humble: a straight blow from the right, the hips turning under it, the left fist tight against the jaw, aimed at his middle. He reads the line and feels something like relief, and then the line does a thing he has never seen a line do.

It splits.

A hand's width short of where his body will be, the one line becomes two, both bright, both whole, both the best fit through their own scatter. On one, the fist lands. On the other, the fist stops a handspan short and lands anyway, the force arriving at the place in front of the knuckles without the knuckles ever crossing the last of the distance. Neither line is the outlier. The graph cannot choose between them because the boy has made sure there is nothing to choose: both are true, equally, at the same time, and the force of the blow has been assigned to the space where they meet.

He understands it the way he understood the light that had weight and nothing to weigh. The same author. The same signature in the work. Two truths held level so that a third can stand on them.

And behind him, at the mouth of the box, the last ring of the chain sounds, and the light that died and began again all the way down his ground comes out of the last joint as something new. It is no longer a marble. It is a sheet, thin as a wafer of pastry, violet-white, standing upright in the air in front of his chest and spreading sideways as it comes, a broad, flat, patient pressure meant to fill the whole of his front.

Two things, from two directions. One wide, to take the room he would move into. One narrow, at his middle, with its force already waiting in the space where his body is about to be.

His mind, the part of him that reads and weighs and decides, would need a long time to do anything about either of them. Half a second to see. Another half to choose. Longer to move. A trained boy's straight punch crosses its distance in the time it takes to blink. His mind is too slow, and it knows it, and it gives up with a strange, quiet grace, the way a man lets go of a rope he cannot hold.

Something else does not give up.

---

Darius called the thing that happens next by no name at all. He saw it once, when Dabney was nine and a stack of practice staves came down off the rack in the yard, a dozen heavy ash poles falling at once onto the place where a boy was standing, and the boy was not standing there when they landed. He was a pace to the left, crouched, one stave caught in his hand. He could not have said how. Darius had looked at him a long time and then said only: "Your body did not ask you."

Dabney named it himself, later, the way he named the eye: **the Greater Hand.**

It closes on him now.

It comes up from the Essence, from the deep store under the plates, the place the Letting drew from and that is still raw and open from it. It comes up as a heat behind the breastbone and a cold in the hands, a surge he can feel in his blood like a tide coming in under a pier, his heart thudding once more, hard, his pupils opening wide in the gold, the skin of his face flushing hot. It comes up into his body along threads.

He can see them, the way he saw the strands of the leaf on the water. Fine bright filaments run out of him in every direction, the threads every living thing has into the Plane of Fate, the lines that tie a body to everything it might do. He has looked at other people's threads all his life. He has almost never been able to look at his own. Now, with the gold on, he can see where they attach: not to his limbs, and not to his skin, but deeper, at the root of every movement he has ever made, at the place in the spine where the nerves that drive the muscles begin. The places a puppeteer would tie his strings, if a puppeteer understood anatomy.

The Greater Hand takes up the strings.

It is his. He knows that with absolute certainty, the way you know your own handwriting. It is the whole of him, the part that is bigger than the boy on the bench, the essence that sat under seven years of Temperance like a river under ice. It has reached up past the slow small mind that usually runs his body and taken the body in its own hands. It moves him the way his mind cannot, at the speed of the threads, which is the speed of the line on the graph, which is the speed of what will happen. A reflex at the knee jumps before the brain has heard of the tap, because the order is given in the spine and never goes up to be approved. The Greater Hand gives its orders lower than that. It gives them at the root.

And it needs a body that can be moved that fast. A body held together by intention is stiff. A body that braces, that guards, that holds a shape, is a body that can be struck, because a held shape is a fixed target. So the Hand does the thing Dabney has never done in front of anyone, and has only done once in his life before, alone in the yard at night, and been sick afterward.

He thins.

**Dilutio**, Darius would have called it, if he had ever seen it, because Darius gave everything a word in the old tongue sooner or later. The thinning. The Greater Hand reaches into the binding that holds the body to its own intended shape, the frame of will that makes a body a single held thing, and dilutes it, the way a physician dilutes a tincture until each drop of the water carries almost nothing of the original and every drop is free. The body does not come apart. Its matter is its matter; its cohesion is its own. But the single will that holds it in one shape lets go, part by part, and every part of him, every grain of muscle and bone and skin, is left answering to its own thread instead.

Each grain becomes a point that asks the Plane of Fate, again and again and again, where it should be.

It is a recursion. The question is asked, answered, and asked again of the answer, faster than any thought: the threads report the line, the grain moves to the line, the line moves, the threads report it, the grain moves again. A steam engine's governor does the same thing, two iron balls on arms spinning on a shaft, flying out when the engine runs fast and closing the throttle, falling in when it slows and opening it, every instant, so that the engine never runs away and never stalls. A Concord natural philosopher wrote out the mathematics of it a generation ago, and every mill in the Accord turns on his paper. Every grain of Dabney is a governor now. Every grain is asking, answering, adjusting, and in between each asking and the next, the possibilities that each grain will not take are ended.

That is where the Ruin is. He feels it in himself with a little shock, the same ash-and-iron taste still at the back of his tongue from the box. The Work of Ruins is still in him, its warrant still open. At every one of the thousand thousand points the Hand has made of him, there is a tiny rupture, a breaking-off, as the possibilities the line rejects are cut away before they can become anything at all. Every possible version of him that would stand where the fist is going is ended at the root, so small and so quick it passes for weather, and the only versions left are the ones on the line.

The body goes fluid.

There is no other word. A solid holds a shape and meets a blow with the shape, and if the blow is harder than the shape, the shape breaks. A fluid has no shape to hold. It does not resist a push sideways at all; it parts, and flows round, and closes again behind. Push your hand into a still pool and the water does not fight you. It is simply no longer where your hand is.

Dabney is no longer where the fist is going.

He does not react to where his body is; his body is not where he is any more. He reacts to where his alignment is, where the line on the graph says the next breath of him will be, and the Hand puts him there, grain by grain, faster than he can follow. In his gold eye, every variation of the punch comes out at him as a loop: a bright curve thrown out from the boy's hip, round through the space in front of his stomach, and back. A thousand loops. Every possible punch the boy could throw in that instant drawn at once, each one crystallising out of the scatter onto the plane of the physical world just long enough to be real and then folding back into the others. Lands. Lands a handspan short. Lands high. Lands low. Lands and doesn't. They loop through the place where his body was and find it full of nothing.

To say his body moves without thought would be an understatement. His body moves the way a marionette moves when the hand above it is better than any hand in the world, every joint drawn by its own string, nothing wasted, nothing braced. His right hip folds back. His spine turns around its own axis, the ribs following the hips, the shoulders following the ribs, a twist that runs all the way up him. His weight drops into his left heel and rolls along the foot. His head stays perfectly still, and the whole of him strings away from the line of the fist like a reed bending away from a current.

Almost all of him.

The paradox has two lines, and the Hand can put him off one of them entirely and off the other almost entirely, and there is no third line to stand on. The force arrives in the space where the two truths meet, and his right side is still turning through the edge of that space when it lands.

It grazes him.

He feels it the way you feel a cart wheel go past too close in a narrow street: a blunt hot shove along the right side of his body, low, a hand below the armpit and a hand above the hip. It catches the lower ribs where they come round toward the back, the ninth and the tenth and the floating eleventh, and the thick slab of muscle that wraps the flank there, the long one that runs from the ribs down to the hip and turns the body. It lands askew and skids. The ribs flex in under it and spring back, and the muscle over them takes the rest and bruises deep, a spreading ache that comes up hot a heartbeat after the blow and then settles to a throb. Nothing breaks. He knows the feel of a cracked rib from a fall off the yard wall at ten, the stab of it on every breath. What he has now is a deep bruise along the side, the kind that goes yellow-black over a week, with a long scrape of skin under his shirt where the cloth dragged. No blood worth the name. A physician would write it down as a contusion of the right flank and tell him to breathe deep twice an hour so his lung did not take a chill.

And the turn that took him off the line has brought his sword round with it.

He has held Aequitas low in front of him since he cut the fence, both hands on the grip, the point toward the floor. The turn has swung it. The Hand does not let go of the sword any more than it lets go of his feet: the threads run down through his wrists and into the grip and, he realises with a jolt of something like joy, on into the steel, because the steel is his thread too, drawn out of the morning in Darius's workroom and gathered into a blade. The sword turns with him like part of his arm, and the turn sets the flat of it square across the face of the sheet of violet-white force coming into his front.

They meet.

The sheet presses. It has been storing every pull his weight ever put on it, through every death and every beginning, and now it spends all of it at once against the first thing in its road, spreading sideways across the flat of the blade as if it meant to wrap him. The force of it goes into the steel.

And the steel gives it back.

This is the one thing in him he has never had to learn. It was there before the Crystal opened. As a small boy he could not be pushed over. Bigger boys in the Palatinate lanes tried, once each, and found themselves sitting down without knowing how, and nobody thought anything of it except that he had good balance. Edward thought it was posture. It was a law. **Equipoise**, he calls it, because the book of Darius's that had the word in it said it meant a weight that exactly balances another. Every force that meets him finds its equal. Push on a wall and the wall pushes back exactly as hard, and that is true of every wall and every push in the world, the law the Concord engineers carve over the doors of their bridge schools. It is true of him in a way it is not quite true of other people. When something pushes on him, its equal arrives from him at the same instant, and he can send that equal anywhere he is touching.

He sends it through the sword.

The sheet pushes on the flat, and the flat pushes back exactly as hard, no harder. The equal and opposite of everything the sheet carries meets it in the steel, and both stop. For one long honey-coloured instant there is a boy standing behind a sword with a plane of violet light pressed flat against the blade, and nothing moves at all: two forces leaning on each other through the one place they meet, and the place they meet is a strip of steel drawn out of his own life, and it holds. The arch from the yard. Two collapses, and the stone in the middle.

Then the load goes where loads go. It runs out of the blade through his hands, up his forearms, across his shoulders and down his spine, the way Gimbzo taught Darius and Darius taught him, through the hips and the knees and the heels into the ground. The ground already owns him. It costs him nothing to let it have the rest. His rear heel slides a hand's width backward across the white stone with a long dry hiss, and the stone under it takes the last of the force with a small sharp crack, a star of hairline fractures opening round his heel like frost on a window. Aequitas rings, one long clear note, higher than steel should sing. The grain down its fuller lies flat as a stroked cat. And the sheet of violet-white, its debt paid in full against something that would not take a single coin more than it was owed, thins, and smears down the flat of the blade like breath fading off a mirror, and is gone.

The honey goes out of the light.

The stadium noise comes back down the long corridor all at once, roaring. The ring of the chain speeds up, falters, and stops, as the last links of his box heal from the faces inward and the cells run back together into one floor. His heart is racing again. The Greater Hand lets go of the strings, all of them together, and his body comes back to him like a coat dropped onto his shoulders from a height.

He nearly falls down.

It is a near thing. Every muscle the Hand used, it used without asking the ones on the other side of the joint to let go first, the way his mind would have done, and they tell him so now, all of them, a hot quivering ache in his calves and the backs of his thighs and the long muscles of his back, a cramp knotting in the arch of his left foot. His sense of where his own limbs are has been scrambled. He has to look down to be sure both his feet are under him. His right side throbs on every breath in, and he finds he cannot take a full one: the breath stops short at a hot bar under the ribs and comes back out shallow. There is a taste of copper on top of the ash and tin. His hands are shaking. The gold in his eyes will not go down. He can feel it there behind each eye, hot and turning and refusing, a thing with its own mind about when it will close.

And something writes itself on the inside of him.

He feels it the way he imagines a page feels the pen. Down in the Crystal, in the place where a practitioner reads his own sheet, a line inks itself that was not there before, small and plain, as if a clerk had made an entry:

*DILUTIO. Signature. Held.*

Once, the books say. A signature writes itself on the Crystal once, the first time it holds, and never again, because after that it is simply part of what the soul is. He stares at the entry the way he stared at the leaf on the water. He has done the thinning once before, alone, and been sick on the yard stones, and the Crystal wrote nothing. This time it held, in front of the whole of Aetherion, against a boy who had found the one road through his law.

He straightens, carefully, a hand flat against his right side.

So much for half, he thinks. The tiers have seen gold eyes. They have seen a boy go loose as water and come back. The ones who know what they are looking at have seen a sheet of force stop dead against a blade and go down into the floor. He has kept the plates on. He has not let the weight out. He has kept every promise he made in the dormitory except the one about being nobody, and that one, it turns out, was never his to keep.

He finds he is not sorry.

"Ow," Dabney says, honestly, to nobody, and the Scriptia carry it to every tier at the same polite weight as everything else, and somewhere up in the dark ring a great many people laugh. Then, because it is true and the other boy should hear it: "That was good."

His breath catches on the bar under his ribs. He lets it, takes only what he needs, and lifts Aequitas an inch from the floor, point low, the cracked star of stone under his rear heel. His eyes go across the field in gold, along the line, looking for where the next thing will come from.

### Dabney's Trait slate and Signature (R76 entry format; provisional until Isaac keeps them, R75-4)

Built on the Four Reflections. At Stage VI his Traits have not externalised, so all four work through his body, blade and perception.

**EQUIPOISE** (*Aequilibrium*; Primary Trait, the Innate Reflection). Every force that meets Dabney finds its equal in him at the same instant, and he can send that equal through anything he is touching, his blade first. The third law of motion held as a standing law in his Essence Core and biased into his Shell. Always running, modest and constant: a boy who could not be pushed over. Used on purpose, it stops a force dead against a conductor and passes the whole of it through his frame into the ground. Cost: almost nothing for the bias; for a deliberate equalisation, the load itself, carried through his joints and paid in the ground. Limits: needs a conductor in contact and square to the force; his frame and the conductor's strength cap it; Aequitas conducts best; nothing against a force with no single partner (a field, heat, poison) or one arriving where no conductor is. Counter: off-angle or two places at once; a load bigger than frame or sword can pass; afterward the ground is damaged. Tell: rear heel slides and the ground cracks. Resonance: [Ur] Balance, [Va] Law.

**THE GREATER HAND** (*Manus Maior*; Secondary Trait, the Tempered Reflection). When the threat is faster than his mind, his whole Essence moves his body itself through his own threads into the Plane of Fate, attached at the spinal centres where motor nerves begin. Faster than conscious reaction, closer to a spinal reflex. First seen under the falling staves at nine. Activation is a physiological surge: heart thudding, pupils blown, face flushed, hands cold. Cost: fatigue debt afterward, muscles fired without relaxing their opposites: quivering ache, cramps, scrambled limb sense, near-collapse when it lets go. Limits: only where the line goes, never past the body's real range; held one breath here. Counter: genuine uncertainty gives it no line; a paradox leaves no third place to stand; the debt afterward is the best window.

**THE MERIDIAN EYE** (*Oculus Meridianus*; Inherited Trait, the Lineage Reflection). Eyes go gold from the inner ring outward, a finer ring turning inside. Possibilities stand up as scatter and smooth into the line of best fit (least squares; stationary action; Huygens' wavelets; Hamilton's principle). Shows what will happen, not what might. Runs without consent; does not close on command. Origin unknown to him; named from Urion's Meridian Pair, a golden and a blue star; only gold has ever shown. Cost: heat behind the eyes; the gold is visible and identifying. Limits: reads the line, not the reason; cannot choose between two equally true lines; useless against what has no line. Counter: stay uncommitted until contact; make two lines equally true; the lingering gold tells where he looks.

**GRAINLESS** (Passive Trait, the Resonant Reflection). His Fate-residue carries no grain, so Fate-readers cannot read his commitments ahead or trace where he has been; a room he sits in long enough settles level round him. Cost: none. Limit: hides nothing physical. Counter: read the boy, not his fate.

**DILUTIO** ("the Thinning"; Signature, permitted by the Greater Hand and the Meridian Eye together). The Hand dilutes the single will that holds his body in one shape, so every grain answers to its own thread, a recursion point asking the Plane of Fate where to be, like a steam governor (Watt; Maxwell, *On Governors*, 1868). Between askings, rejected possibilities are ruptured using any open Ruin warrant. The body goes fluid: no shape for a blow to meet. Not intangibility. Printed on his Crystal the first time it held, here (R75-5). Cost: everything the Hand costs, worse; nausea; first private attempt left him sick. Limits: a breath or two; cannot brace or guard while fluid; needs the Eye open. Counter: a volume rather than a line; two true lines; the debt.

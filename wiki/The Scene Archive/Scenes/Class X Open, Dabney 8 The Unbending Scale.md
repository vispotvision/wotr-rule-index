*Aetherion Academy. The Crucible Field, the opening match of the Class X Open Tournament. The sixth exchange.*

The thread parts under the flat of Aequitas, and nothing happens.

He feels the cut go through. The verdict falls exactly where he laid it, white fire and level steel and the weight of two lifted plates all arriving at once on the singing line, and the line goes, the way a lute string goes when you take a knife to it: a sharp bright snap and then silence. Axis Lock has what it asked for. The frozen field has its one outcome. The thread is cut.

And the thing at the other end of it stays exactly where it was.

He can feel it in his own magic, still there, still listening, with no line running out of it and no line running in. Continuity with no conduit. The Assize turns on it out of habit and finds something it has no column for: a relationship that has lost its bridge and kept everything the bridge carried. The lock releases on time. The verdict was cast, so the cold does not come back up his arms as backlash, but where the backlash should have been there is a strange hollow chill, the feeling of a door swinging shut on a room he was sure he had emptied. The Flame of Judica, white along the blade, stays white and goes quiet. It judged. The judgement is complete. Nothing came of it.

And then for one heartbeat, everything he is doing argues with itself.

The weight he set loose into the field is still there, every lamp still leaning toward him, every shadow still pouring in. He feels it stop being the only down: the floor still slopes toward him, and simply stops being sure of it.

The air in front of him goes wrong.

There is no warning on the graph, no line coming in, no road from anywhere, because nothing comes from anywhere. A broad slab of the air in front of his chest has never been compressed and has already finished expanding from enormous compression, both at once, and the place where the two truths meet becomes a blow. It hits him across the whole front of his body at the same instant, chest and belly and the tops of his thighs, like falling flat onto still water from a height. The breath goes out of him in one long helpless *hah*. His diaphragm locks. For a moment his lungs are empty and will not fill, the old schoolyard horror of being winded, and the bruise along his right side, which he had almost forgotten, wakes up and screams.

The floor goes next. The stone under his feet does not move, and he feels it heave sideways under him anyway, a shove through the soles of his boots with nothing behind it. A stair in the dark that is not there. His feet go out to the left. He goes down onto his right knee on the white stone with a crack he feels in his teeth and slides half a pace on it, the cloth of his trousers tearing, the skin under the cloth tearing with it.

Then the sword.

He feels Equipoise empty. Everything the blade was holding, every shove the bending arena put into his feet and every heave of the drum-skin floor, the load he had kept in the steel to spend on the verdict, is suddenly both still there and already gone. It goes sideways. The air on either side of Aequitas bursts outward in two flat thunderclaps, left and right, and the force of them rips at his sleeves and slaps his face and runs away across the white floor in two broad ripples of chalk and dust. Neither one comes at him. Neither one comes back along the blade into his hands, where it could have broken both his wrists. It goes out to the sides, into the arena, into the stone. Someone chose that.

The heartbeat ends. He feels the thing in his magic fold up on itself and go out, tidily, the way a careful man puts out a lamp before he leaves a room. Nothing is left of it but a faint taste in the workings, like the smell a room keeps after a pipe.

Dabney kneels on the white stone with his sword across his knee and tries to breathe.

---

His diaphragm lets go at last and the air comes in, ragged and huge and not enough, and with it comes everything else.

He has been running on the gold and the plates and the strangeness, and the strangeness is over. The bill comes in all at once, the way the old men at Edward's table said a bill always comes, on a single morning, everything that has been put off.

The Letting first: a tenth of everything he carries, thrown into the air as mist and picked up off the floor and used against him. The box: as much again. The Greater Hand: the debt it ran up in his calves and thighs and the long muscles of his back, which it collected in part and is now collecting in full, every one of those muscles cramping at once into hot stiff knots so that kneeling is the only thing his legs can do. The Gilding: still on him, a thin flat gold over his skin, and draining steadily, the way a lamp drains oil whether you are reading by it or not. The Flame: white along the blade and burning his reserve down with it. The two lifted plates: holding the room bent toward him, and holding it at a price he can now feel in his teeth, a high whining ache in every one of them, as if he had bitten down on ice. The lock: spent, but the hollow cold of a verdict without a consequence still sitting in his hands.

His nose is bleeding steadily now from the left side, not a thread but a run, warm over his lip and his chin and down onto the gold of his collar. His ears are ringing from the two thunderclaps beside the blade, a thin high tone like a wet finger on a glass. His right knee is skinned to the meat under the torn cloth and burning. His right flank, struck by the paradox two exchanges ago and struck again now by the slab of air, has gone past aching to something deep and hot that catches him every time he breathes in, though when he presses his fingers along the ribs, nothing grates, nothing moves where it should not move. Bruised, he thinks. Badly. Not broken.

He feels weak. He has never once in his life felt weak on a fighting floor. He has felt outmatched, outthought, slow, stupid, sick. He has never felt this: the bottom of the barrel, the sound a scoop makes when it scrapes wood instead of water. His reserve is low enough that he can feel the shape of the cask, though the cask is not yet dry.

He looks up.

The boy across the floor is on his feet. He went down onto one knee, and got up, and is standing now with his left hand curled against his chest and blood on his face and his clothes and the floor in front of him, swaying a little, the way a mast sways after a squall. Dabney does not need the Assize to read that. He can see it from here with his ordinary eyes. Whatever the boy did in that last heartbeat, his spirit has been writing cheques his body cannot cover, and the body is paying them now, in front of everyone.

And it was very impressive. That is the thing. Dabney goes back over it in his head the way he would go back over a page of Parun that had surprised him. He cut the thread and the boy had already made the thread unnecessary. He froze the field to a single future, and the boy put the contradiction inside the single future instead of beside it. He held the arena's whole weight in his blade, and the boy found it and threw it away sideways, where it could not hurt either of them. Three answers. Each one exactly what it needed to be, and each one paid for out of the boy's own body.

His spirit is stronger than his body, Dabney thinks, very clearly. And mine is stronger than mine. And we have both been spending the difference.

*I'm absolutely getting yelled at for that,* the boy said.

Something comes up in Dabney's chest.

He thinks at first it is the breath sticking again. It comes up past the bar under his ribs and out of his mouth, and it is a laugh. Not a small one. It comes out of him in a great helpless bark, and then another, and then he cannot stop it, kneeling on the white stone with blood running off his chin and his whole body cramped and shaking, laughing like a madman. It hurts. Every breath of it pulls at the hot bruise along his side. He does not care. It comes from the same place the gladness came from, from under the plates, from the floor of him, and for once he does not put his hand on it. This one he lets out.

He has heard Darius laugh exactly once. It was the night the old man came back from the third Accord tribunal. It had asked him the same question as the first two, and been answered, accurately and at length, about something else. He sat down at the workroom table and opened a bottle he had been keeping for nine years, and Dabney, who was eight, asked whether the tribunal had been very angry. Darius looked at him for a long moment and then laughed, a short helpless sound that seemed to surprise him more than anyone, and said: *They wanted me to be sorry for the wrong thing.* Dabney did not understand it. He understands the laugh now. It is the sound a person makes when the thing he has been carrying turns out to be the right shape after all.

This is a battle.

He has had seven years of forms and readings and drills and the yard and the brick and the basin, and an old man's voice telling him *again*, and he has never once had this. A person his own size on the other side of the floor, as clever as he is, cleverer in places, giving everything they have and getting everything he has back and standing up afterward. He is going to remember this for the rest of his life. He knows it the way he knows his own name. Whatever else happens to him, whatever the gold in his eyes means, whatever the old man in the stands decides to do about what everyone has seen today, he will be able to close his eyes in forty years and see the light come up the chain of his box, link by link, *every ending can be an origin*.

"So am I," Dabney says, when he can, and the Scriptia carry it into every tier of a stadium that is still holding its breath. He lifts his chin toward the third tier above the eastern gate without looking at it. "Getting yelled at. So am I." He wipes his mouth with the back of his wrist and leaves a long red streak across the gold. Then, more quietly, because the boy should hear this part most of all: "You threw it sideways."

He gets one foot under him.

"Thank you."

---

He stands. It takes two tries, and the second time his cramped thigh nearly folds and he has to lean on Aequitas point-down on the white stone like an old man on a stick before it takes his weight. He straightens. The two lifted plates hold the lamps leaning toward him. The gold is still on his skin. The white fire still stands up off the steel.

He raises the sword and points it across the twenty paces at the boy.

And the gladness, which he let out to laugh, does not go back down.

It stands up in him all the way. It looks at the swaying boy at the end of the blade and it wants. It has had a taste of the plates coming off and it wants the rest of them off, and a taste of the field bending, and it wants the field broken. And it knows, as he knows, because it is him, exactly what he has left that he has not used.

The Root Invocations.

The Order keeps them on the last pages of the Index, after the Applied forms, in a hand nobody has used for nine hundred years, and teaches them to almost no one, because almost no one can survive them. They are what happens when a practitioner stops borrowing from an Archon's roots and calls on the Archon's own law directly, the signature working a crowned principle is known by. Every Archon has one. The Balancer's is the first in the book.

**The Unbending Scale.**

Darius has done it twice in his life. Dabney has read the entry so many times he could recite it in his sleep, and has never once said the trigger aloud, not even alone, not even in a whisper. He knows the shape of it as well as he knows the shape of his own hand.

He knows how it works. He weighs it, because that is what he does.

---

The roots are two: **[Ur]**, Balance, and **[Sel]**, Measure, Urion's second glyph, whose office is to quantify flows for judgement and reveal the true weight of intent. Its Wellspring is Judicium again, in the Fulguria family, and its physics, under all the Parunic notation, is the physics of charge.

He has watched a summer storm come in over the Palatinate harbour from the chapter house roof. Darius stood beside him and explained what the sky was doing. A cloud builds up one kind of charge and the ground under it the other, more and more, a difference between them growing like water behind a dam, until the air between them, which is a poor conductor and has been holding them apart, cannot hold them apart any longer. Then the whole difference goes at once, down a single crooked path, in a flash and a crack you can hear across the bay. Lightning is the world settling, all at once, an account it had been letting run up; it adds no new force at all. Afterward, cloud and ground are level, for a while.

The Unbending Scale does that to a fight.

A caster holds the two roots steady for one full breath and names aloud the imbalance in front of him. And the Balancer's own Law of Equivalence comes down onto the field and arbitrates. Every force in the field is made to generate its measured counterweight at once. Every blow in the air meets its equal. Every working meets its opposite. Every raised voice falls to the same level as every other. The escalation stops, frozen, the whole fight brought to a dead level like a cloud and the ground after the strike, and it stays frozen until the true weight of the dispute is spoken aloud.

That is the part people forget. It stops a fight, and only until somebody says, out loud, what the fight actually weighs.

The Index puts it in the Root Invocations at the ninth Stage, Invocation, and above. A Stage Nine caster can hold it for about a breath. A Stage Twelve caster for about four. Dabney is at the sixth with two plates lifted, three Stages under the line, and he knows what the line is there for, because the cost is written beside it in the Index's plain dry hand. The caster's own Aether Shell absorbs the summed difference it has just erased. Every imbalance it levels has to go somewhere, and it goes into the one who called it, and a large enough imbalance can put the caster on the floor or put him out entirely.

The counter is written there too: a second imbalance thrown in faster than the field can re-level. A quick follow-up blow. A shouted lie. Or **[Uur]**, the Shatterfather's Disruption, a competing condition laid in before the levelling finishes. And the boy across the floor has spent the whole match laying in competing conditions faster than anyone has ever laid them.

He weighs it. The Assize does not need to open.

The imbalance in this field is the largest he has ever stood in, and the sum is simple arithmetic. Every paradox the boy has written today was an imbalance held level by a hand. Every plate Dabney lifted was weight. The arena is still bending toward him. The air is still full of the residue of contradictions. If he called the Scale down here, now, it would level all of that. The whole difference of the whole match would come down the crooked path at once, the lightning-stroke, and the ground it would come down into is his own Shell, a Class Two Harmonic Shell on a thirteen-year-old boy who is already kneeling on the bottom of his own barrel.

It would put him out. He is nearly sure of it. It might do worse, three Stages under the line.

And it would freeze the boy across the floor at a dead level, every force in him meeting its equal. Every contradiction still firing in his nerves would be met with its counterweight all at once. Dabney does not know what that would do to a body already held up by spirit alone, and he does not want to know. The Index is silent. Nobody who wrote it ever called the Scale down on a thirteen-year-old.

And then, for the field to unfreeze, someone would have to say aloud the true weight of the dispute.

He thinks about that, with the sword out and the gladness pulling at him like a dog at a leash, and it is that, more than anything, that stops him. Because he knows what the true weight of this dispute is. He has known it since the eastern gate. Two boys who had never spoken to each other walked out onto a floor in front of the whole of Aetherion and found out what the other one could do with his hands, the way Darius said you find out what a man's heart is. That is the whole of it. It does not weigh enough to call a crowned principle down out of the sky for. It does not weigh enough to need a verdict at all.

He turns the Assize on himself for a moment, which he almost never does, because it is like reading your own handwriting upside down. He puts the wanting on the scale. Part of him wants to win, plainly and cleanly, the way any boy wants to win. Part of him wants to see what the Scale looks like coming down out of the sky, because nobody has ever let him. And part of him, the part under the plates, simply wants more, more of everything, and does not care what it costs or who pays. The first part is his. The second part is his. The third part he will have to have a long conversation with, some night soon, on the yard stones.

It seems, very simply, that he does not need to.

---

He shuffles his feet anyway. The gladness makes him. Left foot back half a step, weight down into the cramped thighs, the point of Aequitas coming up a finger toward the line of the boy's chest. He feels the two roots come up in him without his asking, **[Ur]** and **[Sel]**, Balance and Measure, rising toward the front of his mind the way words rise toward your mouth when you are about to say them. He feels the breath start to go in that would hold them steady. Above the open crown of the Crucible the grey cloud at the edges of the parted ring has begun, very faintly, to darken. There is a smell in the air like a struck flint left too long, and the fine hair on his arms and the back of his neck lifts and stands under the gold, every hair at once, the way it stands in the minute before a storm breaks.

*Root Invocation,* the gladness says, in his own voice. *Say it. Name the imbalance. One breath.*

*Should he even do this?*

He looks at the boy at the end of his blade. He looks at the blood.

He lets the breath out before it is full.

The roots go back down. The hair on his arms settles. The darkening at the edge of the cloud stops darkening. He puts his hand flat on the floor of himself, both hands, and the gladness, which got its laugh and wanted everything else, pulls once more against him and then, grudgingly, like a dog that knows the walk is over, lies down.

The plates next. He has never put two back at once. **Restitutio**, Darius calls it, the restoring, the word the Order's notaries write at the foot of a deed when a thing taken is given back, and it is harder than the lifting. The lifting was letting go. The restoring is picking up.

He finds the edge of the lower of the two plates and sets it back. It goes on like a lid on a pot that is still boiling: he has to lean on it, all of him, and hold it there while the bolts find their holes, and every one of them finds its hole with a jolt he feels at the base of his skull. The room answers first. Every lamp over the Crucible swings back on its chain, a hand's breadth, upright, and the flames inside their glass straighten. The shadows stop pouring. The draw-hum rises out of its groan by a whole tone.

Then the top plate, Glory's. It goes on differently, the way a coat goes on in winter, heavy and familiar and smelling faintly of himself, and when it settles he feels smaller in a way he had forgotten he was. Seven years he has lived under it. It fits. The last of the bending goes out of the arena's lines like a held breath let go, the rails and stairs and arches standing straight again, and the long ripple in the white stone under his boots smooths out and is gone. Overhead the ring in the cloud begins, slowly, to close.

It costs. Restitutio always does. The weight he set loose has to go back under the plates, and the plates are heavy, and he is very tired. His knees nearly go again. The ache in his teeth climbs to a peak and breaks.

He lets the Gilding go. The gold sinks off his skin from the face downward, like water draining off a stone, and the white flame on Aequitas shrinks, finger-high to a thread to nothing, and goes out. The air around the blade smells, for a moment, of hot brass and salt. Then only of steel.

He turns the sword over in his hands.

The scabbard is across his back where it has been all match. He reaches up over his right shoulder and slides Aequitas home. It goes in slowly, the length of it hissing against the throat, and the cross comes down against the mouth of the scabbard with a small flat click that the Scriptia carry to every tier at the same polite weight as everything else, and it is the loudest sound in the Crucible.

Dabney Lambert stands in the middle of the white floor with his hands empty and blood on his chin, and the gold in his eyes settling, slowly, back toward brown.

He turns his head and looks at the referee.

He is an Academy proctor in a grey coat gone white at the seams from chalk, a broad short man with a fringe of iron hair round a bald crown and ink on the first two fingers of his right hand, and he is on his feet again at the edge of the silver-red line, grey-faced, one hand still half raised from when the floor went out from under the front rows. He has been looking at the two of them in turn, at one bleeding boy and then the other, for some time. He has not lowered the hand. He has not raised it the rest of the way.

Dabney looks at the hand.

Was he going to call this match?

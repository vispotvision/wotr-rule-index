*The cedar wood on the Kessen road, the cold coming back into it; then the black floor*

---

Muken brought Tsugigane up to meet the spear, and held the note.

He did it clean. He had stood in the cold of the robbed wood with the forbidden thing coming up under HIBI like a river under ice, the web of it, every flaw in everything the rings would pass through, and nobody left behind him to be cut by it, and Faisal's voice in him saying *I forbade it because of what it would do to you, as well, and you have never once listened to that part*. And he had thought of his sister's hands on a wound, looking at the edges and never at the hurt, and he had let the river stay under the ice. One note. One crack. The dead one, ringing at the back of the Sin's neck. He held it in the Kharven breath, in through the nose until his ribs ached, and set his feet in the bottom of his crater, and waited for the point.

It did not come for him.

The Sin of Wrath came on with the spear of fire running out along the Owl's sword and the flawless point at its tip, ten paces, five, the air screaming into it, *iiiiiiiiii*, and Muken braced for it to take him through the breastbone. At three paces the Sin turned the sword in his fist. He turned it point down, the way a headsman turns his blade at the bottom of the steps when the stroke is done and the register closed, and drove it into the frozen road at Muken's feet, to the guard.

[the old court Japonic of the Hall, shouted; a hanging judge reading sentence over a foundry floor] "*ENMA-CHŌ!*"

The ground opened.

Muken had read cracks in salt and stone his whole life, and this went another way: it opened the way a book opens, from a spine, the frozen road and the leaf-mold and the black cedar roots under it folding back on either side of the Owl's sword like pages, and under the pages there was no earth. There was black. A floor of it, polished, wet-looking, so black that the robbed sky over the wood showed in it upside down like the sky in a well. And up out of the opening came the sound, deep, deep under the furnace's roar, a note so low Muken heard it in his pelvis and the soles of his feet and the roots of his teeth before his ears had anything to do with it, the drone of a great bell rung at the bottom of the world, *ooooooooooommmmmmm*, and with it the black-violet.

It ran out from the sword in every direction across the road, Vantabriel's color, the near-black that keeps a faint glow the way a coal keeps one after the flame has gone, and where it ran the world went with it. The cedar wood went. Muken saw it go: the burning trunks and the fallen ones and the snow and the road and the bend where Hayato had stood with his hand out, all of it slid sideways off the edge of the world like a painted scene drawn back on a rope, and behind it was a hall.

He knew the hall. He had never been in it. He had sat in its seventh chair for a month.

The Hall of Verdicts, as it must have been three hundred years ago: the same high narrow windows under the roof-beams, the same seven chairs on their dais, gold and indigo and copper and silver and white and black, and at their head on its own step the red, with the lion's boss at its crest. But the light that came down through the windows was the color of a kiln's mouth, and it came down on a floor that went on and on past where the walls should have been, black and polished and wet, into a distance that had no end. The chairs were empty. On the black floor at the foot of the steps, in the middle of everything, there was a block. A plain thing of dark wood, waist high, scooped on the top where a neck goes, worn smooth by a great many necks. And the floor round the block was dark in a wide stain, darker than the black, that had never dried.

Enma is the king of the dead in the old Japonic stories, the judge at the bottom of the hells who reads each soul's register of what it has done and passes sentence. The Sin of Wrath's Third Release took his name.

"Welcome to the black floor," said the Sin of Wrath. "Kōkan."

He was standing over the block with the Owl's sword in his hand, its fire drawn back down into the steel now so that it glowed only white, and the blood from the back of his neck running into his coat, and he did not look like a man in a hall. He looked like the hall was his body. The black-violet came up off him to the ceiling, and the ember-orange under it, and the kiln-light came down through the windows onto him, and his shadow went out behind him across the black floor longer than the hall.

Muken stood on the black floor in his boots and his red harness with Tsugigane in his cracked hands, and felt the floor under his feet, and understood the first thing that was going to happen to him.

The floor would not take the load.

He felt it through his boots the way you feel ice that will not bear. The road outside had taken the weight of the Sin's stroke; he had sent it down through his bones into the earth and the earth had swallowed it. This floor was the Sin's law, laid down, and it held only what the Sin let it hold, and BARANSU NO SHIGOTO reached down through Muken's heels for a road to send weight along and found the black floor smooth and closed as a mirror. *It needs ground under him*, Faisal had said, kneeling, under the Bell. *Airborne, braced on ice, on a floor that will not take the load, it has nowhere to send the weight.* The wall that had saved him was gone. Whatever landed on him here would stop in him.

"Now we will read the register," said the Sin of Wrath.

---

He came round the block.

He did not hurry. He came round it the way the master-headsman must have come round it three hundred years ago with the condemned kneeling: unhurried, formal, the white sword held low at his side with the point toward the floor. His living eye was on Muken's breastbone. The ring-lines in it had filled with red and were turning, slowly, and Muken felt the reading go over his chest like a hand laid on him, counting.

He had never counted. He had not wanted to. Yusif had told him once, on the gallery floor, when he was twelve and had come home from his first contract with blood under his fingernails that would not scrub out, *don't count them, lad. A man who counts them either stops or starts to like the number.* So he had not counted. The Thresh contracts, and the Lanternless, and the Veyrath ditch where he had learned to fight dirty with men dying round him in the mud, and the war, the whole long last year of the war, the stair at Halcrest and the Foot and the shore and the Skeldmere and the Marrow ice. He had not counted any of it. The Sin of Wrath counted it for him in the time it took to come round the block.

"Three hundred and eight," said Shōki Kurenai Moto.

He said it the way a clerk reads a figure off a ledger. Then he stopped in front of Muken, two paces off, and looked at him, and the ember behind the cooked right eye brightened.

"Three hundred and eight men in your chest, Kōkan. At eighteen." Not a question. "I was forty-one at the block and I had closed the book on four hundred and twelve, every one of them read and voted and lawful, every one of them owed. Yours were not voted. Yours were a boy with a sword in a ditch." The white sword turned a little in his hand. "Entered."

[young northern Kharven, a cutter's vowels, steady, tired] "I didn't count them," said Muken.

"I know. I can see where you put them. You put them under the floor of yourself, where you would not have to stand on them." The Sin lifted the sword. "In this hall, everyone stands on what they put under the floor."

He did not strike with the sword.

He put it into the black floor beside the block, point down, where it stood quivering and glowing, and came at Muken with his hands.

---

*The first entry.*

Muken saw the set. The Eye gave it to him the way it gave him everything: the weight running down the Sin's left leg into the heel, the hip coming round, the right shoulder opening, the gold lines of the whole long wire body lighting up along the path the fist would take before it took it. A hook, short, at the ribs, from a man who had brawled for three centuries on the road and never once thrown a pretty punch. He read it. He moved. Yusif's stick had taught his feet to be somewhere else before his head knew there was a fist coming, and his feet tried.

The floor did not let them.

His boots went out from under him on the black polish the way boots go on wet slate, and it was a refusal more than a slip: the floor did not want him to move and so he did not move, and the fist arrived. It took him on the left side below the arm, through the red harness that Kiyome had laced in the old order and the mail under it and the padding under that, on the lowest three ribs, the ones that bend out over the spleen. Muken felt the lacquer of the harness go first, *pakk*, the red plates cracking like the glaze on a cup, and then the mail rings, and then the fist was in his side and the ribs under it went one after another, *krk, krk, krk*, three short dry snaps like a man breaking kindling over his knee, and the heat of the fist went into the broken place and cooked it. He smelled his own side burning, wool and leather and then the other smell, and the breath went out of him in a spray of red that hit the black floor and hissed.

He did not go down. He did not have the floor to go down on; it would not take him. He stood with three ribs broken and his side cooking and the Sin's fist still in it, and his mouth open, and no air.

"Thresh," said the Sin of Wrath, very close. "The salt contracts. Nine men in a counting-house cellar, because the man who paid you said they were thieves, and you did not ask. You were thirteen." He took the fist out. "Entered."

Muken got his breath back. It came into him in a long tearing gasp past the broken ribs, a sound he did not know he could make, and with it the pain arrived all at once like a door blowing open in a gale. White. Enormous. He made himself stand in it. He made himself look up at the long hollow face over him with its scars that were warm to look at, and he spat the blood out of his mouth onto the black floor between them, *pt*, and his voice when it came was the gallery voice, flat and even, the one he had used to talk frightened men across rotten galleries under Halcrest.

"There," said Muken Moto. "See? Didn't crack."

The Sin of Wrath looked at him for a long moment.

Then he laughed. It was the barking laugh, the one that came out of him when somebody hit him truly hard, though nobody had hit him; a short loud sound like a dog surprised. "*Good,*" he said. "*Entered.*"

---

*The second entry.*

He took hold of Muken's left forearm.

He did it almost gently, the way a physician takes hold of an arm to look at it, the long burning fingers closing round the forearm above the wrist over the red-laced sleeve, and the sleeve smoked. Muken tried to pull the arm back and could not; the grip was a vise heated in a forge. He tried to bring Tsugigane round in the right hand and the Sin's other hand came up and took his right wrist, and held it, and the sword stopped in the air between them with its note still ringing, sour and wavering, aimed at nothing.

"You know what this is," said the Sin of Wrath. "The copper lord told you. They read you my card at the Seat."

Muken knew what it was. It was on the card in Tetsugen's provisioning hand: *his first touch drives heat into one spot of steel, iron, stone or bone.* He felt the heat go in. It went in through the sleeve and the skin and the muscle of the forearm to the bones under them, the two long bones that already had a hairline crack each from wrist to elbow from holding the wall, and it filled them. He felt his bones get hot from the inside. It was the strangest pain he had ever felt, deep and slow and wrong, a fire in the marrow, as if somebody had poured molten lead down the middle of each bone; and through the sleeve where the hand gripped him a glow came up, cherry-red, the shape of five long fingers and a palm, the skin under the cloth blistering and the blisters bursting and the fluid in them boiling off, *tsss, tsss*.

"The Veyrath ditch," said the Sin. "Thirty-one. They were deserters, and they were starving, and they came at your company's carts in the dark with knives and pickaxes because there was grain on the carts. You were fourteen. You killed four with your hands." He let go of the arm.

For one breath the arm was free. It hung at Muken's side glowing faintly through the sleeve like a horseshoe pulled out of the coals.

The Sin of Wrath laid his palm on it again, in the same place.

*Second touch.*

*JIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIIII.*

The heat went out of the bones through his palm all at once. All of it, in an instant, ENSHŌ swallowing it back the way it swallows everything, and the bones it had filled went from a forge's heat to the heat of a cold room in the time it takes a drop of water to fall off a roof, and bone does what stone does when you heat it and then throw cold water on it. Muken heard it before he felt it. A sound inside his own arm, faint and high and many-voiced, like ice on a pond in the first hard frost, *tik-tik-tik-tikitikitikitik*, the hairline cracks in the long bones running and branching and running again through the whole length of them from the wrist to the elbow, a web, a crazing, every flaw in the bones opened at once. The Eye showed it to him. He did not want it to. He saw his own forearm in gold, the two bones inside it crazed all over like glaze on a pot fired too hot, and nothing holding them but the muscle and the skin.

"Third," said the Sin of Wrath, and struck the arm with the edge of his hand, once, a short chop across the middle of the forearm, almost lazy.

*BRAKK.*

Both bones went. They went in the middle where he struck and at a dozen other places where the crazing had opened them, and the forearm folded halfway between the wrist and the elbow at an angle a forearm does not have, and a white end of bone came out through the burned skin on the inside of it, through the scorched red sleeve, with a wet sound like a boot pulled out of mud, *shlk*. The blood came after. It came bright and fast and ran down over his hand, his left hand, the hand he cut salt with, the hand he had held his sister's with on the stair at Halcrest when she was small, and the fingers of it opened on their own and would not close.

Muken screamed.

He had not known that he would. He had been beaten in ditches and in yards and on the ice; Yusif had broken two of his fingers with the stick when he was nine, teaching him to keep his guard up, and he had not made a sound. He screamed now. It came out of him from somewhere under the floor of himself where the three hundred and eight were, raw and high and enormous, and the black floor took it and gave it back from every side of the endless hall at once, as if every one of the seven chairs had a throat.

The Sin of Wrath let him scream.

"That is the sound I made," he said, when it stopped. "At the block. When the blade came down on her. Jinsuke's blade. I made that sound and the Hall heard it and the master-headsman wrote in the register, *the first hand displayed emotion at the sentence*, and they took my name for it." He let go of Muken's right wrist. "Entered."

Muken did not fall. He could not have said why. He stood on the black floor with his left arm hanging broken in two places and the bone through the skin and the blood running off his dead fingers, and his right hand still on Tsugigane's grip, and the sword still ringing. He looked down at the arm. Then he looked up.

"Thought you'd be bigger," he said. His voice had gone high and strange. He brought it down. "Nine men in a cellar, thirty-one in a ditch. You're reading me my *count*." He breathed, and the broken ribs ground in his side. "I know my count. I was there for all of it. You don't get to give it to me like it's news."

"Back," said the Sin. "I am giving it *back.*"

---

*The third entry, and the fourth, and he lost the count of the entries.*

It went on. It went on for longer than Muken would ever be able to say afterward, in the kiln-light, on the black floor, in front of the empty chairs. The Sin of Wrath read the register off his chest one entry at a time in that formal foundry voice, and named each one, and took something for each one in the body.

The Lanternless, the free company, the contract on the Orrel road: twelve. A knee into the side of Muken's left knee, the one that had already gone with a wet pop in the crater, and the kneecap broke under it and slid round to the outside of the leg where a kneecap should not be, and the leg went, and Muken went down onto the black floor at last because the floor had decided now that it would let him, onto his right knee and his good hand, with his left leg out sideways and the broken arm dragging. The stair at Halcrest, and the Tenrai house-guard in the keep: six. A heel on the fingers of his right hand where they were spread on the floor beside Tsugigane's grip, and the two smallest fingers broke, *pchk*, and the heel stayed on them and ground, and the skin of them came away on the hot black polish. The Foot: forty-four. A hand in his hair, pulling his head back, and the other hand open across his face, a slap, and a slap from that hand was a club, and it split his lip on his teeth and broke two of the teeth on the left side at the gum, so that he felt them go loose in his mouth like stones in a shoe and spat them out red onto the floor, where they ticked across the polish and lay there. The shore. The Skeldmere. The Marrow ice. He stopped hearing the numbers.

He did not rage.

That was the thing he held onto. The rage was there. It had come up out of the crack under his breastbone on the road, and it was up still, standing in him like a man on his feet, and every entry put another stone in its hand. He could feel HIBIWARE under it. It would have been so easy. There was nobody here to cut. Nobody but himself and the thing standing over him reading his life off his chest, and the black floor, and the seven empty chairs, and the block. He could have let the river come up through the ice and taken every crack in every one of them at once, the Sin's and the chairs' and the floor's and his own.

He did not, because every time the Sin of Wrath finished an entry he leaned down close and looked into Muken's left eye, into the gold seam, and waited. Muken saw him wait. He saw the living eye with its spinning red rings watch for the rage to come up, the way a man watches a pot for the boil. He wanted it. He wanted the boy to break the right way. Every blow was a question asked of the same place.

*Show me who the true king of the north is.*

So Muken did not show him. He knelt on the black floor with his arm in two pieces and his teeth on the polish and his fingers skinned, and he held the note. Clean. One crack. The dead one at the back of the Sin's neck, ringing sour, and Tsugigane still in his ruined right hand with its silver thread humming.

---

When the Sin of Wrath stopped reading, Muken was on his back.

He did not remember getting there. He was lying on the black floor looking up at the roof-beams of the Hall of Verdicts with the kiln-light coming down through the high windows in long slanting shafts that did not move, the way they had not moved, he understood dimly, in three hundred years, and the Sin of Wrath was kneeling on his chest.

It was a light weight. He had expected it to be crushing. The Sin knelt on him the way a man kneels on a mat, one knee on Muken's breastbone and the other foot on the floor, and the heat came through the cracked red harness into the skin over his heart, and Muken could hear his own skin there begin to blister, small and wet, *pip, pip, pip*, like rain on a hot stove.

The Sin had the white sword again. He held it now in his left hand, close to the guard, and its point was a finger's width from Muken's left eye.

"The Kōkan eye," said Shōki Kurenai Moto.

He said it quietly. The furnace roar had dropped under him to almost nothing; only the deep bell went on, *ooooommm*, at the bottom of the hall.

"The crown eye of the red line. The one that reads every flaw and ranks it worst first. Your father had it. His father before him. The man who sat in that chair three hundred years ago had it." He did not turn his head toward the red chair. He did not need to. "He read the verdict, that morning, with that eye. He looked at her and found the flaw and ranked it and the Hall took his reading and voted. And I stood in the irons and watched him do it." The point of the sword came closer. The heat of it was on Muken's eyelid; he felt the lashes go, *jjt*, and the skin of the lid tighten and begin to blister. "My right eye cooked the morning after. From the inside. I did not choose it." The living eye looked down into the Kōkan one. "I am going to choose yours."

Muken looked up at him.

He did not close the eye. He did not know why, except that it seemed important, and except that the Eye would not close now anyway; it had been torn too wide on the road when he swore, and it was showing him everything. It showed him the point of the sword, which was whole, and gave it nothing. It showed him the Sin's body over him, crazed all over with ten thousand loaded gold cracks, and the open one at the nape, bleeding, ringing sour. It showed him the Hall.

And it showed him what the Hall was made of.

He had not looked before. He had been too busy being broken. He looked now, flat on his back, because there was nothing else left that he could do, and the Eye did what it had always done since the salt galleries and read the hall for where it was carrying its weight.

It was all dead. All of it. The black floor and the high windows and the seven empty chairs and the shafts of light that did not move: every line in it rang sour, the same note as the crack at the nape, a whole hall built out of one moment that should have ended three hundred years ago and had been held open since, the way a door is held open by a man's foot. The whole hall was a morning, kept. The hell the Sin had pulled him into was the one minute the Sin of Wrath had never been allowed to leave.

Except for one thing.

At the foot of the steps, on the black floor, in the middle of everything, the block. And on the block, in the wide dark stain that had never dried, the Eye found a single line that did not ring sour. It was very faint. It was almost nothing. A hairline, gold, running across the scooped top of the block where a neck goes, and it was carrying weight, and it was carrying it the way a living thing carries weight: badly, unevenly, giving and taking, the way a heart does. Under all the dead notes of the hall, one living one. The same small one he had heard on the road under the dead crack, held down like a hand under water.

Memories rang dead in this place like everything else, and this was something else: a tie. A thread from the Sin's chest to the block, so thin it was hardly there, burned nearly through, the place where something had been cut away at the root and had not quite let go. Muken had felt a thread like that once before in his life. He had held one on the Seat road for three days without knowing he was doing it, and let it go in the Plane of Fate, and wept.

SEIREI NO GŌ, the Work of Spirit. *The Work of what lies between life and death. He can feel a soul's thread where it runs, and hold it, or open his hand and let it pass.*

The Sin of Wrath had burned the bond to his wife to break the irons. The card said so, in the copper book. It said the Axis stood at one.

One is still a thread.

The point of the Owl's sword came down toward his eye.

Muken had three things left in him, and the Eye ranked them for him, worst first, because it could not help itself.

He could call. *Call, and I'll come*, Hayato had said, at the last yard of his road, with the mask on, shaking. One road home, held open. He could open his mouth and say the Amagiri's name, and the slight man in the fox mask would put his hand into the black floor from wherever he was and spend his last road to the Seat gate pulling a broken king out of a dead morning, and the Sin of Wrath would walk north after him with the register still open.

He could ring the note. He did not need a blade for it. HAMETSU NO GŌ had never needed a blade; the blade only held the note so his hands were free. The Work of Ruins lives in the Kharven breath, in through the nose until the ribs ache and out through the mouth until nothing is left, and he had broken ribs, and he could still breathe. One breath. The dead crack at the nape was ringing louder than it had ever rung, and every line in this whole kept morning rang with it, the same note, the same death never finished; and a thing that is already dead may be ended. All of it. The floor and the chairs and the light. He could not tell what would be left standing when the morning ended. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps the Sin. Perhaps not.

Or he could reach for the thread.

He had one hand that still worked, and it had two broken fingers and no skin on them, and it was still closed round Tsugigane's grip. He could open it. He could let go of the sword and put his skinned palm flat on the black floor, the way his sister did with a wound, looking at the edges and never at the hurt, and listen for the one living note in the dead hall, and find the thread from the Sin's chest to the block, and hold it. He did not know what holding it would do. He did not know what was at the other end of it. He only knew it was the only thing in this place that was not already over.

The heat of the point was on his eye. The lid had blistered shut on the outside and the Eye went on seeing through it anyway, gold, everything, the dead hall and the living thread and the long burning face over him waiting for him to break.

The deep bell rang at the bottom of the world.

And Muken Moto, on his back on the black floor with his teeth beside his head on the polish, drew one breath, in through the nose until his broken ribs screamed.

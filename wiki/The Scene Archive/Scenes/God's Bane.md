*The Material World, the fourteenth day: a valley with a black-red seam in it, then the sky above the blackened plains*

Faisal felt his brother arrive before the sound of it reached him.

He was standing in the valley where he had landed, at the edge of the seam, with dew on the plate at his shins and a green blade of grass between two fingers that he had been turning in the early light for a long time, because he had not touched grass in seven hundred years and had forgotten that it was cold and wet and that it bent. The sun had come up gold over the eastern ridge. The frost was going off the slopes in long silver breaths. A lark had gone up out of the bracken above him and was hanging there, singing, a small brown speck against a sky so blue it hurt.

Then the lark stopped singing, and fell, and Faisal caught it.

He did not decide to. A hand of gold was under the bird before it had dropped the height of a man, and it lay in the palm stunned, its heart going like a struck drum, and Faisal set it down in the bracken and looked up. Every bird in the valley had gone silent at once. The grass had begun, very gently, to lie down, all of it in one direction, the way a field lies down in front of a wind, and there was no wind. Down the length of the seam the black-red light swelled and brightened, the way an ember brightens when someone far off opens a door, and all through the soil under his boots he felt the Essence of the Material flinch.

*THRUM.*

It came up from the ground and down from the sky at once, a single enormous beat, so low it was felt in the long bones and the teeth before it was heard.

*THRUM.*

The second came before the first had finished. The air shook with it. In the bracken the stunned lark pressed itself flat.

*THRUM.*

A pulse. A heart. A great slow malevolent heart beating somewhere beyond the ridges to the south and east, and every beat of it sending another wave of pressure out through the Material, through the soil and the stone and the water and the thin warm skins of every living thing on the plane. Faisal knew the beat. He had known it longer than mortals had had words. It was the fervor and the malice of his brother, a maelstrom, a sound in his ear like a war-drum played on the inside of the skull; and the strange thing, the thing he had never once in all the ages said aloud to anyone, was that his own heart answered it.

*Thrum. Thrum. Thrum.*

Faster. Lighter. Gold to the other's black. He put his hand flat on the breastplate over it and felt it going there, and felt the thing it always brought with it, rising through him like heat through a cold room: the surge, the bright, terrible quickening that came into him for one being in all of creation and no other. He had never felt it for anyone else. He had kept oaths for eleven thousand years and knelt for seven hundred and never once felt it kneeling. He felt it now.

They were opposites, he and his brother. They stood at the two ends of every scale ever hung: the bound and the unbound, the oath and the breaking of it, the hand that catches and the hand that lets go. Every battle they had ever fought had been the whole spectrum fighting itself. And his heart had always been in it. A part of him, a part he did not pretend away, because Vow-Cant would not let him and he would not have wanted to, loved fighting his brother. Through endless battles across ages nobody now remembers, in places that are no longer places, it had always been there under the grief and the duty: joy. The plain fierce joy of meeting the one opponent in existence who was exactly as much as you were.

This would be the last of it. The final testament. The last chapter of the long story of the Grand Angel and the Grand Demon, a battle thousands upon thousands of years in the making, and both of them had known for a very long time that it would end in the Material, under a living sky, with mortals watching.

He closed his eyes, and for a moment he was somewhere else.

A hum. Low and smooth and endless, a sound like a great bell's note held so long it stops being a note and becomes the floor of the world, and under it, in a courtyard of white stone that no longer exists, the bright quick *klak, klak, klak-klak* of practice blades. Two young things in a ring of light, striking and striking, laughing when they hit. His mother's hum over them like a hand over two heads. His brother's grin, quick and wild, every blow a surprise. His own blocks, patient, square, the same every time. *Klak. Klak. KLAK.* And the hum going on and on over both of them, as if it would never end.

*Thrum.*

He opened his eyes. To the east, beyond the ridges, the sky had gone red.

---

He saw it from a hundred leagues off: the whole southeastern quarter of heaven turning the color of a wound, and in it, vast enough to see across the curve of the world, rings. Crimson rings of script, one inside another, turning against each other like the gears of a tower clock, with black stars burning at their joins and white-red lines crossing between them in a lattice like an execution seal. Over Kushara. Over the Crownlands of the Zettari.

He watched the column come down.

*Dooooooon.*

The sound reached him long after the light did, a deep rolling detonation traveling across a hundred leagues of the Material, and long before it reached him he had felt the thing that mattered more. He felt everything fall. Stones. Roofs. Walls. The old trees along the processional ways going over with their roots in the air. A child off a balcony. A cart off a bridge. A hundred thousand things, all at once, beginning to fall, and every one of them reaching his soul the way the lark had reached his hand, before he could choose; and he could not reach them. They were a hundred leagues off. The hands were already out of him, a dozen great gold palms rising from his shoulders and his back of their own will, reaching east, and they could not go that far, and they hung there in the empty air above the valley, open, with nothing in them.

He had heard the Zettari's oaths for eleven thousand years. He heard one now, a sovereign's voice in a wrecked palace commanding stone to *hold*, and it was a command and no oath, yet it was meant with the whole of a soul, and it rang through the threads like one, and he heard it hold.

He drew the hands back into himself slowly. One at a time.

"I am coming, brother," he said aloud, to no one, in the empty valley. "Do not do that twice."

---

He went up.

He gave the Vow-Light somewhere to go and it went, long curved planes of it shaped round him like the inside of a sail, and the air bent away beneath him, *fwoom*, and the valley dropped, the seam a black-red thread, the ridges folding down, the world opening out under him in its morning colors: green hills going blue with distance, a river flashing silver, the white of far mountains, and to the south and east, across all of it, the red. He rose until the air thinned and grew cold and the sky above him went from blue to the deep violet that comes before black, and he could see the curve of the Material bending away beneath him, and the place where his brother stood.

It was not hard to find. The land there had gone black in a circle a league wide and spreading, the grass burned off it without a single flame, the soil veined in black-red like the back of a hand, and over it hung clouds that had not been there an hour ago, piling up dark and low and lit from beneath. The whole plane leaned toward that point the way a floor leans toward a hole in it. The beat came up from it, *THRUM, THRUM*, so strong now that the air shook with every pulse and Faisal felt each one strike his breastplate like a fist.

He did not look at what stood at the center of the circle. Not yet.

He held still in the thin high air with his white hair streaming upward and the gold of him burning against the violet, and he did the first thing.

*The First Technique.*

Every Celestial of the Midreach is given it at the Singing, the way a mortal child is given a tongue. The Choir calls it Visualisation, and it is the root of everything the Oath-Keepers do. An oath sworn and kept is a real thing; it lives on in the Attraction Layer of the world after the words are gone, the way a footprint lives on in clay after the foot has lifted, and a Midreach Celestial can read that record and make it stand up. They visualise the oaths and the acts of the past, and the past takes shape: a promise kept becomes a hand, a gate held becomes a wall, a vow made at a deathbed becomes a light that will not go out. It is not illusion. What is visualised has exactly the weight of what was sworn, and the weight of a kept oath is the heaviest thing there is. Every gold hand Faisal had ever put out of himself had been Visualisation; every line of OTHSAL's script, every palm of FAIETH, every bit of it was oaths of the past, given back their shape.

In most of the Choir it is a modest art. A Midreach Oath-Keeper can visualise the oaths sworn in one hall, one court, one battlefield, and make a door that will not open or a bridge that will not fall. Faisal had carried every oath sworn on honor under the Lawbell for eleven thousand years, and every oath sworn on honor in the Material for seven hundred more, and they were all in him, the whole record of mortal and immortal faithfulness piled up on his shoulders like the snow of a thousand winters. That was why the Host, before it broke, had given him a second name besides the ones mortals use, older than the Bound Flame and heavier than Grand Angel. The Choirs had called him *God's Bane*: the one piece of the whole great mechanism of law that could not be bought, bent or lied to, the overseer of the planes, the keeper of the shackles of servitude, who had dedicated the whole of his existence to the binding of things to their word. And his brother had always been the other thing. Freedom incarnate. The unbound. The idea of chaos walking around in a body, and loving every minute of it.

Order and Chaos. The Host had never had better names for them.

Chaos was what his brother breathed out. Faisal could see it now even from the height, the black-red Force pouring out of the circle in waves with every beat of that heart: a contradiction, a pressure that got into the laws holding the Material together and made them disagree with themselves, so that stone forgot it was hard and iron forgot it was strong and the light forgot which way to go. Disorder, given a will. In the physics the Measurewrights write in their ledgers it is entropy, the slow running-down of everything toward the gray of the mixed-up and the lost; in his brother it ran fast and bright and red, and it laughed.

Order was what Faisal had instead. Order Magic, the Choir called it, and in him it was simply the shape of what he was. It could take a thing that had been shaken apart into chaotic particles and settle them back into their places, the way a sieve shaken just so sorts sand from gravel. It could reach into a mortal heart going wild in battle and lay the anxiety in it flat as still water. It could come down on a field as pressure, overwhelming, the weight of every kept promise in the world laid on the backs of those who meant to break them. And under all of it, it did one thing: it made things agree. With their word. With their nature. With each other.

He drew a breath. The thin cold air went into him and the Vow-Light in him turned it gold.

He let it out between his lips.

*Whirrrrrllll...*

It went out of him as a spiral, a long thin twisting coil of gold breath turning in the violet air, and as it turned it drew tight, tighter, the way a rope tightens when two men twist it from either end, every strand of it pulled into step with every other strand, until the breath had become a single line of perfect agreement stretched across the top of the sky. And it was too true to hold. A thing that perfectly ordered, in a world his brother had just filled with contradiction, could not sit beside the chaos without one of them giving.

*SNAP.*

*KRRRAAAAAAAAACK.*

The sky split, from one rim of the world to the other. The sound went round the curve of the Material like the crack of river ice in a deep winter when the whole river shifts at once, a single enormous splintering report that rolled from horizon to horizon and on past them, and every living thing on the plane heard it at the same moment and looked up. In the Crownlands, in the salt cities, in the cedar valleys, on the ridgewall and the tundra and the warm southern sea, a hairline of white-gold light ran across the whole width of the sky, east to west, and opened.

Light poured down through it.

It came down in straight lines. That was the strangest thing, the thing every mortal who saw it would try to describe for the rest of their lives and fail: light that did not spread or scatter or glow, light that fell in perfectly straight gold rods from the crack in heaven, thousands of them, as straight as plumb lines, as straight as a law. Ordinary light is a crowd, every wave of it going its own way at its own pace; this was a column of soldiers in step, every wave locked to every other, coherent from end to end, and so it did not spread at all. It fell straight. And every rod of it was bending, as it fell, toward one point: an anchor, a seed, a single bright knot of gold in the air directly above the circle of black ground.

Where the lines met the anchor they did not stop. They pushed. The gold began to spread outward from that point against the black-red, the way ice spreads across a still pond on a hard night once the first crystal has formed, *tsk, tsk, tsskkk*, a front of order racing out through the chaos and setting it straight as it went, every contradiction it touched snapping into agreement with a small hard sound like a key turning in a lock. The two met above the plain in a vast swirling boundary, gold turning against black-red like oil against honey, like two rivers of different colors meeting and refusing to mix, and where they ground against each other the air screamed, *zzzhhhhhhrrrrm*, and threw off sparks of white.

The black-red did not go out. It was not meant to. But its wildness dulled. Its unpredictability, which was the whole of its terror, went slow and heavy and stupid where the gold touched it, the way a wild animal goes slow in deep cold. And underneath the light, all across the Material, a stranger thing began.

Deception became hard to think, let alone to say. Vow-Light, which in a room makes a spoken lie cost a labored breath, was falling now over the entire plane, a whole sky's worth of it, and under that much of it the very idea of a falsehood grew heavy in the mind and hard to lift, like a word on the tip of the tongue that will not come. A thief in a market town reached for a purse and could not, for the life of him, remember why he had wanted to pretend he was not reaching for it. A captain halfway through a lie to his men stopped and told them the truth instead, and stood there amazed at himself. In a hundred thousand rooms people found themselves saying the thing they meant. And the chaos, which lives on contradiction the way fire lives on air, found less of it to feed on with every breath, and fell back from the light the way a hand falls back from a stove.

The light found Faisal too. It covered him from crown to heel in its straight gold, and in it he was very bright and very tall, and everything around him was very simply and finally true.

The spending went out of him like blood. He felt it at once: the long pull at the core of him, the flame drawn up the wick. Half of what he had come down with, gone in one breath. The pale silver line between his wings went thin. That was the price of the First Technique at the scale of a world, and he had known it, and he had paid it, and he would pay the rest when it came due.

Then the sail of light under him went out, and he fell.

---

He came down out of the violet like a star, a streak of gold scored across the red heavens, and the air in front of him packed into a white roaring shock, *fwoooOOOOOM*, and he struck the black ground at the edge of the circle so hard that the earth jumped. *DOOM.* The ground split in a ring round him and the black soil went up in a fountain and came down again as grit, pattering on his plate, *tik tik tik tik*, and the shockwave went out across the plain and flattened the dead grass a quarter of a league in every direction.

When the dust settled he was kneeling.

One knee down on the cracked earth. One hand flat on the ground beside it. The head bowed. His white hair fallen forward round his face like a curtain. Exactly the way he had knelt for seven hundred years under the weight of the cracked Bell at the edge of creation.

He looked at himself, and something moved at the corner of his still mouth.

[softly, almost amused] "Ah," Faisal said. "I see I have forgotten how to land."

It was funny. It was very nearly the funniest thing that had happened to him in seven hundred years. He had come down to the Material for the last fight of his existence, and his body had put him on his knees out of habit, in front of the one being in creation who would most enjoy seeing him there.

He stood up.

He did it slowly, and the whole plain felt him do it: the weight of eleven thousand years of oaths rising off the ground with him, the earth under his knee springing back into shape, *krrnch*, the cracks closing. He stood straight for the first time in front of his brother since the Bell, all the long height of him, the pale plate dull and scratched, the gold at the collar and down the breast catching the falling light. And his wings came out.

They unfolded from his back the way the dawn unfolds from behind a mountain, rising more than opening, two great arcs that spread out diagonally from his shoulders and went on spreading, up and out, until they were wider than a hall is long. They were not whole. The right one was gold, every feather of it, burning softly at the edges like the rim of the sun. The left one was gold to its first joint and beyond that gray, crinkled, half its primaries burned to ash and the ash still falling off in flakes when the wing moved, *fff*, and in the straight gold light from the crack in the sky the broken one shone almost as bright as the whole.

He put his fingertips together in front of his breast, the long fingers of each hand meeting at their tips, and drew a slow breath through his nose, and the energy round him began to turn. It went round him once, gold and slow, a ring of Vow-Light circling his body at the height of his heart, *hmmmmmm*, the sound of a bell's note held under water. Twice. Faster. The ring spread out from him across the black ground, and wherever it passed the black-red veins in the soil went dull and stiff and stopped spreading.

And through the whole of it, under his heart, his brother's heart was beating.

*THRUM.*

*Thrum.*

*THRUM.*

*Thrum.*

Black and gold. Each beat answered.

Faisal lifted his head and looked across the plain at last, at the thing standing at the center of the circle, and the white hair fell back from his face and lay behind him down the length of his back, lifting a little in the wind coming up from the ground.

[slow, finished, every word meant] "It has been a very long time, brother."

He said it the way he said everything. Under the falling light nothing else could have been said.

"You came up laughing. I heard you from the valley. You always came up laughing." A pause, the length of a breath. "I heard the Crownlands fall, too. Every stone of it, and every child. I could not reach them from where I stood. I will tell you a true thing, because I have no other kind left to tell: I have not felt anything as heavy as that since the morning you cracked the Bell."

The ring of gold went out from him again, wider.

"You will say I have grown thin." He looked down at the ashen wing, at the flakes of it falling. "I have. Seven hundred years on my knees under what you broke will do that to anyone. You will say I should have stayed at the edge, where kneeling things belong." The burning gold eyes came back up. "But you knew I would come. You have never once in all the ages been able to leave alone a thing that would not break. So here I am. Unbroken. Standing. Come and see."

The gold palms began to come.

They rose out of the ground round him first, out of the black soil, as if the earth were full of hands and had only been waiting for permission to reach up: hands of solid gold Vow-Light, open, held out, each the height of a man from wrist to fingertip, dozens of them and then hundreds, rising and rising on long gold arms that went back into the light round him. *Shhhaaaaa.* They filled the air. They reached out across the plain in a slow tide, palms out, fingers spread, and where they reached into the black-red Chaos Force they closed on it.

They caught it. That was what they did. That was all they had ever done. The black-red went into the gold palms and the palms closed round it, gently, and held it, and where they held it the chaos stopped being chaos: the hands took its wild particles one by one and set them each in its place, the way a weaver takes up dropped threads and lays them back into the warp, and the energy his brother had spent on breaking things became, in the gold hands, something ordered and still and enormously heavy. The hands grew brighter for it. They drank it. Every grain of disorder the Grand Demon poured into the Material was a grain of something for the Grand Angel's hands to set right, and setting it right was what fed them.

That was the oldest secret of their war, and they had both always known it. Order without chaos is a law with nothing to govern, a scale with nothing in its pans, a hand with nothing to catch. Chaos without order is a breaking with nothing to break, a freedom with no chain to throw off and no one to throw it in front of. Neither of them could have existed without the other. Neither of them had ever wanted to. They were the two pans of one balance, and they fed each other, and they had always been equally matched, because they had always been the same size.

The plain went quiet. Even the beat went quiet, for a moment, between one thrum and the next. The straight gold light came down out of the crack in the sky, and the black-red turned against it in the low clouds, and on the scorched ground between them the hands hung open in the air, hundreds of them, waiting.

Faisal unfolded his fingertips and let his hands fall to his sides. He had never once struck first. He was not going to begin now.

"I have been listening for you for seven hundred years, brother," the Grand Angel of Servitude said. "Speak."

*The Crucible, under the split sky, as the king's blade arrives*

---

The blade came down out of the torn sky into Muken's open palm, and the dead king closed his fingers on it, and breathed with a slow, saturnine deliberation.

Sodoku watched him do it from the barrier's broken revetment with his own hand still on Kurosetsu's hilt. In, slow and deep, through the nose, the old Kharven breath that Muken had taught both his sons on the rime-white practice yard before either of them could lift a real sword. Out, through the mouth, long and even as a knell. It was the most quotidian thing in the world. It was the way their father had always breathed before he did something that could not be undone, and Sodoku's whole body knew that breath, with an antediluvian certainty, before his mind had caught up with it, the way a dog knows the sound of the stick being lifted.

On the out-breath, the world began to crack with a faint crepitus.

The floor was already ruined, a charnel waste. What cracked was everything else: the dark air over the Crucible, the light of the split behind Malphas, the long fall of calcined ash from Rengai's shoulders, the space itself between the dead king and the living sons below him. Fine bright lines ran out through it in every direction from the point where Muken stood, branching, forking, the way a web of hairline cracks runs out across the old glaze and patina of a pot set too near the fire. Reality crazed, and as it crazed it swayed, vertiginous. The tiers leaned with a slow scend, very slightly, and leaned back. The barrier rocked under Sodoku's hand, juddering on a swell that had come in from nowhere. In the stands, people who had been running for the gates stumbled and caught at each other and stared at the ground beneath them, which had not moved, and which every sinew in their bodies swore had.

"Asami..."

Muken said it to the blade, softly, with a gruff solicitude, the way a man speaks to a horse he has ridden for twenty years. Sodoku had heard that name once in his life.

"Hibiware."

The blade woke.

---

He had been twelve. It was the brumal winter Muken came home from the north the first time, from the campaign against Malakar's generals, and nobody in the house at Kharven-Seat had spoken of it at supper, and nobody had needed to. The generals had been men whom rumour held adamantine, unkillable. Three of them had held a pass in the hyperborean ice for a season against everything the northern clans could throw. Muken had gone up alone. The stories that came back afterward with the wounded did not agree about much, but they agreed about one thing: that the king had said a name to his sword, and that afterward the pass had stood the way a cracked bell stands, whole to the eye and dead to the hand, and the three men in it had stood the same way, and then all of it had come down at once, men and ice together, in a great talus.

He had used it once in sixteen years of a reign made of wars, with a parsimony like rectitude. When Sodoku had asked him about it, years later, Muken had said only that a king who needs a thing twice did not understand it the first time, and had gone back to his ledgers and their rubrics.

He was using it now.

The echoes came off him first. *Hibiki*, the Moto called them, the reverberations: a sound below hearing, a fremitus felt in the long bones and the back teeth, rolling out from the king's body in great slow concentric rings like the rings on a pond into which a millstone has been dropped. Each ring was a reverberation of the world itself, sempiternal and low. Where it passed through the air, the air trembled in a fine harmonic shudder. Where it passed through the stone of the tiers, the stone sang, a deep plangent note that Sodoku felt through the soles of his boots and up the bones of his shins into his pelvis. Where it passed through people, they swayed like drunkards at eventide and put their hands to their ears and found nothing to cover.

Smoke began to come off Muken's body, acrid and grey.

It lifted off his shoulders and his scarred forearms and the open throat of his northern coat in long pale wreaths, and for a moment Sodoku thought it was steam, the cold of the seeded flesh meeting some terrible inward heat; and then he understood it was both. The Crown had always cost their father, the way an indenture costs a bondsman. It had been written in the Moto archive, in a plain codicil every child of the house had read: the Overclock tore fibre, ruptured scar, heated the Crystal. The heat was pouring out of a body that had been cellar-cold a minute before, and the cold flesh was boiling off it in a grey reek of burnt hair and scorched tallow. The pale fracture lines in his old scars had gone from amber to an incandescent white.

His Kamigan turned.

Sodoku saw his father's eyes change. The gold of them broke up into sigils, cracks of light spreading through the iris like hoarfrost across a windowpane, and then the sigils began, slowly, to revolve. Round and round the black of the pupil they went in a slow oscillation, and wherever those revolving eyes looked, Sodoku could see the world's fractures brighten. His father did not create the cracks with it. Through the Kamigan he read them: every flaw, every friable seam, every point of fatigue and weakness in whatever he looked at, every place a structure was already waiting to fail, and where he read them, the Hibiware sang them open.

That was the art. Sodoku understood it now, all of it, the way his father's hauteur had never let him understand it as a boy.

Every made thing has a note. A bell has one; a bridge has one; a bone has one; a man has dozens, one for each of the long bones and the skull and the great organs hanging in the body's cavities. Strike a thing at its own note and it answers more than it is struck. Strike it again in time with the answer and the answer grows, inexorable; keep striking, and a bridge that could carry a regiment will shake itself to pieces under a single marching company that falls into step. Every made thing has cracks, too. They are fine as hairs, scattered through the ferrous iron of a beam and the ashlar of a wall and the crystal of a bone, too small to matter on any ordinary day. Each crack is a place where the strain in the material, the tension and the torsion, gathers, the way water gathers at the lip of a weir. When the tensile strain at its tip grows great enough, it does not need any more force from outside. The crack runs on its own, precipitate, feeding on the strain stored in the thing it is splitting. It runs faster than a man can see.

Hibiware put the two together. It struck the world at its own note, and the reverberations found every hairline crack in everything they passed through, and fed it, and fed it, the way the endless small repeated loads of a long life feed the fatigue in an axle, until the crack ran. Glass, stone, steel, bone. Whatever he read through the Kamigan, the hibiki opened like a sundered seam. The Moto said, in the immemorial books, that at the bottom of it the Hibiware did not stop at matter. That the world itself had a note, and a crazing in it like old glaze, and that the Moto's oldest art had been made by a king who wanted to strike there.

Muken drew the blade back into wakigamae.

It was a plain, unhurried motion, the blade going back along his right side, the edge turning outward, his weight settling into his rear foot on the dark air as though the air were a floor of tamped earth. Sodoku saw the blade clearly for the first time. Long, single-edged, the steel of it a dull, plumbeous grey like ash under rain, a temper line running down it in a wandering crack-pattern rather than a wave. It hummed. It rang with the same note as the hibiki, so loud and so deep that the ash falling round Rengai began to dance in the air in a standing wave, and Sodoku's own Kurosetsu shivered in its scabbard against his hip.

His father's Kamigan revolved, and fixed, and the king looked at Sonzai.

Muken swung.

It was a yoko-ichimonji, a horizontal cut flat and wide, from right to left across his body, the kind of cut a man makes to clear a table, and it was aimed straight at the place where his younger son stood. The edge stood far outside any maai that could touch Sonzai, and it did not need to be near. What came off the edge was the reverberation, a single flat sheet of hibiki thrown out horizontally like the wave off the prow of a ship, and wherever it passed, the world crazed into coruscating seams. Sodoku saw the cracks run out ahead of it across the dark air like fulgurant lightning across a stygian sky, branching, racing. A whole web of fine bright fractures opened between the king and his son. It converged on the place where the brother stood.

Sodoku saw the feint inside it.

He did not know how he saw it. The Kamigan in his own eyes flared open at the first breath of the hibiki, spreading through the iris with a concussive force that made his skull ache, and it was not enough. He did not have enough of it to manifest; it was an evanescent flicker of a sight where his father's was a full revolving wheel. But he had enough to read the cracks. He saw the fracture lines running across the tenebrous dark. He saw where they converged, and he saw that they converged on the space just in front of his brother, not on his brother; the true weight of the strike lay a hair to the left of where the edge was pointing, in the place a man would step into if he moved to shield the one the edge was pointing at.

His father was not trying to kill Sonzai.

His father was trying to see which son would step in front of the other.

Sodoku stepped in front of his brother.

He knew it was what the cut wanted, and did it anyway, with all the obdurate fealty of an elder brother. He came off the barrier rail in one long stride, put himself in the path of the converging cracks, and drew Kurosetsu in the same motion, the blade coming out of the scabbard in a single white arc with the gelid frost already crawling up the steel from the guard, and set it crosswise in front of his body with his left hand flat against the back of the blade, braced.

The Hibiware hit him.

There was no impact, and that was the terrible thing. There was no shock of steel on steel; the hibiki did not strike the way a blade strikes. It passed through Kurosetsu, and through his hands, and through his chest and spine and skull with a low susurrus, and as it passed it found the note of every part of him. He felt each bone answer it. He felt his ribs ring like the staves of a struck barrel, his skull hum, a bell, the great hanging organs in his belly sway on their moorings. He felt, deep in the long bones of his arms and his shins, every hairline flaw he had carried for twenty years of fighting light up at once, every old crack that had healed, every cicatrix, every place he had broken and mended, singing, opening, beginning to run.

For one moment, his body was rearranged.

He would never be able to say it better than that afterward. It was as though every particle of him had been lifted a hair's breadth out of its place and held there in a strange superposition, while the wave went through, the whole lattice of his flesh shifted one step along, bead by bead on an abacus. His heart missed, his sight doubled, and his blood stood still in its vessels, supercooled. And then the wave was past, and everything fell back into its place again with a percussive jolt that rang through every joint he had, and he was standing on the broken floor with Kurosetsu in his hands and his brother behind him, alive.

Kurosetsu had done that; he knew it in the same instant. The Frost Resonance Array that Borin had annealed into its steel at the reforge had answered the hibiki with its own note, the note of winter, of stillness, of everything that stops; and where the two notes met inside the blade they had fought, the king's note to crack and the son's note to still, and the stilling had taken enough out of the wave that what passed into Sodoku's body was only a tremor, a rearrangement, and not a crazing. A wave met by its own opposite partly cancels. The cold had cancelled much of it, though by no means all.

The frost along Kurosetsu's blade had crazed from guard to point in a web of white cracks, like ice on a tarn after a stone. Blood came out of Sodoku's nose in a thin incarnadine line. His hands were numb and contused to the wrist, and his teeth ached as though he had bitten down on iron, and somewhere deep in his left forearm an old break he had taken at nineteen had woken up and begun to throb.

He looked up.

His father was looking down at him from the dark air, the Kamigan revolving slowly, the blade held low at his side in zanshin, still humming.

"Naifuru," Muken said.

The Earth Shakes. The name of the strike, given after it had landed, the way the old verdicts had always been given in the hall at Kharven-Seat: after the blow, as marginalia to what had already been done.

He did not look at Sonzai. He had never been looking at Sonzai. He looked at the son who had stepped in front, with something in his face that might have been grief and might have been approval and was, Sodoku thought, the worst thing he had ever seen there, because it was both.

Malphas had watched the king's opening with a connoisseur's cupidity. Then he turned his attention to the Yuno woman.

She had circled the four of them with her blade sheathed and her light drawn back into herself, asking him questions in a voice as clear and cold as a sword drawn in boreal snow. She had wanted to know what they called him, why they revered him, how his exequies would remember him. And then she had flicked her wrist, and her answer to the silence he gave her hung round him in the dark air: a ring of kunai made of pure light, a dozen points of candent radiance hanging in a loose circle round himself and his relics, not thrown, not striking, simply there. A cage of light, a test of his temerity.

He reached out to it.

The light bent. He did not touch the kunai; he had no need to. He had folded space once already today, packing matter down so dense that the world had to curve round it to hold it, and he did a small version of the same thing now, with insouciance, in the palm of his hand, a knot of collapsed matter no bigger than a pea, so heavy that the space around it sagged like cloth under a stone. Every photon runs along the straight roads of the world. Where the roads bend, the light bends with them, a refraction with no glass in it, and it does not know it has been bent. The nearest kunai of light leaned toward his open palm, its long lambent shape stretching, smearing, curving like a reed in a current, and then it slid down the bend of space into his hand.

It went into the putrescent rot. Ferment took it the way Ferment took everything, with a cloying indifference: the light did not burn him, did not blind him, was not reflected or refused. It was swallowed. The white of it went grey at the edges, then a livid violet, then a soot-black like charred paper, and broke down into his skin as a fine dark muck. Its energy went into the bank with everything else, a small exothermic warmth. He felt it settle there, a sapid mouthful.

He did it again, and again. One by one the kunai of light bent toward him across the dark air, stretched into long smeared curves, and went down into his palm and were gone, until the ring round him and his relics was empty and the only light left in the crepuscular dark over the Crucible was the split behind him and the pale smoke coming off the dead king's body.

He closed his hand.

"Names," he said to her, quite gently, "are bindings. Every one of them is a leash with someone else's hand on the far end. I have spent a very long time declining to wear one."

He began to come down, slowly, with a phlegmatic ease.

He let himself sink through the dark air toward the floor of the Crucible like a man descending a stair in his own house, the crimson coat settling around him, his relics drifting down after him at his shoulders. As he came down he breathed out. Long and slow and deep, out of the new Zettari lungs, a breath that went on longer than any living breath could have gone on, cadaverous and endless, and what came out of him was not air.

It was a mephitic fog. Heavy, yellow-grey, thick with a fetor of sulphur and rotten eggs, rolling out of his mouth and down off his chest in long sluggish coils, sinking as it went, because it was heavier than the air around it, the way the gas in a mine sinks into the low galleries where the men are working. It poured off him and down onto the broken floor and spread there in brackish pools, gathering in the craters and the trench and the long fissure where the rot was still welling up. It found the low places. It always found the low places.

The stink of it was rancid and dreadful.

He knew, as the woman in front of him perhaps did not, that the stink would not last. It would fade, quite suddenly, at the moment the fog became thick enough to kill. That was how Miasma worked. That was the benison in it, if there was any. People stopped smelling it a breath or two before they stopped breathing.

He settled onto the floor of the Crucible ankle-deep in his own breath, and smiled at her.

  - *Metaphysics:* "the world itself had a note": the old books' claim that the art reaches the Aether's lattice (reality crazing and swaying).

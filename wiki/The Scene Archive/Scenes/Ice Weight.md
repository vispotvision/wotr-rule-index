*The Amagiri post-house on the Shirasaka road, the last week the pass stays open.*

[wind howling] The skins had picked up weight on the road down, and Asa of the Keld knew it the moment she put her shoulder under the first bale.

She had lashed them herself at the sealing camp, ten skins to a bale, six bales, flesh side in and fur side out the way her mother had lashed them, and at the camp each bale had come up onto the packhorse's frame with one heave. Now it took two. Four days of pass road had put snow into the fur. The snow had melted in the noon sun on the switchbacks and frozen again every night, and the bale against her cheek wore a crust like the skin on a frozen pond. It smelled of what all her life smelled of: seal fat gone rank, the brine still in the hide, wet horse, and the woodsmoke caught in her own coat. Her breath stood out white in front of her face and froze on the fur.

[horse snorts] The bay mare blew and stamped on the line. The light was going blue over the white slope that gave the place its name. Above the post-house the cloud sat on the pass like a lid, heavy, the colour of a dull knife, and there was the first fine snow in the air, the dry kind that squeaks underfoot by morning.

The post-house sat across the road like a gate, which it was. Moto building: a long low roof of dark tiles weighted with stones against the wind, deep eaves, a raised floor, paper in the windows behind wooden lattice, and a yard of trodden snow where the runners changed. A bell hung from the eave on a cord. Under it a board carried two kinds of writing, the Moto script running down and the Concord script running across, and Asa could read neither and had never needed to. Everyone on the north road knew what the board said. The pass belongs to the Amagiri. Pay at the door.

She carried the bales in one at a time and set them on the raised boards by the door, stamped her boots, and went back for the next. Six trips. By the last her shoulders burned and her hands inside the mittens had stopped feeling the cold.

[door bangs] The inside was warm enough to hurt.

A charcoal brazier sat in a square pit in the floor, its coals gone ash-grey with red under them. Over it a black iron kettle ticked on a hook from the rafters. The air was charcoal and pine and something sharp and pickled that made her mouth water, and under it the clean wax smell of the Accord, which got in everywhere the Accord sat down. Along the far wall the runners' gear hung on pegs, straw capes and staffs and rope sandals stiff with frost, and in the corner under the capes stood a stack of sealskins with the fur clipped short for boot-soles. A long counter of pale wood ran across the room, and at one end of it stood the beam.

It was the Accord's thing and it looked it. An iron post as tall as a man, a beam across the top with two pans hanging from chains, and a long box of brass weights beside it, each one stamped on its crown. The brass shone. Somebody polished it. Behind the counter a man sat writing in a book under a lamp that did not smoke.

She looked at his hands first. Clean, long, ink on the side of the right middle finger, a ring with a flat face on the left. A clerk's hands. They had never held a flensing knife.

Then the rest of him. Thin, forty or so, with a face like a hatchet left out in the rain, long and narrow and pale, and a nose that had been broken once and set straight by someone who knew how. His hair was the colour of dry pine needles, thinning at the crown, combed straight back and oiled flat. He wore a black Accord coat with a high collar that sat well across narrow shoulders and had gone shiny at the elbows from the counter, a grey wool scarf wound twice, and fingerless mittens of the same grey. A silver chain crossed his waistcoat with a seal hanging from it. Under the counter his feet rested on a hot brick wrapped in sacking.

“[clipped, upper-class English accent] Collector of the Pass,” he said, without standing. “Septimus Crane. You'll be wanting the outbound duty.”

The other one came through the inner door with a tray.

Asa put her eyes on the woman's hands at once and kept them there. She had been taught it before she could grip a forearm. A Moto reads through the eyes, and the one who meets them has opened the door of his house.

The hands were small and square, the nails cut short, a burn scar white across the back of the left, and they held the tray level with no shake in them at all. The woman herself was short and broad through the hips and solid, in a dark blue quilted coat belted with a plain cloth sash, the quilting worn flat across both shoulders where a carrying pole had sat for years. Asa could see that much without looking up. Black hair cut blunt at the jaw, heavy and straight as a horse's tail, with grey at the parting. Wide cheekbones. A small mouth. Asa did not look higher than the mouth.

“[soft Japanese accent] Amagiri Sae. This is my house.” She set a cup on the counter by Asa's elbow. Steam came off it. “Tea. The road was cold.”

[narrating] Asa did not take it. A guest who drank under a roof owed the roof. That held among the Keld, and she guessed it held everywhere.

“[weathered Northern English accent] Sixty skins. Keld seal, going down to the Seat. I paid at this door last year and it was five bars.”

“[clipped, upper-class English accent] The rate's unchanged,” Crane said. He dipped his pen. “Outbound pelts and dressed skins, two copper the skin, by the Accord schedule. Assessed by weight. On the beam, please.”

“[weathered Northern English accent] I've told you the count.”

“[clipped, upper-class English accent] And I'll write down what the beam tells me.” He said it without unkindness, the way a man says the river is cold. “That's what the beam is for, madam. Nobody's count is the count. Ex officio.”

[narrating] She did not know the last words, and she did not like them.

She lifted the first bale into the pan. The pan went down and the empty one went up, and Crane took brass weights from the box one at a time with two fingers, each one setting down on the brass with a small clean tick, until the beam trembled and came level and hung. He wrote. Then the second bale, and the third. The kettle began to tick faster. Sae knelt by the brazier and worked the coals with a pair of iron chopsticks and did not watch the weighing at all.

At the sixth bale Crane wrote a long line, ran the pen down a column, and moved his lips.

“[clipped, upper-class English accent] Five hundred and four pounds. The schedule rates a dressed sealskin at seven pounds. That's seventy-two skins' weight. At two copper, a hundred and forty-four copper, which is six silver marks. Six bars, if you're paying in Nalūn salt.” He turned the book round on the counter to face her, as though she might read a word of it. “I can take it in salt.”

[narrating] Six.

She had five in the pouch. Five bars for the duty, the same as last year. The sixth was sewn into the belt of her hide-coat on the left side, flat against her ribs, and it was not for the Accord. It was the Thin Weeks bar. At the end of winter, when the cache ran low and the seal fat went yellow in the jars, it went into the pot a scrape at a time, and the little ones at the camp sucked the salt off the bone and did not cry so much. Every household in the north kept one. Her mother had kept one.

“[weathered Northern English accent, hard] There's sixty skins in those bales. I lashed them. I counted them onto the frame and I counted them off.” She took the tally stick out of her coat and laid it on the counter between the cup and the book. Six deep notches, and under each a run of ten small ones. “Count it.”

Crane looked at the stick the way a man looks at a dog that has brought him a dead bird. “[clipped, upper-class English accent] Madam, I don't doubt you can count.”

“[weathered Northern English accent, contemptuous] Then you're wet wood. [scoffs] A liar with a box of brass.”

[kettle hissing] [narrating] Crane put the pen down. He did not stand and he did not raise his voice, but a red line came up his throat above the scarf.

“[clipped, upper-class English accent, cold] Those weights were stamped at the Seat assay, and I'm required to have them proved each spring. That beam is true to an ounce. I don't know what you've packed in your bales and I don't greatly care. What is written is written. Quod scriptum est, stat.”

[narrating] Asa looked at the brass in its box, at the stamps on the crowns she could not read. She looked at his ring, and at the book. Every word that had ever cost the Keld anything had come up the road on paper.

*A clerk. They're all at it. Him and the Moto both, readers the pair of them, and this is how a reader eats.*

“[soft Japanese accent] He believes his beam,” said Sae from the brazier.

Asa did not turn her head.

“[soft Japanese accent] I have watched him weigh for three winters. He is a man who would sooner be right than be paid.” The chopsticks clicked. “He is telling you the truth. Your skins are lying to him.”

“[weathered Northern English accent] Skins don't talk.”

“[soft Japanese accent, dry] They drink, though.” Sae stood, and her knees cracked. She came round the brazier and laid one square hand flat on the top bale, on the crust, and held it there, and when she lifted it her palm was wet. “Four days down from the sealing grounds, sun on the switchbacks at noon and frost every night, and the fur takes the snow and keeps it.” She wiped her palm on her sash. “You have twelve skins' weight of the pass road in those bales. Ice.”

[narrating] Asa looked at the dark wet mark on the blue cloth.

She knew it was true the moment it was said, the way she had known on the road that the bales were heavy and had not stopped to think why. Twelve skins of water. She had carried it down the mountain on her own shoulders and paid the mare's feed for it.

“[weathered Northern English accent] Then I'll knock it out.”

“[clipped, upper-class English accent] You're welcome to. I'll weigh whatever you bring back to the beam.” Crane took up his pen again, mollified, almost kind. “Mind, the proper way is to thaw them and dry them, and you'd want a night for that. By the fire.”

“[soft Japanese accent] The brazier is a copper the scuttle,” Sae said. “Board is four copper. The room by the runners' wall is two. The pass will be shut by morning.”

[wind howling] Outside the wind took a run at the shutters, and the paper in the windows bellied in and went taut.

[narrating] Asa knew the sky over a pass. The sky does not ask whether you are ready. If the snow came down the way that lid of cloud promised, the road would close until the grass came, and the skins would sit in this post-house all winter, and she would sit beside them eating the Moto's rice at four copper a bowl, and in the spring she would walk home owing the Amagiri the price of the skins.

She carried the bales back out to the yard.

The light was nearly gone. The snow was coming fine and steady now, slanting in under the eave, and the bay mare had turned her rump to it. Asa untied the first bale, spread it on the trodden snow, and took the haft of her flensing knife to the crusted fur.

You beat ice out of a pelt the way you beat a rug, but harder, and from the fur side, so the hide flexes and the ice cracks off the hairs without taking the hairs with it. Hit too hard and the hide splits where it has frozen stiff, and then you have a skin nobody at the Seat will pay full price for. Hit too soft and you are only warming yourself. She found the stroke on the third skin: short and wrist-heavy, the haft landing flat every time. The ice came off in chips and in plates the size of her palm and rattled down onto the snow. The burn in her shoulders came back. Her breath went up in clouds and froze in the fur of her hood.

Sixty skins. Every one turned, beaten, turned again, beaten, folded flesh-in, laid back on the pile. Once the mare whickered at something on the road and Asa looked up, and it was nothing. Once a runner came down through the snow with a staff and a lantern and a letter box on his back, and went in under the bell without a word to her, and the door let out the warm smell of the pickles for a breath before it shut.

By the time she was done she could not feel her face, and the snow on the road had covered the runner's tracks.

She lashed the bales again. Her fingers would not tie a knot. She tied them anyway, with the heels of her hands and her teeth.

[door bangs] The heat inside struck her like a blow.

Six bales into the pan, six weighings, the brass going tick, tick, tick in the clean dry warmth. Crane wrote and ran his pen down the column. Sae stood at the counter now, pouring fresh tea into the cup at Asa's elbow, since the first had gone cold.

“[clipped, upper-class English accent] Four hundred and sixty-two,” Crane said. “Sixty-six skins' weight, a hundred and thirty-two copper, which is five silver and twelve.” He looked up. “There's still water in them. You'll never get it all out with a stick. But that's what the beam says.”

[narrating] Five bars and twelve copper. She had the bars. The Keld had never in their lives had copper, and she had none.

“[weathered Northern English accent, flat] I've five bars and the clothes I stand in.”

“[clipped, upper-class English accent] I can't take a part bar, I'm afraid. There's no cutting-house stamp for a half. Five bars and a bar, and I'll write you a credit note for the remainder, redeemable at the Seat chancery.”

[narrating] A piece of paper, for most of a salt bar out of the Thin Weeks, payable in the Seat where nobody knew her, written in a hand she couldn't read.

She put her hand to the left side of her belt, where the bar was sewn. Outside, the wind had found a crack in the shutters and was blowing one thin note through it.

“[soft Japanese accent] I need a skin,” said Sae.

Asa kept her eyes on the counter.

“[soft Japanese accent] For the runners' boots. The soles go through in a winter. A Keld skin, unclipped. I will pay twelve copper for one.” The square hands laid a string of small coins on the counter beside the tally stick, twelve of them threaded through the holes in their middles. [coins clinking] “Twelve. It is a poor price. I know it is a poor price.”

[narrating] It was a poor price. A Keld sealskin at the Seat fetched a silver mark in a lean year.

*Twelve copper.* And the runners' corner held a stack of clipped sealskins as high as Asa's knee, enough to sole every runner on this road for two winters. She had seen it when she came in. She could see it now from the side of her eye, under the straw capes, the fur gone short and even under the shears.

*She read it off me. The bar in the belt. I never looked at her once, and she read it off me anyway.*

Asa didn't know how. She had been careful. She had kept her eyes on the woman's hands and the floor and the brazier the whole time, as her mother taught her. The Moto had looked at something all the same, and had seen the Thin Weeks in it.

She untied the top bale with stiff fingers and drew out the outer skin, the best one, the big bull she had taken herself on the ice at the river mouth, and laid it flat on the counter, fur up. It was still damp. It would dry.

“[weathered Northern English accent, quiet] That one.”

Sae did not look at it. She folded it once and set it on the floor behind the counter, and did not carry it to the runners' corner. She put her fingertips to the tea cup and moved it an inch nearer Asa's elbow.

Asa drank the tea. It was green and bitter and scalding, and it went down into her like a coal.

“[clipped, upper-class English accent] Fifty-nine skins outbound,” Crane said, frowning, and struck the line and wrote another. “Sixty-six skins' weight less one at the schedule, sixty-five, which at two copper makes five silver and ten.” He held out his clean hand.

[narrating] She paid him. Five bars of Nalūn salt out of the pouch onto the counter, grey and squared, the cutting-house stamp on each end. Then she slid ten coins off Sae's string, one at a time, and set them on top of the salt. [coins clinking] Crane counted the bars twice with one finger and the copper once, wrote, blotted, and pressed his ring into a drop of red wax at the foot of the page, and said the words over it the way the Accord said them, as if someone were listening who mattered.

“[clipped, upper-class English accent] Actum coram testibus.”

“[soft Japanese accent] I witnessed it,” said Sae.

[narrating] Two copper were left on the string. Asa tied the string round twice and pushed it down inside her mitten, against her palm, where she could feel the edges of the coins. She put the tally stick back inside her coat and touched the left side of her belt once, and the bar was there, flat and hard against her ribs. Then she went out under the bell to load the mare before the snow closed the road.

Naiser wiped his face with the heel of his hand, once on each side, hard, the way his father used to clear frost from the flat stone above the gate. Naevra was right. He did not have time for this. Five hundred names had been read out over five hundred bodies before the light came, and the light had come, and the work of the living had not paused for any of it.

He was crossing the courtyard toward the Council Room when he felt the heat.

He felt it before he saw it. It came down on him out of the morning like the first breath of a kiln when the door is opened, a dry pressure on the skin of his face and the backs of his hands, and the thread-marks at his temples prickled with it. Above the wall a streak of fire was falling out of a clean blue sky, turning on itself in a tight bright spiral, and the air screamed thin around it. He knew whose it was. There was only one person in the house who came down off a wall like a burning arrow because walking round by the stair was beneath her.

He had one breath. He used it to reach back and pull up the hood of his cloak.

The black founder's mantle had been scrubbed in the night by someone who had not asked him, and it was damp still at the hem, and the high collar and the deep hood came up together and closed over the bare cold nape of his neck where his hair had been. Seradris had not been in the hall. She had not seen him on his knees in front of the tablet. She would not see the back of his neck now. He had never once let her see him in the state the night had left him in, and he was not going to begin on the morning after. Whatever she believed about her eldest brother, he wanted it to go on being true: that he had everything in hand, that the house stood because he stood, that there was nothing in the world she needed to be afraid of while he was in it.

She landed in front of him.

The paving cracked where she came down. A ring of frost that had lain on the stones since dawn went up in one soft hiss of steam, and a gust of hot air rolled out from her and lifted the edge of his hood and set it down again. Behind him he heard the low voices of the mourners at the far gate stop, all together, the way birds stop. In the corner by the well, where one of last night's dead was still kneeling, over and over, to pick up the thing he had dropped, the silver echo of him wavered in the heat like a reflection in disturbed water and steadied again.

Naiser looked down at his sister. It was not very far down. A few inches, no more, and she was holding herself so straight that it was less.

"Find anything new today, Big Brother?"

There was sweat on her brow, and the fire was still going out of her hands in thin curls like smoke from a snuffed wick. The morning sun was on her. He found that he could not look at her straight on for very long, and the brightness was the least of the reasons.

"A scorch mark in the middle of my courtyard," he said. "That's new." He looked past her at the broken stone, the circle of wet black paving where the frost had been. "And an arena full of my soldiers standing in a silence I could hear from the archive steps. I take it you dismissed them."

He let his voice go dry on it, the way it went with her and with no one else. The house expected a particular voice from him, level and finished, the voice of a man who had already decided. With Seradris it had always been allowed to bend a little at the edges.

"You'll have them thinking the blood is a thing you're either born burning with or not," he said. "Some of them were at the river yesterday. Go easy." He paused. "Though not too easy. They were standing like farmers."

He meant it to sound like the elder brother's scolding she would have expected, and for the most part it did. He did not let her see how close he came, in the middle of it, to reaching out and taking her by the shoulders and simply holding on.

She was young. That was the thing he kept coming back to. He had thought she would be broken by their father, and she was not, or not where he could see it, and he understood why, and the understanding sat in his chest like a stone. He and Naevra had had their father for the whole of their growing. Every morning at the gate, every evening at the reading, every hand laid on the head with nothing said. Seradris and Senri had had so much less of him. The war had taken the years they would have been given. A child cannot grieve the whole of what she never had time to hold.

*She'll be all right,* he told himself. *She is still building. There is so much of her still to come.*

He knew that. He made himself know it.

"Brother."

Naevra had come out of the Council Room doorway behind him. She had bound her hair since the night, a long white plait down her back, and there was clean grey cloth at her throat instead of the stained apron, and her face was composed again in the way the house's faces composed themselves, smooth as water over a drowned stone. Only the eyes gave her away. They were rimmed red, and they were already reading him, and he saw them go to the hood on his head and understand it and say nothing.

There was a man with her. One of the gate runners, young, in the house's grey, out of breath and very pale, holding a folded paper against his chest as though it might fly away. He came down the two steps into the courtyard and stopped a respectful distance off, and his eyes went to the scorched paving, and to Seradris, and quickly away again, and he bowed low to Naiser.

"Sire." He had to swallow before he could go on. "A rider, sire. Under a white cloth, from the Zettari. They wish to make peace." He held the paper out. "They want to marry your sister Naevra to the Crown Prince."

The courtyard was very quiet. Somewhere beyond the wall a crow called and was answered.

Naiser did not take the paper. He looked at Naevra.

She looked back at him. She had known; he could see that she had known before the runner opened his mouth, the way she knew most things, through the threads, a little ahead of everybody else. She had walked out here with the boy so that she would be standing next to Naiser when he heard it.

"They expect me to marry my sister away," he said, "like she has no feelings."

It came out of him before he could stop it, and for a moment the level, finished voice was gone entirely, and what was under it showed. It was in the set of his mouth and the way his hand closed at his side. The thread-marks at his temples, which he had spent the whole of the night forcing down, flickered once, silver going violet, and the silver echo by the well flickered with them.

"After all we've been through," he said, more quietly.

Naevra held his eyes.

"You don't need to act like I wouldn't do it," she said.

He looked at her. A year. There was a year between them, no more; she had come into the world so close behind him that for most of their childhood strangers had taken them for twins, his dark beside her pale. They had learned their letters at the same table and their first workings in the same yard. She had held his hand to her heart when he woke from the dreams. And she was standing in front of him now with her chin up and her red-rimmed eyes perfectly steady, offering herself to the house that had spent four years trying to break theirs, as calmly as she would have offered to copy out a page in the archive.

"That doesn't make it right," he said.

"No."

"How do we know they aren't using us?" He turned at last and took the paper from the runner's hand, and did not unfold it. He held it at his side, between two fingers, the way he would have held something that might bite. "For our influence. For our name. They know our family won the war. They know who holds dominion over the Spirit Realm now, and they know what it cost the families who stood in the way of it. The Mahuo. The Seikai." He said the last one evenly. He could feel Naevra not looking at him as he said it. "Every house that got between us and the seam is in the ground or in the dark. The Zettari can count. They aren't offering peace because they love us. They're offering it because they've looked at the board and they don't like where they're standing on it."

The runner had gone very still.

"The only way I agree," Naiser said, "is if they give us something we cannot live without."

Naevra was ready for that. She had always been the better of them at this. Their father had seen it when they were children, and so had Senri, who had told Naiser more than once, in the plain way Senri said everything, that he would have to learn to work with other people one day or he would end up ruling a house of one. He had not liked hearing it. He had never been able to argue with it either.

"They have men," Naevra said. "Builders. Masons, engineers, carpenters, the whole of the Zettari guilds. They have more builders than we have people left." She did not raise her voice. She did not need to. "You saw the Enclave, Naiser. You walked through it this morning. Half of it is standing because it hasn't remembered yet that it's supposed to fall down. We can bury our dead with our own hands. We can't rebuild with them. Not before the winter. Not without them."

He opened his mouth, and closed it again.

She was right. She was always right about this kind of thing, and it was always the thing he least wanted to hear. He looked down at the paper in his hand, still folded, and turned it over once with his thumb. The seal on it was the Zettari boundary-mark, pressed in grey wax: a square, quartered, plain as a surveyor's stake.

"Set up the meeting," he said.

The runner bowed his head so fast that he nearly overbalanced. "Yes, sire."

He went at a half-run across the courtyard to the crow-loft by the east wall, where the house's birds roosted in their long rows under the eaves, black and silent and watching everything. Naiser heard the loft door bang, and the scratch of a pen on a slip of paper, and after a moment the sound of wings. A single crow went up out of the loft and over the wall, beating hard, a small black mark against the morning, and turned south, toward the Zettari and their Crown Prince and whatever it was they wanted from his house.

He watched it until he could not see it.

Then he turned back to his youngest sister.

He reached out and laid his hand on her head. His palm came to rest on her hair, warm still from the fire and from the sun, and he left it there and said nothing at all for a moment, exactly as their father had done with him a thousand times. The hand on the head, from elder to younger, with nothing said. He had never done it to her before. He was not sure, until he did it, that he had the right.

"I'll be gone a few days," he said. "Naevra with me." His voice was level again, finished, the voice of a man who had already decided. Underneath it, only someone who knew him would have heard how carefully it had been put back together. "While I'm away, the villagers below the gate are going to need things. Food, roofs, hands to carry the dead who haven't been carried yet. Whatever they ask for." He took his hand away. "Will you see to them for me, Seradris? Help them with anything they need?"

It was a request, and it was more than one. He asked it the way his father used to ask things of him at the gate before a reading, as though handing over a key. *This is yours now.* He stood in the sun with his hood up and the Zettari paper still folded in his other hand, and waited for her answer.

- **Seradris is never written.** She gets no action, no line, no thought and no reaction. Her post's landing, scorch and question are answered from Naiser's side only: what he sees, the heat on his skin, the arena he heard go quiet. The post stops on his request to her, so her answer is her player's.

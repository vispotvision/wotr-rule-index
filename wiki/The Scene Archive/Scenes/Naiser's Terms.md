Naiser did not rise. He inclined his head to the Sovereign of Kushara, low and exact, the bow a lord of the house gave a lord of another, and then to the Crown Prince a degree less, and he held both bows for a full breath before he straightened.

"Sovereign Shaka-Al. Prince Dougou." His voice was quiet, and it carried to the far end of the long room without effort, the way voices carried in that hall and nowhere else. "You are welcome on Yukari ground. Please, sit. Drink first. It is our custom that no word of business is spoken in this house until the guest has tasted it." He lifted his own cup in both hands. "Whatever is said after, the tea was shared. That much at least will be true."

He drank. The bitterness of the little white flowers went down his throat and sat behind his breastbone, and he let it sit there.

He had felt the wards weigh these two on the road, and he had felt them weigh them again at the gate, and now, with the Sovereign standing in his doorway close enough to touch, he did not need the wards at all. A man's threads lay close to the surface in this room. They always had. That was why his father had chosen it. The Sovereign's were taut and orderly and very heavy, wound tight round a great deal of grief that had been folded up small and put away, and the word he had used, *cooperation*, ran through all of them like a warp thread through cloth. He meant it, every word. Naiser would have found it easier if he had not.

He set the cup down.

"You asked what each of our families hopes to preserve," he said. "I'll answer you. But I would like to begin somewhere else, and I'd ask your patience for it."

He looked out through the open screens for a moment, at the white gravel and the three standing stones and the cedars beyond them.

"I feel for them," he said. "The families the Moto talked into destroying themselves. I feel for them more than I can easily say." His hands lay open on his knees. "They were not wicked men, most of them. They were persuaded. Someone who reads fate for a living came to them and told them what was going to happen, and what they could become if they helped it happen, and they believed him, because a Moto is very rarely wrong about what will happen and very often wrong about what it will cost. So they reached for things they did not understand. They tried to arrange what cannot be arranged. And the spirits paid for it."

Beside him, Naevra had gone still in the particular way that meant she was listening to the room as well as to him.

"You'll have heard that we won the war for the Spirit Realm," Naiser said. "Everyone has heard that. I want you to hear what it was we won." He turned his head and looked at the Sovereign directly. "Have you wondered, in Kushara, why the spring has been so slow these four years? Why the harvest comes in late and thin, and the frost stays in the low fields after it ought to have gone?"

He let the question stand for a breath. He did not wait for an answer to it.

"The Soul Plane was fractured," he said. "Broken, by the insolence of men who did not understand what they were touching. It is the place where everything that has ever lived is kept, and where everything that is going to live is drawn from. When it split, the tides of life and death turned in their beds. Only for a moment. A moment was enough. The dead lost their road. There are spirits drifting through the Inner World tonight that do not know where they are meant to go, and they will go on drifting until someone shows them. The living lost something too." He was quiet for a moment. "That is the slowness in your fields. The land is waiting for something to come back to it that has not come back yet."

"It isn't only the fields," Naevra said.

Her voice was very low and very even, the archive voice, and it was the first thing she had said since the Zettari came in.

"I keep the reports for the valley," she said. "Births and deaths. All of them, in the house's register, the way we always have." She folded her hands more tightly in her lap. "In the years before the split, a child born in our valley drew its first breath inside the time it took the midwife to clear its mouth. Every one. Since the split, the midwives have been writing down how long they wait." She did not look away from the Sovereign. "The longest so far was the length of a full prayer, said slowly. The child lived. Some of them haven't. And at the other end the old are dying slow, and some of them don't go when they should, and their families sit with them days past the hour, holding hands that have already gone cold." She drew a breath. "The tide goes out and comes in wrong. That's what my brother means. It's in our ledgers, in ink, if you'd like to see them."

Naiser let that sit in the room.

"And the Wellsprings," he said. "Do not forget the Wellsprings. The Seikai did not only strike at the Soul Plane. They drank from the Wellsprings to do it, and what they put back into them afterward was not clean. There are springs in the south that taste of it now, to anyone with the tongue to taste. That corruption does not stay where it is put. It spreads, the way rot spreads through a granary. Every practitioner who draws on those springs in the coming years will be drawing a little of it into himself, and most of them will never know."

He paused. When he spoke again his voice had changed, not much, a little lower, a little rougher at the edges, like a blade that has been honed too far.

"If it were not for my father," he said, "there would be no Soul Plane left to be fractured. He went into the seam when the Seikai first forced it, four winters ago, when the war turned to its worst. He went alone, because a door like that will only take one. And he held it." He did not look at Naevra. He could feel her not looking at him. "He held it for four years. He never came back. Everything that still lives in the spirit side of this world, and everything that will be born out of it, is living on the time he bought."

The stream ran on under the boards. Somewhere far back in the hall the maid set down a pot, very softly.

"So." Naiser drew himself up. "You ask what the Yukari want. I'll tell you plainly, Sovereign, because I think you're a man who would rather be told plainly. We want the spiritual balance of this physical world kept. That's all. That's the whole of it. We want it kept, and we want the great families, yours and the Moto both, to understand the weight of what you're doing every time you invoke. Every time you call on something older than you are and ask it to lend you its strength. That is not a small thing. It has never been a small thing. And the war happened because too many people forgot that."

He was silent for a moment.

"I'll be honest with you about one more thing," he said, "because if I'm not, you'll learn it some other way and you'll never trust me again. The fault was not only theirs. It was ours as well. Our own insolence." He said the word as though it tasted of the tea. "The Seikai were our making. Not by blood, or not only. They were made by our refusal to believe that anyone outside this house could ever come to understand what we understood. We held the spiritual knowledge close. We let it be known that no one else could reach it. And when someone did reach it, on their own, without our teaching and without our caution, there was no one to tell them where the edges were, and there was a house that had already decided they were a threat. We made them into what they became. Then we went to war with them for it."

Naevra's hands had stilled in her lap. The fine white threads that sometimes hung in the air around her face when she was reading had come out, faintly, catching the light from the screens like cobweb in a barn door, and they had drawn very slightly taut. Naiser did not need to look at her to feel it.

"That is why I'm going to ask you for what I'm going to ask you for," he said.

He laid one hand flat on the low table.

"If there is to be peace between our two great families, and accommodation, then promises will have to be made, and they'll have to be kept. The first is this. You will never use the spiritual knowledge the Yukari hold, or anything learned from us, to fuel a war campaign. Not against the Moto, not against anyone. Not to win a battle and not to end one. If our knowledge goes out of this valley at all, it goes out to mend, never to take."

He did not wait for a response.

"The second. We will not be made into a banner. We will not become the product of anyone's propaganda, the way the Mahuo were." His jaw tightened. "You know what happened to the Mahuo. Everyone knows. They let their name be carried at the front of other people's armies until there was nothing left of it but the carrying. And when the time came, the Moto stood back and allowed us to slaughter them, because a house that has been turned into a symbol is very easy to destroy and no one weeps for a symbol." He let a breath out through his nose. "I will not let that happen to us. Not for you, and not for anyone."

He turned his hand over on the table, palm up.

"I'm glad you killed Gimbzo," he said. "Truly. He deserved worse than he got. But I want you to understand that he was only one piece of it. One piece of the corruption that exists in the Inner World, and not the largest. The Tenrai and the other lines of the Moto are drawing together as we speak, every one of them, and they mean to force their own people into submission. They are already doing it. They're starving them of what they need, grain and salt and medicine, and calling it order." He paused. "And their people are fighting back."

"In the north most of all," Naevra said quietly. "There are three valleys in the Stark country that have stopped paying the Tenrai tithe. Not stopped being able to. Stopped." The faintest edge came into her voice. "The Tenrai sent collectors. The collectors came back. Every one of them, with nothing in their hands and nothing taken from them, and every one of them walked the whole way home on foot because the Starks had kept their horses. That's in our reports as well."

Naiser nodded.

"I've grown very interested," he said, "in the revolutions in those regions. In the houses that are standing up. In the Starks particularly. Their knowledge, and their might, which is greater than anyone south of them has yet admitted." He looked at Shaka-Al steadily. "And you, Sovereign, are a piece of that puzzle. Your house fought the Moto and survived it. Whatever you decide to do next will tell every one of those valleys whether they are alone."

He drew his hand back from the table and rested it on his knee again.

"But I don't want you to leave this room thinking the Yukari mean to expand," he said. "That we've won one war and are measuring ourselves for the next. We are not. We have no wish for more land. When Sum-gol is settled, we intend to see the royal line of the Mahuo restored there, whatever is left of it, and to hand them the governance of it and step back. We have no want and no desire to control the fate of other peoples, or their spirits. That is the Moto's error. It will not be ours."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Which brings me to the last condition," he said, "and it is the one I will not move on. Your house, and every house that comes to an accord with ours, will give up any intention of controlling or manipulating the spirit world without our direct counsel and our teaching. I don't say that because we want to stand over you. I say it because the last people who reached into the Soul Plane without anyone to show them where the edges were broke it, and my father went in after them, and he isn't coming back." He looked at the Sovereign for a long moment. "Learn from us. We'll teach you. I'd sooner teach the whole world than bury another father for it. But no one touches the spirit side alone again."

Then he was silent. He had said what he came to say.

Naevra lifted her cup and drank, and set it down without a sound, and looked up at the two Zettari across the table, the Sovereign first and then the Crown Prince, with the same perfect composure she had worn since they came in. Only Naiser, who had known her all her life, could see what it cost her.

"My brother hasn't mentioned the other matter," she said. "The one in your letter." Her voice did not change at all. "I'll say it, then, so that it's said and no one has to wonder. Whatever is decided about that, it is decided after this. After the terms, not before them, and not instead of them. Nobody at this table is a payment." She inclined her head, very slightly, toward the Sovereign. "Not on our side, and not on yours."

Naiser said nothing. He did not need to. He sat with his hands on his knees and the katana lying sheathed at his right hand with its edge turned toward himself, and his sister kneeling quiet and white at his left, and the stream running loud beneath the floor, and he waited for the Sovereign of Kushara to answer.

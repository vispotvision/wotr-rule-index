*The Sum-gol Post, at the end of the Kessen road, the ninth morning after the letter; the hour the rice-mist lifts off the terraces*

The Yukari had accepted his terms.

Cozbi had written them himself, at his own sorting table, in the Post's olive ink, on the morning after the boy asked, and sent them by his own crow with the Yukari paying the carriage. There had been a dozen of them. The boy was to be taught the spirit, the Yukari way, by the Lord of the Cedar Enclave himself and nobody lesser. The boy was to come home to Sum-gol at the turning of every season, and his uncle was to be told of every wound. The boy was to take no oath to the Yukari, and to eat at no table where he was not a guest. And the boy was to be accompanied, at all times, by one of the Mahuo's own, who would answer to the Mahuo and to nobody else. Naiser Yukari had sent back a single sheet in a narrow severe hand that Cozbi had read nine times. *Every term. Entered.* And a smoke-stain at the bottom of it where the leaf had burned too near the paper, which Cozbi had chosen to read as a courtesy.

He stood now at the top of the Post's steps in the white fur and the olive, looking down the valley, and the valley was green.

It was spring in Sum-gol. The terraces went down from the house in their long stair to the river, every one of them brimming with water and new rice, so green it hurt, and the mist was lifting off them in the first sun in soft white sheets, *seuruk, seuruk*, like linen drawn off a bed. The salt pans below caught the light in a hundred flat mirrors. The iron hills stood up beyond them, rust and plum. Somewhere a cuckoo. Somewhere the couriers in the yard shouting about a lame mule. And down at the bottom of the valley where the Kessen road came in over the river bridge, a carriage was coming, slowly, black lacquer and silver-violet, behind four gray horses, with the Yukari crest on its door.

Cozbi looked at the boy beside him.

Hiromi was standing very straight in a new coat, olive like his uncle's because he had asked for it to be, with his traveling bundle at his feet and his hair combed flat with water and already springing back up. He was trying to look as if he were not frightened. He was trying so hard that the trying showed in every line of him, the way the grain shows in a board when the varnish is too thin, and Cozbi loved him so much in that moment that it was difficult to breathe.

The boy had the power to change the world. Cozbi had known it since the mud, since the day he went down on one knee in the ruin of his sister's house and put his hand on the boy's head and felt, under his palm, something in the small skull that answered him. The blood is the blood. But it was more than blood. Hiromi was not spiteful. Three years ago the war had come down the valley and taken his mother under a roof, and anything could have grown in the hole it left: Cozbi had seen what grows in those holes, in other boys, in himself. In Hiromi what grew was something stubborn and green and patient, like a seed under snow that has decided it will be a flower when the spring comes and does not mind how long the winter is. Ambition, the old men in the village would have called it, and shaken their heads. Cozbi did not shake his head. He had looked at the boy every day for three years and thought, *this one will bloom*. He only wished he could be sure the spring would be kind.

He could not be sure. So he would send a little of the winter with him.

"Hiromi," he said. "Come here a moment. I have a present for you."

He went down the steps into the yard, where the couriers stopped shouting about the mule and stood back to the walls, the way they always did, and the crows on the Post's roof went quiet along the ridge-tiles one after another. He put out his left hand, palm down, a hand's breadth above the packed earth of the yard.

[a patient host, warm, Sum-gol's soft Korean vowels under a lord's Common] "*Summoning,*" said Cozbi Mahuo.

The air in front of him took a breath.

That was what it always looked like, to the people who saw it. A Founding is a building more than a calling. He had explained it to the Accord's men once at length, with great enjoyment, and watched them go pale. First the boundary: a small Realm unfolding out of nothing in the yard, the size of a doorway, a place where there had been no place, and the light round it bending toward its edge and away from it at once, the way light bends at his shoulder when he is angry; the yard tasted of struck flint, and the couriers flinched. Then the core: a point of his own Crystal pressed into the middle of the Realm, the way a potter presses a thumb into clay to start the hollow, a heartbeat from the wrong side of a door, *kung... kung...*, and under it a fine crystal ticking, *ttak-ttak-ttak-ttak*, as Eidolyn laid down the shape. Then the charter, written into her in the moment of her making: what she was, and what she was for.

He had chosen the template on the long night after the letter, with the light under his door, talking to his mother about it. The Mahuo elders had a fox. Every house in the south has a fox in its stories; the Mahuo's was a nine-tailed spirit of the crossings, a creature of the place between the living road and the other one, who carried children over fords and old women over bridges and played tricks on everybody else, and the elders called her the Bridge, and said of her, approvingly, that she had never once in a thousand years done anything the way she was asked. He had laughed when he chose it. His mother had not. *She'll be a handful*, his mother had said, in the room, in the present tense. *Good*, he had said. *So is he.*

The shape stood up in the doorway of the Realm and stepped out of it into the yard.

She was white, as a nine-tailed fox should be. White hair, long and thick and falling past her waist, with the sheen on it that snow has under a moon. Two tall white fox's ears standing up out of it, lined inside with the palest pink, already turning, flicking, one forward and one back, listening to everything in the yard at once. Nine tails behind her, enormous, soft, swaying in nine directions like a sheaf of white grass in a wind, and at the tip of each one, as if each had been dipped in a pot of paint, a hand's length of vermilion. The Mahuo color. The color he had sworn not to wear until Sum-gol was his by his own hand. He would not carry it. He had given it to the thing that would guard the last of his blood. She wore a maid's dress of the Post, olive and white, with a long white apron and a ruffled cap pinned between her ears, which was the charter's doing and slightly crooked, which was not. Her eyes were gold, like his. They were enormous.

They found him and lit up like lamps.

[bright, high, delighted, a fox's yip under the words] "*Hello, Master!*"

Cozbi smiled. He did not smile like that often any more, and he felt it pull at the thin skin over his cheekbones like a stiff sleeve.

"Hello, Dari," he said. Bridge. He had named her on the long night too. "I want you to go with my nephew, Hiromi, wherever he goes, and keep him safe. You'll be his maid. You'll look after his things and his meals and his sleep, and you'll put yourself between him and anything that means him harm." He held her eyes. "And you'll report to me any change in him, or around him, that matters. Any wound. Any oath anyone asks of him. Anything the Yukari teach him that I would want to know. Do you understand?"

The ears went flat, then straight up, then flat again. *Pparuk.* All nine tails wagged at once, and the wind of it lifted the dust in the yard.

"*Ouuuu,*" said Dari. "That sounds *fun!*"

"It is a duty," said Cozbi, "and a serious one. Try not to cause too much trouble."

Dari bit her lip. Then she stuck her tongue out at him, very slightly, pink, and the gold eyes went to two crescent moons, and Cozbi understood with a sinking certainty and an enormous, unexpected joy that the elders had been right about her, and that he had written the charter exactly as they had told the story, and that she was never going to do anything the way she was asked.

He felt the thread go out of him to her. It was a new weight on his chest, among all the others, a fine black-violet line from his heart to hers, and it would cost him a little every second for as long as she stood in the world. He had done the sum. It was less than he recovered. It was one more thing to carry. Hiromi had said *you're breaking*, at the tea table, and he had been right, and Cozbi had founded a fox anyway, because he would rather break than send the boy down the Kessen road with nobody of his own.

Dari had already turned round.

She had found the boy. Her ears went forward and stayed there, both of them, pointing at Hiromi like a pair of hands, and her eyes went enormous, and then small, and then enormous again, and her nine tails went still all at once in the way a cat goes still before it springs.

"Oh," said Dari. "*Oh.* Is *that* him?"

"That is my nephew," said Cozbi. "Be gentle with..."

She was across the yard. She did not seem to cross it; she was at the steps and then she was in front of the boy, bent at the waist, her face level with his, and she had his cheeks in both her hands and was squeezing them, gently, so that his mouth came out in a little round pucker like a fish's, and his eyes went wide over her fingers.

"*Ouuuuuuu,*" said Dari. "You're such a *cuuuuuuuuuuutie!*"

"Mmph," said Hiromi.

"*Look* at you!" She turned his face this way and that by the cheeks, inspecting him like a melon at market. "Look at your little *coat!* You've got a little coat like Master's! Ouuu, and your *hair*, it's sticking up at the back, *wait*, I'll fix it, no, I won't, I like it, *Master*, can I keep him?"

"You are keeping him," said Cozbi. "That is rather the point."

She let go of the cheeks, and the boy took one breath, and she hugged him.

It was not a hug a person could do. She picked him up off the steps by the middle with his traveling bundle still in one hand and spun him round twice, his boots swinging, and then all nine tails came round from behind her and wrapped him, every one, one over another over another, like a child being bundled into nine white winter blankets at once, until there was nothing to be seen of Cozbi's nephew but one olive sleeve and a boot sticking out of a great soft white heap with vermilion tips, and from somewhere in the middle of it a muffled, furious sound.

"*So soft,*" said Dari, rocking the heap from side to side with her eyes shut in bliss. "*Sooo gentle.* He's like a little warm bun."

"Dari," said Cozbi. "Please try not to suffocate him before he gets to his training."

The tails opened. All at once, like a flower. Hiromi fell out of the middle of them onto the steps on his backside, with his hair standing straight up off his head in a white cloud of fox-fur and his face scarlet from the collar to the ears, and he scrambled backward up two steps on his hands and stared up at her.

[eleven, furious, mortified, a Sum-gol boy's Korean vowels] "*Who the hell are you?*"

Dari blinked at him. She tipped her head to one side, and then the other, the white ears following a moment later, and a look came over her face of such complete and shining stupidity that one of the couriers by the wall made a noise and had to turn away.

"I'm Dari!" she said. "*Dari*, Dari, after all!" She put both fists on her hips and leaned down to him, and all nine tails shot up behind her in a fan like a peacock's. "Your *personal helper!* Yup!" She considered. "And your maid. And your bridge. And your guard. And I'm meant to tell Master everything, so don't do anything you don't want Master to know about, okay?" She winked, enormously, with her whole face. "Or *do*, and I'll think about it!"

Hiromi looked past her at his uncle. Cozbi looked back at him, perfectly grave, and allowed one corner of his mouth to move.

"She was your great-grandmother's favorite story," he said. "I thought you should have her."

The carriage came into the yard.

It came in over the cobbles with its iron tires grinding, *deureureuk*, and the four gray horses blowing, and stopped at the foot of the steps, and the couriers stood back further to the walls, because the Yukari crest was on its door and every courier in Sum-gol had a grandmother who had told them what that crest had done in this valley. A footman jumped down and opened it. A smell came out of it before anyone did: cedar smoke and cold stone.

Naiser Yukari stepped down into the yard.

He was tall even stepping, long in the limb, composed in the way that some men are composed and others only quiet, and he wore Yukari gray with the silver-violet at the seams and a narrow severe longblade at his hip, carried more like punctuation than a weapon. He had a rolled leaf between two fingers, unlit. He looked at the Post, and at the yard, and at the couriers against the walls, and then up the steps at Cozbi, and inclined his head the exact distance that one head of house owes another, and not one hair further.

"Lord Mahuo."

"Lord Yukari." Cozbi did not come down the steps. He was the host. The guest comes up.

Behind Naiser, a girl climbed down out of the carriage by herself, ignoring the footman's hand.

She was about Hiromi's age, a little taller, very straight, with the Yukari composure already on her like a coat cut too big for her that she meant to grow into. Her hair was purple, a clear soft violet like wisteria in the rain, lighter than the black-violet of her father's aura, long and fine and tied back at the nape with a gray cord, and it was the first thing anyone in the yard looked at, and she knew it, and lifted her chin. Her eyes were gray. They went over the yard the way her father's had, the Post, the walls, the couriers, Cozbi on the steps; and then they stopped on Dari, and widened.

Cozbi saw it. Everyone saw it. For one moment the Yukari composure came off the girl completely, the way a bird's alarm goes out of it when it sees a thing too strange to be afraid of, and she simply stared at the tall white fox-woman with the nine vermilion-tipped tails and the crooked maid's cap, and her mouth came a little open, and what was on her face was wonder. *She's beautiful*, the face said. Cozbi watched her think it, and watched her not say it.

Then her eyes went to the boy sitting on the steps at the fox-woman's feet, with his hair full of fur and his face red, and Hiromi, who had been about to be furious, looked at the girl with the purple hair, and grinned at her.

It was his ordinary grin, the one he had for the couriers and the crows. Open, easy, a little crooked, as if the morning were a joke the two of them were in on. He did not seem to know he was meant to be frightened of her. He did not seem frightened of anything, sitting on the steps with fox-fur in his hair. The girl looked at him as if he were a word in a language she had not been taught, and frowned.

"Senna," said Naiser. "This is Hiromi, of House Mahuo." He did not look at his daughter while he said it; he was looking at Cozbi. "He will be your partner in training. I'll be teaching him the spirit, as I have been teaching you. The way of the corridor. The way of the stone." A pause. "You will be fair to him."

"Yes, Father," said Senna Yukari.

Hiromi got up off the steps. He brushed the fur off his coat, mostly, and came down two steps and stopped in front of her, and bowed, the Sum-gol way, from the waist with the hands at the sides.

"It's a pleasure to train with you," he said.

Senna looked at the top of his head while he was bowed. Then, slowly, and a little late, she bowed back.

Dari had sat down on the steps beside where Hiromi had been, with her knees drawn up and her chin on them and her tails spread round her like a fallen white umbrella, and she was looking from the boy to the girl and back, and her eyes were going big, and then small, and then big again.

"*Wuuuu,*" she said, very quietly, to nobody. "I *cannot wait.*"

Naiser came up the steps.

He came up three of them, so that he stood just below the host, which was correct, and lit the leaf from the small coal-box his footman held up to him, and drew on it, and let the smoke out in a slow gray skein that hung in the morning air in front of the two of them and did not drift.

Cozbi watched the smoke stand still and did not look away from it.

"You have my terms," he said.

"I have your terms. Every one is entered."

"Then I'll add one, so that it's entered too." Cozbi did not raise his voice. He never did. He did not try to hide anything in it either, and for a moment the yard heard everything that was under it, and the crows on the ridge-tiles went up all at once into the air, *pudeudeuk*, forty of them, and wheeled away over the terraces. The light at his shoulder bent toward a point and away from it. The air tasted of struck flint. "If you do anything to Hiromi, Lord Yukari, anything, or let anything be done to him under your roof, I will come up the Kessen road to your cedars, and I will kill every last one of you. Every one. Your house, your hall, your stone. I will not need an army. I am the Mahuo." He looked down the step at the girl with the purple hair, and back at her father, and smiled the host's smile. "I would start with the guests."

The smoke between them bent once.

It bent the way smoke bends in Naiser Yukari's presence when a lie is told inside it, and then it straightened, and stood. Cozbi saw Naiser see it straighten. The sentence had been true. The Entered Word took it, and set it, the way a thermoset sets, and it would not come out again.

Naiser drew on the leaf.

"Then let it be entered both ways," he said. His voice was quiet and level and very tired. "If any harm comes to your nephew under my roof, it is mine to answer for. Not yours to collect. Mine to answer." He let the smoke out. "My father went into the seam in Ashura so that the dead would not walk out of it into our valleys. I know who is inside it, Lord Mahuo. I know why you want it opened. One day you and I will have to speak about that." He looked down at the boy on the steps, who was trying to get the last of the fur off his sleeve while the fox-woman helped by putting more on it. "Not today. Today a boy is coming to learn. Let him learn."

Cozbi said nothing for a long moment. The smoke stood between them.

"Entered," he said at last.

He went down the steps then, past Naiser, to his nephew, and went down on one knee on the stone of the Post's own steps, and put his hand on the boy's head, and said nothing at all for a while, the way he had in the mud. Hiromi held still under it. Then Cozbi stood, and stepped back, and the boy picked up his bundle and went down into the yard and climbed into the Yukari carriage behind the girl with the purple hair, and the fox-woman went after him in a long white rush with her tails catching in the door, and the footman shut it on them, and inside the carriage somebody said *ow* and somebody else said *sorry, sorry, ouuuu, sorry!*

The carriage went out of the yard and down the Kessen road toward the bridge.

Cozbi stood at the top of the steps and watched it all the way down the valley, through the green of the terraces, over the river, until it went round the shoulder of the iron hills and was gone. He felt the thread go with it, down the road, stretching, the fine black-violet line from his chest running away south and east after a carriage he could no longer see, and every second it cost him a little, and every second he paid it.

"He's gone, Mother," he said to the empty yard, in the present tense, quite softly. "He's gone to learn."

*The cedar wood on the Kessen road, inside the dome of ABI; the last seconds before it falls*

"Leave me behind," said Muken.

He said it to Hayato and to his aunt, who had come up out of the slush onto the road beside the Amagiri with her face bloody and her hands burned black and her empty scabbard at her hip, and the words came out of him low and slow, stone on stone, and the burning branches in the oven swung all together.

"Take the others," he said, "and go. They can't live through what's coming. You saw what it did to Aldo." His forearms were cracked inside his sleeves and holding Tsugigane up cost him something in each hand like a nail driven slowly. "Take Amelia and Lorn and the copper lord and get them down the road."

Kiyome looked at him.

The silver rings in her eyes had turned when the shard went through her face and they had not turned back. They lay at a slant now, like ice that has cracked and refrozen crooked. She did not wipe the blood off her chin.

[clear, level, the old high register of the house, and breaking] "I don't want to leave without you."

"Stop it." He heard his voice go hard and did not stop it. "Stop that. I'm the king. That was an order. A direct order, Aunt." The word came out before he had decided to use it; he saw it go into her like the shard had, and saw her break, and mend. "This isn't your fight. It's mine. It was always mine. It came up the road because of my blood and my chair, and nobody else is going to be cooked in a wood for it." He looked past her at the furnace, ten paces off, with the Owl's sword in its fist and the blood running down the back of its neck. "If he wants a Kōkan, I'll show him a Kōkan."

Kiyome's lips moved. He knew what they were saying. He had heard her say it at the Foot, kneeling, with the white cord in her hands. *The red before the white.* The oath that bound her to him and did not bind him to anything at all. He could not command her; she had told him that at the Yukari's table. But the oath could. She served the red line. The red line had given her an order.

She closed her eyes.

"Hayato," she said.

From the foot of the bank came the copper lord's voice, hoarse, sixty-one years old, with the drum still under it holding every heart in the oven.

"I'll not obey you, boy," said Tetsugen Akagane. He was on his feet, with the bow in his good hand and the other arm a ruin. "I told you that in the Hall. I said I'd follow." He coughed, and the copper drum stumbled and caught itself. "Following you's trusting you know what you're doing. So I'll follow you down the road, and we'll find out the first time you're wrong." The gray head turned to Hayato. "Do it, then. Before I change my mind."

Hayato did it.

He did it in the gilt fox mask with its narrow smiling eyes, along the last of his own indigo, which was down to a strip three paces wide now with the red eating it from both verges. He touched Tetsugen's shoulder and the copper lord was gone, down some road, and Muken saw Hayato's hand stop for an instant in the air after, the way a man's hand stops when he reaches for a cup that is not where he left it. He touched Lorn, who was on his knees over his sword with his whole body white with rime and shaking so hard his mail rang, and who said through his locked jaw, "Sire," and was gone. He touched Kiyome, who did not look at Muken again, and she was gone. Each time the hand stopped after.

Amelia would not be touched.

She had come back across the road from the fallen cedar with her mantle still smoking, the shield on her arm and the axe in her ruined hand, and she stood in front of Hayato with her feet set and looked at the mask.

[flat, a lieutenant giving a report, and the report was no] "I'm staying."

"Amelia." Muken did not turn round. "Go."

"No." Flat. "I'll be honest with you, because I don't know how to be any other way. I've stood in front of people for ten years. I don't know what I am with nobody behind me." Her voice did not change at all. "I'd like to find out some other day."

"Amelia," said Muken, and this time he did turn, and looked at her, with the gold eye, and let her see what it showed him. The hairline in her pauldron. The cracked shoulder under it. Every flaw in her, lit, that the furnace would find first. "Please."

She looked at him for a long moment. Nothing moved in her face, and nothing moved, and then something did, the second crack in the ice, and she said, in a voice he had never heard her use, very low: "Don't you *dare* die in a wood." And she put out her ruined hand to Hayato herself, palm up, as if she were handing him something, and Hayato touched it, and she was gone.

The road went out of Hayato every time. Muken could see it now, with the Eye: something going out of the slight man behind the mask with each touch, a line in him going dark, like a crack running backward until it was nothing.

"Now you," said Muken.

Hayato stood on the last yard of his road with the red coming in at his heels, and the mask turned toward the king.

"I'll keep the road open, sire," he said. "For when you change your mind." His voice was shaking. "It's the only one I've got left that goes home. I'll hold it. Call, and I'll come."

And he was gone, and the last of the indigo went with him, and the rain-sound stopped, and Muken was alone in the oven with the Sin of Wrath.

---

It was very quiet. The furnace roared, the cedars burned, the red dome glowed overhead with the smoke flattened under it, and it was quiet anyway, because there was nobody left in the wood to make a sound but the two of them. The copper drum had gone with the copper lord. Muken's heart, left to itself in the heat, broke into a gallop, *thudthudthudthud*, and he let it.

The Sin of Wrath stood ten paces off with the Owl's sword in his hand, its edge white along its length, and the blood from the back of his neck running down into his coat, and looked at him.

"You sent them away," said Shōki Kurenai Moto. Hoarse. Wondering, almost. "All of them."

"Yes."

"They were all you had. I watched you. I counted." The living eye went over the empty road as if it were reading a ledger with every line struck out. "And you sent them away so that I could not take them in front of you."

"Yes," said Muken. "That's what a king's for."

He did not know he believed it until he heard himself say it. Then he did.

He came up out of the crater. His knee screamed at him and he let it scream, and stood on the road with Tsugigane in both cracked hands and the dead note still pouring out of the blade, and looked at the man his family had made.

[young northern Kharven, a cutter's vowels, low and slow, each word a stone on a cairn] "You want to spread your hate. You want to kill me because of what my family did to you." The burning branches swung. "Fine. You came up the Kessen road for a Kōkan. Here I am. I'll not run, and I'll not send anyone else to stand in front of you for me. I'll face you myself." He took one breath, in through the nose until his ribs ached, the Kharven breath, and out. "And I'll face all of them. Every house my blood wronged. Every name in the cellars and the burnings and the closed books. I'll face them myself, one at a time, the way I'm facing you, and I'll answer for it, and I'll not hide behind a chair." He set his feet. "By my honor. In front of you. That's my oath."

He felt it take.

A vow spoken aloud is paid on the body at once, Faisal had told him, kneeling under the Bell: the Continuum does not extend credit. It took him in the left eye. The gold seam in the iris tore, the way it had torn on the stair at Halcrest, wider than it had ever gone, and a line of blood ran out of the corner of the eye and down his cheek like a tear, and the heat behind it went from a brand to a white light, and the Eye opened further than it had ever been allowed to open, and he saw.

The Sin of Wrath was shaking his head.

"You think," said Shōki, slowly, "that a little sentiment will change what happens." The words came out of him low and then not low, rising, the furnace rising under them. "You think you can stand in the road with your sword and your oath and your sweet sad face and say *I'll answer for it*, and it will be *answered?*" He laughed, and the laugh was blood. "*IT WILL NOT CHANGE WHAT HAPPENS. YOU CANNOT ESCAPE THIS!* They said they would answer for it too! Seven chairs! They answered for it with my wife's *head!*" The aura came up off him in a roar that shook the dome. "*I WILL TORTURE YOU, KŌKAN, THE WAY THEY TORTURED ME! I WILL TAKE IT ALL!*"

He came forward.

And as he came, the oven came with him.

Muken saw it begin with the Eye, and understood a heartbeat later what he was seeing, and his hands went cold on the grip the way they had not gone cold since the cellars. The dome of ABI was falling. Hayato had said twenty seconds, and the twenty seconds were gone, and the card said that when the field fell, everything it had kept would leave at once, a blast from the boundary inward and outward that would flatten the wood. Instead of leaving, it was being pulled.

The Sin of Wrath was pulling it in.

The Hall Irons on his wrists carried part of the boundary of his Domain; it was on his card in the copper book, and the copper lord had read it to Muken at the Seat, and Muken had not understood it until now. The Sin had hold of his own field by the irons, the way a fisherman has hold of a net by its cord, and as the field fell he was drawing it in. The red dome over the wood came down. It came down out of the sky in a great slow collapse toward the man in the middle, the whole hemisphere of it folding in on him from every side like a bubble of red glass drawn back into the pipe that blew it, and everything it had held came in with it: every breath of heat the oven had kept for two and a half minutes, the burning cedars' heat and the crater's and the steam's and the Owl's and the copper lord's and Aldo Brennick's, all of it pouring inward across the wood toward the Sin of Wrath in long visible ribbons of shimmering air, *hwoooooooooooom*, and the cold came in behind it. Muken felt it. As the heat went past him toward the center the air on his face went from an oven to a spring evening in a single breath, and the steam on the road froze to rime, and the fallen cedars, robbed of their heat, went black and smoking and out.

All of it went into the Sin. Into the Hall Irons. Down his arm. Into the Owl's sword.

And the Work bent.

Muken had heard of it from Faisal and never seen it. Every line of the Moto carries the Works of Hataraki, and the Kurenai carry the Work of Spirit under the shadow of Ruins; in the Sin of Wrath the Spirit had gone out at the block, and Ruins was all that was left, alone, turned on its own corruption, which is annihilation. The Work of Ruins ends a thing. Muken's own, held to his oath, ends only what is already dead. The Sin's ended whatever it was pointed at. It had been Obsession that bent it that way, three hundred years of it, the whole of a soul held in one shape so long that the Work had taken the shape too; and now it took the Karmic Fire, and the whole stored heat of a fallen Domain, and the ember-orange and the black-violet of the man's own aura, and it bent all of them into one line.

The flame became a spear.

It ran out along the Owl's sword from the Sin's fist, a long blade of fire twice the length of the steel, ember-orange at the root where it came out of the Hall Irons and white further out and then a color past white, a color that hurt the Eye; and as it ran out it narrowed, and narrowed, the whole stored heat of the oven being pressed down into less and less room, the way a smith draws a bar out under the hammer until the whole of the iron is in a point, until at the very tip of the Byakuya katana there was nothing at all to see. A point. A place in the air so condensed with fire and with ending that the light round it bent inward toward it and did not come out again. It made no sound. Everything round it made sound. The air screamed going into it, a thin high whine, *iiiiiiiiiiiiii*, and the frost on the road ran away from it in cracks.

Muken looked at it with the Eye wide open and the blood running down his cheek, and the Eye looked for a crack in it, the way it looked for a crack in everything.

There was none. A thing truly whole gives the Eye nothing. The point at the tip of the spear was the most whole thing he had ever seen. It had been pressed so hard there was no room left in it for a flaw.

Behind it, at the back of the Sin of Wrath's neck, the dead crack was ringing. It was ringing louder than it had ever rung. The spear had pulled the whole of the man's Obsession into one line through his arm, and the line ran back along his arm and over his shoulder and into the old fracture at the nape, and pressed on it, and the sour note in it was rising, wavering, so loud now that Muken could hear it over the scream of the air going into the point.

And under it, still, that other note. The small one. The living one. Held down.

Tsugigane rang in his hands. Its silver thread, where his sister's needles were folded into the steel, was humming.

He was alone. There was nobody behind him now. No Amelia, no Lorn, no aunt, no copper lord, no Hayato. Nobody for HIBIWARE to crack if it woke. He could feel it under HIBI the way you feel a river under ice, the web of it, every hairline flaw in everything the rings would pass through: the Sin, the spear's steel, the road, the cedars, his own bones. Faisal had forbidden it. Faisal had said it would fracture his Crystal along its grief, at Stage V, if he called it. Faisal had said it belonged to Refraction and he was a boy at Splintering with a man's coat on his shoulders. But Faisal had forbidden it because of the people. And the people were gone.

The Sin of Wrath came on with the spear.

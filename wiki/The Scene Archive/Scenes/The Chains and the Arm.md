*The Crucible, the dark air above the split floor, moments after Naifuru*

---

Sodoku was still bleeding from the nose when his brother let the Calamity out, and he felt the world's fractures, with a strange recalcitrance, change their minds.

He felt it through the Kamigan first. It was a flicker in the half-lit sigils that had read his father's cracks a moment earlier with such pitiless, adamantine clarity. The crazing that Naifuru had laid across the dark air was still there, a lattice of fine bright seams hanging in the gloaming over the Crucible, humming at the world's own note. Then the violet-black miasma of distortion poured off the man behind him, and the seams began to stutter. The hum lost its harmonic. Where there had been one deep, coherent note, the old Moto note his father had struck into the world, there were suddenly a thousand discordant notes at once, a cacophonous din, every crack ringing at a different pitch.

Sodoku understood it, and the understanding was gelid and exact. Naifuru was a resonance. A resonance lives on coherence: every push arriving at the same moment in the same swing, every echo adding to the last. The Calamity was coherence's anathema. It was noise poured into the oscillation, a turbulence that struck every crack at a random phase, so that the pushes that should have added cancelled, and the standing wave that had been building toward rupture smeared into a meaningless tremor. It did not fight the Hibiware's strength. It ate its meaning.

His brother breathed in, and out.

The space round them bent with a slow torsion. Sodoku watched the fractures in the air stop running and begin, instead, to pull apart. Each bright seam split down its length, its two faces sundered and drawn away from one another as though a hand had taken hold of either lip and pulled, and every crack that split branched, and every branch split again, until the lattice Naifuru had woven was a fraying, outward-spiralling web, all its threads fleeing the centre. A crack that branches spends itself into dross. Sodoku had seen it in the forge-yards at Kharven-Seat, where a flawed billet, half-annealed, under the hammer would sometimes shatter into a hundred harmless shards rather than one clean, killing split: every branch takes a share of the strain, and none is left with enough to run. The orders of reality round them were spiralling outward, the fractures dissipating into a vertiginous filigree of nothing.

Then the chains went out.

They came off his brother's arms in a long whipping coil, sable links shot through with violet light, and wrapped three times round Muken's right forearm, from the elbow down to the wrist that still held Asami. The links bit. Where they touched, the algid seeded flesh smoked.

Muken looked down at the chains with a saturnine calm and closed his left hand on them.

"You are still fuelled by anger," he said.

It was not a rebuke. It was the voice in which he had once said *your stance is too wide* on the brumal practice yard, a plain statement of a fault, set down for the student to pick up or not.

Then the dead king pulled.

He did not pull straight. He turned. He set his weight on the dark air the way a man sets it on hoarfrosted ice, wound the chain once round his left fist to shorten it, and swung his whole body round in a circle with the chain as the spoke and his brother at the end of it, the way the hammer-throwers at the northern fairs spin with an iron ball on a wire until the ball sings. And as he turned he drew the chain in, hand over hand, shortening the circle, and the shorter the circle grew the faster his brother went round it, a spinning skater pulling in his arms, the same speed packed, inexorable, into a smaller ring until the air screamed round the two of them in a spiralling cyclone of ash and violet light. Once round, and twice, with a skirl like pipes. Then Muken let the circle open at the bottom of its arc and brought the chain down like a thresher's flail, percussive, and his brother went down with it, out of the sky, toward the floor of the arena.

The chains did not break.

Each revolution, Sodoku saw, had cost the king. Every turn of the circle had dragged the links tighter round his arm, and the links were Calamity, and Calamity on flesh was necrotic ruin. The skin of the forearm had blackened to an eschar where they bit. The muscle beneath had gone grey and ropy, the sinews standing out like scorched cord, fibre tearing with a fine sound like wet linen torn along the weave. Smoke rose off the arm in a dense acrid column.

Muken looked at the ruined arm with the same phlegmatic patience he had looked at his son.

Then he broke it, himself, with a deliberation that bordered on rectitude. He set the forearm against his own knee and bore down, and the bones went with a dull, gristly crack, both of them, the radius and the ulna, snapping cleanly along lines Sodoku could see in the Kamigan before they parted: his father had read his own fractures first and chosen the ones that would carry the Calamity's corruption away with the broken piece. The ruined section of the arm hung for an instant at a sickening, livid angle, black and smoking.

It reset.

The Fractura that read the cracks had read where the bone was friable enough to break, and now Petralon closed them. Sodoku saw the bone draw back into line on its own, the jagged ends grinding together and locking, the old scar-lattice spreading across the break like frost across a pane, tough, nacreous, faintly glowing. Fixatio held the reset bone in place with obdurate force, the way a splint holds a limb. But it was more than healing, and Sodoku knew it, and the knowledge turned his stomach. His father's body was a Requiem construct in seeded, cadaverous flesh. It did not grow forward. It *returned*. Borin had built that soul to repeat the self it had been, and the flesh round it, whenever it was torn far enough from its pattern, went back to the pattern the way a bent spring goes back to its rest, with none of the hysteresis of living tissue. The arm did not mend. It reinitialised. The blackened skin sloughed away in curling flakes and beneath it lay the forearm exactly as it had been at the moment of the king's death, scarred, brown, whole, its patina of old scars intact and their amber lines glowing.

Except that now the scar-lattice ran through it like the iron armature in a revetment. The second wound of a kind always cost his father less than the first; that had been true in life, and the Requiem had kept it. The Calamity had found the arm once. It would find it harder now.

Muken flexed the fingers and drew the arm back, unhurried as eventide.

Sodoku was already moving.

He had been moving since the chains went out. He had read the opening before his father made it: the instant of the self-inflicted break, the arm hanging useless, the king's guard open, a postern left unbarred down the whole right side. He came up off the broken floor with Kurosetsu in both hands and the frost-crazed blade dragging a plume of white spindrift behind it, crossed the last of the maai in a single long step with his rear foot driving into the stone, and cut.

It was a kesa-giri. The most immemorial cut in the Moto school, the monk's-stole cut, it ran from the right shoulder, down across the body, to the left side. His hips turned into it. His weight came down through his front heel. The blade went down the diagonal with all of him behind it, and the frost on the steel shrieked with a high sibilance as it met the air.

His father put his arm in the way.

The arm that had just reinitialised, the fresh scar-lattice running through it. Muken raised it, palm out, and met Kurosetsu's edge with the inside of his forearm as calmly as a man raises his arm to ward off a branch, and the blade cleaved through the skin, through the muscle, into the bone, and stopped.

It stopped there. Sodoku felt it stop the way a man feels an axe stop in a knot of oak, a concussive shock that rang up through both his wrists into his shoulders. The edge was buried to a third of its width in his father's forearm. The scar-lattice had caught it. And along the blade, from the edge inward, Sodoku saw the frost of Kurosetsu begin to spread into the wound, white rime crawling across the dark blood, freezing the fibre, and saw the amber light in his father's scars rise to meet it, impact-stillness meeting winter-stillness inside the bone, the king absorbing the force of the blow and banking it, as he had always banked force, as scar and ledger for a heavier verdict later.

Father and son stood on the tenebrous air with the sword between them.

Muken looked at him over the blade. The Kamigan revolved slowly.

"Better," he said.

Malphas let her believe it.

He had seen the beam gathering at the tip of her finger, a point of white so bright it lit the mist round them like a lantern in caliginous fog, and he had felt it coming the way he felt everything now, through the Yukari nerves, a hinge in the dark about to swing. He could have bent it. He had bent her kunai a minute before with a knot of collapsed matter in his palm, and he could have done it again. He chose not to. He let the light come.

It came faster than light should come. She had called it tachyonic, or something in her had, and Malphas, who had read every paper on the subject that the Pyraeon library had ever been foolish enough to acquire, knew what the word was supposed to mean, and how much sophistry hid in it: a thing that could never travel slower than light, whose energy fell the faster it ran, a particle the equations allowed and the world had never shown anyone. Whether her beam was truly that, or only a working that wore the word, it did not matter now. It went through his left shoulder in a seam of candent fire, and it burned.

He let it.

He let the light's own push carry him. Light presses on what it strikes; every photon carries a tiny shove, and a beam bright enough to burn a hole through a man carries shove enough to stagger him, and he did not brace against it. He let his body be thrown back through the dark air by the pressure of her radiance as if he were a leaf in a bellows-blast, his crimson coat snapping, and when her blade came after the light, a diagonal cut from shoulder to hip, its non-metal edge superheated until it parted his flesh like steam parting snow, he let that land too. He let it all rend him. His new Zettari chest, dense as ashlar, opened under her blade in a long seared furrow. The wound cauterised as it was made; the flesh along its edges charred, crackled, flaked away into calcined nothing. Pain came up through the new nerves, real pain, a febrile scream of it from shoulder to hip, the first pain he had felt in two years, and he found to his surprise that he was grateful for it.

He hung forward on the dark air with his arms dangling, lugubrious as a marionette whose strings had all been cut at once.

Blood came up into his mouth, saline and hot. He spat it into the mist, a dark incarnadine gout, and it hissed where it struck the smoke of her compound. His head hung. Under the hood of crimson silk his eyes shimmered, the gold of the Moto blood gone dark and bloody, a crimson burning down at the bottom of it like coals under ash.

She had counted out his two mistakes to him like a scrivener marking a slate.

He let her count.

Then he moved.

There was no wind-up, no gathering, nothing she could have read. Every thing she had poured into him had gone into the bank: the tachyonic light, the superheated compound of her blade, the photoelectric current that had driven the mist, the bonds she had straightened between her particles into whatever she believed them to be. On contact with his flesh, all of it had malformed. The straight bonds had gone slack and tangled, the way the fibres of flax go slack in the retting pond; the compound had dissolved into a putrescent muck of its own constituents; the light had gone to soot. It was his: all that energy, swallowed and broken down and stored, a cloying wealth.

For the first time since the Becoming, he could spend it as fast as he had taken it.

The new body had given him back the path his turning needed, and his Conversion, frozen for so many years, had begun to move. He turned her light into motion, where before he had only ever been able to turn what he swallowed into heat or rot or the stygian star that hung over his palm. The energy of her own attack poured out of his bank into the dense Zettari muscle of his legs and back. The muscle took the impetus and *went*.

He crossed the space between them faster than her eyes could have built him in the mist, a precipitate blur. He was hanging broken in the air in front of her, and then the place where he had hung was empty, an afterimage of crimson silk smeared across the smoke, and his left hand, the hand with the lattice scar in its palm, the hand that seeded, was closed round her throat.

It closed gently. That was the worst of it, if she was the kind to notice: it closed with exactly the solicitude of a man lifting a glass by its stem.

He held her there in the mist, close enough that she could see the blood still wet on his lips and the seared furrow across his chest beginning, very slowly, to bubble at its edges with mephitic black.

"Seasoning," he said. His voice was soft and dulcet, resonant in the ruined chest. "You said your name was seasoning to what you hold." He tilted his head. "Salt preserves. That is all seasoning has ever been for. It keeps a thing from becoming what it would become if it were left alone."

His thumb rested lightly against the side of her neck, over the great vessel there, where he could feel her pulse beating against the scar like a fremitus.

"I am the opposite of salt."

*The waystone at the Kessen fork, past midnight, the aurora burning low; and the Tinker's Gorge, at the change of shift*

The pressure came up out of the ground like blood comes up through snow.

Shōki was standing at the fork where the drovers' road met the old Kessen road, under a stone lantern taller than he was, when the thread in him went taut. The aurora had sunk low by then, a long fringe of green and rose lying along the northern ridge like a hem of fire, and the snow at the fork was blue in its light and very still. Then, at his feet, it was not blue. Black-red came up through it in threads, the color of old blood held to a lamp, fine as hair at first and then thicker, crawling out of the frozen ground and up over his boots and up the folds of the hakama and in at the cracks of his skin, the way roots go into a wall. They found the thread under his Crystal and wound themselves round it and pulled.

Not a tug, this time. A grip.

He felt his Crystal take the strain. The whole lattice of him, Core and Layer and Shell, bowed toward the ground like a bough under wet snow. Something in his chest creaked that had never creaked before. The purple at the cracks of his skin dimmed to the color of a bruise, and for a moment the black-red was brighter than his own fire, crawling over him in a net, and the stone lantern at his shoulder threw his shadow on the snow in a color that was not his.

A voice came up the thread with it.

He heard all of it. He stood at the fork with the snow melting round his boots and the black-red net on his skin and listened to every word, and his face did not change. It mocked him. It told him it had watched him, every hour of the three hundred years, every breath on the slab, every curse, every time the memory came and stained him and the heat rose. It told him it had enjoyed it. It told him to run along and find his justice, and that it would be waiting when his justice failed him. And in the middle of it, very quiet, with the whole weight of the deep places behind it, it told him the one thing it had come up the thread to say.

*Your wrath belongs to you. Your soul belongs to me.*

Shōki was not fazed.

He looked down at the black-red threads crawling on his chest, and at the place under his breastbone where they went in, and he thought, with a kind of distant clarity, that the voice was right, and that it did not matter. His soul. What was that now. He had looked into his own chest once, the morning after the Hall, with the Kurenai eye, and the eye had cooked in its socket for what it saw. He knew what was in there. He knew it better than the thing at the bottom of the thread did, for all its watching.

A shell. That was what was in there. A hollow shell, burned out at the center, that had been a man once and a headsman and a husband, and was now a casing for a fire going somewhere. Not a person chasing a destiny. A destiny, wearing what was left of a person, the way the Hall's irons were wearing his wrists. Something had been done to him. A transgression had been laid on him in the Hall of Verdicts by seven chairs and a red one, and he had been carrying it ever since the way the black floor carries the stain, and everything he was now had grown out of it, the way a callus grows out of a wound and becomes the hand.

Let the thing at the bottom of the thread own the soul. It was welcome to it. It had bought a scorched room with nobody living in it.

He thought about the thread itself, because the thread was what the voice had come up, and the thread was what it held.

He could feel it, now, all of it, the way a man can feel the whole length of a fishing line when the fish turns. And it was not one thread. It ran out of him into a knot. His Attraction Layer had been a Kurenai's once, ordered and tight as the cords on a tally-board, every bond strung where the line and the vow and the marriage had put it. Now it was a ruin. Where Tsuyu had been there was a burned end, black and curled, the place Gisei had cut and the Plane had taken. Where his line had been, the Kurenai, there was a frayed cord they had cut from their end when they struck his name, still hanging off him into nothing. Where Jinsuke had been there was a short hard stub that ended in the black floor. And through all of it, under all of it, wound round every severed end and tied into every knot, the pact went down into the dark, black-red and thick as a hawser, and the whole tangle hung off it like wet rope off a hook.

His mind was that. His consciousness. A thread that had been mangled and tangled until there was no telling which end was which, and every pull on any part of it pulled on all of it, and that was why the memory came the way it came, and why the heat rose the way it rose, and why a voice saying *Tsuyu* up a thread from the bottom of the world could make the snow at his feet go to water in a single breath.

He let it. The snow went. The black-red net on his skin hissed and smoked where his fire came up through it.

"You hold a shell," he said aloud.

His voice went out into the empty fork, low and rough, and the stone lantern hummed faintly with it.

"Hold it, then. I am not in it."

The grip on the thread did not ease. He had not expected it to. But the black-red threads on his skin stopped climbing, and hung there a moment, and then, slowly, went back down the way they had come, into the snow, into the ground, into the dark, leaving the hook set and the thread taut and a faint smell of iron in the cold air where they had been. He could feel the patience at the far end. He could feel that it would come again, and that next time it would not grip. It would haul.

*Then I had better be quick,* he thought, *and I had better be thorough.*

He turned to the lantern.

He had stopped at the fork because of it, before the thread went taut. It was old, older than any road in this country, a stone tōrō of the kind the seven lines set up wherever their law ran, square-capped and heavy, frosted to the waist, its firebox dark. Round the shaft, under the frost, someone had cut the seven beasts of the seven lines, one to a face, in a band of low relief. He put his hand on the stone and the frost ran off it in a sheet of water, and the beasts came up out of the white one after another as his heat went round the shaft.

The White Fox of Byakuya, sly and narrow. The Lynx of Shirogane, all claws. The Hawk of Tenrai, wings back for the stoop. The Moth of Amagiri, soft and blurred where the frost had eaten it. The Fox of Akagane, with its brush wound round its feet, and he stood looking at that one for a long time, because it was her line's beast and she had worn it in copper at her belt the day he married her, and he could remember the exact weight of the clasp in his palm when he undid it that night and could not remember what it had felt like to undo it.

The Crow of Kurenai. His own line. Black stone, the same as the rest, and somebody long ago had picked out its eye with a chip of red glass that was still there, glinting at him in the low aurora light like the single red enamel eye on his own belt.

And on the last face, above the others, larger, coiled round the top of the shaft so that its head rested on the lantern's cap, the Dragon of Kōkan.

He looked at the dragon. Then he looked down at his right hand, the one the First Release had turned into a dragon's maw in the Pit, the crown line's beast, the beast no Kurenai had ever been permitted to wear.

He did not know why it had chosen his hand. He had wondered, on the slab, in the long dark, and found no answer, and stopped wondering. He did not wonder now. He only looked at the stone dragon with its head on the lantern-cap and the stone crow below it with its red glass eye, and felt the tangle in his chest shift and settle round a new knot.

It was not only the boy.

He had told himself it was the boy. Muken. One Kōkan, one red chair, one silence in a Hall. But the lantern stood at a fork in the snow with seven beasts on it, and every one of them had sat in that Hall the morning they voted, and every one of them had a line, and the lines had gone on. Children and grandchildren and great-grandchildren of the hands that had voted, sitting in the same chairs now, under one crown, wearing the same beasts on their belts. The silver that had spoken. The gold that had spoken. The black and ash that had walked her down the floor. The copper that had wept and done nothing.

The whole house had done it. The whole house would answer for it.

And somewhere in the knot of all those lines and chairs and beasts there was the thing he had not let himself think in three hundred years, because the Plane had taken the part of him that would have thought it gently. That it had not begun with Tsuyu. That a house which could vote a clean woman to the black floor in one morning had done it before, and would do it again, and had been doing it since before there was a Seat to do it in, chair against chair, line against line, the Moto feeding on the Moto. That his grief was only one thread in a cloth the whole family had been weaving out of its own suffering for a thousand years.

He would find the end of that thread. He would pull it. And he would watch the whole cloth come apart.

He took his hand off the lantern. The stone steamed.

Behind him, as he walked north up the Kessen road, the seven beasts stood bare and wet in the aurora light for a moment before the frost began, slowly, to come back over them.

Forty miles west, Vaashuuk Snorr-Khal felt the black-red come up through the floor of a hay-barn, sighed, and did not open his eyes.

"Yeah, yeah," he said. "I heard ya."

He was lying on his back in the hay with his arms folded behind his head and his stolen fur hat over his face, and he lay there while the thin black-red threads crawled up through the straw and up the hem of the plum velour and in at his collar and found the thin little thread under his Crystal and closed on it. It hurt. He let it hurt. He listened, under the hat, to everything that came up the thread, all of it, all the way to the end, and when it was done he lifted the hat off his face with one claw and looked up at the rafters, where an owl was looking down at him with great disapproving eyes.

"Fourteen days," he said to the owl. "Then he pulls. Says he won't tug no more." He put the hat back. "Guess I better get movin'."

He did not move for another hour.

Then he got up, brushed the hay off the velour very carefully, one strand at a time, checked the pompadour by touch, settled the hat on top of it, and went out of the barn into the gray before dawn, and started walking west, toward the gorge.

He walked.

He did not make a chaise. He did not drift. He walked on his own two feet through the snow with his platform boots sinking to the ankle and his breath going up white, mile after mile, the way he had not walked anywhere since the night the bellows-roof came down. The little glass bubble bobbed beside him and he did not lie on anything. He was tired. He was always tired. He kept walking. And if anyone had been there to see the heavy-lidded lamp-oil eyes under the fur hat, they would have seen that they were not half-shut at all.

The Tinker's Gorge was a crack in the western shield a mile long and three hundred feet deep, and it was the brightest place in the whole Iron Mandate.

It had to be. There was no daylight at the bottom of it, so the goblins who lived there had made their own. Draw-lamps hung on chains from every ledge and gallery in strings of brass and green glass, dozens of them, hundreds, and the light came down the walls of the gorge in a great warm cascade of lime and amber and pink and copper-gold, pooling on the floor where the workshops were. The workshops were built into the rock in tiers, one above the other, connected by ladders and rope-bridges and little iron cage-lifts that went up and down on counterweights with a clank and a hiss. Every one had a door painted a different color, because the goblins had nothing else that was theirs to paint. Steam came out of a thousand pipes. Sparks came off a thousand wheels. The noise was tremendous, a continuous ringing roaring clattering music of files and hammers and lathes and drills, and over all of it, from every gallery, the bright hard sound of goblin voices calling measurements to each other in Vaross, the *r*s trilling, the vowels held long.

And at the head of the gorge, where the road came down, there was a tower of black basalt with the fist on its banner, and in the tower there was a bell, and when the bell rang the shift changed, and when the shift changed four thousand goblins put down one set of tools and another four thousand picked them up, and the work did not stop. It had not stopped, Vaashuuk knew, in nineteen years.

He came down the road into the gorge at the change of shift, just as the bell was ringing.

Nobody noticed him. Goblins were coming up the ladders from the night shift, gray with tiredness, their eyes red-rimmed behind soldered goggles, their fingers black with flux and stained with burns, and goblins were going down the ladders to the day shift, already tired, already rubbing their eyes. They passed him on the road in their hundreds and did not look at him, because he was a goblin in a coat, and coats were none of their business, and nothing that did not come with a quota was any of their business.

He walked down to the floor of the gorge. He found the biggest workshop on the bottom tier, the valve-seat shop, with its door painted a bright brave yellow, and he went in.

It was long and low and hot, cut back into the rock, lit with green draw-lamps, and in it there were three long benches and ninety goblins at the benches, filing. Each one had a little brass blank in a vice and a file in her hand, and each one was working the blank down to a valve-seat, true to a hair, by eye and by feel, the way his mother had. The filing made a soft hissing whisper all down the shop like rain on a roof. At the far end, on a raised platform, there was a hobgoblin with a ledger.

He was huge, by goblin standards, seven feet of gray-green muscle in a foreman's leather apron, with tusks capped in iron and a rank-cut on his shoulder plate, four cuts and a crossbar, and he was writing in the ledger with a steel pen and not looking up. When Vaashuuk came in he did not look up then either.

"You're late," he said. "Bench three. End stool. Quota's sixty seats."

Vaashuuk did not go to bench three.

He went to the middle of the shop, between the benches, where the goblins could all see him, and he took off the fur hat and held it against his chest, and he stood there in his scorched plum velour with his pompadour bright in the green light and his beauty mark painted fresh across the bridge of his nose. One by one, down the benches, the files slowed.

A goblin at the end of the nearest bench looked up at him. She was older than most of them, small and hard, with her goggles pushed up on her forehead and one lens cracked, and a burn scar down the side of her neck like a splash of wax, and she looked at his coat and his hair and his gold canine with deep, flat, tired contempt.

"Who in the Pit are you supposed to be?" she said. "The Imperator's fancy-boy?"

"Vaashuuk," said Vaashuuk. "Hold the first vowel." He smiled at her. "What's your name, handsome?"

She stared at him. Somebody further down the bench laughed, a short shocked bark, and then put her hand over her mouth.

"Grrikka," the old goblin said at last, as if it had been dragged out of her. "Bench one. Thirty-one years."

"Thirty-one years," said Vaashuuk. "On a stool. Filin' seats." He looked down the benches. "How many of you got thirty years? Twenty? Ten?"

Nobody answered. But the files had stopped. All down the shop, the soft rain-sound had died away, and ninety goblins were sitting with their files in their hands, looking at the goblin in the coat.

"Quota," said the foreman from his platform. He had looked up now. His voice was not loud. It did not need to be. "Sixty seats a stool. Every stool that stops is a stool that's short." He put the pen down. "You. Coat. Bench three. Or out."

Vaashuuk looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked back at Grrikka.

"When's the last time you had a day off?" he said.

She opened her mouth. Then she shut it. Then, slowly, the contempt went out of her face and something else came into it, something that was not hope, because hope was too much effort, but was the place where hope would have gone if she'd had the strength for it.

"Don't know," she said. "Before the Gorge. Before my kids was born." She looked down at the file in her hand. "There's no days off. There's the shift and the other shift."

"Yeah," said Vaashuuk softly. "I know." He put the fur hat back on, very carefully, over the pompadour. "My ma made valve-seats. In the yards under Gorr-Keth. Thirty years on a stool, same as you. Went blind at it. Nobody ever let her lie down. Not once. Not one mornin'." He looked round the shop, at the green lamps and the black fingers and the red-rimmed eyes. "So here's what I'm gonna do. I'm gonna lie down. Right here. Right in the middle of your floor. And I'm gonna stay lyin' down for as long as I feel like it. And anybody who wants to can lie down with me."

He did it.

He lay down on his back on the stone floor of the valve-seat shop in the middle of the Tinker's Gorge, between bench one and bench two, with his hands folded on his belly and his ankles crossed and the fur hat tipped down over his eyes, and he lay there.

Nobody moved.

The foreman came down off his platform. He came slowly, down the three steps, along the floor between the benches, his iron-capped tusks gleaming in the lamplight, and he stopped over Vaashuuk and looked down at him. He was so big that his shadow covered the whole of the goblin in the coat.

"Get up," he said.

"Mm. No."

"Get up," said the foreman, "or I'll put you up."

He bent down and took Vaashuuk by the collar of the plum velour, in one huge gray-green fist, and began to lift.

He began to. The fist closed on the velour and the arm drew back and the great shoulders bunched to haul, and Vaashuuk, under the hat, yawned.

It was a small yawn. Barely a yawn at all. But the air round the foreman's fist went thick and glossy, all at once, like honey poured into a cold glass, and the arm that had been drawing back stopped drawing back and went on stopping, slower and slower, a hand's breadth, a finger's breadth, a hair, and then hung there in the green light, perfectly still, the knuckles locked in the plum velour and the shoulders bunched for a haul that would not finish. His face changed as he felt it. Behind his eyes, his mind went on at its own pace. His body did not.

All down the benches, ninety goblins stared.

"Here's the thing, handsome," said Vaashuuk from under the hat, comfortably, to the foreman's frozen face. "The harder you pull, the thicker it gets. You just keep pullin'. Take your time. We got all day." He lifted the brim of the hat with one claw and looked up at the hobgoblin, and the lamp-oil yellow under the heavy lids was bright as a struck match. "Matter of fact. We got all the days. All of 'em. From now on."

He let the hat fall back.

There was a long silence in the valve-seat shop. Outside, the ringing roaring music of the gorge went on, files and hammers and lathes, the cage-lifts clanking, the steam hissing, four thousand goblins at work.

Then, at the end of bench one, there was a small sound. A file being set down on wood.

Grrikka got off her stool. She did it stiffly, the way a body gets up that has been sitting thirty-one years. She looked at the foreman, locked in the air with his fist in a goblin's collar, and at the goblin on the floor. Then she pushed her cracked goggles up off her forehead into her hair, and she lay down on the stone beside him, on her back, and folded her burned black hands on her belly.

"Huh," she said, after a moment, to the ceiling. "Floor's warm."

"Told ya," said Vaashuuk.

Somebody else set a file down. And somebody else. And then the soft wooden clicks were coming all the way down the three benches, one after another, like the first drops of rain on a roof before the downpour, and goblins were climbing off their stools, and lying down, one beside the other, on the warm stone floor of the Tinker's Gorge, under the green lamps, in the morning.

Vaashuuk lay among them with his hat over his face and listened to the files go quiet, one by one, in the yellow-doored shop at the bottom of the gorge. It was the sweetest sound he had ever heard.

It was a beginning. A small one. He had fourteen days before the thing at the bottom of his thread stopped tugging and started to haul, and he did not intend to waste a single one of them working. But he had never said he wouldn't work at not working. And there was a great deal, under the coat and the hair and the yawns, that nobody had seen yet. Nobody at all.

Above them, at the head of the gorge, the bell in the basalt tower began to ring, out of turn, the long hard alarm-peal of a stoppage, and up and down the tiers, at a thousand benches, a thousand goblins lifted their heads from their work and listened.

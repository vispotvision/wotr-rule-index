*Varrak-Thur, under the Seat. Four days after the Drawing-Off.*

The tin had a dent in it the size of a thumb, and Borin had been at it since the first bell.

He worked it at the side bench under the vent shaft, where apprentices learned to planish spoons, because the great anvil would have taken the job off him. The Vaults smelled the way they smelled at the first bell, of coal-dust gone sweet in the banked fire and the lye in the slack tub, of the wet hide on the bellows, and under all of it the dry smell of the walls, struck flint and old heat, which every Dawi child is born knowing. The bellows hung slack. Water came down the vent in single drops into a bucket somebody had set there before the Accord had a name, and nobody had moved it since. The only other sound in the long room was the hammer. Tick. Tick. Its face was no wider than a thumbnail.

It was a carter's crib, the round tin case with a press-on lid that every shaft-man in Stannvaard carried down at the start of a shift. Cheap plate, tinned over iron, the seam crimped and soldered by a tinsmith who had been in a hurry. Someone had painted the lid green once. The green had worn down to a few flecks caught in the crimp. The dent sat on the shoulder where lid met body. It had gone in when the end kaas came off the stack and down onto the lift step, and the lid had come off in a dead man's hand.

Borin had set it over the ball stake with the round of the stake fitted up under the round of the dent, and he worked from the outside in. A man does not strike the middle of a dent. The middle is where the metal went. The rim is where it was pulled from, and the rim is where it wants to go back, so the blows start there, light and overlapping, each a hair inside the last, the tin turned a finger's width on the stake after every round. Strike too hard and thin plate stretches. Then it has more metal than room, and it will pop in and out under a thumb like a frog's throat for the rest of its life. Borin had watched apprentices ruin a good tankard that way in a quarter of an hour. At forty he had done it himself, and Master Haggi had made him wear the tankard on a cord round his neck for a month, and the hold had called him Tankard until he was sixty.

Tick. Turn. Tick.

The Forge wanted to help.

He felt it the way a man feels a dog leaning on his shin. The Vaults remembered every piece of metal that came into them; it was what they were for. Let go, the room would have taken this little tin back to the morning it came off the rolls, every stress given up, the dent gone in a breath and the green in the crimp, very likely, a shade brighter than it had any right to be. And then the tin would be his. Whatever the Forge mended kept a thread back to him, and he would feel this one wherever it went for as long as he lived: a shelf in a room up on the rim, a midden, a hole in the ground. He carried seven threads already that he could not set down. He did not want an eighth for a carter's dinner.

So he held the Forge folded, down to the bench and his two hands, and paid for it in the hinge of his jaw. By the fourth bell the ache sat under his left ear like a bad tooth.

The burn across the back of his left hand had gone from glaze to new skin in four days, pink and tight, lifting at the edges like old varnish. Eleven days at the anvil for a Guild factor upstairs, who wanted his grandfather's sword made to look as if it had never failed anybody. The factor had not paid. Borin had entered that too.

*The boy's hands went first. The tips, then the knuckles.*

He set the hammer down on the bench.

Not here. He did not say that name inside the Vaults, and if he was quick he did not think it, and he had been quick for a long time.

He picked the hammer up again.

At the fifth bell the dent was gone. He ran his thumb over the shoulder of the tin and found nothing, then turned it under the vent light and found it anyway, a faint dull patch where the hammer had dressed the tinning thin. A smith would see it. A carter never would have. He pressed the lid on, and it went home with a small flat click, the same click it had made at the start of every shift since the carter first carried it down the shaft.

He had thought he would like the click more than he did.

He put the crib inside his coat against his chest, took up the hammer he called his walking stick, and banked the side fire with two shovels of slack. Then he went up.

The hold stair ran a long way from the Vaults to the cask-hall, cut square and worn to a dish in the middle of every tread, and Borin folded himself down a little more at every landing, the way a smith closes a damper a notch at a time so the fire does not choke. By the top his collar was wet. He touched the lintel rune going through. Its edges had been worn round at the height of a palm. A hand lower, at his height, there was a second worn place that was mostly his.

The cask-hall had not recovered and was pretending it had.

Where the main burst, the flags had risen a finger's width along their joints, and they had not gone back down. Every rack on the left wall now stood on shims of split ironwood driven under its feet, and a lip of raised stone ran across the floor at every joint, ready to take a boot in the dark. The Guild had been down and sealed the main with a new brass collar. It was bright as a minted mark against the old conduit, which had gone the colour of strong tea, and the Guild's red wax sat on the gauge housing beside it with the stamp still sharp. There was a new man in the housing, young, with a Guild ribbon at his buttonhole. He stood up when Borin came in. Then he did not seem to know why he had stood, and sat down again.

The pitch-pot spat on its brazier by the door. Yeast, wet oak and tar. The draw-hum had gone back below hearing where it belonged, and it lived in Borin's back teeth on the side where his jaw already hurt.

Valtrin was at the croze-bench cutting grooves in a set of staves. He did not look up. "Second bell," he said. "He went up at the second bell."

What was left of the stack stood where it had stood, re-chocked, short by the twelve that had cracked. Their staves lay in a bundle by the drain for the burning. Kalrin lay on top of it on his back with his good hand under his head and the other hanging off the edge of the top tier, palm up and open, the fingers curled loose the way a sleeper's fingers curl.

Borin listened to the breath. Long, slow, a catch at the top of it. Asleep, then. His little brother had slept on the beer since he could climb.

*Four days.*

He did not go straight to the stack. He went to the end post of the nearest rack, where the column of knife strokes ran up the wood and over onto the next post, and he looked at it a while, as if it were a gauge. Then he went to the stack.

“[clears throat] Little brother.”

Nothing.

“Do you know the difference between you and a kaas?”

The hand on the edge twitched.

“A kaas,” Borin said, already laughing, “gets up off the floor once it's full. [laughs]”

“[coughs] I heard you on the stair.”

Kalrin sat up. He did it carefully, with the right hand only, and the left came up off the edge of the stack and lay in his lap as if it belonged to somebody else. The hall was cold enough that Valtrin's breath showed at the bench, and Kalrin's upper lip was beaded with sweat. It ran into his moustache. He wiped it with the back of his good wrist.

“You come down that stair like a loaded cart with the brake gone,” Kalrin said. “[coughs] Every landing. I felt every landing.”

Borin stopped.

*He's Welled. He can feel it now.* Three hundred years his brother had stood beside him in rooms and felt nothing more than a warm draught, and four days ago that had ended, and Borin had not thought of it once. He took the fold in tighter. It went into the jaw. The lamp flames, which had been standing a shade too straight in their frogs, leaned back toward the shaft draught where they belonged, and Kalrin let out a breath and his shoulders came down.

“Better?”

“Better.” Kalrin slid off the end of the stack the way he always had, landed on his heels, and put out his left hand without being asked.

Borin took it and turned it to the nearest lamp.

Four days ago the whole palm had been grey and grained like hone-stone, the fingers stiff, the hand set half open around nothing. Now the fingers were fingers. The skin had come back pink and creased, a little shiny, peeling at the tips the way skin peels after a scald. The grey had drawn back across the palm and pooled in the heel of the hand, a patch the size of a salt bar's end, and there it stopped.

He put his thumb on the edge of it, and the Gift came up through the thumb.

It came the way it came through a cracked blade before he struck it, when he could feel through the steel where it would go: the grain, the run of the flaw, the one place it was waiting to open. Through his brother's skin the stone law sat where the current had put it, in the meat of the heel and down into the small bones under it, laid in rank like the courses of a wall. He felt along its margin. A set that is spreading has a soft edge, the way frost has a soft edge in the hour it creeps across a puddle. A set that is drawing back has a soft edge too.

This one had a hard edge, all the way round.

It was going nowhere. In or out.

*Set. It will stay where it is.* He could not read past that. Nobody could. The Gift told him what a thing was. It had never once told him what a thing would do, and he had never stopped asking it.

“Well?” Kalrin said.

“[sighs] The fingers are yours.” Borin kept his thumb where it was. “The heel stays.”

“Stays how?”

“Like that. Stone. It's set fast and it's set clean, and I can't tell you more than that, so don't ask me to.” He let go. “Close it.”

Kalrin closed it.

It came slowly. The fingers folded down over the grey and met the heel of the hand, and there was a small dry sound when the nails touched it, like a thumbnail drawn on a whetstone. Kalrin looked at the fist he had made. He opened it and closed it again, faster.

“Huh,” he said.

He went to the open kaas on the croze-bench, the one Valtrin had just finished grooving, where the head sat loose on top waiting to be seated. Valtrin moved his hands out of the way without being asked. Kalrin lifted the head, set its edge into the croze on the near side, and tipped it level. You seat a head with the flat of your hand. Every cooper in the hold did it that way: you strike it in the middle with your palm, hard, and on the first stroke it goes down a little and on the third or fourth it goes home, and by the end of a long shift your palm is red to the wrist.

Kalrin struck it once with the heel of his left hand.

The head went home on the first blow. All the way round. It sat in the croze as if it had grown there.

Valtrin looked at it, then at Kalrin's hand, then at the head again, and said nothing at all, which from Valtrin was a great deal.

Kalrin took the driver from the bench and set it on the first hoop. Strike. Turn. Strike. The hoop walked down, and the staves came together under it, and the last gap closed. He rapped the cask with his left knuckles and it gave one note, the burin's note, the note of a true angle, and the note went down the hall.

“[laughs] Three hundred years,” Kalrin said. “Three hundred years I've been using a mallet like a fool.”

Borin laughed. It came up out of him the way it always did, from the belly, too big for the joke, and it went down the vault and came back off the far end, and somewhere in the middle it turned into something else. He sat down on the nearest kaas. His knees put him there.

“[sniffles]”

He pressed the heel of his own hand into one eye and then the other. The new skin on the back of it pulled. The tin was hard against his chest under the coat, the dressed shoulder of it pressing on his breastbone, and the lamp flames had gone straight up in their frogs again. He took the fold back in before Kalrin could feel it and found he could only manage half.

“[sniffles] Give me a moment.”

Kalrin crouched in front of him, which put their faces level, and Borin saw that his brother's lip was beaded again and the boy did not wipe it.

*Boy.* He was three hundred and some.

“It's Welling,” Kalrin said gently. “That's all it is, Borin. The second rung. Apprentices do it. [laughs] Every lad you ever had at the Forge did it before he could grow a beard, and none of them made you sit down.” He put his right hand on Borin's knee. “You're a Grandmaster. You'll shame the hold.”

He thought it was pride. It sat in his face plainly, a little shy, the look of a younger brother who has waited three centuries to do one thing the middle one could be proud of and has just watched the middle one sit down over it.

Borin let him think so. He could not have said the other thing in the cask-hall any more than in the Vaults. The name would not come up the stair.

“I am proud,” he said. That much was true. “[clears throat] I'm allowed. Look at that head. Valtrin, look at that head.”

“I'm looking at it,” said Valtrin.

The draught on the stair changed. Torin came down into the hall the way he came into everything, all of his weight going straight down through his heels and none of it wasted, and touched the lintel rune once, and stopped. He looked at Borin sitting on a kaas with his face wet. He looked at Kalrin crouched in front of him. He looked at the cask on the croze-bench with its head seated, the mark of the knuckle-rap still faintly damp on the stave, and last at Kalrin's left hand.

“Show me,” Torin said.

Kalrin stood and held it out and opened and closed it.

Torin did not touch it. He looked at it for the space of three breaths, as long as he looked at any entry before he wrote it. Then he opened the ledger on the end of the stack and took the pen from behind his ear. His second finger was black to the knuckle. He wrote, and read it aloud as he wrote, flat, in the reeve's cadence, the copula closing the line.

“Kalrin of this hall. His hand, closed. The heel, set. On.”

He blotted it. Then he looked at Borin again and waited the way Torin waited, without moving anything.

Borin wiped his face with his sleeve and stood. His jaw was sore on both sides now. He knew what the room needed, and it was the only thing he had ever been any good at besides the anvil.

“Torin,” he said. “Why will a Dawi never lend you his angle-set?”

Torin said nothing.

“Because he has to keep it on the square.” Borin was laughing before he got there, as he always was, so the last word broke in half. “[laughs] On the square, Torin.”

Torin's face did not move. Nothing on Torin moved.

Valtrin took out his knife, went to the end post, and cut another stroke under the last.

Borin heard the blade bite the wood and felt his chest come back to its right size around the tin.

“His mother sent down,” Torin said. “She'll be at the cage at the sixth bell for his last shift's pay. I've counted it out.” He touched the purse at his belt. “She asked after his crib. I told her the hold had it.”

“The hold had it,” Borin said. He took the tin out of his coat.

Torin looked at the shoulder of it. Borin watched him look. A reeve had no smith's eye, but he had his own, and he would see what he saw.

“Mended,” Torin said.

“By hand. At the side bench.” Borin turned it in his fingers. “Not in the Forge.”

Torin nodded once, slowly, as if that were a thing that could be entered, and perhaps it could. He did not ask why. He had been the eldest for four hundred years, and he had stopped asking the middle one certain things round about the time the middle one stopped saying a certain name.

Borin held the tin out to Kalrin.

“You sat with him,” he said. “You give it to her.”

Kalrin looked at it. Then he took it in his left hand. The fingers came down round the green-flecked crimp, slowly, and closed, and the grey heel of his hand took the weight of the tin against the lid where the dent had been. He held it the way a cooper holds a full cup on a moving lift, and went down the hall past the shimmed racks to wait by the cage.

*Kharven-Seat, the king's private chambers at the top of the east tower. Spring, six months after the Great War of the North. Early afternoon.*

Six months on, and they were still singing about him in the taverns under the Seat.

When the wind came the right way up the tower he could hear it, a fiddle and forty drunk voices hammering out the verse about the mud at Shimane, the one where the Ashen Blade walked into a Keld wedge and came out the far side with his sword red to the hilt. They always got the number of riders wrong. They always made it bigger. And every time Muken Moto heard it he thought of the one thing the song never mentioned, which was that he had been too late.

He sat in the window seat in a kimono of black silk worked with gold dragons, the coils pouring from both shoulders down the sleeves so that when he lifted an arm the dragons lifted with it and their scales threw little sparks of light onto the floor. The robe hung open to the waist because there was nobody left in this tower who would tell him to close it, and the sash sat loose and crooked at his hips. Across the back, in gold thread laid over the dragon's spine, ran the unified banner of the Moto houses, seven lines and one blood, the thing he had bled the North to raise. A bowl of tea had gone cold on the low table an hour ago. His white hair hung long and uncombed past his shoulders, a few strands fallen across his eyes, and he did not push them back.

Below the window lay Kharven Fields, and Kharven Fields had forgotten the war.

That was the part he could not forgive it for. In autumn the fields had been churned black, cut with wheel-ruts and grave-trenches, the burned standpipes of the old district main sticking up out of the mud like the ribs of something dead. Now the whole valley had gone mad with color. Fireweed ran pink along every trench line, so that he could trace where the Keld had dug in by where the flowers grew thickest. Cornflowers came up blue between the rows, buttercups in yellow drifts where the horse lines had been, and on the far slope somebody's apple orchard had survived the burning and stood in full white blossom, so bright under the afternoon sun it hurt to look at. Guild crews in orange coats were relaying the main along the north road, and he could see the new brass of the gauge-housings flashing where they caught the light, could hear, very faintly under the tavern song, the hum of the draw coming back into the Seat's pipes, district by district, like blood coming back into a frozen hand.

Everything was healing. Everything except whatever it was in him that had gone still.

He knew what the nights would bring. They brought the same thing every night. The gate of the Shirogane house at Kharven-Seat, the iron spikes along the top of it, the rain, and on the third spike from the left, his sister's head, her hair matted dark and her eyes half-open as if she had been interrupted in the middle of telling him to hold still. In the dream he always ran toward the gate, and in the dream it always got further away, and he always woke with his hands clenched so hard on the bedframe that the oak had split under his fingers. Fractura read the split for him every morning whether he wanted it or not: the grain line, the stress line, where it would go next. There were nine cracked bedframes in the Seat's stores now. The steward had stopped asking.

Before, there had been the revenge. It had been a fire to stand in front of, a road with an end to it. Every Shirogane who had stood in that hall and watched, every Keld chief who had sold her, every lord who had looked away. He had found them. He had settled every account. The Ashen Mandate of Kharven had taken their debts and he had paid them out in full, and when the last of them was gone he had walked out into the yard in the snow with the sword still in his hand and waited to feel something, anything, and the thing he felt was the snow.

Yukazuri was the reason for all of it. And she was under the stones on the ridge, and she would never see the fireweed.

Somebody knocked.

He knew the knock. Two raps, a pause, a third, precise as a man laying out a ledger. "You may come in," he said, and did not turn from the window.

Godfrey Greymane came in with his gray cloak swinging and the brass hand of his office hanging on its chain against his chest, polished so bright it threw a spot of gold onto the wall. He was long and narrow and gray in every particular but his hair, which was black and tied at the nape, and the dark circles under his eyes had not gone anywhere since the war; Muken had stopped expecting them to. Down in the yard, through the open window, Lorn Stark was bellowing at a line of Imperial Guard recruits that if they held their shields like that against a Keld lance they could save everybody the trouble and dig their own holes now.

"My lord." Godfrey stopped three paces in and looked at the cold tea, and the open robe, and the uncombed hair, and his mouth went thin. "You're still sitting up here. Idle. Alone in your chambers."

Muken said nothing. He let the silence answer, the way he had learned in the North that silence could, and it occurred to him only afterward that the silence had said more than he meant it to. Something of what he carried was getting out these days. He could feel it sometimes, leaking from him at the edges of a room, the Judgment Field gone sour, servants walking wide around his door without knowing why.

"So you've noticed."

"The whole Seat has noticed." Godfrey came to the low table and stood over it with his hands behind his back. "And you won't give a single one of the women I've sent up here a chance."

Muken turned his head at last. "So that was your doing." The daughter of the Ashbury house who had spent a whole supper telling him how many songs she knew about Shimane. The Thornhaven cousin who had asked, before the soup, what the crown jewels were worth. The widow from the river towns who had looked at the scars running down his chest the way a buyer looks at a horse's teeth. "I don't want some girl who's only interested in me for the things I've done."

"You are a king." Godfrey's voice dropped into the tone Muken had heard him use on the Greymane foot when they broke ranks, and on Muken himself the night before the Skeldmere, a voice with a father in it. "A king needs a wife. A queen. If you fall, the realm has no heir, and the seven lines you sewed together come apart at the seams the morning after your funeral. So you need to climb out of this pit, my lord, and act like one."

"I never asked to be king."

Godfrey laughed, short and surprised out of him. "No. But you're the only one the North will kneel to, and the North doesn't take refunds." He leaned down a little. "You're eighteen years old. Act your age. Eat something. Comb that."

Muken pulled the robe closed across his chest and tied the sash with one tug, which was about as much as he was prepared to concede. "Is that any way to speak to your king?" he said, and something at the corner of his mouth moved, the nearest it had come to a smile in weeks.

"It is when my king's an idiot." Godfrey straightened. "I brought someone."

Muken rolled his eyes so hard he felt it in his skull.

"Don't give me that look. You'll like this one. She fought on the Thornwall when the Keld came over it. Amelia Greymane, Lieutenant of the Shieldwardens."

She came in through the door behind him and the room changed shape around her.

The first thing Muken saw was the hair, because it was impossible to see anything else first. It was red. Red like rowan berries after the first frost, like a forge-coal when the bellows catch it, a deep living red that had nothing to do with the gray of her brother or the gray of her cloak. It was braided tight across the crown of her head, three Shieldwarden braids pulled back hard so they could not get in her eyes in a fight, and below the braids the rest of it fell loose and heavy down her back nearly to her belt, wild at the ends where the wind had been at it on the road. Her face was pale and long, the Greymane face, all straight lines, with a nose that had been broken once and set a little to the right, and a thin white scar running from her left cheekbone back toward the ear. Her eyes were heavy-lidded and gray-green, the color of river ice with weed under it. Nothing moved in them. Nothing moved in her face at all.

She was tall, near enough his own height, and broad through the shoulders the way a woman gets from carrying a shield for ten years. She wore a quilted gambeson of oxblood red under a fitted chest-plate, and the plate was worked with a fine lattice of etched lines that he recognised, after a moment, as the old Greymane ward-pattern, the one their grandfathers had cut into the stones of the ridge. Over both shoulders her pauldrons stacked in overlapping blue-steel lames, riveted, scratched bright along every edge, and a mantle of rough brown wolf fur sat over them, the pelt matted and ragged where blades had caught it. A long cloak of dark red wool hung behind. Her gauntlets were scarred, the leather between the plates cracked at the knuckles. And slung across her back, its haft running from her right hip up past her left ear, was a war-axe with a bearded blade as long as his forearm and a hooked spike at the top, the steel dark with oil, the edge a clean bright line.

She smelled of axe oil and horse and road dust. She bowed her head.

"My lord."

"Oh, give over, Amelia," Godfrey said. He looked at Muken. "You didn't fare too well with my other choices. So." He was already turning for the door. "I'll take myself off. At least have a talk." The door shut behind him with a click, and they could both hear him not walking away for a good three seconds before his boots went off down the stair.

Amelia lifted her head and straightened her back, settling her weight squarely over both feet, the axe-haft shifting against her shoulder. The Judgment Field told him, without his asking, where every joint of her armor was loaded and where it would give. It told him the left pauldron had a hairline crack in its second lame, old, badly hidden under the fur.

"I'm sorry for my brother, my lord." She spoke the way a soldier gives a report, every word set down flat. "He means well. He always means well, it's his worst habit. But I understand why a man of your standing wouldn't want a fighting woman. You don't have to pretend for his sake. I'll tell him you were kind about it."

Muken shook his head. "Nobody said that." He looked at her, properly, for the first time, the way he looked at an opponent across a yard. "I just don't want you to love me for the title. For what I am. That isn't the kind of man I am." He paused. "You look like you've been through a lot. I'd have thought you'd be the last person to want the crown."

Something shifted in her face then. It was small, only a tightening at the corner of the mouth, but on a face that still it was like watching ice crack on a pond.

"Love you," she said.

"That's what he's after, isn't it?"

"My lord, I've known you about as long as it takes to boil an egg." She looked at the cold tea. "May I sit? My knees have been on a horse since Thornhaven."

He waved at the cushion across the low table. She unhooked the axe from its clip first, one smooth practiced motion, and laid it on the floor beside her with the blade turned away from him, which was a courtesy he had not seen since the North, and then she sat cross-legged in her plate with a creak of leather and a clink of lames. She picked up the cold tea without asking and drank half of it.

"That's gone cold," he said.

"I've drunk worse out of a helmet." She set the bowl down. "I'll be honest with you, because I don't know how to be any other way and it's too late to learn. I didn't come up those stairs because I want your crown. And I didn't come because I'm in love with you, because I'm not. I don't know you. I came because Godfrey asked me, and because he's been sleeping three hours a night since midwinter, and when I asked him why he said it was you." She held his eyes. "He sits up worrying about you the way our father used to sit up over him. And he's barely past twenty himself, and he's already buried that father. So I came to see what was worth all that."

Muken sat very still.

Every woman Godfrey had sent had wanted something. He had not needed to be clever to see it; the Debt Recognition Field marked hostility and appetite the way a nose marks smoke, and he had felt their wanting pricking along his skin from across the table, a hundred little hooks. He reached for it now, almost out of habit, letting the Field open around Amelia Greymane the way he would against a stranger in a yard, and it came back with nothing to mark. Whatever she wanted, it had no hooks in it. The Field found only a solid, quiet, load-bearing weight sitting across the table from him like a stone in a river.

It was so strange that he laughed. It came out of him rusty and short, like a door that had not been opened all winter, and he put a hand over his mouth as if he could catch it.

Her brows went up a quarter of an inch, which on that face was a shout. "Something funny, my lord?"

"Muken," he said. "Call me Muken. Up here, at least." He lowered the hand. "You're the first person in six months who's walked in here and wanted nothing from me. I don't know what to do with it."

"I didn't say I wanted nothing." She tapped the cracked pauldron under the fur, so lightly the steel only ticked. "I want my Shieldwardens back on the ridge where they belong instead of standing about in your corridors. And I want a smith who doesn't charge me a month's pay to fix this."

"Second lame," he said. "Left side. It'll split on the next hard blow to the shoulder."

She went still, and then she looked at him with something new. "How'd you know that?"

"I always know." He turned his right hand over on his knee and looked at the palm. The sleeve fell back from it and the gold dragon slid with the silk. "That's the thing nobody sings about. Fractura, Judicium, the Eye of the Splintered Work, all of it. I look at a cup and I see where it's going to break. I look at a wall and I see the seam. I look at a man and I see the knee that'll go, the rib that's healed wrong, which blow ends him." He closed the hand. "I look at a woman across a supper table and I see all the places she'd crack if I held her too hard. My bed's oak and I've split nine of them in my sleep. What do you think I'd do to a wife?"

He had not meant to say any of that. It was out of him before he could stop it, and the room was very quiet after, only Lorn's voice small in the yard below and the draw-hum in the walls.

Amelia did not look away. "Go on," she said.

"When I was angry, I knew what I was for." He looked out at the fields, the pink trench lines, the white orchard. "Every morning there was a name I hadn't settled yet. I'd wake and think of it and I'd be up and armed before the fire was lit. I was good at it. I was the best thing in the North at it. They put a crown on me because nobody else could do what I could do to a Keld wedge." He swallowed. "And then I settled the last one, and I stood in the snow and waited, and there was nothing. No peace, no joy, nothing. Just me, standing there, still holding the sword. And I thought, this is it. This is what I am. A thing for breaking things, and I've run out of things to break."

"And you're eighteen."

"And I'm eighteen, and every lord in the Hall of Verdicts looks at me like I know what I'm doing, and I don't. I don't know how to tax a river town. I don't know how to be kind to the Ashbury girl when she sings me a song about the worst day of my life. I don't know how to sleep." His voice went thin at the end, and he hated it, and he could not stop it. "She'd have known. My sister. She was the one who knew how to sit with people. I was the one who knew how to hurt them. If one of us had to come back from that hall it should have been her."

He had not said it aloud before. Not to Lorn, not to Godfrey, not to Kiyome. It sat on the table between them now beside the cold tea.

Amelia was quiet for a long breath. Then she started unbuckling her left gauntlet.

She did it slowly, strap by strap, the way a person undresses a wound, and pulled the gauntlet off and laid it on the table, and held out her bare hand across the cold tea for him to look at. It was a big hand for a woman, long-fingered, and it was ruined. The knuckles were ridged with white scar, lumped where the bones had broken and knitted. The two smallest fingers were set crooked, bent toward each other like old men leaning together on a bench. There was a puckered burn on the back of it the size of a coin, and the palm was so callused from the axe-haft it had gone shiny and yellow.

"My father took me to court three times before he went up the ridge," she said. Her voice had not changed at all, still flat, still a report, but she was not looking at him anymore; she was looking at the hand. "He wanted me married into one of the river houses. Somewhere safe. Every time, the young lord would bow, and take my hand to kiss it, and feel this." She closed the crooked fingers slowly, as far as they would go. "And you'd see it in his face. Like he'd picked up a dead bird. Three times." A breath. "So I stopped going. I took up the axe instead. My father said a Greymane daughter didn't carry a shield on the ridge, and I said then I'd carry it somewhere else, and I did."

"The Thornwall," Muken said.

"The Thornwall." She opened the hand again. "When the Keld came over the north face on the second night, I had thirty-one Shieldwardens on that section. We held it till the Byakuya archers came up. I walked off it with twenty." She said the next part to the hand. "I say the eleven names every night before I sleep. Torvald, Ebbe, Hamund, the two Ashcroft lads. Every night. I'm frightened that if I miss one night I'll forget one, and then he'll just be gone, like he never stood there."

Muken did not say anything. He did not have to; she was not finished.

"And you want to know the worst of it, since we're telling." Her jaw worked. "When my mother died I was fourteen, and I cried for a week, and then I stopped. And I haven't since. Not at her pyre, not for my father, not for the eleven. It just won't come anymore. Men look at me and they see the Grey Wall, that's what the Shieldwardens call me, the Grey Wall, and they think I'm hard, and I let them, because it's easier." The flat voice cracked, just slightly, a hairline in the second lame. "Truth is I'm stuck. I've been standing in front of other people so long, holding the shield up, I don't know what I am when there's nobody behind me. Godfrey's got you now. My father's gone. The war's done." She looked up at him at last, and those river-ice eyes were bright and dry and furious with themselves. "I don't know what I'm for either, Muken. So don't you sit there and tell me you're the only one."

The tavern song had stopped below. A gull went over the tower, shouting. Muken looked at her ruined hand lying open on the black lacquer of the table, and at her face that would not let itself move, and something in his chest that had been locked since the snow turned over, heavy and slow, like a bolt drawn in a door.

He reached across the table and took her hand.

He did it carefully, the way he had never done anything carefully in his life. The Eye of the Splintered Work showed him all of it: the old breaks in the knuckles, the crooked set of the two small fingers, the scar tissue pulling across the burn, every place where this hand had been hurt and healed and been hurt again. He saw exactly where it would break. And he held it anyway, gently, his thumb over the ridge of her knuckles, and it did not break. It just lay there in his, warm and rough and heavier than it looked.

"There," he said. "See? Didn't crack."

Amelia stared at their hands. Her throat moved. "No," she said, very quietly. "Didn't."

"Her name was Yukazuri," he said, and it was the first time in six months he had said it aloud, and it came out of him like a tooth. "She was a surgeon. A good one. The best in the North. She had this way of putting her fingers on a wound and you'd just go calm, the whole room would go calm, you could feel your own breathing slow down. And she'd say, *Hold still. You can dislike me when it closes.* Every time. To me, to Lorn, to Keld prisoners, to a dog once that got its leg caught in a wolf-trap. Same words." He laughed, wet and broken. "She used to fix my hands after I'd been fighting. Every time she'd tell me I was an idiot and every time she'd fix them. I never once said thank you. I thought there'd be time."

He was crying now. He felt it before he knew it, the heat at his eyes and the wet on his face, and he could not stop it, and he did not try. Six months of it came up out of him, and the King of the North sat in his window seat in his gold dragons with his white hair in his face and wept like a boy, and Amelia Greymane did not tell him it was all right, because it was not, and she did not tell him his sister was at peace, because neither of them knew that.

She got up instead. She came round the low table with her plate creaking, and sat down beside him on the window seat, close, and put her shoulder against his. That was all. Her armored shoulder, the cracked one, set against his, solid as a wall, taking his weight the way a Shieldwarden takes the weight of the man beside her in the line. A faint warmth came off her where they touched, rust and gold, her aura rising just enough to be felt, with a low sound under it like the ring of a struck shield-rim fading out.

He leaned on it. He let himself.

They sat like that while the shadow of the window lattice crept across the floor, and Muken cried himself out, and when he looked sideways at her at last her face was still perfectly still and her eyes were running. Two clean lines, down past the scar, dripping off her jaw onto the wolf fur. She was not making any sound at all.

"Amelia," he said.

"I know." Her voice was thick. "I know. Don't." She wiped her face with the back of the ruined hand, hard, as if the tears had insulted her. "Six years. Six years, and it's a boy king in a dressing gown that does it. Godfrey's never to hear about this, do you understand me? Never. I'll put that axe through the door."

"He's probably still listening at it."

"He's not that stupid." She paused. "He is that stupid."

Muken laughed again, properly this time, and the sound filled the room and went out the window into the blossom and the brass and the noise of the yard, and down below Lorn Stark stopped shouting mid-word and looked up at the tower.

When it had gone out of him he sat back against the window frame and looked at her, at the red hair and the wet scar and the broken nose and the plain flat terrible honesty of her, and he felt something he could not name yet. He would not call it love; he would not insult her with that after an hour. It had a pulse in it, though, and he had not felt anything with a pulse in it since the snow.

"I'll pay for the pauldron," he said.

"You will not."

"It's going to split."

"Then it splits, and I'll mend it myself." She lifted her chin. "I'm not here to be bought, Muken. You said so yourself. You don't want a woman who's after your gold. Well, I don't want a man who thinks he can buy me off with a smith." She stood, and bent, and picked up the war-axe off the floor, and clipped it back across her shoulders in one movement. "I'll tell you what I'll take."

He looked up at her.

"You've been sat in this tower six months. You've gone ashy under all that brown, and your hands shake. I saw them." She nodded at the window, at the yard, at Lorn and the recruits. "Come down. Spar with me. You and me, the Seat's yard, now. You won't have to look at me and wonder where I'll crack, because I'll be trying to take your head off with this, and you'll be too busy." Something moved at the corner of her mouth, the second crack in the ice. "You want to know if I'm after the crown? Nobody courts a crown by hitting it with an axe."

Muken stared at her. Then he stood, and pulled the sash loose and retied it properly this time, a hard double knot, and swept his white hair back out of his face with both hands. Below the window the fireweed burned pink across the whole of Kharven Fields, and the draw-hum rose in the walls as the Guild crews opened another valve, and he felt, faint and cautious as the first warmth of spring in frozen ground, the Judgment Field stir around him for the first time in months, waking with no enemy in it.

"Fair warning," he said. "I hit hard."

"Good." She was already at the door. "So do I."

She pulled it open, and Godfrey Greymane, Hand to the King, who had very definitely been standing with his ear against it, stumbled half a step into the room, caught himself on the frame, straightened his cloak, and looked from his sister's wet face to his king's wet face and back again with his mouth slightly open.

"Not a word," said Amelia.

"Not a word," said Muken.

Godfrey closed his mouth. He stood aside. And the King of the North went down the stairs after the Lieutenant of the Shieldwardens, three steps behind her red hair, toward the yard and the light.

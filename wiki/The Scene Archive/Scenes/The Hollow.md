*The Kessen ford in spring flood, the hour of the cock going to the hour of the dog*

The steam came down out of the air over the ford like a slow white rain, and when it cleared enough to see, the old man had sat down.

He had not fallen. Shōki watched him do it, with one eye, through the drifting white: Raizan Tenrai Moto lowering himself onto the flat stone in the middle of the Kessen the way an old man lowers himself onto a garden bench at the end of a long walk, one hand on his knee, carefully, as if the stone might move. The green flood went past him on both sides, loud and white-lipped, and he sat in the middle of it with his long legs folded under him and the river spray freezing in a white fur on the hem of his coat, and he did not look like a man who had been dragging the Sin of Wrath down a town stair by the nose for a quarter of an hour. He looked like what was left of one.

The river had done to him what it had done to Shōki, from the other side. When the Kessen tore the cushion of steam off Shōki's legs and the water flashed to vapor all at once, *KSSSHHHOOOOM*, the old man had been standing three paces away on this stone, and he had been inside the blast. Steam is worse than flame. Flame is a thing a man can flinch from; steam comes into him. It had come in through the collar of his coat and up his sleeves and down the back of his neck, and where it had touched him the skin had gone a wet shining red and then loosened, and now it hung from the backs of both his hands in pale, wrinkled sheets, the way the skin of a scalded peach hangs from the fruit. His right cheek, the side the blast had come from, had come up in a single long blister from the white beard to the eye. And he had breathed it. Shōki could hear that from where he stood. Every breath the old man drew went into him with a small wet catch in it, *khh*, *khh*, the sound of a throat that had been scalded raw down to the lungs.

Raizan reached into his coat with a hand that was mostly blister, and came out with a small lacquered tin, black, with a gold crane on the lid, the one dry thing on him. He opened it. Inside were cigarettes, thin, paper-rolled, a dozen of them in a neat row, and a fold of waxed matches. He took one of each.

*Tssk.*

The match flared on the rough of the stone. He cupped it against the spray with his ruined hands and lit the cigarette and shook the match out and dropped it into the river, where it was gone before it touched the water. He drew on the cigarette. Then he coughed, and the cough came up from the burned bottom of him, *KHHAK, khhak, khhak*, wet, tearing, bending him forward over his knees, and when it let go of him there was blood on the white beard. Bright. Frothed. He looked at it on his sleeve, and wiped his mouth with the back of the hand that was mostly blister, which left a long red smear and a strip of loose skin on the cuff.

He smoked a while, looking at the man in the river.

[old man, soft and courteous, an old Japanese court accent; the voice scalded hoarse] "I know of you," said Raizan. "Did I tell you that? I have known of you since I was nineteen and standing at the back of the Hall in a servant's gray." *Khh.* "Everyone in the Tenrai war-schools knows the Kurenai. Your line was the thing we were taught to be afraid of, the way children are taught to be afraid of deep water. The headsmen who feel nothing." He drew on the cigarette, and the end of it glowed orange in the white. "And then one morning a headsman felt something. In front of the seven chairs. I watched it happen. I have thought about it more mornings than I have thought about my wife."

The river roared past between them.

"You only wanted to live," Raizan said. "That is all it ever was, under everything. You were born hot in a cold line, and they spent your whole childhood laying wet cloth over you, and you let them, because you were good, and you wanted to be let alone. A wife who did surgery with her hands and laughed at your fevers. A house. The register closed at the end of every day and nothing in your chest you did not choose to keep there." *Khhak.* Blood again. He did not wipe it. "You wanted to live without your fate being decided for you. A quiet life. A man's life. That is a small thing to want. It is the smallest thing there is. And they took it out of your hands in front of you, in one morning, by a vote, for a death I had caused." The spokes in his pale eyes turned once, slowly, in the steam. "That is why your wrath is what it is. It is as large as it is because what was taken was so small, and so entirely yours. Wickedness has very little to do with it, Shōki."

---

Shōki did not answer. He stood chest-deep in the green flood with the steam going up off him in a thin white column and the blood running out of the broken glaze of his skin in long red ribbons downstream, and his dead eye's ember had sunk to the dull coal of a fire at the end of the night, and he listened.

He had not been talked to in a long time. People talked *at* him: they screamed, they bargained, they prayed, they told him what they would give him. The Kurenai had read him sentences. Nobody had talked to him in the way that one man sits by another man's bed and tells him what is wrong with him, quietly, because there is no longer any reason to lie. He found that he did not know what to do with his hands.

[soft, hoarse, a physician delivering a diagnosis he has delivered many times] "You never died," said the old man. "Did you know that? Each time they tried to execute you. The Hall, with Jinsuke's blade. The thirteen bells on your belt. Every one of them was a sentence of death carried out properly, by people who knew exactly how, and none of them took." He tipped ash into the river. "I wondered about it for fifty years. A man of the Kurenai, killed thirteen times by the best executioners in the north, and walking. I thought it was strength. I was wrong." He looked at Shōki with the Tengan open, and the fine spokes of light turned and turned in the gray. "I have been reading your knot all morning. Every way your next hour could go. I have read the knots of more men than anyone living, and every one of them, even the strongest, even the ones who were not meant to die that year, has at least one strand where it stops." *Khh.* "Yours does not. Not one. I have looked at all of them, and in every way you can go from this river, you go on."

He drew on the cigarette, and it trembled, and he steadied it.

"Yes," Raizan said softly. "It all makes sense now. You could not die. They could not kill you. It is not in you to finish. That is your law, is it not? *What is done to me is never finished.* You swore it at the bottom of the steps with your wife's head coming away, and the Plane took the oath and made it true all the way down. A death is a thing that is done to a man. And nothing done to you can be finished." The white beard moved. "So you do not finish."

He looked at the bells on the Sin's belt, thirteen small iron bells, silent now in the water.

"You became the Sin of Wrath. The Malformation of fate itself. Obsession so intense, so overwhelming, that it closed the knot of your life on itself like a fist, and nothing gets in. The Great Wrath. *Hakanashi.*" He said the old court word gently, the way one says a name at a grave. "The Hollow. The Unburied. You burned the bond to her to break the irons, and what was left in the place where she had been was the wrath, and the wrath has been holding you up ever since, the way a spine holds up a man." *Khhak. Khhak.* "And it will not let you go. Not even into your own hands. I have looked. There is no strand in your knot where you do it yourself." The old man's voice, scalded as it was, went quieter still. "You were even incapable of killing yourself. Were you not? You tried."

The river went on roaring. On the far bank, the hundred faces packed along the shore had gone silent.

Shōki's left hand had closed. He had not closed it. It had closed.

He had tried. In the first century. More than once. The law had answered each time, the way it answered every blow from anyone: a blade turned against himself was a blow, and a blow must be answered before he does anything else, and the answering was the end of the trying, every time. HAKANASHI does not discriminate between a Kurenai assassin and its own owner's hand. He had stopped, eventually. He had never wanted to live, after; the law simply would not let him lose the argument.

Nobody had ever known that. Nobody had ever been close enough to look.

---

[iron dragged over a foundry floor, low] "And you," said Shōki, "are curious about nothing."

He came up out of the river.

The water fell off him in sheets as he rose, *shhhhaaaaaa*, and where it fell off the broken glaze of his chest and his arms it hissed but did not boil; he was too cold for that now, a furnace banked to coals. He stood up to his waist in the green flood with the steam going up off his shoulders and the blood coming out of his mouth. It came from somewhere deep, a slow dark gout of it that lurched down over his lip and his chin and dripped onto the cracked black spiral of the Covenant Scar, where the river had split it across. He did not wipe it away. He looked at the old man on his stone, three paces off across the white water, smoking.

"Are you not curious, Doom Breaker," he said, "why I came here?"

He did not use the name. He never used anyone's name. A title was a count, and a count he could say.

"I am going north. You read that from the road. To the Seat. To the black floor and the seven chairs and every heir of every line that sat in them that morning." *Gkh.* Blood. "There is a boy in the red chair now. Kōkan. He was not born. He did not vote. The Kōkan chair voted, and he is its heir, and his blood is the blood that sat in it." The ember in the dead eye stirred, faintly, like a coal breathed on. "I have read every chest in the north since the black floor, looking for the hand that stopped that lord's heart. I have closed the book on two hundred men on the way, every one of them heavy enough to fire me. I have never once closed it on a man I was not sure of."

He took one step toward the stone. The river parted round him, steaming.

"Something is pulling me. You saw it. A black hand. It has my soul, and it pulls toward the chairs, and it is very strong, and I do not know any longer which of the steps I am taking north are mine." *Gkh.* "So before I reach the boy, I had to know. Whether I could still be stopped. Whether there is anything left in this that is me, or whether it is only the law and the hand. I had to be tested." The living eye held the old man. "And I had to be tested by someone I would not regret killing."

He let that stand in the steam between them.

"You are the only man alive I was certain of."

---

The old man smiled round the cigarette. It was a real smile, tired, warm, the smile of a go player who has just seen the shape of the board at the end of a very long game and found it was the shape he had expected.

[soft] "Then let us see if you can be stopped," said Raizan, and put the cigarette down on the stone, carefully, so that it would not go out.

And vanished.

"Kamikakushi."

The word came out of him soft as a door closing in a quiet house, the old court word for one the gods have taken, and on it the old man's aura went below sight altogether: the faint black-violet that had hung round him all morning like the shadow of something else sank into nothing; the HUSH that had kept the dogs from barking went to the very top of its Grade, so that even the river seemed to sound fainter where he had been; and THE BLINK ran on every pair of eyes on the far bank at once, a hundred of them, and found in all their overlapping darks a single gap wide enough to step through. The flat stone was empty. The cigarette lay on it, burning, a thin blue thread of smoke going up from it into the white.

For as long as the First Release held there was no angle Raizan had forgotten and no watcher he could not pass. Not even a second watcher behind the first. Not even a sense that did not blink. Out here in the cold river air, a man's body heat was only one warm thing among a hundred on the bank, and Raizan had gone into that hundred like a stone into a pond.

Shōki stood in the river with his fists at his sides, and did not look for him.

He did not need to look. There was a thing the old man had not read on the card, or had read and could not answer, because there was no answer to it. Thirty-one coins lay in Shōki's purse under one name. Every one of them was heat that had been dealt by one soul and tagged by the Reigan with the soul that dealt it, and banked on Vantabriel's hysteresis where nothing decays, and the bank could be spent only against its author. Heat that can only go one place knows which place that is. The black-violet rim of Shōki's aura, thinned to a line by the river, had thickened toward the old man all morning, wherever the old man stood. It did not care whether he could be seen.

It was thickening now. To the left. Behind. Low.

A man can hide from every eye in the world. He cannot hide from what he owes.

*FWOOOMPH.*

Shōki turned on his heel in the river with the whole of his ruined body behind the turn, the flood going up round him in a white fan, and put his right hand out into empty air at the height of a man's breastbone, palm flat, toward the place his debt was pulling.

And the empty air had a chest in it.

*THUD.*

His palm landed flat on Raizan's breastbone, over the navy-black coat, over the gold knotwork clasp at the throat. KAMIKAKUSHI broke like a soap bubble, *pff*, and the old man was there, half a pace from him in the white water, the needle-hand that had no needle drawn back for a blow he had not thrown, his scalded face very close, the spokes in his eyes wide. He had seen it coming. The Tengan had shown him the hand. It had been the likeliest thing. He had stepped into it anyway, because there had been nowhere else on the river left to step.

Shōki looked down at the hand on the old man's chest, and the Reigan, which had read that chest as nothing all morning, turned its ring-lines on it at a finger's distance.

And the veil was gone.

KAMIKAKUSHI had taken everything the HUSH had to give, and spent it hiding the body, and there had been nothing left over to hide the record. The gray quiet over the old man's Attraction Layer had blown away like fog off a pond when the sun comes up, and under it lay the chest of the Doom Breaker, open, at last, to the one eye in the north that had spent every year since the Hall looking for it.

Scar on scar on scar. Fifty-eight years of needles. Every killing laid down in the Layer the way the dose is laid down in fired clay, thin gray line after thin gray line, packed so tight they had gone black, and at the very bottom of them all, older than the rest and heavier, one line that was not gray but a deep, dull, rust-red. A lord at a feast, with his heart stopped. And a woman on her knees on a black floor, because of him.

[the voice of a hanging judge, reading the count] "Four hundred and twelve," said Shōki Kurenai Moto.

His voice did not rise. It did not need to.

"Entered."

---

He breathed in.

It came into him like air into a bellows, *HHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHH*, a long dragging draught that pulled the steam off the river toward his mouth in a white spiral and went down into him and kept going, down past the drowned ribs and the cracked glaze, into the bank. The broken Covenant Scar on his chest flared, black-violet in every crack of it, so bright that the river round them lit purple from beneath. The Hall Irons on his wrists went from black to red to white in the time it takes to say it, *hssssss*, the chain-stubs glowing like wire in a forge, and the river boiled round the white iron in a ring of mad white bubbles.

It was a full beat of tell. Every Kurenai child knows it. The old man knew it.

He did not step away. He stood in the river with the hand on his chest and the spokes in his eyes slowing, slowing, and looked at Shōki's face from a finger's distance, and his scalded mouth moved in the white beard into something very like the smile he had smiled at the go board.

"*GŌKA.*"

The Karmic Fire.

It went into the old man through the palm on his breastbone the way a seed goes into wet ground, and it took. It is the oldest working of the Kurenai turned inside out: the line that kills without anger had, in its headsmen, a fire meant to burn the condemned without the headsman's hate in it, kindled only by the weight of what the condemned had done. Shōki had kept the fire and lost the rest. Tarturon seeded it in the struck body with its own oxidiser in it, a reaction that carries its own air, so that it burns at thermite heat underwater, in sand, in a sealed vault, and cannot be smothered, because water only turns to steam around it and bursts. And it burns by the record. TSUMI-YOMI reads the chest, and the count is the fuel, and a man with no killing in him takes GŌKA at its base and no more. A man with four hundred and twelve takes it at the weight of four hundred and twelve.

And then the bank. Thirty-one coins in a purse, all one name, spendable only against their author, and the author's chest under his hand. Shōki poured them in. All of it. The needle at the nape; twenty-nine stones on the stair; one on the breastbone. Every pinprick the old man had paid into him that morning, given back to him at once, with the force of the original blow returned, and the force of all of them together.

*FFFFFWWWWOOOOOMMMMMMM.*

---

Raizan felt his blood begin to sizzle.

It started at the place under the palm, over his heart, and it went in past the skin. He felt it go through the breastbone and into the great vessels behind it, the aorta and the vena cava, the fat warm tubes that carry everything, and he felt the blood in them begin, very quietly, to boil. *Tssssssss.* It was a sound he heard from the inside, in his own ears, the way a man hears his own heartbeat in a quiet room. *Tsss. Tsss. Tsssss.* From the heart it went out, along every vessel at once, the way a fire goes out along the lines of oil spilled on a floor: down the arms, up the throat, into the face, down the long legs into the river. Under the scalded skin of his hands the veins stood up in a sudden dark tracery, black, swollen, steaming, like the veins in a leaf held up against a lamp. The small vessels in the whites of his eyes went one after another, *pik, pik, pik*, until his sight was red. Steam came out of his pores. Out of the scalded cheek, where the blister broke and ran. Out of the corners of his eyes. His heart, which had kept time so patiently for seventy-seven years, went *thrum* against the palm, hard, and then *thrum-thrum-thrum* very fast, like a bird beating at a closed window.

And the strangest thing: it did not explode.

It should have. A fire like that, at that weight, placed in a body, should have gone off like a powder-store and taken half the ford with it, the bridge and the bank and the people on it. Raizan's Tengan had shown him that strand, among the likeliest, a white flash and a crater in the river. It was not the strand that came.

Because the other hand was on him too.

Shōki's left hand had come up while the right poured the fire in, and laid itself flat on the old man's chest beside it, a second touch on the same place, and the second touch was the swordsmith's. *YAKI-IRE.* The quench. The art that drives heat into a thing with the first touch and pulls it back out through the palm with the second, in an instant, ENSHŌ swallowing it. Only this time it quenched the fire as it burned, drawing the Karmic Fire's heat out of the old man's body through the left palm almost as fast as the right palm put it in. The fire burned in Raizan by the weight of his sins, and its heat, instead of going outward into the river and the ford and the child on the bridge rail, went into Shōki.

Into the furnace the river had banked to coals. The Sin of Wrath was drinking it.

Raizan saw him take it. The broken glaze of Shōki's chest, the cracked Covenant Scar, the crazed white skin of his arms: under the black flakes, the ember light came back into every crack at once, dull red and then orange and then a hard, blinding yellow, the way a smith's iron comes back up through its colors when it goes back into the fire. The steam pouring off the Sin's shoulders went from white to clear, the river round his waist went from hissing to boiling, *BLBLBLBLBLBLBLBLBL*, and the dead eye's ember climbed from coal to flame. The aura came back round him, Caloria orange, grained like a filament overdriven, the black-violet rim thick and hard as a horseshoe. The river had taken from him what it had taken. He was taking it back out of the man who had led him into the river.

He was burning the old man's sins to warm himself.

It was, Raizan thought, through the boiling in his ears, very nearly elegant. He would have liked to tell him so.

---

He was dying. He knew it with perfect calm, the way he had known every other doom he had ever read: by looking. The Tengan was still open, the last thing in him still working, and he turned it on his own knot, and there it was, the strand he had watched creep closer for fifty years every time he broke someone else's. The window. The go board. The plum blossom falling. *This morning.* It was not long. Twenty heartbeats. Perhaps thirty, at the speed his heart was going.

He could have spent them on the name. He had offered it. The Sin had not answered. He found, now, that he did not mind.

He looked past the Sin's shoulder instead, with the last of the eye, because the eye showed what was likely, and something on the bridge was suddenly very likely.

The child.

The little girl who had climbed up on the bridge rail to see. Her mother had noticed now. The mother was screaming, running along the bridge planks with her hands out, and the girl had turned at the scream, the way children turn, and her foot had gone off the wet rail. Raizan saw her go over, very slowly, the way things go when the Tengan shows them: the small body tipping back over the rail, the arms coming up, the mouth open, the brass draw-lamp on its chain swinging away from her hand where she had grabbed and missed. She went into the Kessen twenty yards upstream of them with hardly a splash. The flood took her at once. It was carrying her down toward the ford. Toward the boiling water round the Sin of Wrath, which was boiling harder every heartbeat as he drank.

Ten heartbeats, and she would be in it.

And Raizan, dying, reading, looked at the Sin's knot one last time, the knot he had said all morning had no ending in it, and saw that he had been wrong.

There was one strand. One. It had not been there a moment ago; it had come into being when the child went off the rail. It was very thin. It was the only strand in the whole clenched fist of the Sin's fate where the black hand's grip did not reach, because it was the only one where the Sin did something the hand had not foreseen. It was the strand where Shōki took his eyes off the old man, and turned his head, and looked outward.

*True Asami looks outward.* It was on the card. *Anything that could turn his eyes outward and hold them there long enough would leave the Unburied nothing to grip.*

The old man did not know what was at the end of that strand. The Tengan shows what is likely, and that strand was the least likely thing on the river. It might be the end of the Sin. It might be the end of the law that held him up, and so of him. It might be nothing. It might be a girl pulled out of a river.

He could not tell. He only knew it was there, and that the Sin had to choose it, and that he had perhaps breath for one word.

He did not use the name.

[a last breath, scalded, utterly gentle] "*Look,*" said Raizan Tenrai Moto.

---

Shōki's hands were on the old man's chest. The fire was going in through the right and the heat was coming back through the left, and the warmth of it was in him like wine, like the first fire of winter in a cold house, and the old man's count was burning down under his palms, four hundred and twelve and three hundred and something and less, and HAKANASHI held him there. Thirty-one blows. He had to answer them. He was answering them. A thing done to him could not be left unfinished, and this was the finishing, and the law would not let go of his hands until the book was closed.

And behind him, upstream, a woman was screaming.

And the old man had said *look*.

The river boiled round him. The draw-lamps on the bridge swung. Something small and dark was coming down the green water toward the white boil round his waist, very fast, and the black hand on his soul pulled north, and the law pulled at his hands, and the heat of four hundred and twelve men's deaths was pouring into him through his left palm like the first warmth he had felt since the black floor.

He had a heartbeat. Perhaps two.

*Naori Yukari · the waiting bench, and the yard at Senri's Gate*

---

The first thing Naori Yukari ever saw with the blood was a woman drowning.

He was three. She was the miller's wife from the lower terrace, and she stood in the yard alive and laughing at something her husband had said, flour white on her forearms to the elbow, while Naori watched the river close over her face in a spring that had not come yet. He had a cup of warm barley in both hands. He dropped it. His mother lifted him and asked what was wrong, and he pointed at the laughing woman and could not say what he saw, because nothing had happened. There was only the shape it was going to take, standing in the air behind her like a second shadow cast by a sun nobody else could see. The thaw came that spring, in the twelfth week. The miller's wife went down to the river with the washing on her hip, and the ice at the bank was rotten under the snow.

That was the beginning of it, and it never ended.

The elders had a name for what lived behind his eyes. Inzensho, the Illumination of the Total Cause: the rarest of the three ways the Ketsumyōgan could open in a Yukari child, surfacing perhaps three times in the written record. His father read the threads of the world sideways, the way a carver reads the grain of a block, across everything that was present in the room at once. Naori read them forward. Every person he looked at trailed their causes ahead of them like a wake that had come loose of its boat and gone on alone, and the strands ran out across the days toward things that had not collapsed into being yet. The faculty did not care whether he wanted it. It read along the time axis the way an ear hears whatever sound arrives, and the Crystal behind his breastbone had begun to grow around it before he could walk, layer on layer, faster than the bloodline records said a child's Crystal should grow.

Most of what it showed him was small. A man would turn his ankle on the third step of the granary. A bitch would whelp six and lose two. A ward-carving would crack along its second glyph in the hard frost, and the carver would curse and start again. Small things he learned to let pass, the way a person learns to stop hearing a mill wheel.

The large things he never learned to let pass.

He saw a border patrol come back up the valley road with one horse led and its saddle empty, and he knew whose saddle it was a season before the man was dead. He saw a cart overturned in a ditch on the southern corridor with the driver's leg pinned under the axle, the man screaming for an hour before anyone came. He saw fires. Those were the worst. A long hall with its cedar beams alight and the sap boiling out of them in hissing gold beads. A terrace of bodies laid in rows under a line of trees, covered with whatever cloth was to hand. Children among them. He saw men in a foreign colour doing things in a village he did not know to people who had stopped begging. He saw his own mother's hands, the ones that smelled of ink and rice starch and held his face every morning, lying still on a floor somewhere with the fingers curled. Some of it never came. The threads ran toward many endings at once and only one of them would commit, and he could not tell which. That was its own cruelty. Every horror he saw was a possibility with a body, and he had to carry all of them, because he could not tell the one that would come from the nine that would not.

So he stopped reaching.

A child who sees where the warmth will be damaged learns to stop warming his hands. When his mother held him, he saw her hands on the floor, still, the fingers curled, and he went rigid in her arms until she set him down. When the carvers' boys asked him to come down to the river, he saw the river. When old Hanuri gave him a sweet bean cake at the festival of lanterns, he saw her hip break on the winter ice and the long bad season after, and he said thank you and set the cake down untasted on the bench, and walked to the edge of the practice yard and stood with his hands folded behind his back. People in the enclave began to say he was a cold child. They said it kindly, mostly. Yukari children were strange. His father had been strange. But the cold set in him the way frost sets in a stone, from the outside first and then down through the grain, until by eight there was very little of him anyone could touch. He watched. He was polite. He spoke in short words placed like inscription points, and he did not cry, because crying asked other people to come closer, and closer was where the threads were worst.

---

The morning his father struck him down was the coldest of that winter.

The practice yard at Senri's Gate sat on the terrace below the long hall, beaten earth laid over limestone and fenced on three sides by cedar rails gone silver with age. Frost stood on the rails in a white nap. The limestone breathed its mineral smell up through the earth even in deep cold, sharp and clean as a struck flint, and under it the cedar beams of the hall above were weeping a little sap that froze in amber drops along the eaves. Somewhere down the terrace a carver was already at his work. The chisel carried up to him: tap, a pause, tap, tap, the pause the man took to listen to the stone before he committed the next stroke. Through the soles of his feet the yard's ward-glyphs hummed in their stations along the rail-posts, each one a slow low note in the ground, the valley's Cymorath singing back to him at a pitch his father had taught him to hear when he was six.

He was eight, and his breath stood white in front of his face, and he had been in the yard since before the light.

His father had put a wooden katana into his hands in the autumn. White oak, as long as his arm and shoulder together, the grip wrapped in grey cord that had gone dark with his sweat in the weeks since. It was heavy for him. The weight lived in the forward third of the blade, and holding it in a guard for any length of time put a burning ache across the tops of his forearms and into the backs of his shoulders. Niran had told him the ache was the point. Niran had told him a great many things that autumn, in his quiet voice, in the order of work: feet, then hips, then the breath, then the hands, then the blade, and the eyes last.

The eyes last. That was the trouble.

Niran Yukari stood across the yard from him with an oak blade of his own held low and loose in one hand, as if he had forgotten it was there. Six feet of him, lean as a drawn wire, black hair tied back past his hips and catching the thin light with the thread-shimmer that was not quite iridescent and not quite black. His breath came slower than Naori's, much slower, and it barely clouded. His eyes were the dull crimson of a coal under ash, the iris long since swallowed whole by the corona, and in that red field the thread-patterns turned slowly, the way they always turned, reading. Being looked at by him felt like being inside a measurement. Naori's own eyes were not like that yet. At eight the crimson still stood only as a ring at the edge of his irises, a band of red around brown, but the threads were already turning inside it, and they did not turn sideways the way his father's did. They tracked forward.

"Again," Niran said.

Naori raised the oak.

He knew the cut before his father moved. That was the Inzensho, and it was not a gift in that yard. Before Niran's weight had even settled into his front foot, the threads had run ahead of it and shown Naori the end: the oak coming down in a long diagonal from his father's right shoulder, and where it finished, which was the left side of Naori's own head, just above the ear. He saw it land. He saw his own skull take it. He saw himself on the ground with the frost under his cheek.

And his body tried to defend against the thing he had seen.

It did it without asking him. Every muscle in his arms fired at once, the ones that should lift the blade and the ones that should let it lift, pulling against each other until both elbows locked and the oak stood in the air stiff as a fence post. His shoulders came up toward his ears. His chin tucked. His weight shifted back onto his heels to get away from a blow that had not been struck, and in shifting back he lost the ground under him, and the oak in his hands was raised to meet the cut at its end, at the place above his ear where the threads had shown him it would finish. It was the right place for the end of the cut. It was the wrong place for the cut.

Niran's oak never went there.

It came into the space between Naori's blade and his body while his arms were still locked against a future, turned on his father's wrists in a small flat circle, and dropped onto the crown of his head with a dry sound like a knuckle rapped on a door.

There was no pain at first. There was white. The yard and the rails and the frost tipped sideways, and the tap of the carver's chisel down the terrace went very far away, and then the ground came up and struck his shoulder and his cheek, and the frost on the beaten earth was so cold it burned. The pain arrived after, a great bright ringing that started at the top of his skull and ran down into his teeth. His oak had gone somewhere. He could not find his hands. A taste like a copper coin sat at the back of his tongue, and his eyes ran water he had not decided to let them run.

He lay there and breathed. In, white. Out, white. The ringing went on.

Niran had placed it to the hair's breadth, and some distant part of Naori that was already a Yukari knew it even through the ringing. A blow like that from a grown man's arm, at a Stage like his father's, could have opened the skull like a gourd. It had not. It had been put down on the crown of his head with exactly the weight it took to fold an eight-year-old boy to the ground, and no more, and the restraint in it frightened him more than force would have. Force would have meant his father lost his temper. This meant his father had decided.

Bootsoles on frozen earth. His father's shadow came over him and took the thin light away.

"I am so disappointed, Naori."

The voice did not rise. Niran's voice never rose. It came down to him level and quiet, the way it came down to apprentices in the carvers' yard, and it was the quiet that made his eyes run harder.

"When you defend, you show fear," his father said. "When you attack, you are afraid of being killed. I can hear it. Your breath goes high in your chest before I move. Your grip tightens until the cord creaks. You set your feet to run before you set them to stand." He let that rest a moment. "How do you expect to protect your mother? Your sisters? Anyone you see in your mind, if you are afraid?"

Naori got an elbow under himself. Then a hand. The yard tilted and he waited for it to stop. When it stopped, he sat up on the frozen ground with his legs folded under him and his hands in fists on his thighs, and he did not touch his head, because touching it would be admitting it hurt.

His father crouched in front of him. Close, the scent of him was cold stone and cedar and the faint bitter edge of ink, and the thread-marks along his forearms glowed a dull red where the sleeves had ridden up, wrist to elbow, deep enough to show in the grey morning. He set the oak down across his knees. Then he reached out and put his hand on Naori's shoulder.

It was a light hand. It did not grip. But Naori felt the read go through it all the same, the Substrate Voice his father carried in his skin: a hum that passed through the cloth of his jacket and into the bone of his shoulder and down into the Crystal under his breastbone, measuring. He knew what his father was hearing. The Crystal running hot. The Inzensho turning in it too fast for the body around it. A boy's heart going like a hare's.

Niran looked at him for a long time.

"You cannot hope to be a protector," his father said, "if you are too busy being worried about your own life."

Naori said nothing. The ringing had faded to a throb in time with his pulse, and every beat of it said *you saw it, you saw it land, you saw it and you could not stop it*.

"You remind me of myself." Niran's thumb moved once against his shoulder. "So desperate to protect that you would give your own life for it. You would set your own well-being on the fire for the fight and call that love." The crimson in his eyes darkened a shade. "That notion is stupid. Do you understand me?"

Naori did not understand him. Those were two different things. Afraid to die and willing to die. They could not both be true of one person, and his father had just said both of him in the same breath.

"Listen to me clearly, boy," Niran said.

The carver's chisel had stopped down the terrace. Naori did not know when. In the silence the ward-glyphs along the rail-posts went on humming in the ground, low and steady, and the frozen sap ticked on the eaves of the long hall above them as the thin sun touched it.

"You think those are two things. The fear and the giving." His father spoke slowly, each word set down in its exact place, as if he were cutting it. "They are the same thing. A man who is afraid to die and a man who has made his peace with dying are both looking at the same place. The grave. Neither of them is looking at the sword in front of him." He tapped the oak across his knees with one finger. "The coward stares at it and flinches. The martyr stares at it and walks toward it, and thinks himself brave. Both of them have already left the fight. Both of them are already standing at the end."

Naori's throat had gone tight. He swallowed. "I see the end," he said.

"I know."

"I saw it land. Before you moved."

"I know." The hand on his shoulder stayed where it was. "Your eyes run to the end of every thread. They always have. You saw my cut finish on your head, and so you defended your head, at the end, where the cut was going to be. Your arms locked against something that had not happened yet. Your feet tried to run from it. You fought my sword where it was going to be, and so you were not there for my sword where it was." His father's head tilted, very slightly, the way it did when he was listening to stone. "My cut had a middle, Naori. Every cut that has ever been struck has a middle, and that is where I changed it. You were not there to see me change it, because you had already gone ahead to watch yourself fall."

Naori looked at the frost between his knees. A single crystal of it had grown on a fleck of chaff, a tiny branched feather of ice. He watched it instead of his father's face, and for a moment he did not see where it went. For a moment it was only frost.

"When you were three," Niran said, "you saw the miller's wife go into the river."

Naori's head came up. The movement sent a bolt of pain through his skull and he did not care.

"Your mother told me," his father said. "You never did. You have never told me any of what you see, except once, on the stone above the gate." The crimson field turned. "I know what the Inzensho shows you. Not the pictures. I cannot see your pictures. But I know their weight. I have watched you set down cakes you did not eat. I have watched you stand at the edge of this yard with your hands behind your back so that no one would take them. I watch you stand at the edge of everything." His voice did not change, but something under it did. "You have seen what is coming for the people you love, and you have decided that the way to carry it is to stand very still and very cold and wait for the moment when you can throw yourself in front of it."

Naori's eyes stung. He held them open. He would not blink, because if he blinked the water would go.

"I know that plan," Niran said. "I made it too."

The wind came down off the ridge and moved through the cedar rails with a dry whispering sound, and the frost on the near rail lifted from the wood in a glittering breath and settled again. His father did not look at it. He reached up and pushed the sleeve of his jacket back from his left forearm, past the glowing thread-marks, to where a long pale scar ran across the muscle like a seam in leather.

"A ship," he said. "I was thirteen. Younger than I should have been to be on it, and alone." He pushed the right sleeve back off his shoulder and turned so Naori could see the second scar, a ragged crescent where something with teeth had closed on him. "A Greater Wolf. And here." He touched the line on his jaw, thin and white under the stubble. "Sōma, sparring, when I was proud and he was quicker." He let the sleeves fall. "Do you know what those are?"

Naori shook his head. It hurt.

"Choices," his father said. "Each one was a worse thing I turned into a smaller thing. The ship would have taken my arm, and I gave it a scar instead. The wolf wanted my throat. I gave it my shoulder. And every time, Naori, every time, I was thinking of living and never once of dying well. I was thinking of the next morning. The next wall to carve. The next meal." He paused. "A man who wants to die for the people he loves hands his body over at the first price anyone names. A man who means to live for them bargains. He gives the arm to keep the throat, and the shoulder to keep the heart, and he fights for every inch of himself, because every inch is something they will need tomorrow."

Down the terrace the chisel began again. Tap. A pause. Tap.

"Do you know how I learned your grandfather was dead?"

Naori did not answer. He had heard the name Naiser. He had heard it spoken at the stone above the gate at midwinter and seen his father's hand go flat on the rock afterward. Nobody had told him more than the name.

"I felt the thread go," Niran said. "Far away. I could not reach it. And then I learned what it was four months after it happened, from a man who had carried the news across the sea." His voice stayed level, but his jaw moved once, a small hard shift under the scar. "He died in a stone room below the daylight. In chains. And he was thinking of me when he went. That is what I was told, by a man with no reason to lie about it. He was thinking of me." He was quiet for a breath. "He was a brave man. He would have died for anyone in this valley, and he did die, and it saved no one, and I found out four months late. The bravery did not reach me, Naori. Only the gap did. Only the empty place where he should have been."

The wind went through the rails again. Naori's fists had opened on his thighs without his deciding it.

"Your grandfather taught me to keep the size of what I am hidden," Niran said. "So that people would see a son instead of a weapon. He was right about that, and I have kept it. But he taught me one wrong thing too, without meaning to, the way fathers teach most things." He looked down at his own hands, the ink ground into the creases, the thread-marks glowing faintly at the wrists. "He taught me that a protector is someone who spends himself. Who pours himself out on the ground for others until there is nothing left, and calls the emptiness a gift. I believed that a long time. I walked toward every bad thing I could hear coming with my chest open. I wanted it to take me instead of them." He lifted his eyes to his son's. "And your mother told me, the first winter we had together, that she did not want a man who would die for her. She wanted a man who would come home."

He let that stand in the cold air between them.

"Your mother keeps the kitchen warm," Niran said, "for a boy who comes back through the door. She does not keep it warm for a name cut into a stone."

The tears went. Naori felt them go, hot on his frozen cheeks, and he could not stop them, and he hated it, and he did not wipe them, because his hands would not leave his thighs.

His father did not look away from them. He did not soften his face either. He only waited, the way he waited for stone to tell him where to cut.

"Father," Naori said. His voice had gone small, the six-year-old's voice from the stone above the gate, and he could not make it older. "What do I do with it. With what I see."

"Do you know the words on Senri's tablet?"

Naori nodded. Every child at the Gate knew them. "All things with art," he said. "Focus on the innards, and you will understand the outer world."

"The innards," his father said. "The middle." He picked up the oak from across his knees and held it out to him, grip first, the grey cord dark with the boy's own sweat from the morning. "Your eyes will always run to the end. That is the blood. You did not ask for it and you cannot give it back, and I am sorry for that, more than I have words to tell you. But your eyes are not the whole of you. You have feet. You have hips. You have breath. You have hands. And all of those live here, in the middle of the thread, in the one moment where the sword actually is. The end shows you where it is going. The middle is where you can still change it." He held the oak steady between them, the point not moving at all. "The end belongs to the threads. The middle belongs to you."

Naori looked at the oak for a long moment, at the dark cord and the pale grain and his father's ink-creased fingers around the blade.

"See the end," Niran said. "Let it show you. Then come back. Come back to your feet. Come back to the ground under them and the breath in your chest and the weight in your hands, and stand in the middle, where I am. If you see your own death in the threads, good. Now you know one place not to be. Use it. Bargain with it. Give it your shoulder to keep your heart. But do not stand there staring at it, waiting for it to come and let you off the work of living."

Naori's hand came up off his thigh. It was shaking. He took the oak by the grip, and the cord was cold, and his father's fingers stayed on the blade for one more breath before they let it go.

"You want to protect your mother," Niran said. "Your sisters. Every face the threads show you on the ground. Then you must live. That is the whole of the art, under the cut and the guard and everything I will ever teach you: the will to stay alive, held so hard you would crawl back through the mud to it. A protector who dies has protected exactly once. A protector who lives does it again tomorrow, and the day after, and every day the threads show him something coming. The one who is still standing when it arrives is the only one who can do anything about it." He rose to his feet, and the frost cracked under his boots. "Your life is not yours to spend, Naori. It belongs to everyone who will need you after today. Do not ever be so proud as to throw away a thing that belongs to them."

Naori sat on the frozen ground with the oak across his knees the way his father had held it. The throb in his skull beat steadily. The ward-glyphs hummed along the rail-posts. Down the terrace, the carver listened to his stone and struck.

"Enough for today," Niran said quietly. He turned toward the steps up to the long hall, where the smell of rice steam and ginger had begun to come down from the kitchen, and stopped with one foot on the first step. He did not look back, and his voice came down to the yard as level as it had been all morning. "It won't be enough forever," he said. "But it is enough today."

He went up the steps.

Naori stayed in the yard a long time after. When the cold had finally gone through his trousers into his legs, he stood, and set his feet, and raised the oak, and closed his eyes. The threads came anyway. They always came. He let them run out to the end and show him himself on the ground, and then, slowly, the way a man pulls a bucket up out of a well hand over hand, he brought himself back. Back to his feet on the beaten earth. Back to his breath going white in front of his face. Back to the ache across the tops of his forearms and the weight in the forward third of the blade.

It was the first time in his life he had ever tried to be in the middle of anything.

He was not good at it. It would be years before he was any good at it at all. But when he went up the steps at last, with his skull still ringing and his cheeks stiff with dried tears, his mother was at the door of the kitchen with her ink-stained hands wrapped in a cloth, and she looked at the lump rising on the crown of his head and then at his face, and he did not see her hands on the floor.

For one breath, he only saw her.

---

The waiting bench is cold under him. It is always cold, in every arena he has ever sat in, as if the builders of fighting places all agree that a man about to bleed should first be reminded he has a body.

Naori Yukari sits with his forearms on his knees and his hands loose between them, and he does not fold them behind his back. He made himself stop doing that, in the end. It took a long time.

Above him the stands are a long uneven roar, rising and falling like surf on shingle. The stamp of feet comes down through the boards over his head as a slow vibration in the timber, a hundred heartbeats out of time with each other. The Cymorath reads it for him whether he wants it or not: the structural resonance of the seating, the sway in the third beam from the left where a peg has worked loose, the low pressure-wave of a crowd drawing breath together at something on the sand. The tunnel mouth smells of raked sand and old blood. There is lamp oil in it too, and leather, and other people's sweat gone sour with waiting. Out on the floor two attendants are raking where the last bout ended. The teeth hiss through the sand, and under the hiss comes a faint wet clump where the sand has drunk enough to cake.

His eyes are fully crimson now. The corona swallowed the last of the brown when he was eleven, two years sooner than it took his father's, and the threads turn in the red field slowly and constantly, always forward. They are turning now. The shape of the bout ahead of him lies out on the sand, its strands running toward their endings, many endings at once, some of them with his own body at the end of them on the ground.

He lets them show him. He has learned to let them show him.

Then he comes back.

Back to the cold of the bench under his thighs. Back to the weight of his feet flat on the stone, the left a little forward. It is his father's stance. His sister swears he stands in it, and he still tells her he does not. Back to the breath in his chest, low and slow, the way a man who never once raised his voice taught it to him in a frozen yard. His hand drifts up without his deciding it and touches the crown of his head. That is where the oak came down. There is no mark there. There never was. His father placed it too well for a mark.

*The end belongs to the threads.*

He lowers his hand to his knee.

*The middle belongs to me.*

Out on the floor the rakes are finished, and the attendants are walking off with them over their shoulders, and the roar of the stands is gathering itself the way the wind gathers itself on the ridge above Senri's Gate before it comes down through the cedar.

Naori sits on the bench, prepared for the tournament. He is still alive, and he means to stay that way. He sets his feet.

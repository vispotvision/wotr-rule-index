*The blackened plain under the cracked sky, the fourteenth day, past noon; inside OTHSAL, and then outside it*

Faisal felt the flower begin to shake, and understood it before the first petal cracked.

*Thrrrrrrrrrrrrm.*

It ran in from the edges, the way a shiver runs in from the fingertips of a cold man: the outermost ribbons of gold curled over the rim of the sky trembling like plucked strings, then the next layer in, then the next, the hum running down the script toward the center of the Domain where he stood. *TWANG. THUNK. TWANG. THUNK.* Every verdict OTHSAL handed down on the Unkeepable Word was the correction of the one before it, and every correction was the fault the next had to correct, and his brother was leaning on it in time. Faisal heard the rhythm of it and knew it at once, because he had heard it before, in mortal things: a bridge in the Old World that had stood a hundred years under carts and cattle and the weight of whole markets, and had torn itself off its own piers one spring morning because a company of soldiers marched across it in step. It had not taken a heavier army. It had taken a patient one.

His brother had always been the most patient thing in creation when there was something to break.

[quietly, almost admiring] "Ah," Faisal said. "You found the note."

The pain came then, and it came from his own flower. OTHSAL was grown, not lent, and a thing a man has grown out of himself over eleven thousand years is not separate from him. Every shudder of the petals ran down the gold into his body along the lines it had grown out of, and his body was already half light and half crack and very little else. The first shudder went through him like a hand shaking a cage, *Grrrk*, and something in his chest let go: a rib, the fourth on the left side, snapping clean under the dented plate. The second shudder took the one beneath it. He felt the broken end turn inward and go into the soft wet weight of his lung, and when he breathed in, the breath bubbled.

*Khh. Khhrrr.*

Blood came up his throat warm and thick and tasting of copper and gold. He swallowed it. It came up again, and this time he let it come, out over his lip and down his chin in a long bright string that dripped onto the plate, *tap, tap, tap*, and ran into the dents.

He would not let the flower fall. Not yet. There was still a way.

---

*TALGRIM.*

The Order of Precedence. The Host's oldest clerks would have known the name: *tal*, the tally kept, and *grim*, the order in which things are entered. It is the quiet art beneath every other art of the Midreach, and it was the thing about Faisal that the Host had feared more than his hands or his Domain: not the power to make a thing so, but the power to decide in what *order* things are so. Every event is a member of a set. Every set can be arranged. A ledger with the same entries in a different sequence tells a different story; a battle with the same blows struck in a different order ends with a different man standing. A hundred thousand outcomes lie scattered across every moment, like cards spread face-down on a table, and most of what any working does is shuffle them. Faisal could take up the deck, and choose the order of the cards.

He reached into the resonance and began to rearrange it.

A bridge shakes itself apart because the blows come at its own note, evenly, one after another, every one landing on the swing the last one made. Break the evenness and the swings stop adding up. That is why armies break step on bridges. Faisal took the verdicts of OTHSAL, the snap and the spine, the snap and the spine, and permuted them. His law could not stop them; it could choose when each one fell. A spine where the next snap should have been. Two snaps together and a long gap. Three spines in a ragged row. The same set of verdicts, all of them true, all of them handed down, in a sequence that would never once fall in time with itself.

[Len] he wrote along the ribbons, Line, that defines a clean sequence between two events. [VaM], Moment, that isolates a single instant from causal drift. [Ho], Order, that restores chronology. The script ran out across the shaking gold in a great spiral, and the rhythm of the Domain broke step.

*TWANG-THUNK. THUNK. TWANG. ... TWANG-TWANG. ........ THUNK.*

The shudder in the petals faltered. The hum running in from the rim of the sky lost its beat, went ragged, fell to a stammer, and for the space of a breath the great golden flower of OTHSAL stood almost still above the plain, swaying gently, like a tree after a gust has passed.

It cost him. Talgrim at the scale of a Domain is the rearrangement of a hundred thousand moments a breath, every one of them taken up and set down somewhere else by hand, and every one of them weighed. It drew on what was left of him the way a candle draws on its wick when the wax is gone. His vision went red at the edges. Faisal felt the small blood vessels in the whites of his eyes give way one after another, *pik, pik, pik*, like tiny threads snapping, and the gold of his irises swam in a field of scarlet, and two thin lines of blood ran down from the inner corners of his eyes over his cheekbones, like tears that had chosen the wrong color.

He blinked it away. He could still see. The flower was holding.

And then it was not, because his brother did what his brother always did, and found the new rhythm inside the broken one.

Faisal felt the Cataclysm come into the script again, a little, on every swing, and every swing it came in on the beat he had just made. There is no order so irregular that patience cannot learn it. Every shuffle he made, his brother heard, and leaned on, and every new arrangement Faisal set down became, after a few breaths, a new rhythm to lean on. *Twang. Thunk. Twang-twang. Thunk.* The petals began to shudder again. Faster. Faster than before, because now the Domain was answering two masters, his ordering and his brother's push, and the two were running against each other through the law like two hands wringing a cloth.

*THRRRRRRRRRRRRRRRRRRRRRRRMMMMMM.*

---

There was one other way, and he had known it from the start.

He felt it the way a man standing at a cliff's edge feels the shape of the drop without looking down. He was Order, and the word was what he was, all the way through, under the plate and the light and the failing flesh, the same way his brother was Chaos all the way through. Order embodied. Lawfulness given a body and a voice. And Order, embodied, at the end of everything, with all the weight of the Bell in it and nothing borrowed left, could do one last thing that nothing else in creation could.

It could make a rule.

A rule for the world itself, deeper than any working, set down at the root, beneath the Axiom of the Kept, beneath every law of the Material, so that the world could never afterward be otherwise. He could feel the words of it ready in his mouth in Vow-Cant, and they were very simple. *No oath may contradict itself.* That was all. One clause, set as the foundation stone of reality, and the Unkeepable Word would not be a paradox any longer. It would be nothing. A shape of sound with no meaning, falling out of his brother's mouth onto the dirt. OTHSAL would stop shaking. The verdicts would stop. And his brother, who had built himself of contradictions since before there were mortals, would find himself standing in a world where his deepest nature could no longer be spoken.

The Host had had a name for that rule once, in the days before the Bell, when there were Celestials who wanted it written. *The Stilled World.* A world of perfect order. Every word meaning one thing. Every act one consequence. Every choice already made.

And no one in it would ever again be free to say a thing that made no sense, or to do a thing nobody had foreseen, or to change their mind halfway through a promise, or to break, or to begin again. The mother in the salt gallery would not be free to hope two things at once. The boy on the frozen lake would not be free to spare the man he had beaten. The Stilled World would have no chaos in it anywhere, and so it would have no room in it anywhere. Every mortal in the Material would wake up the next morning a little more obedient and never know why, the way the thief in the market town had forgotten why he wanted to lie, and they would go on forgetting, a little more every day, until they were all as perfectly kept as the Tally in a Choir's ledger, and as alive.

And Order cannot outlive the Chaos it is made against. He knew that too. Without his brother there would be nothing for him to be the opposite of. He would not even have the dignity of winning. He would only stop.

He had always known the rule was there. Every Midreach Celestial does. Most of the ones who were Severed before the Bell, the ones whose names stopped mid-Tally, stopped because they reached for it.

Faisal looked at it for a long moment in the shaking light, and then he put it down, the way he had put down the Crystal, the way a man puts down a sword he could have used and has decided not to.

[softly, almost tenderly, to the gold] "No," he said. "I will not do that to them. I will not do that to you."

He had not carried the weight of the world for seven hundred years so that the world could be still. He had carried it so that it could move.

---

OTHSAL broke.

It went at the rim first. The outermost petal, the one curled over the very edge of the sky, shuddered once more on a beat he could no longer move it off, and cracked across like a pane of gold glass, *PAKIIIN*, and the crack ran down its length and into the petal beneath, and that one cracked, *PAKIN*, and the next, *PAKIN-PAKIN-PAKIN*, the whole vast flower of script splitting along a thousand lines at once, the Vow-Cant spilling out of its ribbons in loose gold letters like coins pouring from a split purse, and then the whole sky of it came apart.

*KRRRRRRRSSSSSSHHHHHHHHHHHHHHHH.*

And it fell.

Petals of written law, each one the size of a field, each one heavy with every oath it had held, came down out of the sky in great tumbling sheets of gold, edge over edge, spinning, flashing, and below them lay the whole Material World, the ridges and the valleys and the river towns and, far off at the very edge of the falling, the smoke of the Crownlands where people were still crawling out of the rubble of the morning.

Faisal saw them begin to fall, and he did not decide anything.

The hands were out of him before he could choose. The last of them, all he had: a hundred, then two hundred, rising from his shoulders and his back and the ruin of his wings, rushing up into the sky toward the falling gold, and catching it. A palm under every petal. A pair under the largest. Hands catching the edges of written oaths as big as fields, holding them, and setting them down soft on the black plain, on the hillsides, on the empty ground away from any roof, one after another after another, *fwoom, fwoom, fwoom*, the way a man sets down a sleeping child.

*How many things can you catch before those lovely hands finally break?*

He had heard the question that morning. This was the answer. This many.

The hands began to break.

They went the way his fingertips had gone, gone clear at the ends and then cracking, the fine white lines running up the gold fingers and through the palms, and when they broke they crumbled inward, like sugar, like sandstone, into a fine bright dust that drifted down after the petals they had caught. He felt every one of them go. A hand is not separate from the one who reaches with it. Each one that broke, broke in him; something tore along his forearms at every crumbling, and the dark skin of his arms split in long ragged lines from wrist to elbow, *shhrk, shhrk*, opening down to the gold-shot red of the flesh beneath, and the blood came out of them not running but beading, bright, in long rows like the beads of a broken necklace.

He caught the last petal. He set it down.

He had nothing left to catch his brother with.

The Domain's ruin came home to him all at once. Everything OTHSAL had held, every verdict, every bound word, every second spine and every snapping rope it had handed down since it opened, had nowhere left to stand when the flower fell, and it went back into the one who had grown it, the way the heat of a smith's forge goes back into the forge when the doors are shut, the way the Funeral of Heaven had gone back up its own trench.

*DOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOOM.*

It struck him in the chest, at the open place where the Crystal had been, and picked him up off the stone he had made, and threw him.

He went back across the plain like a doll flung by a child in a temper, end over end, a long white streak of light and blood through the dust and the falling gold, and struck the ground, *KRAKK*, and went up again, and struck it again a hundred yards on, *KRAKK*, and the plate on his back burst open along the spine like a split log and the edges of it went into him. His left wing caught the ground on the second strike and folded the wrong way at the root, *KRRRNCH*, the long gold bone of it snapping at the shoulder joint so hard he heard it over everything, and the wing, the many-colored wing the mortals had given him, tore half away from his back and hung by a ribbon of muscle and light and dragged. The third strike took him across the side of the head. The gold cuff of laurel leaves tore out of his ear, and the ear with it, and went spinning away into the dust, *tink, tink, tinktink*.

He came to rest on his back in the black dirt at the bottom of the trench his brother's spear had plowed, and lay still.

---

The pain was extremely bad.

It was not a thing he had ever had to learn about before. Celestials are hurt, but a Celestial's body is held up by the Throne-network the way a lantern is held up by its chain, and the pain of a lent body is a distant thing, like a bell heard across a valley. He had cut that chain himself, with his own hands. The pain of the body he had now was his. It came up from everywhere at once: the shattered ribs grinding when he breathed, the lung filling slowly, every breath a wet bubbling rattle, *khhhh, khhhh*; the plate driven into the muscles of his back; the torn shoulder where the wing hung; the split arms; the ear that was not there; the long white cracks across his face and throat that had begun, very slowly, to widen, with light pouring out of them like water out of a broken jar.

He was fading. He could feel it. The last of his Essence was coming off him the way gold leaf comes off an old icon in a draught: a flake, and then another, drifting up off his skin in tiny glinting scraps and going out in the air. Under the scraps there was less of him each time, the dark skin going translucent, the shape of the bones beneath beginning to show through it in gold. His right hand, lying in the dirt by his side, had gone clear to the second knuckle. He could see the black soil through his own fingers.

A few minutes. That was all that was left. He knew it the way he had always known the weight of things.

And as the minutes began, the thing at the bottom of him that had always ordered the world began, at last, to come loose.

Talgrim had been his for eleven thousand years: the order of things, the arranging of the set. Now there was not enough of him left to hold it, and it turned in him like a key in a lock that has rusted, and slipped, and the moments of his life began to fall out of their order.

Things slowed down.

He saw a speck of gold dust from one of his broken hands drift down past his face, and it took a very long time to fall, the way snow seems to take a long time when you lie on your back in it and watch. He heard his own heart, *thrum*, and then the next beat did not come where it should have; it came later, much later, so that between one beat and the next there was room for all of this:

*Klak. Klak. Klak-klak.* A white courtyard. Two practice blades. His mother's hum over everything. His brother laughing because he had landed one.

The Bell cracking. Eleven minutes. The toll falling and falling and Faisal standing in it, whole, alone, with the weight coming down.

A boy on a frozen lake, falling out of his own body under an iron fist, and a hand under him.

Marium's tears on her round face, and his thumb wiping them away.

A lark, falling. A hand under it.

They came in no order at all. The first moment of his life, and the last morning, and the eleven thousand years between them, all shuffled together like a deck of cards thrown in the air, and every one of them face-up, and every one of them, he found, lying there, was a thing he would keep. There was not one he would trade.

*Thrum.*

His heart beat again, at last, very far off.

---

He opened his eyes. They were full of blood. Through it the sky was red and gold, with the crack still running across it, and the drifting petals of his fallen Domain lying gold all over the plain like leaves after an autumn storm, and dust, and smoke. And between him and the sky, a long way off and coming nearer, the shape of the one he had come down to meet.

And Faisal, lying in the bottom of a trench in the ruin of everything he had built, with his wing torn half off and his ribs in his lung and his body coming apart into light, could not help it.

He smiled.

It was a whole smile, with blood in the teeth. It was the same smile that had come over his face in the white courtyard when his brother landed a blow, before there were Choirs, before there were Bells, before either of them had any idea what they would be made to carry. He had borne all of it, all this time, every oath and every Bell and every age, so that his brother could have his freedom. Deserving had nothing to do with it. Freedom is a thing someone holds the floor up for. A freedom with nothing under it is only falling; a breaking with nothing to break is only noise. Somebody has to keep the world solid enough to be torn, and true enough to be lied to, and ordered enough that a wild thing can run across it and feel the ground under its feet. He had done that. For eleven thousand years and seven hundred more, he had held the floor up so that his brother would always have somewhere to stand while he broke things.

And he had been glad of it. That was the part he had never said. Every age of it, he had been glad.

He tried to lift his head. It would not lift. He let it lie in the dirt and looked up along the length of his own ruined body at the figure coming, and breathed in, *khhhhh*, through the blood, and spoke. It was a whisper. He had never needed more than a whisper. Under what was left of the Vow-Light, every word of it was true.

[a whisper, wet, smiling] "You kept your word, brother. Every one. I heard them all." A cough, and red on his lips. "I am not angry. I want you to know that before you reach me, so that there is no mistake about it. I was never angry. Not once. Not at the Bell. Not this morning. It was never personal, between us. We were only two people with work to do, at the two ends of one scale, and we did it with our whole hearts." His bloodshot eyes crinkled. "And I could have stopped you. Just now, at the end. You felt it. There was a rule I could have written, and you would never have spoken again. I put it down. Do you know why?"

The gold dust drifted down over him.

"Because then there would have been no one left in the world who could surprise me," Faisal said. "And I would have missed you more than I can carry."

His clear hand moved a little in the dirt, as if to reach, and could not.

"I do not have long. A few minutes. I would like to spend them looking at you, if you will let me. I have not looked at you, really looked, since the courtyard. I was always too busy kneeling." The smile again, small and helpless. "You have grown very tall."

Above him, in Midreach, through the crack in the gold floor, a small Celestial with pink wings had both her hands pressed over her mouth. Behind her, on the high mantle, an egg of gold light that had been humming all morning hummed a little louder.

Faisal did not see either of them. He was looking at only one thing, the way he had looked at it at the very beginning of everything, across a white courtyard, with a practice blade in his hand.

He said the name. He had not said it once all day; he had always called him brother.

"Malakar."

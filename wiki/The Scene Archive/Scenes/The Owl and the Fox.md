*The cedar wood on the Kessen road, the second evening, the last of the sun going red in the treetops*

---

The air round the Sin of Wrath had begun to turn.

Lorn saw it start at the man's feet. The steam that had been going straight up off the bare black ground in its slow white column bent, all at once, as if a hand had taken hold of it, and began to wind round him, round and round, faster, the way water winds round a drain. It took the dead snowdrops up with it, and the brown cedar needles, and the ash off the smoking bark, and spun them in a tall loose funnel twice the height of a man, *swshhhh, swshhhh*, a dry whispering roar like a broom on a stone floor going faster and faster. Lorn had seen a thing like it once, in the second summer of the war, when the Thornwall granary burned and the heat over it got so fierce it pulled the air in from every side and twisted it into a pillar of fire that walked across the yard on its own and lifted a cart. This was that without the fire. This was a man so hot that the world had started to wind itself round him like thread on a spindle.

And under the heat there was the other thing.

Lorn did not have a word for it. His father had not left him one. He knew anger; he had grown up with men who shouted and men who did not, and he knew the cold kind that a Stark carries in the jaw and the hot kind that comes up in a drunk. This was neither. It came off the man in the black coat like the heat did, in a long slow pressure against the face, and it went into Lorn through the eyes and the skin and sat down in his stomach and stayed there, heavy, as if he had swallowed a stone, and it was not his. That was the worst of it. It was somebody else's hate, three hundred years of it, and it had filled the cedar wood the way smoke fills a room, and he was breathing it.

Behind him the Stark riders were going down. He heard them go, one after another, the way a line of fence-posts goes when the frost heaves the ground: the creak of saddle-leather and then the soft heavy fall of a man in mail into snow, and the horses screaming and wheeling away riderless into the trees. Rob was on his hands and knees in the road, retching. Somebody was praying in a high thin voice, the Hollenmark prayer for a bad crossing. Lorn did not look round. A Stark does not look round.

He thought it through instead, the way his father had taught him, one step and then the next, like a man crossing ice. The man was eighty paces off. The Stark sword was at his hip, a kilogram and a half of good Hollenmark steel. To draw it was half a breath; to set his feet and bring the point to the ground for the line was a whole one. And he had seen the man step out from behind the cedar, and the snow come down off every branch on the road at once, and he knew in his body, the way you know a horse is going to go over before it goes, that the man could cross eighty paces before Lorn's hand was on the grip. He would be drawing his sword when it was already over. He would die with it half out of the scabbard, and the king behind him, and the Greymane girl, and every man on this road.

So he did not draw. He stood with his hands at his sides and his weight down through his heels and the rime on one half of his beard, and for the first time in his whole life he felt the sweat come out on his forehead and stand there in beads, and run, and not freeze.

He had never been afraid of anything that had a body. He was now.

"Sire," he said, without turning his head. "Get back."

The king walked past him.

His feet did it before Muken had decided anything, the way Yusif had taught them on the gallery floor with the stick: stop thinking, lad, and let the feet go where the fight is. They took him past Lorn's shoulder and past the end of Amelia's shield, where she had planted it in the road in front of him with the rust and gold coming up off her like heat off a hearth, and her ruined hand came out and caught the red lacing at his arm and held it.

"Muken." Flat. Very quiet. "Don't."

He did not shake her off. He stood there with her hand on his arm and let the Eye open, because there was nothing else in the world he knew how to do.

It opened all at once and it hurt. The heat behind his left eye went to a brand, and the gold seam in his iris tore wider by a thread, and the cedar wood came up before him in its load and its faults the way a salt face comes up under a lamp: every trunk with the weight running down it in fine gold lines into the roots, every branch with its crack where the snow had bent it, the road with the frost-heave under it, the riders on their knees each with his own small gold fractures where the war had hit him and he had healed crooked. And in the middle of all of it, eighty paces off, a man who was nothing but cracks.

He had never seen anything like it. He had looked at men with the Eye a thousand times and found the one place in each where the weight had gathered, the bad knee, the locked elbow, the rib that had knit badly. The man in the black coat was crazed all over like glaze on a pot that has been fired too hot, ten thousand fine gold lines running across him in every direction, his skin, his bones, the iron at his wrists, and every one of them was full. That was what Muken could not stop looking at. A crack in a salt face is where the weight goes when it has nowhere else to go, and every crack in this man was carrying weight, loaded to the edge of giving and not giving, the way a gallery ceiling carries a mountain over a void and holds for a hundred years and then comes down all at once in a single morning. The strain in him was enormous, more than Muken had known one body could hold, and it was pressing against something from the inside, a ceiling, a lid, the way water presses against the ice on a pond in a hard frost, and it could not get out.

And the Field was screaming. It had marked the man before Muken could see him, a pressure in the Eye so heavy it bent him at the neck, and it did what it always did and ranked him: one name at the top, and under it, so far down they were hardly there, everyone else in the world.

The weight came down on Muken. He felt it arrive the way the Lawbell's weight had arrived in the place made of threads, all at once, from above, six Stages of it, and his knees wanted to go the way Rob's had gone. He did not let them. He did what Faisal had shown him, kneeling, under the Bell: he let the weight come into his shoulders and go on through, down the long bones of his legs, through the soles of his boots into the frozen road, where it could do what it liked. The ground under him cracked in a ring, *tchk*. He stayed on his feet.

All the possibilities were in front of him. That was the Eye's whole curse. It showed him where everything would break, and he could see the road breaking, and the riders, and Lorn's mail, and Amelia's pauldron with the hairline in its second lame, and her shoulder under it. He could see the cedars going. And he could see one place on the man where the gold was brighter than the rest, low on the back of the neck, where a crack ran straight across the top of the spine as clean as a cut, the oldest crack on him, older than all the others, and every other crack ran into it the way streams run into a river.

The man shifted his weight off his left foot.

Muken saw the gold run down the right leg and gather in the heel before the man had moved at all, and knew he was coming, and knew it was already too late to do anything about it.

*Kiyome had been in the mist for nine miles.*

Hayato's road had carried them down the Kessen way in the dusk the way the Kessen carries a leaf, the three of them and nobody else, gone to cold gray fog along every bend of the track the Amagiri had walked and written down for eleven hundred years, and the whole of it she had felt the heat ahead of them growing like a sunrise on the far side of a hill. At the last bend she felt the mist begin to boil. Hayato said, in the fog, in his apologetic voice, *this is as close as the road will hold, my lady, I'm sorry*, and the fog came apart round her into nothing, and she came out of it at a dead run onto the frozen road forty paces behind her sister's son with the light already coming up off her.

She did not call it. She did not need to call it. It came up out of her the way it had come up out of her alone in the white house a thousand mornings behind the closed door: the glass of her aura breaking into a prism, the beads at her temples ringing instead of clicking, *rin, rin, rin*, a whine at the very edge of hearing, high and thin and clean, and every shadow on the road splitting into two. And out of the light, ahead of her, went the feathers.

The Byakuya beast is the Owl, and the Owl is carved over the door of her house, and the house's oldest working has the Owl's shape for the same reason the Owl hunts as it does: nothing hears it come. They went out from her in a long white fan, a hundred of them, two hundred, every one the shape of an owl's flight-feather and as long as a man's forearm, so thin they had no edge to look at, only a plane of white light with a cold rainbow burning along its rim. They made no sound at all. That was their sound. They went past Lorn Stark's shoulder and past the boy and past the Greymane girl's shield, so close that the girl's braid lifted in the wind of their going, and crossed the eighty paces in the time it takes a candle flame to bend, and went into the Sin of Wrath at the waist.

He had already been moving. His weight had come off the left foot and the right heel was down and the long wire body was beginning to unwind toward the king, and he met the feathers coming. They did not cut him the way steel cuts. Each feather was light held so hard and so narrow that where it touched it burned the surface off whatever it touched into vapor, the way a lens burns a hole in a leaf, and they went across his belly in a dozen white lines, *shu, shu, shu-shu-shu*, opening the crazed glaze of his skin from the left hip to the right in long shallow seams. Under the skin was the muscle of the belly wall, the straight bands either side of the navel and the slanting ones over the flank, and the feathers went into it a finger's depth and stopped there, because Kiyome had stopped them there.

There was no blood. ENSHŌ took it. Every seam the feathers had opened flushed cherry-red along its length and swelled and closed itself in a hiss of steam, *jūūūū*, and the meat under it glowed like iron in a forge and then went dull, and the feathers' heat went into him and stayed.

The cutting was the least of what they did.

The rest came in the instant each feather was inside him: Luminalis, which shows what is under a surface, lit the Sin of Wrath through from the belly to the spine like a lantern held behind a paper screen, and for one breath Kiyome Byakuya saw everything in him. She saw the scalded right lung where the river's steam had gone down into him at the ford, torn and weeping. She saw the heart going under the breastbone at a pace no heart should go, a horse at full gallop, *doku-doku-doku-doku*. She saw the great furnace of heat banked in the tissue of the arms and chest, a forge's worth of it, and the black-violet hysteresis of Vantabriel holding a few thin threads of it apart, tagged, owed: the bank. It was nearly empty. He had spent it on the old man. And she saw, at the back of the neck, along the line where a headsman's blade falls between the vertebrae, the open fracture in his Crystal that had never closed, glowing, with every lesser crack in him running into it.

She saw, too, where the feathers had gone into the bank. A thread of near-white had come into the black-violet already, tagged with her name. She had expected that. Every blow they landed was going to go there.

She came out of her own light onto the road between the boy and the furnace.

It looked to the boy, she learned afterwards, as if she had been made out of the feathers: as if the last of them had folded itself back into the shape of a woman. She had only been running behind them faster than eyes can follow, which is a different thing. She stopped with her heels in the road ten paces from the Sin of Wrath and the snow flashing to steam round her boots, and spun the spear out from behind her back, a long white circle in the air, *hyun*, and brought it down level with its point at his breastbone.

She was dressed for it. She had not been dressed for it in front of anyone in her life. The harness of the Byakuya guard, white lacquer laced in red silk, the reverse of the harness Kasane had worn, because the white line keeps the red, and the red lacing was the first thing on her that anyone in the north had ever seen: cuirass and skirts of lamellar plate the white of fresh snow, sleeves of fine mail under white silk, the glass beads of her veil pinned back from her face so that they hung at her temples, and the halo-ring at her crown throwing a pale gold circle on the steaming ground round her feet. Her grandmother had laced it. The spear was the field spear of the house, a cross-bladed jūmonji yari as long as she was tall and a forearm more, its straight blade and its two curved side-blades forged in one piece, its shaft lacquered white, and it had hung on the wall of the white house beside her sister's red harness for sixteen years. Her katana rode at her hip. She did not intend to draw it here. The air was too wet.

"Byakuya," she said. Clear, level, finished. "Kiyome Byakuya, of the White Line. The red before the white." The rings in her eyes lay flat and silver as ice. "You will not go one step nearer him."

The Sin of Wrath looked at her. He looked at her breastbone, the way the card said he looked at everyone, and the fine ring-lines in his living eye turned and filled with red light, and she felt him read her: a heat going over her chest like a hand, counting. She knew what he found there. Eleven men, in sixteen years, on fields nobody lived to describe. Not enough to fire him. He had to burn her at his base, and he knew it, and she saw him know it.

*Gogogogo.* It was in the ground. The whole wood was humming with him.

And then the fox barked.

It came from the high bank above the road to the east, among the cedar roots, a sharp hoarse yelping cry, *kon, kon*, the sound a red fox makes in the winter woods when it wants the whole valley to know it is there, and under it, felt through the soles of her boots before she heard it, a heartbeat. A slow one, enormous, a drum struck under the earth, *dooon... dooon...*, that made the steam on the ground shiver in rings.

Tetsugen Akagane stood up out of the cedar roots with the bow already drawn.

He had brought the war-bow of the copper line, which nobody at the Seat had known the copper line still owned: a tall Moto bow, longer than he was, lacquered copper-ochre and bound with red-bronze wire at the grip, its upper limb twice the length of the lower so that he held it below the center, the old way. The string was at his cheek. A second arrow was clamped crosswise between his teeth like a dog with a bone, its fletching brushing his ear. His young face was perfectly calm. The black curls had come loose over his brow. And round the head of the arrow on the string, round the copper broadhead that the Akagane forge had made because a copper blade comes out of the quench softer and not harder, his aura had gathered in the shape of a mouth: red-bronze, bright arterial red at the lip, the long narrow jaws of a fox open round the arrowhead, every tooth a needle of light.

[low, the rumble of a cart going over a bridge] "*Kitsune-ya*," he said, round the arrow in his teeth, which should not have been possible, and let go.

*BYON.*

The bow-string slapped his bracer and the arrow went. It crossed the sixty paces to the furnace in a flat red streak with the fox's jaws open in front of it, and the air screamed round it, *hyuuuuuuuuu*, and that was what undid it. The Sin of Wrath could not see it coming; it was behind his left shoulder, on his blind side, where the cooked eye was. He did not need to see it. A thing that moves that fast through the air heats the air in front of it, and a fox's mouth of Akagane blood-work is warm the way blood is warm, and the man had lived three hundred years in a skin that knew heat the way a spider knows its web. His head did not turn. His left hand came up and back over his shoulder in one short flat slap, *pashi*, and closed on the shaft a foot behind the head and stopped the arrow dead in the air an inch from his ear.

The fox's jaws closed anyway.

They closed on his wrist where it held the shaft, *gabu*, the light-teeth going in through the crazed white skin at the inside of the wrist where the veins run close under it, and Anima Spirare went into his blood with them, and hung on. Kiyome saw it in the Luminalis that was still on her, the red-bronze threads running up the inside of his arm in his own veins like ink in water, toward the heart. The bite did nothing else, and was meant to do nothing else. A fox bites a deer's leg to hold on, and to know where the deer is going.

In the Sin of Wrath's fist the arrow burst into flame from end to end, *bou*, and the fletching went up in a puff of sparks, and the copper broadhead went soft and bright and began to droop over his knuckles like wax off a candle and drip, glowing, onto the black ground, where it sizzled.

He turned his head, and looked up the bank, and the rim of black-violet round his aura thickened toward the man with the bow like a hackle rising.

[a hanging judge reading sentence over the roar of a foundry floor; an old Japonic court accent, hoarse with steam] "*Who the hell*," said Shōki Kurenai Moto, "are you?"

The anger in it shook the needles off the cedars. It was not cold any more. Kiyome heard that, and did not like it. The Sin of Wrath in her grandmother's stories had read his sentences in a voice like iron dragged on a foundry floor. This was a man shouting.

Tetsugen took the second arrow out of his teeth and set it on the string without looking at it.

[deep, flat, old northern iron] "Akagane," he said. "Tetsugen. Lord of the copper line, of the Blood and Heart Arts, the copper chair." He drew. "You married one of mine. She's in my book. Page four hundred and eleven. I read her when I took the chair, and the Hall's own record on her page says the lord died with a needle in his nape, and no Akagane ever killed a man that way in a thousand years." The string came to his cheek. "So I'll say this for the record, since I've never had the chance. I don't like what they did to her. I don't like what you've done since, either. And I'll not let you have the boy."

Something moved across the Sin of Wrath's face. It was so small that a man without the eye would not have seen it, and Kiyome's eye saw everything: the long scarred mouth went slack, and the jaw like a blade's spine came open, and for one heartbeat the face of a man who had been a husband came up through the face of the Sin like a drowned thing coming up through ice.

Then it went down again, and his eye went to Tetsugen's breastbone, and filled with red, and Kiyome watched the trap close.

She had known it would. It was on the card, in the copper book, in the Byakuya's own register of what walks the north: TSUMI-YOMI cannot stop reading, and Ignivale's barrier between his intent and his fire is nothing, so that a record heavy enough fires his heat before he chooses, and he wastes himself on the first butcher in the room while the real threat walks behind him. And Tetsugen Akagane had signed for every sack on the Sennen road. She saw the count go into the Sin like a blow. She saw the ring-lines in the living eye spin until they blurred. A Zettari border town; the columns between it and the river; Hiroe; the Kurogane pit; forty years. Thousands. A mountain of dead stacked in one man's chest, more than the Doom Breaker had carried, more than anyone the Sin had read since the black floor.

His heat fired.

He did not choose it. She saw him not choose it. The breath went into him like a bellows, *GOHHHHHHHH*, the whole long chest filling, and the black-violet spiral of the Covenant Scar on his breastbone flared through the open coat, and the Hall Irons on his wrists went from cherry to orange to a blinding white, and the burning arrow fell out of his hand. GŌKA, the Karmic Fire, which is placed and never thrown, which burns by the weight of what a man has done, being gathered in the right fist for a chest that had done more than any chest he had touched in three hundred years. His weight went into the right heel. He was going up the bank.

"Tetsugen!"

"I know," said Tetsugen, and did not move, because he was the bait, and bait does not run.

Kiyome went.

She went for the working in his fist. KAGAMI-WARI looks at a technique instead of its owner, and she looked, with the Luminalis still in her, and saw the Karmic Fire forming in the fist the way you see the inside of a lantern through its horn: the Tarturon reaction being seeded into the bones of the hand, the self-oxidizing fire that needs no air, and round it, holding it in, the boundary of the working, the seam where the fire met the law that told it where to go. It was a third gathered. The full price was not yet paid in. She had perhaps half a breath.

[clear, quick] "*Kagami-wari.*"

*Ba.*

She crossed the ten paces between them in one long lunge with the spear low, and the white of her went across the steaming ground like a lamp swung on a chain, and instead of thrusting she drew the spear back along her own hip and cut upward with the right-hand side-blade of the cross, a short hooking stroke that took his right fist on the knuckles as it came back for the blow, exactly where the seam was. The curved side-blade met the iron of the Hall Irons at the wrist with a high hard *kiiin* and slid off it onto the back of the hand. It split the crazed skin over the knuckles of the first and second fingers to the bone, and the white of the knuckle-bones showed for an instant, glowing faintly orange like coals under ash, before ENSHŌ closed it in a hiss.

And the working came apart.

It came apart along its own join, clean as a mirror falling in two halves. The Karmic Fire that had been gathering in his fist lost the law that held it there and fell off his hand like water off a turned bowl, a fistful of white-hot reaction that needs no air, and it dropped onto the black wet ground at his feet, onto the snowmelt pooled in the leaf-mold, and the water flashed to steam inside the fire all at once.

*ZUDOOOOOOOOON.*

The ground under them went up. A third of a Karmic Fire is not much of one, and it was enough. The steam blew out of the earth in a white dome as wide as the clearing and took the black leaf-mold and the dead snowdrops and the stones and the roots of the great cedar up with it, and the blast went through Kiyome's harness like a horse kicking a door. It picked her up off her feet and threw her back across the road. She turned in the air, the spear held out from her body so it would not take her ribs when she landed, and came down on her heels and one hand in the slush twenty paces back, skidding, the lamellar of her skirts plowing a furrow through the snow, *zazazaa*, and stopped with her palm on the ground. Her ears were ringing. There was a long thin burn across the back of her left hand where the steam had come in at the cuff, already blistering, and she could taste blood where her teeth had closed on her cheek. She stood up.

The great cedar was falling. The blast had torn its roots out of the ground on one side, and it went over slowly, the way the very old trees go, groaning, *mishimishimishi*, the whole red-brown column of it tipping away from the road into the wood with its top still in the sun, and came down across three of its neighbors with a long rolling crash that shook the snow off every tree for a hundred yards. Behind her on the road somebody was shouting. A horse was screaming. The steam came down over all of it like a warm rain.

And in the middle of the crater the Sin of Wrath was standing where he had stood, with the ground blown out from under him in a pit to his knees and his feet still planted on the last of it, and his right hand smoking, and his head turning.

Toward her.

He had to, and that was the other half of the trap, the half she had been more frightened of. HAKANASHI answers every blow, from anyone, before it does anything else, and she had landed one. His heat had fired at Tetsugen's chest; his law turned him to her. She saw it take him the way a hook takes a fish, the body turning against the direction it had been going, the face furious, the living eye leaving the bank and the bow and the man with thousands in his chest and coming round to her, because the knuckles were split and a thing done to him could not be left unfinished.

*Steer him*, Tetsugen had said. *That's not a way to win. But it's a way to steer.*

He came.

He came across the twenty paces faster than her eyes could follow him, and her eyes could follow almost anything. She did not see him cross; she saw the place he had been fill with an afterimage of him, a man-shaped smear of orange light hanging in the steam, and the steam itself torn open in a tunnel behind him, and then he was there, inside the reach of the spear, close enough that the heat off him blistered the white lacquer of her cuirass where it faced him and the glass beads at her left temple went soft and ran. The right fist with the split knuckles was coming round in a back-handed swing, not a punch, the forearm flailing out from the shoulder with the stub of broken chain on the Hall Irons whipping round ahead of it like the lash on a flail. Iron-fist brawling. Three centuries on the road. No art in it at all, and all the weight of a man who could lift millions of tonnes.

The Byakuya guard has too little in its arms to block a blow like that, and is built to be somewhere else when it lands. Her Reflex had seen his shoulder start before the fist did, the way the eye catches the first stir of a branch before the bird leaves it, and her head went back and to the right a hand's width on her neck, and the chain stub went past her face so close that the heat of it took the lashes off her left eye, *jiri*, and the fist after it, and the wind of them both hit her cheek like a hot iron held near it. The blow went on past her into the cedar behind her shoulder, which it had not been aimed at, and the trunk exploded where the chain hit it. Splinters as long as her arm came out of the far side of the tree on fire, *BAGOOOM*, and the top half of the cedar leaped off the bottom half and spun away into the wood turning end over end, burning.

He was open. The swing had pulled his whole body round to the right, the left side of him facing her, ribs and armpit and the long flank where the feathers had already cut, and she was inside the arc of his arm with the spear held short in both hands. She put the point into his left side below the ribs. The straight blade went in through the crazed skin and through the slanting muscle of the flank and stopped, two fingers deep, against something in him so dense it was like driving a spear into an anvil, *gochi*, and ENSHŌ swallowed the thrust before it could go further and turned it to heat in him, and the blade came out glowing at the tip and the wound shut behind it like a mouth.

Every blow into the bank. She felt her own name go into the black-violet a second time.

His left hand came down and took hold of the spear.

It closed on the shaft just behind the head, and then slid forward onto the steel of the cross itself, the palm flat on the straight blade where it met the side-blades, and the steel under his hand went cherry-red at once. A handprint. She saw it come up on the blade, five long fingers and a palm, glowing, the color running out from it along the steel the way color runs through a blade in a forge, straw and plum and a temper blue at the edges, *chiki chiki chiki*, the tick of metal heating faster than metal should. YAKI-IRE: the first touch. The card was very clear about the second. Never let him touch the same place twice; the second touch takes the heat back out through the palm in an instant, and the steel freezes brittle as glass, and the third blow breaks it.

He let go of the shaft. He drew his hand back from the blade a finger's width, the palm open, to lay it on the handprint again.

"*Musubi.*"

Three turns of white light round his wrist, *kachi, kachi, kachi*, quick as a woman tying a cord round a sword-guard in a doorway, and an amber click as Fixatio set them. His hand stopped in the air a finger's width from her spear. It stopped the way a fly stops in amber. The heat coming off the palm went on coming, and the hand did not move, and for one heartbeat the Sin of Wrath stood with his left hand bound in the air in front of him and looked at it.

Then he pulled.

She had known he would be able to. He was stronger than her in the one thing that decided it; she had felt it the instant the knot set, like trying to hold a bull by a silk cord. His Sovereignty went into the binding the way a man's shoulder goes into a door, and the knot held, and held, and Fixatio's hold went down into her own arms as the cost of holding it, and her fingernails split on the spear-shaft one after another, *pichi, pichi*, the blood coming out round them onto the white lacquer, and the white light round his wrist began to crack.

[through her teeth, still level] "Now would be a good time, my lord."

[from the bank, unhurried] "I'm aware of it."

*BYON.*

The second arrow came in low from the side. It took him behind the right knee, in the hollow where the hamstring tendons run down to the shin, and the fox's jaws went in with it, *gabu*, and the copper broadhead went through the tendon on the outer side of the knee and out through the front of his shin below the kneecap, and stopped there with its fletching quivering in the back of his leg. It did not cripple him. ENSHŌ took the force out of it, and the leg did not buckle. It did not need to cripple him. It was a blow, from the side, from somebody he was not looking at, and HAKANASHI wrenched his head round toward it before he could finish anything else, and the left hand he had been pulling out of her knot to lay on her spear went back with it.

The knot broke. *Pakin.* It went to white dust in the air. She let it go and stepped back out of his reach with the spear held across her body and her split fingers bleeding down the shaft, and drew a breath, and found that her lungs hurt, because the air she was breathing was as hot as the air at the mouth of an oven.

He stood between them with an arrow through his leg and his head turning from one of them to the other.

It was like watching a bull in a ring. She would remember that afterward and be ashamed of it, because he was not a bull; he was a man. But it was what it looked like. HAKANASHI pulled him at Tetsugen, because the arrow was the last blow; and TSUMI-YOMI pulled him at Tetsugen, because the copper lord's chest was a mountain; and something in him, something new, something that had not been in her grandmother's stories, pulled him past both of them at the boy on the road behind her, and the three things pulled at once and he stood in the middle of them with his whole body shaking, the heat coming off him in waves that bent the light, and the crater round his feet beginning to glow.

He reached down and pulled the arrow out of his leg. He did it the way a man pulls a splinter, quickly, by the fletching, and the shaft came out through the front of his shin and the copper head with it, glowing, and the hole it left closed in a puff of steam. He looked at the arrowhead in his hand. Copper. It had come out of his flesh soft, not brittle. He looked up the bank at the man with the bow.

"You came in copper," he said. "Akagane."

"I came in copper." Tetsugen set a third arrow on the string. "My line's known about your quench for a thousand years. We married you anyway."

The Sin of Wrath went for him.

There was nothing to steer this time. He went straight up the bank in a line, and the line was fire. Where his feet touched the snow it went to steam and where they touched the roots they went to charcoal, and the afterimages of him hung behind on the slope one after another like a man climbing a stair made of his own light, three, four, five of them, fading orange in the steam. The third arrow met him halfway up the bank and he did not slap it away; he let it hit him in the chest, and it hit him square on the Covenant Scar over the breastbone and the copper broadhead folded against him like tin, and the fox's jaws bit and held, and he did not stop. He came over the lip of the bank at the copper lord like a rockfall.

And Tetsugen dropped the bow, and put out his empty hands, and said a word Kiyome had never heard him say.

"*Kaese.*"

Give it back.

She saw the face go. Forty years of it. THE COPPER HEART let go of the hour it had been holding since before Gimbzo's father died, and the young man's face that had sat in the copper chair through every vote in her lifetime fell off the bones under it like a mask coming untied, and under it was a man of sixty-one. Lines came in at the gold eyes and round the mouth and down the cheeks in deep plain furrows, a soldier's lines, a provisioner's, a man who has been tired for forty years. The hands went knotted at the knuckles. And the Crystal under him came up through the copper the way the face had come off it, the whole Sovereign weight of him, Dissonance, that he had been holding at Refraction's face for longer than she had been alive. The slow pulse in his aura became a drum. *DOOON. DOOON.* The red-bronze went red as fresh blood in a basin, and the smell of it came down the bank to her, copper and something sweet like the air after a thunderstorm, and the snow round his feet went pink.

The Sin of Wrath came over the lip of the bank into his arms.

They met chest to chest. There was no other way to put it. The Sin's right fist came in, a short plain hook at the ribs with the heat of a forge in it, and Tetsugen did not try to be somewhere else, because he was not built that way; he took it on his left forearm, high, the arm folded hard against his own side, and the blow drove the forearm into his ribs and she heard a rib go under it, *bokin*, a short dull crack like a green branch stepped on, and the heat of the fist went into the forearm and cooked it. The sleeve of white silk under the copper cape flashed to flame and the skin under the sleeve went white and then brown and then black in a long patch from the wrist to the elbow, and the smell came down the bank to her, roasting meat, and she had to make herself keep looking.

He did not let go. His burned left forearm came up over the Sin's right arm and clamped it against his own ribs, the elbow locking down, and his right hand came in under it, open, the palm flat, the Akagane way, and laid itself on the Sin of Wrath's breastbone over the arrow he had already put there.

He had had the heart since the first arrow. The fox had bitten and held on, and the Blood and Heart Arts had run up the veins of the wrist to the chest and listened, and for the last half-minute Tetsugen had been counting it: a heart going at a gallop, two hundred beats and more, a heart at forge heat, a heart that had not stopped in three hundred years for anything any Kurenai headsman could do to it. Every heartbeat has a window, a few hundredths of a second as the muscle resets itself for the next stroke, and a blow over the heart in that window and only in that window throws the whole rhythm into a quiver that does not pump. Boys die of it, struck in the chest by a ball. The Akagane have known about the window for a thousand years. At two hundred beats a minute the window comes round three times a second.

Tetsugen's palm arrived in it.

[very low] "*Aka-te.*"

*DOKUN.*

It was not a hard blow. ENSHŌ swallowed what force there was in it and turned it to heat, the way it swallowed everything, and banked it. It could not swallow the timing. The heart under the Covenant Scar took the palm in the one instant it could not take anything, and stuttered, *do-do-do-do-do*, and lost its stroke, and went into a fine, frantic, useless quiver, like a bird beating its wings against a closed window without getting off the sill.

The Sin of Wrath stopped.

The furnace roar round him stopped with his heart. It was the strangest thing Kiyome had ever seen. For three hundred years he had not had a moment without the blast-furnace sound under him, and now there was nothing, and the whole wood heard it go: the steam stopped winding round him and hung in the air, and the needles stopped falling, and the fire in the fallen cedar seemed to pause. Shiin. His face went gray under the crazed glaze. His knees went. He went down on one knee in the pink snow on the lip of the bank with the copper lord's palm still flat on his chest, and his living eye was wide open and looking at nothing, and his mouth was open, and no breath came out of it.

*One*, Kiyome counted, without knowing she was counting. *Two.*

His heart started again.

She saw it start in the Luminalis, and she saw how, and understood a thing about him the copper book had not known. Ignivale, the Wellspring of the Spark, which had fired his heat before he could choose, fired again. It fired into his own chest, a crack of white spark inside the breastbone like a lightning stroke inside a cloud, *BACHIN*, and the quivering heart took the shock the way a drowned man's heart takes the shock of a surgeon's fist, and clenched, and found its stroke. *DOOOKUN.* And the furnace came back up under him all at once, a roar out of nothing, *GOAAAAAAAAAAAA*, as if somebody had kicked open the door of a kiln, and the steam that had hung in the air was flung outward in a ring and the fire in the cedar leaped up twice its height.

He came up off his knee with the copper lord's burned arm still locked over his own, and his left hand found Tetsugen's throat.

Tetsugen's knife was already in his hand. Kiyome had not seen him draw it. The old soldier's hand goes to it on its own, the card said, when a room goes wrong, and his right hand had left the Sin's chest and gone to his belt and come back with the horn-handled meat knife, and put it into his own arm. He cut the burning meat off his own left forearm, where the heat of the Sin's fist was still cooking him, in two long strokes, *zoku, zoku*, the way a butcher takes a slab off a carcass, a strip of black and red flesh the length of his forearm falling away into the snow, because the card said a fire like that lives in what it touched and the only way to end it is to cut out what it touched. Under it the muscle was raw and red and the white of the bone showed through at the wrist, and then the blood came, bright arterial red, and stopped coming almost at once, because his Hemostasis was the best in the north and the arm was already closing, the Copper Heart pulling the edges of it together like drawstrings.

The Sin's hand on his throat had begun to glow.

[a croak, the old iron gone hoarse] "There," said Tetsugen Akagane, round the hand, very calmly, sixty-one years old, with half his forearm in the snow and his face going purple. "Now we're both old men."

The Sin of Wrath looked at him.

Kiyome saw the second thing come up in that face, the drowned thing, and this time it stayed for longer than a heartbeat.

"You voted," said the Sin. It came out of him as a question, the way a man asks one. "Your chair. That morning."

"My chair," said Tetsugen. "Not me. I wasn't born." He coughed against the hand. "I've voted for plenty since. You'd have found me in the book either way."

The hand on his throat tightened.

Kiyome came up the bank.

She came up it as fast as she had ever moved, faster than she had moved against her sister in a thousand mornings behind the closed door, and she did not use the spear on the Sin of Wrath's back, because he had to answer every blow and if she struck him there he would answer it through the throat in his hand. She put the spear's shaft across the front of his left forearm instead, between the hand and the elbow, and hooked the right-hand side-blade of the cross behind his wrist, and pulled, with her whole weight going back through her heels, the way Amelia Greymane pulls a man off his feet with the beard of an axe. The arm did not move. It was like pulling at the bough of a cedar. But the hand on Tetsugen's throat opened a finger's width under the drag of it, and a finger's width was enough for a Grandmaster with a broken rib and a ruined arm, who twisted out of it and went down the back of the bank in a long rolling slide in the snow, and came up on one knee at the bottom with the bow somehow in his good hand again, coughing, his throat burned in the shape of four fingers and a thumb.

Which left her at the top of the bank with the Sin of Wrath, and the spear across his arm.

He looked down at it. At the steel of the cross, where his handprint still glowed cherry-red on the straight blade where it met the side-blades. She had put the spear on him exactly where he had already touched it.

She saw her mistake in the same instant he saw his chance.

His right hand came across his body and laid itself flat on the handprint.

*JUUUUUUWAAAAAH.*

The heat went out of the steel through his palm all at once. The cherry-red handprint went black, and the black ran out from it through the whole head of the spear in a spreading stain like ink dropped in water, the steel of three centuries freezing in an instant into something hard and brittle as glass. YAKI-IRE: the second touch. And his knee came up, the right one, the one with the hole in it, into the spear-head from below.

*PARIIIIN.*

The cross-blade of the Byakuya field spear shattered like a dropped bowl. It went into a hundred pieces, the straight blade and both curved side-blades, and the pieces went everywhere, a spray of black glittering steel, and some of them went into her. A sliver the length of her little finger went into her right cheek under the cheekbone and through it, and she felt it grate on her back teeth; another went through the red silk lacing at her shoulder between two lames of the white plate and into the muscle over the shoulder blade; a dozen small ones went into the white lacquer of her cuirass and stuck there quivering like burrs in a coat. She did not cry out. Her head rocked back with the one through her cheek. Blood came into her mouth and down her chin, bright on the white, and she felt the rings in her eyes turn once, slowly, silver, and her composure go out of her all at once like a breath, and the boy was on the road below them and could see it.

And mend.

She stood at the top of the bank with a broken stick of white lacquer in her hands and a shard of her own spear through her face, and looked at him, and he looked at her, and between them the air was so hot it shook.

He had not answered her yet. She had dragged at his arm. HAKANASHI did not care that it was a drag and not a strike; it was a thing done to him, and he had to answer it, and she was within reach, and she had nothing left in her hands to be anywhere else with. The katana was at her hip. The air round them was steam from the crater and the snowmelt and the burning cedar, wet as a bathhouse; HAKKŌ's channel would wander in it like a drunk on a road and arrive wherever the water pulled it, perhaps in her own feet. She had known it would be like this. She had told Tetsugen and Hayato it would be like this, in the white house, with the cups between them. *My light wants dry air. He walks about in his own steam.*

She drew anyway. She would rather die with the blade out.

[from the road below, flat, a lieutenant giving a report] "*Stonefast.*"

Something gold and rust-red went up off the road like heat off a hearth, and the ground under the Greymane girl's boots cracked in a ring, and Kiyome did not have time to understand what it meant before the Sin of Wrath's head turned, again, the way it had turned for the arrow, the way it turned for everything, past the girl and down the bank to the king.

Muken had come off the road. He was at the foot of the bank, ten paces below them, with Amelia Greymane planted in front of him with her shield up and her heels sunk and the war-axe off her back, and Lorn Stark beside them with the Stark sword out at last and its point drawn through the slush in a line, the white rime rising along it in a low wall a hand high and as long as one stroke of his arm. And behind the two of them, between their shoulders, her sister's son, with Tsugigane drawn and the white cord at its guard and his left eye on fire.

Kiyome had looked at that boy every day for a month and never seen him like this. The heat behind his left eye had torn the gold seam in his iris wide, so wide that the amber fleck in it had gone to a coin of molten gold, and the light from it was falling across the steam between him and the Sin of Wrath in a single thin bright line. She knew what it was. Her own eye showed her what his eye was showing him, because Luminalis shows what is under a surface and the gold in the Kōkan eye is the surface of something: a crack. One crack. The Eye of the Splintered Work had looked at a man made of ten thousand loaded cracks and found the one they all ran into, and laid it out in gold across the air for anyone with an eye to see, straight as a rule, from the boy's eye to the back of the Sin of Wrath's neck, where a headsman's blade falls between the vertebrae.

Where the block had been.

The Sin of Wrath felt it there. She saw him feel it. His hand went to the back of his own neck, slowly, the way a man's hand goes to an old wound when the weather turns, and the living eye found the boy at the bottom of the bank and the ring-lines in it filled with red and spun, and stopped.

[low, cracked, enormous, and for the first time not entirely certain] "*Kōkan.*"

The fire in the cedar roared. Tetsugen, on one knee in the pink snow, was setting a fourth arrow on the string with a hand that was mostly raw meat, and gray to the roots of his hair, and Kiyome could see the bank in the Sin of Wrath through the last of her Luminalis, the black-violet threads of it with her name in them and the copper lord's, and it was nowhere near the line. Not a tenth of the way. Two Grandmasters had spent everything they had shown the north in forty years on him for the length of three breaths, and filled a twentieth of what it would take to crack him from inside. Up the road, at the last bend, where the fog was boiling off the ground in white rags, Hayato Amagiri was standing in his indigo rain-cape at the very edge of the mist with one hand out, the way a man holds a door, and his mouth was moving, and she knew what it was saying, because she had heard him say it a hundred times. *Two days and a road, sire. The road's open behind you.*

She took the shard of steel out of her cheek with two bleeding fingers and threw it in the snow.

[clear, level, the old high register of the house, and the voice of a woman who has kept an oath sixteen years with nothing at the end of it and now has something] "Muken." The rings in her eyes lay flat and silver. "Go with Hayato. *Now.* The red before the white. You go, and we hold him."

The boy did not move. His eye was full of gold, and the line ran out of it across the steam to the back of the Sin of Wrath's neck, and the blade in his hand had begun, very faintly, to ring.

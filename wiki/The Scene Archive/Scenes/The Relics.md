*The cauldron-hall beneath the Mother's last cell, then the sky above the Crucible*

---

The last of the crimson went into him at the hour the bells above would have rung vespers, and Malphas felt his heart beat for the first time in two years of sepulchral quiet.

He sat in a throne of bronze that was half a chair and half an alembic, its arms chased with crimson enamel in the pattern of a vine that bears fruit and rots on the stem, its back a cage of brazen ribs through which ran the tubing: gut sheathed in vulcanised rubber, seven lines of it, descending from a rack of gauge-housings and glass reservoirs above the throne and entering him at the throat, at both wrists, at the hollow of each elbow and twice beneath the breastbone. The gauges ticked. The reservoirs, which an hour ago had held a liquid the colour of a pomegranate split in the sun, were dregs now, an incarnadine lees clinging to the glass. Every drop of it was inside him, and it was fermenting with the slow exothermic warmth of a mash tun.

He let it. He sat very still with his hands on the arms of the throne and attended to it with the obdurate patience of a retter's son watching a pond, and what he felt was so strange, after two years without a body, that for a long moment he simply could not name it.

Pulse. That was the name. A wet, muscular, percussive thud under the sternum, and then another, and then a rhythm, the ventricles closing and the valves snapping shut behind them and the blood going out under pressure into a tree of vessels that had not existed a month ago. Breath came after it. His new lungs drew air into themselves without his asking, and the air was cold and redolent of brass polish and formalin and the faint sweetness of the crimson, and he tasted it. He had not tasted anything, sapid or foul, since the Becoming. The Becoming had taken the living body and left him the cadaverous skull in the hood and the Golden Remnant at his hip, a seat in the chest and a second seat in gold, and nothing in either seat that could feel the cold of a cellar on a tongue.

He had a tongue now.

He had everything now. That was the point of the crimson, its whole sempiternal promise.

The draught was his own culture, grown for nineteen months in the caliginous dark of this hall from three seed-stocks: the blood of the Moto line, taken from the cold body of the man who had once been their king; the flesh of the Zettari, taken from Dougou's son; and the fine nerves of the Yukari, taken from a man who had died in chains below daylight. Three bloodlines that had never in their recorded history consented to share a body, and every one of them would have rejected the others in the first hour, the way a living body rejects a graft with implacable rancour, its sentinels finding the foreign tissue by the small proteins it wears on its surface and tearing it down cell by cell. Malphas had not fought the rejection. He had fermented it. His culture dissolved the little surface marks by which flesh tells *self* from *other*, broke them down to a putrescent muck in which no sentinel could find anything to hate, and then the Soul Forge had done the rest: had taken three lattices of essence that wanted nothing to do with one another and folded them, under a master's hammer, into one.

So this must have been what Moto blood felt like.

It ran hot. That surprised him. He had always imagined the Moto as cold, the way their northern court was cold, but the blood that came out of his new heart was febrile and quick and carried in it something he could only call *attention*: a pressure behind the eyes, a readiness to look and, looking, to see through. His eyes had begun to glow with a refulgent heat. He could feel it, a heat in the orbits and a lambent light on the inside of his own lids when he blinked, as the alchemical workings ran up out of the blood and into the brain, laying themselves along the optic nerve like gold wire along a groove. The Moto read through the eyes. He would read through them now.

And this must have been what a Zettari body felt like. Dense. He had expected strength and found density, a heaviness in the bone that was not weight but packing: the trabeculae inside his long bones laid closer than any human's, the sinew thick as hawser, the muscle fibre a finer and tougher weave than the flax his father used to lift from the ponds. A body made for attrition and built to be perdurable. A body that took a blow and stored its shape as cicatrix, as the Zettari did, as Dougou's son had done his whole short life. He flexed his hand on the throne's arm and felt the forearm answer with the tensile give of a drawn cable, and he understood for the first time why the Zettari had never once in seven hundred years believed they could be beaten.

And under both, finest of all, like a susurrus beneath speech, the Yukari. Threads. He had not known what the dead man's nerves would give him until the crimson reached them, and then the hall had filled, all at once, with a vertiginous lacework of fine taut lines that only he could feel: threads of consequence running from every person in the room to every other, humming at pitches that told him how near each one stood to breaking. The Yukari, by some immemorial gift, heard danger as a process with a hinge in it. He could hear the hinges now. He could hear Borin's, very near, loud as a rale.

It all transmuted. It all became one in the vessel, Moto and Zettari and Yukari annealed under the Soul Forge into a single living frame that wore his name. And the thing he had waited for since the Becoming happened at last, quietly, deep in the architecture of him, with a sensation like a rimed lock giving under oil.

His Conversion moved.

For two years, and for years before that, he had taken in more than he could turn, and the surplus had sat in him like entropy in a sealed flask. The path that could have raised his turning was written for a living body, and he had given the living body up, and so the gap had widened every day, inexorable, intake at the ceiling and turning frozen and the difference banked in him like steam in a sealed pot. Now he had a body again. A body that the Body Path could be written into. He felt the old frozen figure stir, felt the first of everything he had banked begin to move through him toward use instead of only pressing outward against his refractory walls, and the relief of it was so enormous that his new eyes filled with water, and he let them, and the tears ran hot down a face that had not had skin on it for two years.

---

Nuvilak stood at the foot of the throne's plinth with her mask in her hands.

She had prepared all three bodies with the same care she gave obsequies. She let nobody else handle a body she had prepared, and she had stood in this hall through nineteen months of the work with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her forearms stained an ochreous yellow to the wrist with fixative, and now she lifted the mask and set it on her face: a plain thing of lacquered black with no mouth, a seam down the middle, two narrow slits for the eyes. Behind it her breath stopped showing. Malphas watched her settle it and said nothing, and she, with the phlegmatic economy of a woman who had crossed sea-ice, would have said nothing back.

Across the hall, on the flagstones in front of the throne, Borin Ironheart knelt in an attitude of fealty he had been made to take.

Seraphine held him there. Malphas could see the hands that did it, which nobody without the eyes for workings would have seen: palms of pale opalescent violet rising from the reliquary harness on her back like smoke made courteous, a nurse's cradle under each of the Dawi's shoulders, a mason's brace across his back, a porter's haul on each wrist, gentle, firm, absolutely unmoving. She stood behind him in her chapel visor with her bone-fingered hands folded and did not look at what she was doing, with all the sangfroid of a ward-sister. The hands looked for her. *He will hurt himself if he fights them,* she thought. That thin apologia was her only reason for holding him.

The Master of the Soul Forge had been made to work under an indenture written in his own blood. His beard, the great charcoal-and-iron beard he braided into three trunks, had been hewed short on one side where they had held him at the anvil by it. On his thighs his hands lay open and livid, three fingers on the right, two on the left. Every stump had been cauterised to a black eschar with a hot iron by somebody who knew exactly how to do it and had taken no particular care. A Dawi of the Body Path works his essence out through his hands and the hammer-stroke, through direct contact with the thing he makes, and so with a torturer's quotidian patience they had taken the fingers one at a time, each time he refused, and each time left him enough to go on. He had gone on. Three Requiems lay behind him in the dark of the hall, and they had cost him what Requiems always cost him, and more, paid in a currency no scrivener could tally.

"I commend your craftsmanship, Borin."

Malphas said it sincerely. He found, with some surprise, that his new voice was deeper than the old one, resonant in the barrel of the Zettari chest, and that it carried in the hall the way a plangent cello carries.

"Without you, I would never have found the bodies. Not as they were. Not the souls in them." He lifted one hand from the arm of the throne and turned it in the gaslight, studying the new skin on it, still wan as a cellar plant, the pale fine hair on the back of the wrist, the vein. "You were hard to come at. A man so connected. Kings petition you; the Guild factors petition you; the condemned petition you on the same bench. I had to kill your little band of protectors to get you alone. I am sorry about that. They died well, and very quickly."

Borin was sweating. It ran down out of his hairline into his eyes and he did not blink it away, and when he spoke his voice came up out of his chest with the clangour of iron dragged on stone.

"You might as well kill me now." He set each word down with care, the way he set down a candent billet. "I don't give a shit about your little speech."

Malphas laughed.

It surprised him as much as it surprised anyone, a sound of pure insouciance. It came up out of the new lungs and the new throat as a real laugh, warm and delighted, and it went on, and the tubing at his wrists jerked with it, and the gauge-needles above the throne began to oscillate. When it was done he wiped his eyes with the back of a hand that had skin on it.

"You will want to hear it, though. You made half of it." He leaned forward in the throne with a scholar's hauteur. The crimson in him moved with a slow viscosity. "I have perfectly combined my knowledge of alchemy with my genius for magic. Do you understand what that means? For three hundred years the Academies have kept them in separate buildings, as though catalysis were a heresy. The alchemist reduces a thing to its metal and its slag; the practitioner commands a thing by what it is called. Nobody ever put the two into one vessel. The vessel always broke." He spread his hands. "I made the vessel. I have made my body impervious. I have the body of a Zettari, Borin. The skills of a Moto. The spiritual connections of the Yukari. Three bloodlines that spent seven hundred years killing one another, and they are all in one heart now, and the heart is mine."

He stood up out of the throne, though his hands kept their hold on its arms a moment longer, with something like diffidence.

"I will stain this world in blood. All those who oppose the new order. You call the ones I have raised *the dead*." He turned his head, slowly, to the tenebrous dark at the side of the hall. "I call it their true form. Their pure form. What a thing is when everything that was ever evanescent about it has been fermented away. What do you think, Muken?"

The King of Kharven stood at the edge of the gaslight, in the penumbra.

He had stood there through the whole of the conversation, still as a pillar of ashlar. Nobody had forgotten that cyclopean stillness. He was dressed the way Nuvilak had dressed him, in a sable long-coat of northern cut, his white hair tied back for a crown he no longer wore. The old scars, a whole palimpsest of them, ran over his deep brown skin where the coat stood open at the throat, and in the scars, faintly, the pale fracture lines of his old Hataraki strain glowed amber. His body was still algid. You could see it if you knew where to look: his breath did not fog in the chill of the hall, and the skin at his temples had the faint glaucous bloom of a sloe. Seeded flesh, cellar-cold, walking and speaking. Under it, in the vault of his chest, sat the rebuilt soul that Borin had hammered back together out of the fragments Malphas had brought him, the memory and the doctrine of a king, a codicil appended to a finished life, and none of what a king might have grown into after.

His eyes were gold. The amber ring round the slate-grey iris had spread through the whole of it, and the gold coruscated.

"I think," Muken said, in the voice that had ended arguments for sixteen years in the hall at Kharven-Seat, "I will enjoy returning to my children."

He paused, the way he had always paused, and let the silence, saturnine and heavy, carry the rest of the verdict. Then he added a sentence his living rectitude would never have allowed.

"Perhaps I can get them to understand."

Malphas smiled. He looked past the king to the corner of the hall, where the second relic sat in the corner's frowsty dark.

Rengai Zettari sat on the floor with his back to the wall and his forearms on his knees, in a drift of fine calcined ash that had not been there when Nuvilak laid him down and had been accumulating round him ever since, the way snow gathers round a fencepost. The air near him was drier than it should have been, astringent in the nose. Malphas's new eyes wanted to blink. Dougou's son lifted his head and looked at nothing in particular, his charcoal hair cut short and uneven, the old heat-scars at his jaw catching the light with a faint metallic sheen, and the corner of his mouth went up at one side, in the sardonic smile that had once read wrong to everyone who saw it and now read worse.

"Don't look at me," Rengai said. "I just work here."

And the third, at the back, standing because he had been told to stand, like a recusant at the back of a chapel. Naiser Yukari wore the cuts from his captivity under Cozbi still, long dark seams on his forearms and throat where the chains had been, preserved by the fixative exactly as they had been on the day he died, their edges gone the colour of livor. As Malphas watched, the dead man lifted a hand and pushed a pair of reading-lenses up into his grey hair, a small, absent, diurnal gesture he must have made ten thousand times in his life. Then he stood with his hand raised and could not remember why he had raised it.

The Requiem constructs repeated their former selves with mouldering fidelity. They could not grow past them. That was the flaw in Borin's art and Malphas had studied it for nineteen months with a scholar's cupidity; it was why Naiser touched lenses he no longer needed and why Muken paused where he had always paused. It did not trouble him. A ferment does not need its yeast to understand the bread.

"You see," Malphas said, turning back to the kneeling Dawi, "people forgot these three. They were relics. Nobody thought of Rengai. Nobody thought of Muken, or of Naiser. They were afterthoughts. Heroes who died to represent something, so that somebody else could stand on the representation and give a speech." He came down one step from the throne, and the tubes in his arms drew taut with a faint juddering. "But I ask you this, Borin. Why must their deaths be set in stone?"

He let that hang in the hall, under the corbels of the vault.

"You can mend metal. You can bend it to your liking. You take an ingot that has cracked in the quenching and you heat it and fold it and hammer it until the crack is a seam and the seam is the strongest part of the blade. Everyone calls that craft." His voice was very gentle, almost dulcet. "Why can I not manipulate the whims of death?"

---

Borin looked up at him.

It cost him something to lift his head against Seraphine's palms, and he paid it with the temerity of his whole kind. His dark eyes had been warm and amused all his long life. They were neither now. His mouth worked once in the hacked ruin of his beard before anything came out of it, acrid with old blood.

"You're disgusting." It came hoarse. "A maniac. You think because a thing *can* last, it ought to." He breathed in, shuddering, round whatever contused thing they had broken in him. "I held the quench for my own apprentice. Thurgan. He was turning to stone from the hands inward and he asked me to let him go, and I held the bloody quench steady while the Forge took him, because some things reach a place where lasting is the cruelty." He spat on the flagstone at the foot of the throne. "It isn't right. Manipulating the dead. Disturbing them. You don't get to drag a man back up out of his rest to stand in your cellar and *repeat himself*."

Something in the hall shifted.

Malphas felt it before he saw it, through the Yukari nerves: a hinge in the room, swinging on its pintle with a faint sibilance. Behind Borin, one of Seraphine's nacreous violet palms had flickered. Only one, the porter's haul on his left wrist, its light gone thin and guttering like a candle in a sough of wind. Her palms withdrew where the dead were mocked; Malphas had known that about her when he took her on, and had wondered, idly, where the limit of her probity would fall. It seemed it fell somewhere near the word *repeat*.

He stood up the rest of the way, and his new knees showed no senescence at all.

He came down off the dais, and the tubing, a leash of gut and gristle, went with him as far as it would go. The tube at his throat tore out of its housing with a wet sucking pop and sprayed crimson across the bronze and its old verdigris. The lines at his wrists and elbows pulled taut and snapped one after another, rataplan, and the glass reservoirs above the throne shattered as their gauge-housings were wrenched off the rack, raining brass and needles and splinters of glass across the dais behind him. He did not notice. He had stopped needing the throne some minutes before and had only been waiting to find out.

He took Borin by the throat with one hand, a garrotte of five new fingers, and lifted him.

Seraphine's palms let go a fraction before they would have been torn off, and the Dawi came up off the floor like a sack of anvils, all of his dense and antediluvian weight hanging from five new fingers, and Malphas held him at arm's length with no more effort than a man holding up a lamp. The Zettari forearm did not tremble. Borin's boots kicked once, in a precipitate spasm, a foot above the flagstones.

And the hall began to bend.

Malphas had not quite meant it to. He was still learning the body; he had a great deal banked and a frozen lock that had only just begun to turn, and when he rose from the throne the pressure came with him. It went out of him into the room the way heat, empyreumatic and dry, goes out of a kiln when its door is opened. The flagstones under his feet sagged toward him with a faint crepitus of settling stone. The lamps on the walls leaned their russet flames inward. Every line of the hall, the edges of the floor, the ribs of the vault, the long iron racks where the reservoirs had hung, bowed in a slow torsion downward and toward the place where he stood, as though the whole room were a cloth and someone had set a heavy stone in the middle of it. A corrupted spiritual presence: that was the rubric the Accord's assessors would have written, if any of them had lived to write anything. To Malphas it was the bottom of a fresh-dug well in chthonic stone.

"No," he said to the dying Dawi in his hand. "I won't kill you here."

Borin's lips were cyanosed. Malphas loosened his fingers enough to let a little air in, precisely enough, with a physician's solicitude.

"You shall be my sacrifice. In front of all of them."

Then he made a door.

The Living Cauldron's whole art was to break a thing down to what it was made of, and he did it now to the air of the hall, and to the stone, and to the very small things the air and stone were made of. He reached into them with Dissolution and adjured the bonds that held each tiny particle apart from its neighbour that they had held long enough. They let go. The particles fell inward on one another, fermenting, collapsing. They packed closer than any matter is meant to pack, a phase transition no furnace had ever forced. A sphere the size of a fist, stygian black, hung in the air before him, so dense that the space around it had to bend to hold it. A great enough density bends space the way a heavy stone bends a stretched cloth; every natural philosopher since the lineage halls had known that much, if only as marginalia. What none of them had known was how to keep the bend from closing. A hole folded through space pinches shut in less time than a photon takes to cross it, unless its throat is propped open by something with less than nothing in it, a substance whose energy is negative, which pushes where everything else pulls.

Malphas was Voidic. His Shell ran inverted. His Nihiloth did not impose law; it cancelled the law already present, and the cancellation was a hollow, a lack, a pressure that pushed outward against everything. He poured it into the throat. The sphere of collapsed matter split down its middle like a seed-case, and between its two halves the space of the cauldron-hall folded over on itself and touched another space, very far away, and held.

Through the opening he could see a sky, and the scud of high cloud in it.

It was a bright clear afternoon sky, the kind of blue that makes a crowd lazy, and under it, a long way down, a great raked oval of pale sand and broken stone, and round the oval, tier upon tier, thousands upon thousands of faces.

He walked into it with the Dawi in his hand. Behind him, without being told, the dead came after, carrion-quiet.

---

The sky over the Crucible went dark in the middle of the afternoon.

It did not cloud over. It darkened from the top down, the way a room darkens when someone draws a heavy curtain across a high window, the blue going to slate and the slate going to a fuliginous umber shot through with veins of crimson light, all in the space of three breaths. The wind died. Every pennant on every pole round the arena's rim dropped and hung limp in the gelid stillness. Up in the highest tiers the crows that nested in the arena's iron roof-trusses went up all at once in a black cacophonous cloud and did not come back down. The Pressure of something two full Bands above anyone on the floor did not trouble with lamps or with bodies first. A Band gap changes the weather, and the weather changed.

Then the tiers felt it.

It went through the stands like a brumal tide coming up a beach. Men sat down hard on the benches, their knees gone to torpor without their deciding it. A woman in the lower tiers went to her knees in the aisle with a sound that was not quite a word, a broken imprecation. Children began to cry, all over the arena at once, a thin high ululation that rose and spread and would not stop. Up in the judges' box, under the oriel window, somebody struck the bell to halt the bout. They kept striking it, and the tocsin rang on and on over the dark arena long after there was any bout left to halt.

And in the middle of the dark, high over the centre of the oval, the air split.

Malphas came out of it walking.

He walked out onto nothing as though it were a floor, and his crimson attire trailed behind him through the opening, a banner of blood: a long coat of deep sanguine silk, cut to the new body and stiff with embroidery of black thread in the pattern of the vine on the throne. The Golden Remnant came after him at his heel, hunched and gold, its chained skull swinging to the rhythm of a slow knell. Lit from beneath by the split, his new eyes glowed fulgurant with the hot gold of Moto blood. In his right hand, held out at the full length of his arm over the whole Crucible for every soul in every tier to see, hung Borin Ironheart by the throat. He meant every tier to see it.

Two figures stepped out of the opening's embrasure after him and stood on the dark air at his shoulders. The King of Kharven on his left, white-haired, gold-eyed, still. Dougou's son on his right, in a drifting halo of ash that fell in a slow grey snow of scoria onto the arena floor far below. Behind them both, a little apart, in a strange lassitude, as a man stands who is not certain he has been invited, Naiser Yukari, with the dark seams of his chains at his throat.

Malphas looked down.

The floor of the Crucible was a charnel ruin. Three craters, a long trench gouged through the sand beneath the northern barrier, slabs spalled and sundered and heaped like talus, and blood in long dark arcs across all of it, two children's worth of it, sprayed and spattered and pooled. In the trench lay a white-haired boy on his back in his own blood, with something faint and lion-shaped standing over him on legs of glass. A little way off stood the other boy, barefoot, his white shirt spattered red. The two of them had fought each other half to death for the entertainment of everyone in the stands, and nobody in the stands had been watching anything else.

They were watching now.

Malphas lifted Borin a little higher.

He did not hurry it. He wanted it seen. He laid his left hand flat against the centre of the Dawi's chest, over the breastbone, and let Dissolution into his palm, only a little, only enough.

The flesh gave.

It went soft under his hand the way clay goes soft under a potter's wet palm. Dissolution did not cut. It reached into the tissue and told the bonds that held it together, the long ropes of collagen that knit skin to fascia and fascia to bone, the small proteins by which every cell grips its neighbour, that they could let go now, and they let go, and Borin's chest became for a moment something that could be shaped. Malphas pushed his hand into it. The skin parted round his fingers without a sound, the fat beneath it unctuous and yellow. The muscle of the chest wall slid apart like wet loam. The breastbone, dense old Dawi bone that had borne the weight of a forge-hammer for four hundred years, softened under his palm to the give of cold butter, and his fingers went through it into the cavity beneath.

Borin screamed.

It was a terrible sound, a raw bellow, the threnody of a chest being opened, and it went out over the dark arena and every face in the tiers heard it. His boots kicked. His ruined hands came up and clawed at the crimson sleeve, three fingers and two, and could not close.

Malphas found the heart.

It was hammering. He felt it slam against his palm, the big Dawi heart, every beat a percussive blow against the inside of his hand. He closed his fingers round it. He felt the slick sac of the pericardium tear under his fingertips, felt the muscle of the ventricles clench and clench again in his grip, felt the great vessels above it, tumid with pressure, the thick ropy trunk of the aorta and the softer veins beside it, pulled taut as hawsers as he drew his hand back.

Then he drew it out.

The vessels stretched, and stretched, and tore. They went one after another with a sound of wet canvas rived along the seam. The aorta parted last, and when it parted the blood came out of Borin's chest in a geyser: a thick dark fountain, ferrous in the nose, driven up into the air by the last stroke of a heart that was no longer in him, so high that it hung for an instant at the top of its rise in the crimson light from the split, a column of red against the dark, and then came down. It came down over Malphas's sleeve and over the Dawi's face and in a hot saline rain onto the ruined floor of the Crucible below, across the craters and the trench and the two boys, and in the front tiers people screamed and flung up their arms as it spattered the barrier in front of them.

Malphas opened his fingers on the Dawi's throat with a kind of benison.

Borin fell.

He fell like a dropped anvil, heavy and straight, his opened chest gaping and his beard flying up round his face, toward the floor of the arena where the two boys lay and stood. And in Malphas's left hand, held up at the full length of his arm in the crimson light for the whole Crucible to see, Borin Ironheart's heart went on beating.

It beat in his palm. It did not know it had been taken from its ossuary of ribs. A heart carries its own timekeeper, a small knot of cells high in the wall of its right chamber that fires on its own and needs nothing from the brain to keep firing, and for as long as the muscle had fuel in it, it would go on beating in an empty hand: clench, release, clench, each beat a weaker spurt of dark blood from the torn mouths of the vessels down over his wrist. Somewhere below, a falling Dawi had perhaps ten seconds of thought left in him before his brain, starved, guttered into syncope. Malphas held the heart higher.

Then, like a lord surveying his demesne, he looked across the arena at the strongest of them.

He found them all at once. The Yukari nerves in his new body hummed with them; they stood out of the crowd like lit beacons out of a crepuscular coast. Asante, in his place. Niran Yukari, standing, very still, his face turned up toward the third figure on the dark air at Malphas's back, toward the man pushing reading-lenses up into his hair. Sodoku Moto, the Exiled Lion, risen from his seat, and Muken at Malphas's left hand had turned his head and was looking down at his son with his golden eyes, and did not say anything, because he had never needed to. Sonzai. And the children: the Future Generation, Class X, the prodigies, the Paragons, scattered through the lower tiers in their Academy coats with their faces sallow and turned up to the sky.

Malphas filled his new lungs.

"LOOK!"

The word went out over the Crucible with the whole Zettari chest behind it and the Moto blood in it and the Pressure of a Band gap underneath, and the tiers shook with its fremitus.

"Look at them! I have brought back the relics you FORGOT!" He swept the hand with the heart in it toward the three figures behind him, and a spindrift of Borin's blood went with it across the dark. "Rengai, whom you never thought of! Naiser, whom you let rot in chains! Muken, your king, your *king*, whom you buried and sang over and left in the ground! You made them monuments. You made them lessons. You made them into something to stand on." His voice cracked with the force of it and he did not care. "I have given them new LIFE. I have given them new MEANING!"

He held the beating heart up over the whole of the arena like a viaticum offered to the dying.

"WHO AMONG YOU STILL WISHES TO STOP ME?"

The hammer was already in the air.

He saw it with the Moto eyes a fraction before he heard it, the whirr climbing in a Doppler shift as it came. It came up off the floor of the Crucible from the shadow under the eastern barrier, where a man had been standing since before the sky went dark: a big man, six and a half feet of siege-built muscle in the argent and gold of Sancta Lux, his deep red-brown face turned up and his hammered-gold eyes lit from within. He had not been in the tiers. He had been on the floor, at the barrier, waiting, and he had thrown before the split had finished opening, before the speech had begun, as though he had known, to the instant, where in this sky Malphas would step out. The Archpaladin. The Hammer of the Divine Threshold. Darius.

And he was moving even as the hammer flew. Malphas saw him go across the floor in three long strides, under the falling Dawi, and catch Borin out of the air against his chest with both arms, and keep going, dragging the body away from the place beneath the split, away from the dead on the dark air, with the last torn ends of the great vessels trailing from the open chest across his white tabard.

"He is not yours," Darius said, flat, without lifting his voice, and the words carried anyway.

The hammer came on.

It was a great thing, plain-headed, the haft wound in gold wire, and it came up through the dark air end over end with a deep thrumming whirr, carrying behind it a long tail of white-gold effulgence like a comet's, the Archpaladin's Radiance riding the steel. Without the sentence it was no verdict. Without the Archpaladin planted and speaking it was only a very strong man's hammer, thrown. It was still a hammer thrown by a man whose blow had been measured in the Academy's own ledgers against the yield of a small country, and the air in front of it went incandescent with the heat of its passage.

Malphas lifted one finger.

The hammer met it.

It did not stop the way a thing stops against a wall. Nothing rang. Nothing broke. The hammer's head came to the tip of his raised forefinger and stopped there, all at once, in the dark air high over the Crucible, as though it had flown into the bottom of a well and the well had no bottom.

It went into him. That was Ferment, the first thing he had ever been and the last thing the Becoming had left him: nothing that entered his reach was blocked when it reached him. It was swallowed. The white-gold light riding the hammer went grey where it touched his finger, then a sick plumbeous grey along the whole head, then guttered out, as the Archpaladin's Radiance was broken down into black muck and drawn in through the skin of a single fingertip. The terrible impetus of it went the same way. A body in motion carries its motion as energy and as push, and he took the energy and banked it, as he had always banked everything, like a charge poured into a battery. The push he gave to the fold in space still standing open at his back, which had more than enough room in it for one hammer's worth of shove. The steel itself, damascened along the haft, he could not swallow. A blade could still touch him. So the steel stopped on his skin, and pressed, and he held it there.

He felt it go into the bank like a freshet into a full cistern. It was very heavy. He had known it would be; the Archpaladin did not throw small. The pressure in him that had only just begun to move through his new Conversion rose to meet it, and for an instant he felt the old familiar fullness against his walls, the sealed pot near the boil. Across the back of his raised hand, under the new skin, the veins stood out black as clinker.

He let it be seen. Let them all see it. He tipped his head back and smiled up into the dark at the great hammer balanced on the tip of his finger, hanging there in the crimson light with its last tail of Radiance dying off it in grey dross, and down below, in the ruin of the arena, the Archpaladin of Sancta Lux knelt in a spreading pool with Borin Ironheart's opened body across his knees, and looked up at the hammer, and did not look away.

In Malphas's other hand, the heart beat once more.

Then it was still.

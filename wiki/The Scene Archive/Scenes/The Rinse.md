*An Accord washhouse, fourth Guild hour.*

The blood would set if she left it in the hot water. That was what troubled Ida Rusk first, before she saw the man beneath the receiving bench or the knife he held against his own thigh. He had dragged a clean bolster down with him. Red spread through its white ticking, expensive feather stuffing sucked flat inside it, and for one stupid, miserable moment she wanted to make him pay.

“Cold tub,” she told him. “If you mean to bleed on my things.”

He looked up. Rain had combed his black hair into strings against a narrow, beardless face, though the grey at his temples made him older than she had first thought. His coat fitted badly across the shoulders and well at the waist; somebody had let the seams out with a different thread. Then she looked at his mouth. He held it open to breathe without moving the bolster pressed to his side. His knife was a little eating blade with a cracked horn handle.

“Back door,” he said. “Please.”

“Name first.” Ida stayed behind the tub. She wanted something she could give the Watch if he died under her bench.

“Tomas. Tomas Rill.” He swallowed, watching her face. “There’s nobody to send for.”

Beyond him, the passage to the yard stood open. She could have stepped through it and shouted upstairs for Annis and Ruth. Instead she stayed beside the rinse tub with her sleeves rolled above her thick forearms, the skin from wrist to elbow pink from heat. At forty-six she could lift a soaked blanket alone, but the back step had begun to hurt her knees. She considered how slowly this man would get over it.

The laundry's barrow stood in that passage. Beyond its upturned handles, the yard gate was locked from the alley side until the carter came at dawn. She had complained about that too, after Ruth burned her wrist and found herself trapped outside in the rain. Master Pell blamed thieves. Asked what they had taken, he told her to mind the washing. If she helped this man into the yard, there would still be the gate, and the next yard's wall, and whatever had put the knife in his hand. She reached for the bolster instead, meaning to put something cheaper beneath his wound. He caught her wrist.

“Leave it.” His voice had gone small. “It helps if I hold it.”

She let him keep it and lifted the bench cloth over his shoulder. He smelled of rain and old sweat, with the sharpness of fear coming through both. The knife lowered a little. She wished he had come when the others were awake, or gone to the bakehouse, or chosen any door in the lane except this one, where she was alone with work that would already keep her until morning.

Two knocks came at the street door. Tomas stopped breathing through his mouth. He watched the latch.

Ida had the tub between herself and the entrance, the mangle behind her, the drying sheets hanging to her right in three close rows. Overhead, Annis rolled in bed. Ida knew the creak of the loose board beneath its foot; she had slept in that room before Annis took it. She thought of calling up before the latch lifted and a stranger entered without waiting.

He shut the door carefully. A short sword hung naked beside his right thigh, its point trailing rain onto the tiles. One cuff was turned back. The other hung loose over his right knuckles. He was broad through the chest and soft below it, with a cropped brown beard and cheeks pitted by an old sickness. His good coat smelled of wet wool. A second man stayed on the threshold, thin, holding a cudgel against his leg.

“Berrick.” The thin man looked at Ida, then at the stairs. “We were told he’d be alone.”

“Shut the door, Cale.” Berrick kept his eyes on her. “You’ve had a caller.”

“Collection starts at six.”

“We'll collect him now.”

Berrick shifted the sword towards the gap between the tub and the wall, its point still below her waist. She had washed shirts opened by blades. Some arrived with the man still warm enough in her memory that she could hear him complaining about starch.

“He went through.”

Berrick looked past her at the open passage, then at the spreading red beneath the bench. She followed his eyes. Rainwater dripping from Tomas’s coat had carried the blood farther than she thought.

“You could have let us walk,” he said.

She threw the rinse water at his face.

It was cooling water, unpleasant rather than scalding, and he got his left arm up in time. She had hoped he would stumble into the door. Instead he came through it with his eyes shut, driving the sword towards the place where her middle had been. She struck his wrist with the empty pail. The point skidded along the tub's iron rim and caught in her apron as she turned; cloth tore against her belly, close enough that she felt the pressure through her shift.

She ran towards the hanging sheets. Cale swung his cudgel at her as she passed, but the corner of the tub shortened his reach. Wood struck her left side below the breast. Her breath stopped. She struck the floor with one knee and caught herself against a wet sheet, dragging it down until the pegs snapped loose above her.

Cale stepped onto it.

She pulled. His boot went forward with the linen and his other leg stayed behind. He caught the drying line. The wall hook pulled halfway out of its rotten plug under his weight. The line sagged across his shoulder and brought another sheet down with it; for a moment his face disappeared into the warm white folds while he swore and tore at them with the cudgel. She knew that hook would come out. She had hung the small things at that end for months. There was something dreadful in finding a use for every repair they had put off.

He fell heavily onto his hip, keeping hold of the cudgel, and struck at her knee from the floor. The blow missed because she was already crawling. There was no room to stand without meeting him, so she pushed beneath the next sheet on her elbows, feeling each breath scrape the hurt place inside her ribs.

Behind the cloth, Berrick's boots slowed. He could walk around the rows. She knew it as soon as she heard the change, and her first satisfaction at hiding became an urgent need to get up.

The laundry pole rested in its hooks against the wall. She hauled it down. The ash shaft, polished by years of wet hands and longer than a man was tall, knocked the lamp bracket as she turned. Light swung over the sheets and showed her the dark shape coming around their far edge.

She thrust at his face.

He turned his head, caught the shaft on his left forearm, and shoved it against the wall. Her hands slid on the wet wood. Before she could pull it free, he stepped inside its length and drove his fist into her mouth.

Her teeth cut her lower lip. She tasted the old iron taste of the standpipe water, stronger now, and felt something hard and loose against her tongue. He struck again. This time she ducked into his chest, letting go of the pole to catch his sword wrist with both hands. His beard rasped against her forehead as he bent over her, breathing onions into the narrow space between their faces.

“Stay still,” Berrick said, almost into her hair.

She tried to scream upstairs, drawing what breath she could. Only a breath came out. He pushed her backwards, steadily, and she went because his arm was longer and because each attempt to brace her feet drove pain through her side. A sheet brushed her ear. Then the mangle's wooden feeding shelf met the small of her back.

She had told Pell to move it. Every week she had told him, until he asked whether she meant to pay for a larger room. Now the corner pinned her between the shelf and Berrick's belly, with his sword hand trapped low against her apron. His free hand closed around her throat, pressing her head back until she could see the stained ceiling above his shoulder. She bit him. He jerked away and pulled his sword arm back. The edge drew across her left palm before she could release him. For a moment she felt only a tug; then her little finger and the finger beside it lay slack against the hilt. The blade came free. Blood ran hot to her elbow.

She turned sideways as he thrust again. The point went into the feeding shelf, and she struck his forearm down against it with the heel of her right hand. He pulled. The wood held for less than a breath, but in that time she saw his loose cuff lying across the folded sheet already fed between the rollers.

“Tomas. Turn it.” He had crawled as far as the mangle's other side. He must have moved while she fought. His eyes rested on her hand.

“Turn the bloody handle.” Tomas reached up.

Ida pushed the hanging edge of Berrick’s cuff under the fold with her right thumb, then snatched her hand away. Berrick struck at her with his left fist. It caught the top of her head and sent a flash of light across the room, but the crank was moving.

The first movement took the wet fold. The second drew Berrick's cuff into it, bunching wool against linen until his wrist struck the narrow space between the rollers. He abandoned the sword and tried to wrench his hand out. Ida leaned across the shelf and threw her weight onto his elbow, pinning it with the whole breadth of her chest.

He could still have pulled free of her. She felt him beginning to do it, his shoulder rising under her chest, but Tomas got both hands onto the handle and dragged it down. Cloth tightened. Berrick's knuckles flattened against the upper roller, then went beneath it with a noise she had heard once before, when a woman fed a sleeve through without taking her hand out.

His knees struck the floor. He screamed into the wooden shelf.

“Stop,” Ida said. The handle moved another fraction. “Stop. He's caught.”

Tomas let go. He folded over the bolster, clutching it against his side where fresh blood was soaking through. Ida kept her right hand on Berrick's shoulder, though he had stopped fighting her and was trying instead to find somewhere to put his forehead. His free hand opened against the floor.

The cudgel knocked against a tub behind her.

She turned too fast. Pain shortened her breath again, and she had to lean against the mangle while Cale came around the fallen sheets. He was limping. His eyes moved from his companion to the empty crank, then to the sword stuck in the feeding board beside her hip.

“Back out,” she told him. He shifted the cudgel into both hands.

She tried to take the sword. Her left hand closed with only three fingers, spilling blood over the grip, and the shock of that failure held her still. Those two fingers belonged to her. They touched the wood. She stared at the useless fingers, waiting for them to obey, until Cale's next step brought him beyond the end of the tub.

With her right hand she found the mangle handle.

Berrick raised his head. “Don’t.”

“Tell him.”

Cale stopped, but his cudgel stayed up. Upstairs, a door opened. “Ida? What have you broken?” Annis called, and Ida heard herself answer in the voice she used when a girl tried to carry too much down the stairs.

“Annis, stay up there. Open the front window. Shout for help.”

Cale glanced towards the ceiling.

She lifted the handle enough to take up the slack. Berrick made a wet, pleading sound and seized her skirt with his free hand.

“Cale, get out,” Berrick said. “Get out, you bastard.”

For a moment Cale looked as though he might hit him. Then he backed towards the street, keeping the tub between himself and the mangle. She watched his boots. At the door he turned and ran.

Annis began shouting. A window across the lane opened, then another, and the washhouse filled with voices asking questions nobody inside could answer. Jory Fenn from across the lane appeared carrying a fire iron. Ida fixed her eyes on his striped braces, which came down with his wife’s washing every fortnight. He had put them on over his nightshirt. She kept hold of the crank until he stepped inside.

“Jory, shut that,” she said. “Stand against it.” He did, looking at her face.

Only then did she take her hand off the mangle. Her right fingers had cramped around the wooden grip. She eased them straight against her skirt, one at a time, while blood from the other hand pattered onto the drip tray. Jory pointed the fire iron at Tomas and asked whether he was one of them. She said no. Tomas waited under her stare with the bolster clutched to his side, offering no proof of anything. At last she told Jory to keep everyone in the room until help came, herself included.

Berrick was crying. Tears and rinse water ran together through his beard, dripping onto the tiles between his knees. He asked her to turn the rollers back. She looked at the sleeve drawn tight over his wrist and remembered the woman from years ago, how someone had pulled before the pressure was lifted. “Pell knows the pressure screws,” she told Jory. “Call him down here. He can lift the rollers off.”

Tomas sat against the bench, his eating knife on the floor beyond his reach. He had lost a shoe. The grey sole of his stocking was darned in the same dark thread as the alterations to his coat.

“You'll want that,” she said.

He looked where she was looking and began to laugh. It hurt him. He stopped with his mouth open and waited until he could breathe again.

“They'll come back.”

She believed him. The street door trembled under a neighbour's impatient hand, and she made Jory ask who it was before lifting the latch. Annis came down with Ruth close behind her, both barefoot, their hems gathered above the wet stairs. Annis went straight to the clean-cloth shelf. Ruth stopped beside Ida and began to cry, trying to undo her own apron so she could put it over the hand.

“Ruth, fetch somebody who can mend it,” Ida said. “You know the surgery at the market. Knock until they open.”

“Your mouth too,” Annis said. She caught Ruth’s apron before it touched the wound and pushed it aside.

“The hand first.”

Ruth ran upstairs for her shoes. Ida sat on the low bench while Annis tied linen around her palm. Across the room, Pell arrived in his nightshirt and stood in the rinse water staring at his machine. She had expected him to shout about it. When he saw Berrick kneeling at the rollers, he crouched without a word and put both hands to the pressure screw.

The district main hummed behind the meter. Its little brass needle trembled as it always did, charging for the heat still rising from the abandoned tubs. She watched it while Annis tucked the loose fingers into place. Her whole arm shook. The pressure hurt, and she welcomed the hurt until it became too much and she asked for a moment.

Tomas had crawled nearer. He reached towards the knot, paused, and looked up at her.

“Hold that end,” she told him.

He held it. With her right hand she drew the linen tight.

I · The Fold

*Enma's Register, the Hall of Verdicts, a third of it gone into the dark; the first minute of the morning passing*

[harsh, rasping, Japanese accent, disbelieving] "No... it can't be..."

The Sin of Wrath said it into the base of the block, with his cheek in the old dark stain that had never dried and the blood running out of his shoulder in pulses onto the black polish. Then he lifted his head and said the rest of it up at the boy standing over him.

[shaking, low] "I will not accept this..."

He shook his head. It went slowly from side to side against the stone, once, twice, the long braids dragging through the red, the way an old bull swings its head at a gate it has already charged and found shut.

He read the boy. He could not stop reading. TSUMI-YOMI had the living left eye on Muken Moto's breastbone before Shōki had decided to look, the ring-lines turning in the iris and the red capillary light crawling at its rim, and the record came up out of the boy's chest the way it always came, scar laid on scar, the dead stacked in him like fired plates in a kiln. Three hundred and eight. He had counted them on the road. Under the count, where a practitioner's reserve should have stood like oil in a lamp's bowl, Shōki read nothing at all. The boy was burning on the wick. Shōki had seen that once before, on a Kurenai scaffold three centuries gone, in a condemned clerk who had spent the last year of his life in one night to finish copying out his own sentence, and had died with the brush in his hand. Muken had gone past the bottom of what he had. Whatever he swung the gray sword with next, he would be swinging it with himself.

Neither did Shōki have what he had come in with. He knew it with the same flat certainty a headsman knows the weight of the neck under his edge. The GŌKA in the floor behind the boy's shoulder had taken a killing blow's whole fury into his own polish. The black-violet rim of his aura had thinned on the side that faced the boy, the bank he had built on the road paid out and spent. And the arm was gone, and three hundred years of quench and temper had gone with it as glitter. His own count gave him a handful of turns at full output, and fewer than that. The fever in his scarred face was dropping. The drop frightened him more than the stump.

This fight had to end for now.

It tasted like ash. A Kurenai did not adjourn. The register closed or it stayed open. But the law he had made of himself at the block was HAKANASHI, *what is done to me is never finished*, and a law that says never finished also says not yet. He took the not yet in his teeth and bit down on it.

Above him, the boy yawned.

Shōki saw it happen and saw what it meant in the same instant. Through the hairline seam of daylight at the hall's far edge the lavender light was still coming in, thick as honey off a spoon, and with it the Sloth's drowse, that heavy air the goblin leaks the way a stove leaks heat. It had gone into Muken's jaw. The boy's mouth stretched open on it. His gold-lit eyes went heavy for one beat. And the point of Tsugigane, which had hung a hand's breadth over the dead crack at the back of Shōki's neck, came down a beat late.

Shōki rolled his head to the left on the stone.

The gray blade came down where the nape had been. The boy had put his weight behind it from the right shoulder, the edge falling straight, the harness creaking as his torso turned; the Eye had shown him the crack and the yawn had stolen the beat between seeing and cutting. The point took the side of Shōki's neck instead, a glancing stroke that opened the skin over the long strap of muscle running from behind the ear to the breastbone, a finger's length, shallow, and drove on past it into the black polish with a short bright *kin* like a nail struck into slate. Shōki's flesh went cherry-red along the cut and steamed. ENSHŌ drank the little force there was in it and sealed the edges with a hiss, *jitt*, and the blood that should have run boiled instead to a rust-colored breath and was gone. The sword stood in the floor. The boy hung over it, both hands on the grip, swaying in his lit harness, his face gray as ash.

Shōki drove energy outward in a spiral.

It came up out of the open fracture under his breastbone, the line of the block that had run through his Crystal for three hundred and fifty years and never closed, and it came up as hatred, because that was the only shape anything in him came up in now. He took it from the bank. ONNEN had kept every blow the boy had landed on him as heat, tagged with the boy's name, and ONNEN would only let him spend that heat against its author, and its author was standing over him. He spent it. It went out of him through his corrupted Shell, the Rot-Born Shell that amplifies everything it carries and eats at itself doing it, and three parts in every ten of it came off him as plain waste heat, so that the polish under his knees went soft as wax and the air round him crawled like the air over a summer road. His aura flared to its full color, ember-orange grained like a lamp filament driven past its limit, the black-violet hem at its edge thin as a widow's mourning ribbon on the side toward the boy. The furnace roar came up under him with a whine wound into it, the sound a bellows makes when a smith pinches the nozzle, GWOOOOOOO, and over it a rising whistle, *hyuuuuuuu*. The hall smelled of scorched iron and wet ash.

He set the heat turning.

Every forest fire in the Kharven steppe that runs before a hot wind sooner or later stands up a fire-whirl: a column of burning air that begins to turn as it rises, and narrows as it climbs, and turns faster the narrower it gets, the way a skater on the Marrow ice spins faster when she pulls her arms in to her chest. A turning thing keeps its turn. Draw it in tight and the turn has nowhere to go but faster. Shōki set the heat of the bank turning round the boy, low and wide at first, a ring of fire the width of the hall's steps going round the two of them at a walk, and then he drew it in. It tightened. It climbed. It went round them at a trot, at a gallop, a spinning wall of ember-orange so fast that it stopped looking like fire and began looking like spun glass, and the kiln-light shafts that had moved one hand's breadth in three hundred years bent toward it, every bar of them leaning into the turn, and the dust standing in them was snatched up off its place and went round with the rest.

Then he bent the page.

The Register was a page Vantabriel held curled round one morning. He had always felt it that way, the way a man knows the shape of his own sleeping-mat in the dark. It had a far edge, where the seven chairs had stood, and that edge had already folded once, turning inward toward him like a leaf in a book when the boy broke the iron. The Sloth's seam stood in the fold, a crack of cedar daylight. The natural philosophers of Stannvaard drew it for their students on a sheet of paper: two marks at opposite ends of a long page are far apart if you must walk the paper between them, and no distance at all if you fold the page until the marks lie one on the other and push a pin through both. He took the far edge of his own hell and folded it the rest of the way down, under the block, under his knees, until the cedar daylight lay beneath the floor of the hall like the bottom of a well.

The cost of it came through the stump. The bank thinned toward nothing on the boy's side. The polish round him blistered and ran. And his torn lung, low on the right where the boy had opened it on the road, bubbled and filled with the effort, so that every breath he drew to hold the fold dragged a wet rattle up through his chest.

He reached up with his one hand and took Muken Moto by the harness.

The long fingers went in under the red lacing over the boy's breastbone, where the cords Kiyome Byakuya had laced in the old order still burned red-gold from inside, and closed on them. The boy saw it come. Shōki watched the gold in both his eyes flare and rank it, the Eye showing him the hand on the harness and every crack in the hand, worst first; but the yawn still had his body, and the shell of him had nothing left in it to move with, and he hung on his sword's grip and could not let go of it fast enough. Shōki came up off his knee. His left foot drove down into the softened polish and the whole drawn-wire length of him rose off the floor behind the one arm, and he pulled the boy down to him, down past his own shoulder, down into the turning throat of the spiral where the floor had opened like the drain of a basin.

Tsugigane came out of the black floor with a long shriek of steel on stone and went with its owner.

The spiral took the boy. It wound him in. Shōki felt the harness lacing tear out of his fingers, *bichi*, as the turn caught the weight, and watched the boy go down the funnel of fire feet first and spinning, the broken left arm bound across the chest, the splinted knee, the furrowed gray face with its two gold eyes still open, still ranking, smaller, smaller, a red-and-gold figure falling away down a well of burning glass toward the cedar daylight at its bottom. Far down, through the fold, Shōki saw branches. Black cedar boughs, rimed with frost. The boy struck them. He went through them in a burst of white rime and snapping wood, *bakibakibaki*, and was gone into the gray light of the robbed sky on the other side.

The fold snapped shut. It closed like a book slammed on a desk, *BATAN*, and the cedar light went out, and the hall shrank again round him by another third, the black-violet edge of the Register coming in so close that he could have walked to it in ten strides.

He was alone in his hell.

He screamed.

It came up out of him from the open fracture, from the block, from three centuries of a morning that never ended, and it came out of the torn lung and the burned throat and the empty socket of the arm, and the Hell Fire came up with it. It stood up out of the black floor round him in a ring, white-orange at the root, ember-orange in the body, black-violet along its licking crown, the last of everything the kept morning had ever banked, thermite fire that carries its own breath and wants no air, roaring, GOOOOOOOOOOO, and the shortened hall went the color of a kiln's mouth from wall to wall.

[screaming, raw, Japanese accent] "I NEED MORE POWER!"

He dropped to his knees.

He slammed what was left of his right arm into the floor. The stump was a hand's length of shoulder ending in the clean flat face where Tsugigane's Resolve had taken it, the bone showing white in the middle of it like the heart of a cut branch, and he brought it down onto the black polish with his whole weight, *DON*, so that the bone ground on the stone and the blood squirted out round it in a hot fan and the pain went up through his neck into the back of his skull like a nail driven home. He held it there. He ground it in.

The floor gave him what it had.

Three hundred years of banked heat lay in the polish like coals under ash, and he had opened its seams once already for the Hell Fire; he opened them now for himself. The heat went up into the stump. And his body, which was the shape of his law, began to do what the Kurenai scholars had never once been able to explain to the Holy Sea's examiners about the Unburied: it began to grow back.

The cut face of the shoulder clouded over. A skin came across it first, thin and wet and pink as the skin on scalded milk, sealing the open meat. Under that skin the flesh lost its shape. Shōki felt it go: the muscle at the stump's end forgetting it had ever been muscle, the bone's end going soft, every cell under the new cap turning back into the plain, unshaped stuff a child is built from in the womb. The salamanders in the cold pools of the Hallenfeld mines do exactly this when a mine-cat takes a foot off one of them. The wound grows a cap. Under the cap the flesh unlearns itself into a bud. And the bud remembers the limb, from the stump outward, and builds it again in order: bone, then muscle, then skin, then the small bones of the hand last of all. His Regeneration was ordinary for a man of his Stage and no more. The heat was what drove it. The bank fed it the way a forge's bellows feeds a fire, and the bud swelled out of the stump, a blunt white-red shoot like a peeled bamboo heart, steaming, ticking as it grew, *pichi... pichi... pichi*, every tick a new layer laid.

It cost him. He felt the fever spike back up into his face, hotter than it had been on the road, the scars across his brow and cheek going from warm to burning. He felt the last of his reserve going out of him into the shoot in long pulls. The torn lung filled. He coughed, and the blood came up out of him in a dark gout and struck the polish and boiled where it landed, rust-colored, stinking.

He knelt in the ring of Hell Fire with the bud growing out of his shoulder and thought.

The boy had people. He had said so with his whole body when he grabbed the iron. *All those who raise their hands up against the ones I love.* Shōki had felt it go into the hall as it was sworn, entered, the way a sentence is entered. It had made the boy strong for one stroke. A man who swears for others puts his strength outside himself, where it can be reached.

[low, a hanging judge's voice, wet with blood] "I'll just have to take everything from him..."

TSUMI-YOMI could not read what a man would do. It read only what he had done. It did not need to. The boy's own mouth had read it out on the road. Shōki laid the names down in his head the way a clerk lays out the warrants for the morning's work. The white line of the Moto, the Byakuya woman who had laced him into that harness in the old order with her own hands and sworn over a lamp that he was hers to keep. And the girl in the cedar. The Greymane girl. He had put her into a tree trunk eight paces deep, and the boy had screamed her name.

[quiet, precise, Japanese accent] "Perhaps killing Kiyome... or even that Greymane girl..."

The heat in his face went down a shade as he said it. The fury cooled into something narrower. It went in, to a point, the way a blacksmith's fire goes from a roar to a single white bead under the bellows when he wants it for one rivet, and the point was very bright. He coughed again. More blood. He let it fall.

On his shoulder the shoot opened. Five buds came out of its end, small, wet, red, the fingers of a hand no bigger than a child's, and they curled, and steamed, and closed together into a fist.

II · The Reckoning

*The Kessen road at the edge of the cedar wood, under the iron hills; the robbed gray sky, frost on the road*

The cedar wood broke open fifty paces up the road from the carriage, and a boy came out of the sky.

Naiser saw the seam first. It hung in the air at the wood's edge where the Sloth had drifted to it an hour ago, a crack of black-violet the height of a door standing upright over the frozen ruts, and lavender glass bleeding out of it in slow glossy drips. Then the seam lit from inside, orange, spinning, a little whirl of fire turning in its depths like a coal seen down a well, and through it came a red-and-gold shape falling feet first. It came out of the crack forty feet up among the cedar crowns. It went through them. Branch after branch broke under it, *bakibakibaki*, each bough throwing off its rime in a white burst as it snapped so that the boy came down through a falling cloud of his own frost, and the last bough bent under him and dropped him, and he struck the road.

He struck it on his back across the frozen ruts. The ground there was clay frozen as hard as tile, and the harness took most of it, the lacquered plates over his spine slapping the ice with a flat hard *bachi* and splitting, and the back of his burned head struck after them. He bounced once. He lay still in the frost with his broken left arm bound across his chest and the gray sword still in his right hand, and from the long cut across his face the blood began to run out onto the white, steaming in the cold.

Muken Moto, said Itomi, the sight that holds, and laid the boy's thread out for Naiser like a line drawn on paper. It was very thin. It was the thinnest thread he had seen on a living body since the slope above the river. There was no reserve on it at all. The boy was burning on himself, and the harness alight on him was the only thing holding his shape.

Above the seam the Sin of Sloth lay on a fresh slab of glassed air and looked at the boy in the road and said, small, [thick, drawling, gravelly street accent, dismayed] "Aw. Aw, no. He's out. The Wrath's still in."

Naiser regarded Vaashuuk.

The goblin was mending. Naiser could see it, and the Lambert count set it down in figures without his asking: the black skin over the back of the cut hand had drawn closed into a pink seam and the two fingers that had hung dead in his palm were twitching, trying to open, the cut cords under the seam groping toward each other like severed vines; the notch the kunai had torn out of the long ear had a fresh pink rim on it; the flank where his daughter's blade had opened all three plies of muscle was pulling itself shut, the torn edges crawling toward one another under the soaked black shirt. The wound in the thigh where Hiromi's auger had wrung the big muscle round like a rope was the slowest. It still bled. It bled the way everything about the goblin happened, dark and thick and slow, a dormouse's heartbeat pushing plum-colored blood out of a hole that would have emptied a faster creature. His body had been built to sleep through what killed others. It was sleeping through this.

Naiser looked at Hiromi.

The boy sat in the carriage doorway in Dari's lap with nine white tails wrapped round him to the chin, awake now, his broken nose swollen purple across his face and his fingertips black to the first knuckle where the Z had come out through them. He was staring at the boy in the road. Dari's ears were flat to her skull and her eyes were going from Muken to the seam to Naiser and back.

He looked at Senna.

His daughter stood beside the off-wheel with her right arm bound to her chest in a sling of gray cord where he had put the shoulder back in on the Kessen road, her face the color of the frost, the kunai on her left forefinger by its ring. She had not been told to stand there. She had chosen the place where the carriage wheel covered her right side. She was looking at him.

[low, stern, measured Japanese accent] "I'll handle this now."

He took the leaf from his mouth. It had burned down to a finger's width of ash and ember, and the smoke from it, which should have stood in the cold like a gray thread, was leaning toward the goblin in long lazy loops, caught in the glass. He dropped it in the frost of the road and set the toe of his boot on it and turned his heel once. The ember went out under the leather with a faint crunch of ice, *jari*. Then he laid one finger on the guard of the longblade at his left hip, along the edge of the plain iron disc, and left it there.

[quietly, almost kindly] "I suggest you run."

He meant it. He was offering the creature the door at the end of the hall.

Vaashuuk stared down at him from his slab. Then he laughed, high and offended, with all the long vowels back in it.

[shrill, outraged, drawling] "RUN!? WHO D'YA THINK I AM, SOME SORTA PUSSY?"

The words went out of him into the cold, and Naiser felt them meet his Trait. THE ENTERED WORD set what was said near him the way a cure sets resin, and it cured only what was true; the padding that hid a thing did not set and fell away. A goblin who had never once finished a sentence he meant was speaking within twenty paces of the head of the Yukari.

[airily, then faltering] "No matter... I'll just kill ya in a single... I'll just start..."

He stopped. His bottle-green eyes went wide. He had heard it come out of his own mouth plain: *I'll just start*. He touched his torn ear with one claw, very fast, the way a man touches his purse when he feels a hand near it.

He yawned.

It was enormous. It cracked his jaw. It went out of him in a long wavering groan, *haaaaaaaaaam*, and Naiser felt it go into his own jaw from fifty paces off and set his teeth against it, and behind him heard Hiromi yawn, and Dari, and the carriage horses blowing long and loose through their lips. The air on the road went toward glass. It came in round Naiser's shins first, then his knees, then the whole of him, thick, clear, the frost-mist over the ruts stopping where it stood in white rags, the steam rising off the boy's blood in the road slowing and slowing until it hung over him like gauze. TAR TIME. The goblin had found the strength to start one more thing. Naiser watched the lavender shimmer thicken round him and the halo of hanging droplets turn, all of them together, toward the man with his finger on his sword.

Then the goblin reached for his single attack, and Naiser watched him reach for it, and read it the way he read everything, worst first.

Vaashuuk did not hit people. He had said so to the children on the causeway, and the Lambert count had kept it. He lifted his good left hand, palm out, and closed his claws slowly toward his palm, the way he had closed them on the dike to stop Hiromi's breath: the glass in front of Naiser's mouth began to stiffen from honey toward pitch, a plug of it forming a hand's breadth from his lips. A practitioner breathing hard would have drawn on it and found nothing. Naiser was not breathing hard. He breathed as he had breathed on the river-stones in the yard, in for six and hold and out for six and hold, slow, under the pace at which the stuff stiffens, and the thickened air flowed into him like cold syrup and filled his chest.

The two notes of his aura drifted together until they were one.

"Seisan..."

Settlement. The Reckoning, the account struck at the end of a form. RYŌKE NO KATA came up in him with its whole rise, and the charge for it came up from the Sovereign Crystal under his breastbone through a Shell so fine that near enough everything he called went where he sent it; the small part that strayed came off his fingertips as a warmth on the cord of the hilt, as if he had held it near a lamp. It was a large bite. A little over a quarter of everything he carried went into one exchange. His breath and his heartbeat fell into one count. The gray of his aura went black-violet at its edges like black silk drawn over a blade, the air round him dimmed as if somebody had gone along a corridor turning down the lamps, and the sound of it was two low notes become one with a long hall's echo under them, *ooooonnnnn*, and over the echo a small dry click like a bead pushed home along the wire of a Lambert counting-frame. *Pachi*.

And on the edge, as it came out of the mouth of the scabbard, the light went out.

Naiser had told his daughter on the causeway that he did not have the gift his mother had had. He had told her the truth. What the Ketsumyōgan carries of Ashura's way of seeing is a fragment, and a fragment is all he had ever carried: the Yukari look that leaves a room as it finds it. It was enough for one plane, one edge, one stroke. Along the polished steel a thread of black ran out from the guard to the point, blacker than the shadow under the carriage, and where it ran there was nothing on the blade to reflect the gray sky.

Lay a long rope out on a yard and send a hump down it from each end at the same moment, one hump flicked upward from the left and one flicked downward from the right. Where they meet in the middle, for one instant, the rope lies dead flat on the ground, the up and the down cancelling each other exactly, and then they pass through each other and go on. The goblin's glass was a slowness that the air was carrying, a law passed from grain to grain the way a hump passes down a rope. The null-light on the edge was its mirror. Wherever the edge went, the slowness met its own opposite and lay down flat.

He drew.

His left thumb pushed the guard forward off the scabbard's mouth a finger's width, *chiki*. His left hand drew the scabbard back along his hip as his right hand drew the blade forward out of it, the two hands parting like the halves of a pair of shears, so that the steel left the wood at the speed of both arms together. His right foot drove forward off the frozen clay. His hips turned behind it, left to right, and his right shoulder followed the hips, and the arm followed the shoulder, the whole long frame unrolling behind the edge from the heel up, and the blade went out from his hip in a straight flat line at the height of a goblin's raised forearm.

It drifted through the spiritual slowness enacted round him, and the slowness was cut in two.

The road saw it before Hiromi did. The frost-mist hanging over the ruts parted along a single plane, as cleanly as a bolt of wet silk under a tailor's shears, the two halves of each white rag peeling away from the line and lying back, and along that plane the glass simply was not glass anymore. A sheet of plain cold air ran from Naiser's hip to the goblin's slab in the middle of the honey, a flat path no thicker than the blade, and the blade went down it at its own speed. Behind it, where the null-light had passed, the hanging blood-steam over Muken fell to the road in a sudden shower of warm drops, and the frost on the ruts along the line cracked in a straight seam like the ice on a pond when a boy throws a stone across it.

Vaashuuk read nothing. Inside his glass every blow that came at him came at a crawl, and he had spent ninety years learning that he never needed to see anything fast. He had raised his good hand to set a pane, lazily, the waiter's wave, and the pane formed in front of the hand, glossy, and the edge came down the cut plane of plain air and the pane was not there anymore where the edge touched it. It went to nothing. Its two halves fell apart and lay down like the rope.

The edge took him at the right forearm.

He had raised the wounded hand too, the one with the twitching fingers, without thinking, as a man raises both hands against a thrown thing. The edge went in at the wrist on the outer side, between the two long bones of the forearm, the radius on the thumb side and the ulna on the little-finger side, and it went up between them. It raked along the arm toward the elbow, splitting it the way an axe splits a green stake down its grain: through the tough sheet of membrane that binds the two bones together along their length, through the deep muscles that turn the hand palm-up and palm-down, through the long cords that close the fingers, and through both the arteries that feed the hand, the one that pulses at the thumb side of the wrist and the one on the little-finger side. The forearm came open from wrist to elbow like a forked stick, the two halves bowing apart on either bone with the hand still hanging from both. The sound was a single wet tearing stroke, *zubaaa*, and then a grate as the edge kissed the ulna's ridge at the elbow, *gori*.

The cut did not stop at the elbow. Naiser had not asked it to. It came out of the split arm still carrying the whole long unrolling of his body behind it, and it smashed against the goblin's chest.

It took him across the front of the chest, left to right, below the collarbones: through the olive cloak and the black shirt and the pale green silk tie, which parted and floated away in two pieces, through the skin and the broad flat muscle that pulls the arm across the body, and into the breastbone under it, where it scored a line across the bone's face, *gichi*, and stopped against the bone with the force of the whole cut still coming. That force went into the goblin. A creature half a man's height with Fortitude worse than a farmhand's met a cut thrown from a Stage XII man's heel, and it lifted him off his slab of air the way a kicked bucket comes off a step. He went back through his own glass, slowly at first, then faster as the cut plane of plain air carried him along it, and struck the trunk of a cedar twenty paces behind the seam with his shoulders and the back of his head, *DOSUN*, and the tree shook from its root to its crown and dropped its whole coat of rime onto him in one white slide.

The blood came after the sound. It came out of the split arm in two dark fans, slow, plum-black, thicker than ink, and out of the long cut across his chest in a sheet. It hung in the glass round him in beads. It hung in strings. It hung in the cold air over the road in a slow red web.

Vaashuuk sat in the snow at the cedar's foot with his split arm in his lap and looked at it. He looked at it for a long time. His mouth was open on a yawn that had cracked halfway and stayed cracked.

Naiser was not done.

He felt the black-light come up in him. It came up from the place under his breastbone where the two bloods kept their count, the Yukari and the Lambert, the sight and the ledger, and it came up as pressure, a great slow weight of it, the weight a Stage XII soul carries in the room round it whether it wants to or not, pressing out from him in every direction. The cedar trunks along the road groaned. The frost on the clay cracked into plates under the pressure and the plates jumped, *pishi... pishi*, and at the carriage the four gray horses went down on their knees in their traces all together with a clatter of harness. Dari's ears flattened. Hiromi's breath stopped in his throat.

[low, formal, the house's old Japonic] "First Release..."

He spoke it with the house's name, aloud, and so it came with its whole rise.

"Sōi... Nimaime."

The Twin Mantle. The second page.

His aura settled on him like a coat. It came down over his shoulders in a long fall of black silk, silver-violet at every seam where it met itself, high at the collar and wide at the sleeve, and for a breath the two halves of him showed, laid one over the other like two pages held up to a window: the Breath's long line, the Yukari sight that reaches for every thread in a place, and the Lambert count, the bench that reads a blow before the shoulder commits to it. Two pendulum clocks hung on one beam will swing themselves into step overnight, each nudging the other through the wood. The old clockmakers called it the odd sympathy, and swore at it. The beam was his Crystal, and the clocks had been agreeing for twenty years, and in the Mantle they agreed completely: every motion of his arrived exactly as the count had already decided it would. The charge for it came from the Crystal steadily, in a long pull like a river through a sluice-gate, and the Mantle would hold about two score breaths from a full reserve, and his was well short of full now. The waste came off him as heat. The rime on the cedar needles round him melted in a ring and dripped. The two notes of his aura went to one note and the hall's echo under them deepened until it was the echo of a whole corridor of halls, and the black-violet pressure in the air went darker still, and the smell of him came down the road on it, cedar smoke and cold stone.

Then he cut the goblin at multiple angles.

Hiromi could not see him move.

Naiser knew it, because the Itomi showed him every thread on the road, and the boy's thread did not twitch. What Hiromi saw, Naiser learned later from the boy's own mouth: a lord in gray standing on the road with his blade drawn and level and his coat of black silk on his shoulders, and then the same lord standing in the same place, and between the two there was nothing at all. Only the road changed. Things happened along it after he had gone through them. The frost in the ruts lifted in a long straight trench toward the cedar, the trench opening behind nothing, as if a plow had gone down the road with no oxen. A wave of pressure struck the carriage a breath after, and the lamps swung, and the glass in the door-window crazed. The air over the trench split with a sound like a sail torn top to bottom in a gale, and after it, late, a crack that hurt the ear. Afterimages of a man in black silk stood up along the road in four places and faded like breath on a mirror. And blood spluttered out everywhere.

He went in on the goblin's left side, his right foot driving off the clay and his left coming down on the cedar's raised root, and cut from his left shoulder down to his right hip across the body in the long diagonal the Moto yards call the monk's-sash stroke, *kesa-giri*, the edge entering at the top of the goblin's right shoulder where Hiromi's pike had already chipped the shelf of bone and going down through the meat over the collarbone and the slope of the chest; Vaashuuk's eyes had not yet begun to turn toward him, and the cut opened the round cap of the shoulder over the old pike-hole and split the collarbone where it crosses to the breastbone with a short dry *kachi*, so that the right shoulder dropped a finger's breadth on its broken strut, and the blood came out of the opened shoulder in a sluggish rope; and he was already gone round the trunk on the turn of his hips, so that the edge coming back on the rising diagonal from the goblin's left hip, *gyaku-kesa*, met the goblin's body leaning the way the first cut had dropped it, and laid open the left side of the belly above the hip through the skin and the first two plies of muscle, a hand above where Senna's kunai had cut the other side, shallow, deliberate, so that the dark gut-lining showed for an instant and did not tear, and Vaashuuk began, very slowly, to look down at himself.

The third cut came low and flat across both thighs a hand above the knees, *yoko-ichimonji*, the single straight line, Naiser's weight dropping onto his bent left knee on the frozen root and his right arm sweeping the blade level from the hip, and it went through the big four-headed muscle on the front of each thigh where it narrows into the thick cord that holds the kneecap and straightens the leg; the left thigh already had the auger's burned hole in it and parted round the old wound like rotten cloth, and the right parted clean, and both cords sprang back up into the thighs with a sound like two bowstrings cut at once, *bun, bun*, so that whatever the goblin did now, his knees would never again straighten under him. The fourth and fifth went behind him at the heels, one and then the other, the point flicking in from Naiser's left and then his right as he passed behind the trunk, through the black platform boots above the buckles and the leather and the skin into the great cord that ties the calf to the heel, the tendon a runner breaks when he hears it go like a pistol; both of them went, *pan... pan*, and the goblin's feet hung loose at the ends of his legs like the feet of a hanged man. Vaashuuk had begun to open his mouth. The sixth cut took the oiled black crest off his head at the roots, the edge passing so close over the scalp that it parted the hair and not the skin under it, and the crest lifted off him whole and turned once in the glassed air like a black feather and hung there in front of his face.

Naiser stood on the road where he had started, with the longblade level in his right hand.

Then it all arrived at the goblin at once.

The six cuts opened together. Blood spluttered out of him in every direction. It came out of the shoulder and the hip and the thighs and the heels in dark plum fans and spatters and long thick ropes, and the glass that still clung round him took it and held it, so that for one instant the Sin of Sloth sat at the foot of the cedar inside a cloud of his own blood hanging in the air round him like red smoke round a struck censer, every drop of it separate, glittering in the gray light. Then the null-light's plane closed behind the last cut and the glass round the cedar went to plain air, and the whole hanging cloud fell at once. It came down on the snow and the cedar's roots and the goblin's lap and the road in a single heavy patter, *batabatabata*, and the white round the trunk went red for three paces in every direction.

The goblin was not dead. Naiser had placed every cut where it would not kill. He was crippled through, both knees and both heels and the sword arm and the shoulder, and the Lambert count set it down plain: at the pace his sleeping heart pushed blood, the slow bleed from a dozen opened vessels would take the night to empty him, and his mending would close a share of it and not enough. He would likely bleed out before morning, somewhere on his back in the snow, still meaning to get up.

On the carriage step Hiromi said, very quietly, [young, awed, Korean accent, whispering] "I couldn't see him move. I couldn't see him move at all."

Dari wrapped her tails higher round the boy's face. [hushed, urgent] "Don't look, Hiromi-sama."

He looked.

Naiser turned the blade over and drew the back of it once across his left forearm, along the gray sleeve, to clear the edge. Then he slid it back into the scabbard. It went home with a soft whisper of steel on wood, *shu*, and the guard met the scabbard's mouth with a small final click, *chin*.

He looked at the snow round the cedar, and at the red web of blood hanging and dripping from the low boughs, and at the spatters across his own boots, and his mouth thinned.

[dry, faintly distasteful] "What a mess..."

III · The Lie-In

*The same road, the same breath; the Mantle still on him*

Vaashuuk laughed.

He laughed at the foot of the cedar in the red snow with his split arm in his lap and his feet hanging loose and his crest lying in the blood beside him like a dead crow. It came out of him wet and high and with almost no breath behind it, and it would not stop.

[wet, wheezing laughter, drawling] "What a mess, he says." He coughed, and a dark thread ran down his chin. "What a mess. Ouuuuu, handsome. Ya cut real pretty. Ya cut prettier'n anybody ever cut me." His head lolled back against the trunk. "But I'll tell ya a secret, 'cause watchin' ya figure it out's my hobby. Everybody leaves a mess. Every single body. I'm the only one ever lies down in it."

The jokes ran out on the last word. Naiser heard them run out. The goblin's voice went thin and the long vowels shortened, the way they had on the causeway when his daughter cut his ear.

He yawned again.

It was the second yawn. It came up out of him slow and deep from the bottom of his chest, longer than the first, so long that the frost-mist over the road shivered with it, and Naiser felt it come for his own jaw and set his teeth, and felt it go past him into the carriage, into the horses on their knees, into the children. And under the yawn the slow pane-ringing hum that had sounded under the Sloth all day came up and held. It held one note. It held it like a glass that has been struck and will not stop ringing, *hmmmmmmmmmmmmm*, and Naiser's skin crawled under the Mantle.

He knew it. The record had it under the goblin's name: the Second Release.

[faint, sing-song, almost asleep] "Thaal-Snorr..." Vaashuuk said, in Vaross, with his eyes shut. "Lie-in."

THE LIE-IN came out of him like dawn coming up through a window nobody has slept behind. It opened in one long breath from the foot of the cedar out across the road, past the carriage, past the seam, past the boy lying in the ruts, a hundred and fifty paces in every direction, and its color was the lavender of a sky at the hour when a sick man who has not slept sees the window go pale. The charge for it came out of the goblin's Crystal through a Shell that wasted nothing; the small warmth it shed melted the red snow under him into a pink slush. It was costing him, and costing hard: Naiser watched the goblin's thread pull, a long steady draw from what was left in him, the kind of spending he had never in his life been seen to do. It pulled him at about a two-hundredth of his full reserve in every breath, and there was not much left in him to pull from after the loops on the causeway.

And nothing on the road could start.

Naiser saw it take hold of everything at once. A fire wants a push before it catches, a little climb of heat over a hill before the wood will burn by itself, and the hotter the wood is already the more of it is ready to make the climb; that is why a damp log on a cold hearth only smokes, and why a coal from the bed of a hot fire lights anything you lay it on. Every beginning in the world is like that, a fire on a cold hearth or a hand lifting off a lap or a thought turning into a word. Each needs its small climb. The Lie-In raised the hill. Whatever was already moving went on moving at its own pace, and anything that wanted to begin had to climb three times as far.

Over the carriage a crow had come down out of the cedar crowns, black with the violet sheen on it, to see what the blood was. It had lifted its wings to land on the carriage roof, and the downstroke would not begin. It hung on the air with its wings up like a struck cross and its feet out and slowly, slowly, began to fall.

Senna had started toward Muken.

She had come off the wheel at the first sight of the boy in the road, and her first step had been ordinary, and the second step was hers to begin, and it would not begin. Naiser saw her body try. He saw the muscles of her good leg tighten to push her off and the push come late, as late as if every nerve from her brain to her foot were a rope she had to haul hand over hand. She leaned forward and her foot stayed on the clay, and she leaned further, and it came up off the ground at last as if it were pulling out of mud. Her face went white with fury.

[tight, quiet, Japanese accent] "Father, I can't..."

Hiromi was trying to found something. Naiser saw the clear wet green of the boy's aura flicker round him in Dari's lap and gutter. He had reached for his Crystal the way he had reached for it on the dike, and the core would not light; the bead of his Crystal that he pressed into the middle of a Realm to start the hollow would not press. He set his teeth on it. Blood ran out of his broken nose again. Nothing came. Dari had lifted one hand with fox-fire curling in her palm, a little blue-white flame, and the flame sat in her palm and shrank to a bead and stayed there, and would not grow.

And on the road Muken Moto's chest rose, and stopped, and stayed stopped.

Naiser watched the boy's thread on the Itomi. It was the cruelest thing the Domain did, and the goblin had not aimed it; he did not aim. A heart starts every beat. Every beat is a small spark that runs through the muscle of the heart from the top down and has to be begun again a moment later from nothing. A breath is begun again every time. The boy had nothing in him to pay the raised price with. His burned chest under the lit harness came up one more breath, very slowly, as if it were lifting a stone, and his heart on the Itomi stuttered and caught and stuttered again, every beat late, every beat weaker than the one before, a drum played by a man falling asleep.

What was already moving kept moving. The goblin's own law said so. Naiser's Mantle was moving. It had been running on his shoulders when the Lie-In came up, and it ran still, the black silk falling from his shoulders and the silver-violet burning at the seams; it would not stop because a hill had risen in front of things that had not begun. It was also burning him. Every breath he stood there the river ran through the sluice out of his Crystal, and the Mantle's two score breaths were going by one after another while he counted them.

The blade in the scabbard was not moving.

He knew it before he tried. He tried anyway, because a count is a guess until it has been checked: he laid his thumb against the guard and pressed, and the guard came forward off the scabbard's mouth as if the scabbard were full of cold resin, the steel dragging in the wood, a finger's width taking the time of a long breath. To draw it again, he would have to begin the draw against the hill, at three times its price, with the Mantle eating at him all the while, and a third of what he had left would go into that one beginning before the edge was clear of the wood.

Vaashuuk opened one eye.

[drowsy, smug, slurring] "See... see, that's the thing about puttin' yer toys away, handsome. Gettin' 'em out again's work." His split arm lay in his lap. His feet lay in the snow at the ends of his legs like dropped gloves. He was bleeding out, slowly, and he did not mind. "Nothin' starts in here. Not yer sword. Not yer little girl's feet. Not the kid's birds. Not..." He moved his head a little toward the road. "Not his heart, neither. Real sorry about that one. He's a good-lookin' kid under all that." The eye drooped. "Nothin' ends in here neither. We just lie here, all of us. Nice an' quiet. Till my friend comes out."

The seam glowed.

Naiser turned his head and saw it. The black-violet crack at the wood's edge, the height of a door, was going orange from inside, heating the way the door of a banked stove heats when the fire behind it is being fed, and the frost on the cedar boughs round it was running off them in long silver drips. Something on the other side pressed against it. He saw the shape for one breath through the glowing crack: a small hand, red and wet and steaming, no bigger than a child's, laid flat against the seam from the far side, the fingers spread.

A fire already burning keeps burning in the Lie-In. A thing already moving keeps moving.

The goblin had not set this field to kill anybody. He never did. He had laid it down across the road the way he laid everything, to stand in the way of other people starting, and to wait, until someone else came along and finished.

Naiser stood in the frost with his thumb on the guard and the Mantle burning on his shoulders and laid the account out, the Lambert count setting each line in a clean hand.

The Lie-In had a counter in its own law. A stronger will could contest it from its edge, and his Sovereignty stood far above the goblin's; the Corridor would take the road away from the Sin hall by hall. But the Corridor was a Second Release, a new beginning, and every beginning cost three times its price on this road, with the First Release already eating him. It would leave him with very little when the thing behind the seam came through.

He could walk out. Bodies could leave it slowly, the record said, and he could lift the boy from the ruts, and his daughter, and walk them all a hundred and fifty paces down the road at the pace of a funeral while the boy's heart forgot itself one beat at a time on his shoulder.

He could finish the goblin, and the field would fall when its owner did. The goblin could not finish anything; Naiser could. That was what the creature was counting on. Every dead thing on this road would be Naiser's to have finished, and the Sin of Sloth would have done nothing at all.

Senna's foot came down on the clay, one step nearer the boy in the road, and her eyes found her father's.

Muken's chest rose, very slowly, and stopped.

The small red hand pressed flat against the glowing seam, and the seam bowed outward under it like the skin of a drum, and the drips of melted frost fell off the boughs above it one after another into the steam.

Naiser Yukari's thumb rested against the guard of the longblade, a finger's width out of its scabbard, and the silver seams of the Mantle burned on his shoulders, and he had perhaps a score of breaths of it left.

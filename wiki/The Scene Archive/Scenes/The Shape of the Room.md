*Aetherion Academy · the Crucible floor, Hiromi Mahuo's bout, from the second crater*

---

The ward-lines under his back flashed white and went dim again, and Hiromi lay in the bottom of the new crater looking up at the sky over the Crucible and taking stock of himself the way his mother had taught him to take stock after a fall from the practice horse: the far parts first, then the middle, then the head.

His legs answered. His hands answered. The back of him, from the shoulder-blades to the base of the spine, was one broad dull ache where the floor had met it, and the floor had come off worse; he could feel the broken plates of it shifting under him like ice on a pond. His belly was a knot where the downward fist had landed, and every breath had to be coaxed past it. His throat was raw where the forearm had locked across it, and when he swallowed there was blood in the swallow, not much, a mouthful. He turned his head and spat it onto the stone. Then he lay still for one more breath, because he had decided to, and because a boy who leaps up the instant he lands is a boy telling everyone in the tiers how badly he needs to.

They had been real blows. Every one of them had been placed by somebody who knew exactly where the motion in a body lives and how to borrow it: the roll caught at the shoulder and spun into a flip, the stumble taken and lengthened, the kick laid into the line of his own forward fall so that his weight and the boy's foot met in the middle of his chest with nothing between them to argue. Hiromi admired them the way he admired a well-made binding in another summoner's circle, wholly, with a professional greed to understand. They had also come into him lighter than they should have. He had felt that at each contact, a strange shortfall, like a coin weighed on a merchant's scale and found clipped at the edge, and he knew where the missing part had gone, because he had felt it go. It had stayed behind in the boy.

He would come to that. First there was the dragon.

---

He could see it without opening his eyes wider, because it was not in the air above him. It was in the Soul's reflection of the arena, the second Crucible that lies over the first like a pane of glass over a painting, where every practitioner's Crystal burns as a lamp and every working has a shape, and where his Lion, which had left its body behind, now stood entirely. A black dragon stood over it there. Its neck was colossal, ridged with spines burning green; its head was a pale skull scoured by wind; and one foreclaw was planted on the haetae's shoulders, pressing the guardian of Sum-gol down toward the floor of the reflected world while green light gathered in the dragon's jaws.

Gorgi's son. Hiromi did not need to be told that this soul had not been made by anyone's Dominion. Nothing about it had been recalled, commanded or arranged. It was born, and it was old, and it had a Name of its own and a will of its own, and the bond that tied it to the boy on the far side of the crater had been offered and accepted between two living souls with no glyph of Hiromi's anywhere in it. That was true, and Hiromi granted it gladly.

It was also beside the point. The dragon was a soul. And the very fact of being a soul was enough to take its authority from it here.

Irath's law did not stop at the edge of what Hiromi had made. The law of Sovereign Will was older and wider than any summons; it was the principle that Essence obeys conviction, and the Archon's own page in his father's books said what that law governs in plain words: hierarchy within the Wellsprings and within the structures of souls. Not within summoned souls. Within souls. Wherever two wills meet in the Soul, Irath's law is the thing that decides which of them is above, and it decides by conviction, and the Crucible was a sealed jar in which Hiromi's conviction had been laid over every grain of Aether since the Lion's first star. *Here, all of this, is mine to arrange.* A dragon's body could have stood in that jar and been only a great beast in a room. A dragon's soul, entering it, entered a hierarchy. To be a soul at all was to be rankable, and to be ranked there was to be ranked against the one whose will the room already wore.

Hiromi watched it try. The dragon's presence bore down on the Lion, and with the weight came a turning, a long green spiral that wound about the haetae's will and pulled, as though to wring the guardian's purpose out of it and draw it off into a band of utter nullity, a ring of nothing that went round forever and arrived nowhere. Hiromi felt the pull of it on the Lion as clearly as he would have felt a hand on his own sleeve. It was a terrible, ancient, patient strength. On any other floor in the world it might have worked.

It failed here. The spiral closed on the Lion's will and found, underneath it, something massive.

What the dragon met was not the Lion. The Lion was a memory and a verdict, and memories and verdicts can be wrung. What the dragon met was what held the memory, and that was Hiromi, and under Hiromi was the law Hiromi had never once named aloud. In the Soul's reflection, around the seated haetae, the dragon's green spiral came up against a resistance that had the texture of hands: strands and fingers of amber light, uncountable, rising out of the Lion's pinned body and closing on the green coils from beneath, not with any violence, simply holding, the way the roots of a great tree hold the hill that a flood is trying to carry off. They moved when Hiromi breathed. In and out, with his breath, in the bottom of the crater, as though the Archon's own hands were living and breathing inside every breath he drew. He felt them breathe. He had felt nothing so large in his life, and it was entirely calm.

The dragon's jaws came down on the haetae's neck with the green light pouring out between its teeth.

The flame that met them was amber.

It came up out of the Lion as the jaws closed, out of the place where its heart would have been if it had had one, and it was the colour of the King's Heart in the constellation of the Tyrant's Crown, the star of the Archon of Will: the fire of Irath's station, the fire that builds thrones. It washed over the black jaws and the scoured skull and down the burning spines of the neck, and wherever it touched, the dragon's form went still.

It petrified. That was the only word for it, and Hiromi understood exactly why it was the word. Fire is the oldest way there is to make a soft thing permanent. A potter's clay in the kiln goes from something any thumb can reshape to something that will hold its form for a thousand years: the heat drives the water out of it and fuses its grains into one another until it is a single locked body, stone made by hand. The haetae at the gate of Sum-gol was stone. Irath's fire was a fire that fixes things in their place in an order, and what it did to the dragon's form in the Soul was what a kiln does to clay: it fired it. It fixed the soul's shape hard, rank by rank, into a rigidity that could no longer flow or coil or spiral. A thing fixed like that in a hierarchy that is not its own cannot keep standing where it stands, because its standing has been changed under it. The Soul is the plane of what a thing is. The amber flame had just decided, under a law Hiromi held, what this soul was here.

And the jar pushed it out.

The dragon was launched apart from the haetae, out of the Soul's reflection of the Crucible, with no more say in the matter than a cork has when the bottle is pressed. Its soul did not end. Hiromi took care to feel that it did not; whatever else he was, he was not a boy who broke other people's living things. It was displaced. Its standing shifted from the Soul into the Material, out of the plane where it could wrestle a remembered guardian and into the plane where the arena's floor and dust and air were, the plane where the Weight lay. It came into the physical space of the Crucible like a great breath let go into a cold room, spread through it, and settled under the lid of the sealed jar with everything else. What it chose to do there was its own business. It was no longer standing on his Lion.

In the reflected Crucible, the haetae rose from under where the claw had been, shook its spirit-mane once, and sat back down in front of the gate.

---

Then the boy, and the harder question.

Hiromi went back over the last stretch of the fight in order. He went quickly, with the floor shifting under his back; he needed it straight before he stood.

The boy had shed the rest of his strike through his own body before Hyeonmu could keep it: down the shoulder, down the spine, split across both hips and out through the soles of his bare feet into the stone. It was the trick of a lightning rod, which saves a church by giving the strike a better road to the ground than the steeple, and it had worked. The shell kept only the first fraction. Hiromi did not grudge him that. He had the fraction.

Then the boy had stopped buying.

That was what he had said with his body, as plainly as if he had shouted it: that he no longer needed to draw his motion uphill out of Hiromi's slope, because the Crucible was already full of motion, and he could simply gather it. The tremble of the ward-lines. The settling of the fragments. The dust, the air sliding through its own pressure differences, the shell's slow pulse, even the rise and fall of Hiromi's own coat. All the small motion in the room, running every which way the way small motion always runs, and the boy had reached out over all of it and begun turning its millions of tiny directions into one direction, his. The floor had gone quiet under him. Hiromi had felt it go quiet, and had felt a cold admiration so sharp that it was nearly fear.

It was the oldest dream in natural philosophy, and Hiromi knew its name from a paper he had read by lamplight at ten and lain awake over. A tiny clever doorkeeper stands at a gate between two rooms of air, watching every mote, letting the quick ones through one way and the slow ones the other, until without any labour at all one room is hot and the other cold, and the jumble of the air has been sorted into usable force for nothing. The paper had called it a demon. Every natural philosopher who had ever met it had wanted it to be true. None of them had ever caught it free, because it is not free. To sort a mote the doorkeeper has to see it and know it and choose, and every choice it makes, it has to remember and then forget to make room for the next, and the forgetting costs. There is a least price for forgetting a single choice, small beyond imagining, and it is never zero, and it is always paid as heat, in the one who forgets. The disorder in the room does not disappear when a demon orders it. It moves into the demon.

So the boy had not stopped buying. He had only moved the till. Every one of those millions of tiny directions he had turned into one direction, he had had to know and choose and let go of, and the price of every one of them had been paid into the only place it could go, into the boy's own soul, into the pathways that did the knowing. That was where the shortfall in the blows had gone. That was where the clipped edge of every coin had stayed. His soul was getting less and less efficient at the very work it was best at, because every bit of order he made in the room was disorder made in himself.

And then the boy had turned his art on the Weight and on the slope, and that was where he had made his real mistake.

He had waited for them to act. Clever again: Weight called itself a pressure with no arrow and GRASP called itself a slope with no speed, and so the boy had let them be, until the moment either of them did something to him, and in that moment the doing had a motion in it, compression running through flesh, Aether climbing against a gradient, a recognition passing into its enforcement, and he had taken hold of the motion there and bent it into closed loops around his body. A sheath of circulation, shoulder to back, back to hip, hip to leg, round and round, the Weight never resolving inward and the tax never finishing its crossing. The coin went round the counter forever and never reached the drawer.

It was exactly the same move as everything else the boy had done since the bell, and it had the same hidden assumption under it that all the others had, the one thing Hiromi had not yet taken from him.

He was still treating Hiromi's Dominion as something in the room. As a thing that acted inside space, like a hand or a weight or a gust, that came from one place in the room and went toward another, so that if he was quick and clever enough he could step aside from it, or bend its path, or wind it into a loop and keep it busy. He was trying to escape it. Every move he had made since the jar sealed had been a move toward a way out.

He had not understood what the jar was.

When Hiromi laid **[Dom]** over the Crucible at the first star, the glyph had not said *here, all of this, is mine to push*. It had said *mine to arrange*. And the deepest arrangement in any room is not the things in it. It is the room. The distances between places. Which way is straight. What it means for two arrows at two ends of the floor to point the same way. Space is not an empty box that things sit in; Hiromi had read it in the papers he was not meant to understand, and believed it before he could prove it, and proved it to himself on a slate the winter he was twelve. Space has a shape, and its shape is the thing every motion obeys. A falling stone does not fall because something pushes it. It falls because, in the shape of the space around the world, the straightest line it can take leads down, and it simply takes the straightest line it has.

Within the jar, the shape of the space was his. The idea of space, the idea of existing inside the Aether or around it, was part of what he held. Moving could not take the boy out of it; moving was done inside it. He could not bend the Dominion's path, because the Dominion had no path; it was the shape of all the paths. Every loop the boy wound around himself was a loop drawn on Hiromi's floor.

And so the Dominion did not have to tax him any longer, and Hiromi stopped asking the slope to charge the boy a fare. He took the slope away entirely and let what was under it show.

---

He sat up in the crater.

It took a moment and he gave it the moment, one hand pressed flat to the ache in his belly, the other on the broken rim. The boy across the wreck had lowered his stance; one bare foot had drawn back against the fractured stone and the loose motion of the whole Crucible was turning toward him again, dust beginning to spiral instead of fall, rubble trembling toward alignment, long invisible currents tightening in the air. He was gathering for something. Hiromi could read that as easily as he read a gauge.

He did not let it rule him. He had been beaten across this floor by somebody better at bodies than he would ever be, and the lesson in it had been put into him plainly and with great skill: *you are a summoner, and up close you are mine*. He took the lesson and set it down, unanswered, the way one sets down a letter to reply to later. Then he stood up in the bottom of the crater and lifted both his hands.

He lifted them palm up, slowly, the way his father lifted his to begin the great circles, and as they rose, he took hold of the room.

There were threads in it. He had always known there were threads in it; he had felt them since the binding circle at six, the lines along which the Aether lay, the grain of space itself, running through the Crucible like the warp and weft of a loom. Under the jar they had become his. He closed his fingers on them now and pulled, and they came, strand after strand, out of the air and the stone and the dust, gathering into his hands, each one bearing his will along its length, each one carrying the metaphysical weight of the law that had just put a dragon's soul out of the Soul. He could see them, and so could anyone with eyes for workings: long fine lines of amber light drawn taut from every corner of the oval down into his two fists, a whole loom's worth, the room's whole fabric gathered in a boy's hands.

He paid for them with the boy's own coin. Down in the vault of Hyeonmu's shell, under the lid, lay the first fraction of the boy's strike, translated into Essence and held. Hiromi opened the lid and let it run up the threads like oil up a wick. It had been the boy's. It would hold the boy's floor in its new shape for him.

Then he pulled the threads tight, and the room bent.

Nobody could have pointed to the bend. No wall leaned. No floor buckled. A spectator in the tiers would have said that nothing had changed at all, except that the dust in the air, which had been spiralling toward the boy, suddenly could not decide which way was the spiral. The space of the Crucible had taken on a shape, curved inward around the white-haired boy standing in his crater with his fists full of light, the way the space around a great star curves inward around the star; and in a curved room, the meaning of *the same direction* goes away.

That was what the boy had built his whole new strength on: millions of little motions, turned to point the same way. But in a curved space there is no single *same way*. Carry an arrow from one end of a curved floor to the other along one road and it arrives pointing one way; carry the same arrow along a different road and it arrives pointing another. Neither is the true *same*, because there is none. On a curved floor, two arrows at two distant places cannot even be compared without a road between them, and every road gives a different answer. All the boy's gathered order, his millions of aligned motes, came apart in the space of a breath into millions of arrows that had each been aligned to a direction the room no longer agreed existed. The spiralling dust scattered. The rubble that had been trembling into line fell out of line. The long invisible currents in the air unwound and collapsed into ordinary eddies.

And the sheath. The loops the boy had been winding around his body to keep the Weight going round instead of in. A loop on a flat floor closes: walk round it carrying an arrow and you come back with the arrow pointing just as it pointed when you set out. A loop on a curved floor does not. Walk round it and you come home with the arrow turned, by just as much as the floor is curved inside the loop. Every circuit of the boy's sheath now came back to its starting place a little twisted. A twisted loop leaks. The coin that had been going round and round the counter, never reaching the drawer, now came round each time a little off its track, and a little more, and a little more, until it fell into the drawer by itself.

The Weight came in.

And with it, the tide.

That was the part Hiromi felt most clearly through the threads. It was the part of his Archon's law that had always frightened him a little when he imagined it. A body that does not fight the shape of the room falls through it freely, and feels nothing; a man falling from a tower feels no weight at all until the ground. But a body that accelerates against the shape, that drives itself off the straight line the room has for it, feels every bit of that driving as force, and in a strongly curved place the straight line is not the same for the head as for the heel, or for the left hand as for the right. Close to anything vast enough, a body's parts each want a slightly different road. The boy's whole art of movement was the art of making every part of his body go one way at once, ankle and knee and hip and spine all firing in a single line. Here, in Hiromi's room, there was no single line for all of them. Every time the boy drove his body as one coherent thing, the room pulled its parts apart along their different straight lines, and the more violently he drove, the more violently it pulled. To accelerate here at all was to go against the tide of the place, against laws he had himself helped lay into it by aligning so much of the room to his own direction, and the place answered every acceleration by taking his body by its separate parts and spinning them, shoulder from hip, hip from heel, as though he stood close to a mass of terrible density at the bottom of a spiral that had no floor.

The boy's earlier blows had already paid some of that price. Hiromi knew now why every one of them had come into him clipped. They had been thrown into a room that was only just beginning to take this shape, and the shape had taken its share off each one at the moment of contact, in the one place a blow is most coherent and most committed: the striking body.

Every blow from here on would cost more, and the room would see to it without Hiromi needing to watch. It reacted to motion now the way a pond reacts to a stone, everywhere at once, by itself.

Hiromi came forward.

He climbed out of the crater with his fists full of light and the threads of the room trailing from them, and as he walked, the shape of the space walked with him. The curve was centred on him. It went where he went. Every step he took toward the boy, the whole room leaned a little further toward the place where he was going, and anything in it that was not fighting the lean began, very gently, without any force acting on it at all, to fall his way. The loose stones on the floor began to slide toward his feet. The dust drifted down the curve to him and settled on his boots.

The boy was in the room.

He would feel the lean. He would feel that the straight road from where he stood now ran toward Hiromi; that if he did nothing at all, he would begin, very slowly, to fall down that road, and that nothing pushed him there and so there was nothing to turn. His art turned forces. Falling, in a shaped room, is the one motion in the world that has no force in it.

To stay where he stood, he would have to accelerate away. To strike, he would have to accelerate in. Either way, the room would answer.

Hiromi walked out onto the broken floor with his hands raised and the threads drawn tight, his throat raw, his belly knotted, his back one long ache, and a grin on his face as wide and bright as it had ever been.

"You're good at bodies!" he called. His voice cracked on it and he did not care. "Better than me. Much better! I'll learn it from you." He took another step, and the whole Crucible leaned with it. "But you keep looking for a way out of the room."

Another step. The stones slid. The dust fell toward him.

"There's no out. It's all room."

He tightened his fists on the threads.

"Of course."

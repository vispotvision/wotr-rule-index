## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Eight: The Inheritance

---

> *Of all the questions that were asked about him in that first winter, in the cedar halls of the Yukari and the white house of the Byakuya and the Hall of Verdicts itself, only one mattered, and nobody who asked it could answer it. Was the boy anything like his father? Could he be what the seven lines would need him to be, if he lived long enough to be anything at all?*
>
> *The answer is known now. It was not known then. The chroniclers of the north who came after, who had the whole of his life laid out before them like a road seen from a hill, would name him among the greatest kings the north has ever had, and some of them would name him the greatest. But that winter he was sixteen, and tokenless, and nobody's son that anybody would admit to, and he had a cut across his ribs that would not close, and a woodpile to see to.*
>
> *From the* Annals of the North-Forged Crown

---

### I · The Woodpile

He was chopping wood when Rob came for him.

He had been chopping wood for four days. The surgeon had told him not to. His sister had told him not to, in the voice she used for men on carts, and then in a worse voice, and then had stopped telling him and taken to standing at the edge of the yard with her arms folded watching him do it, which was worse than either. But there was a village at the bottom of the ridge under Kharven's Foot, forty houses in a fold of the hill where the Kessen road came down out of the pass, and the Tenrai had held the Foot over it for eleven years. Every winter of the eleven they had taken the village's wood for the garrison's fires. There were families in those forty houses who had spent the Thin Weeks breaking up their own furniture to burn.

So he chopped wood.

He did it one-handed, mostly. The left arm was still strapped across his chest under his shirt, where Shigure Tenrai's edge had come in under the mail and opened him from the armpit down across the ribs. It had been stitched twice. It had come open once. It hurt in a deep, dull, grinding way every time he breathed in, and in a bright, sharp way every time he swung the axe, and he had stopped noticing the difference between the two somewhere around the second day. He set the rounds on the block with his right hand, and took the axe up in his right hand, and brought it down, and the round split and fell, and he set up the next one. A full stack, against the wall of the old Tenrai barracks, as high as his shoulder and twice as long as a cart, every split face bright with pine resin that went gold where the low sun caught it. Then another. Then the boys from the vanguard who could still lift things carried it down the ridge on their backs, a load at a time, to the forty houses.

He had not planned it. He had simply looked at the village from the broken wall on the first morning after the battle, at the thin smoke from its chimneys going up straight and blue into a sky like hammered steel, and at the great stacks of seasoned pine the Tenrai had left behind in the fort's woodyard, more than the garrison could have burned in three winters, and gone down and picked up an axe.

By the third day, there were men from the village coming up the ridge to help him.

By the fourth, there were children. They could not lift the axe. They carried the split wood to the stack and laid it, carefully, the pale ends all outward, the way their grandfathers had laid it. An old woman with no teeth and a face like a winter apple had come up with them, wrapped in three shawls, madder red and moss green and a blue gone soft with washing, and had stood watching him split through half a morning, leaning on a stick. Then she had said, in the thick slow vowels of the northern hill country, *How's your stack, lad?* And when he had looked at her, not understanding, she had pointed at the long pale wall of split pine against the barracks with her stick and said it again. *How's your stack?*

"Full," he had said.

And she had laughed until she had to sit down on the block, and then she had told him that it was the oldest question in the north, older than the Moto, older than the roads: what you asked a man in winter to find out if his family would live till the spring. *A full stack speaks louder than a full mouth,* she had said. *Your father must have taught you that.*

He had not known what to say to that. He had gone on chopping.

He was splitting a round of old knotted pine, with the sweat running down inside his shirt and his breath going up white and glittering where the frost took it, and his left side singing at him, when Rob Stark came across the yard. Rob still walked with the stick, but he walked faster with it now. He stopped at the edge of the woodpile and waited for the axe to come down.

The round split. The halves fell.

"Lord Ulf wants you," Rob said. "And Lord Wystan. In the gatehouse." He paused. "Now, Sarge."

Muken leaned the axe against the block and straightened, slowly, with his good hand pressed flat against his ribs. Rob was looking at him. Rob's face was doing what Stark faces did, which was close up like a door. But it was closing up harder than usual, and his eyes would not quite meet Muken's.

At the edge of the yard, Yukazuri had unfolded her arms.

### II · The Gatehouse

The gatehouse of Kharven's Foot had been the Tenrai captain's quarters, and it still smelled of her: some sharp clean southern resin they burned in their lamps, and oiled lamellar, and ink. Shigure's table was still there, and her chair, and a rack on the wall where her long curved sword had hung. Somebody had taken the Tenrai banner down from over the hearth and put the Stark wolf up in its place, and the Greymane wolf beside it, and the two grey heads looked down from the wall at the room with their painted teeth bared and their painted eyes picked out in yellow, the brightest colour in the room after the fire.

Ulf Stark sat at the table. Wystan Greymane stood at the window with his back to the room, looking out at the broken wall and the ridge and the long road going down into the south. The wolfhound lay under the table with its head on its paws, and it lifted its head when Muken came in, and thumped its tail once on the floor, and put its head down again.

There was a letter on the table in front of Ulf. Grey wax. A gold seal, broken, the Tenrai heraldry on it still sharp enough to catch the firelight.

"Sit, lad," said Ulf.

Muken did not sit. He stood in the doorway with his hand pressed to his ribs and looked at the letter.

"You've heard the news," Ulf said. "Or you've heard something. You've a face like a man who's heard something."

"Rob's face," said Muken. "Not mine."

Ulf almost smiled. He put his maimed hand flat on the letter.

"We've been called back," he said. "Wystan and I. To Kharven's Seat. To the Hall of Verdicts. To answer to the council for the Foot." He turned the letter over with one finger, so that the broken gold seal lay uppermost. "It's the old summons. The one that binds every Warlord of the north to come and give account to the seven lines when the seven lines call. My grandfather answered it once. My brother answered it a month ago." He let the letter lie. "Kagenori's hand. And his own seal."

Muken stopped breathing.

"You can't go," he said.

It came out of him flat and quick, before he knew he was going to say it.

"You know what's waiting for you if you go back there. You *know*. Everybody in the north knows. They'll put you on your knees in that circle the way they did Torvald and they'll take your head off on the floor of it and send it home in a cask." His voice had begun to rise. "You can't go. Either of you. You can't."

"Nonsense, boy," said Ulf Stark.

He said it gently. That was the worst of it.

"Think it through," Ulf said. "Like you thought through the wall. If we don't go, what happens?" He did not wait. "Kagenori has his excuse. That's what the summons is for. It's not an invitation, lad, it's a door he's holding open. We walk through it, or he walks through ours. If the Warlords of Stark and Greymane refuse the old summons, the seven lines can call the north in open rebellion, under their own law, and come up the Kessen road with every house-guard they've got before the snow's off the passes. Before we've buried our dead. Before the Foot's walls are mended. Before the boys you led up that ridge have had time to learn which end of a spear's which." He spread his hands. "And we lose it all. Everything we bought with Torvald and the thirty at the breach. Gone by midsummer."

"Ulf's right, boy," said Wystan Greymane.

He had turned from the window. He stood with his long grey hands clasped behind his back and the white winter glare off the ridge behind him, and his long grey face was perfectly calm.

"Our lives," he said, "can be spent for the people. That's what they're for. That's what they've always been for." He looked at Muken steadily. "We're old men. We've had our years. We've had more of them than most of the men who've followed us. And the north doesn't need us any more, the way it needed us a month ago." He paused. "They have you now."

Muken stared at him.

"Why?" he said.

His voice cracked on it. He did not care.

"Why does anybody else have to die? We took the Foot. We *won*. Thirty of them are lying out there by the breach with their eyes closed, and the red-haired one with his spear in his hands, and we won. Isn't that enough? Isn't that *enough* for them?" He took a step into the room. "Why do they get to decide? Those six in their chairs. Why do they get to decide the fate of everybody else in the world? Who lives and who starves and who goes to the post. Why do you have to go and kneel on their floor and die like that, like... like *cattle,* on their script, because a man in a gold chair wrote you a letter?"

Neither of the old men answered at once.

Ulf looked down at his own hands on the table, the whole one and the maimed one. When he spoke, his voice was very quiet.

"That's just the way this life is, lad," he said. "That's all. There's no more to it than that." He was quiet for a moment. "We give. And we give. And then we give some more. And nobody promises us anything back for it, not the Sky, not the lines, not the people we give it for. We're not guaranteed a thing. Not a harvest. Not a spring. Not a good death in our beds with our grandchildren round us." He lifted his head. "We give anyway. Because the alternative is a world where nobody does. And I've seen what that world looks like, Muken. I rode in it with Gimbzo for two summers. I'll not leave it to my sons."

Muken shook his head. He could not stop shaking it.

"It's not right," he said. "It's not right. You shouldn't have to."

"No," Wystan agreed. "We shouldn't."

He came across the room. He was very tall, and he stopped in front of Muken and looked down at him from his great grey height, and then he put one long grey hand on Muken's good shoulder, the way he had at the foot of the ridge in the dark before the Foot.

"But we have to," he said. "And our duties aren't finished yet. Not quite."

---

Ulf Stark stood up.

He did it slowly, as if his knees hurt him, and they probably did. The camp chair creaked under him and then was silent. The wolfhound lifted its head. Ulf stood at the table with the broken letter in front of him and the Stark wolf on the wall over his head, and he put his maimed hand flat on his own chest, over his heart, and looked at Muken.

"As the Warlord of House Stark," he said, "of Hollenmark and the eastern March and the borders, by the right of my father and my father's father, and by the right of my brother Torvald, who held it before me and named me to it, and who died for it on the floor of their hall..."

His voice had dropped into an old cadence, slow and heavy, like stones being set one on another.

"I swear," said Ulf Stark, "that all the power of the house of Stark, and all its tools, its riders and its spears, its roads and its halls and its granaries, its debts and its oaths and its friends, are left to you, Muken. Muken the Tokenless. To hold for the north, until you see fit to put them down." He did not take his hand from his chest. "Before witnesses. Before the Sky."

Muken could not speak.

Wystan Greymane stepped back, a pace, so that he stood beside Ulf. He put his own long grey hand flat over his own heart.

"As the Warlord of House Greymane," he said, "of the Ridgewall and the western wall against Undaar-Keth, by the right of my fathers, who have held the ridge since before there was a north to hold it for..."

He did not raise his voice. He never did.

"I swear that all the power of the house of Greymane, and all its tools, its wall and its watch, its men and its wolves and its long memory, are left to you, Muken the Tokenless. To hold for the north. Until you see fit to put them down." He paused. "The ridge does not move. Before witnesses. Before the Sky."

The fire settled in the hearth. Out in the yard, faintly, someone was splitting wood: a child, by the uneven rhythm of it, striking three times for every split.

Then the door behind Muken banged open.

### III · The Sons

There were two of them. They came in out of the cold together, fast, with snow on their shoulders and their breath still going white, as if they had run the whole way from the stables.

The first was a Stark. Anyone would have known it from across a field. He was perhaps twenty-two, and built the way the Stark men were built, broad and heavy through the back and shoulders, but there was something less finished about him than about Rob or Ulf: as if the frame had been laid down and the weight not yet put on it. His hair was brown, cut short in the northern military style. His jaw was the Stark jaw, wide and square, and Muken saw it tighten on the left side, hard, before he said anything at all. He had deep brown eyes set under a heavy brow, and they went straight past Muken to the old man at the table.

The second was a Greymane, and he was grey already. His hair was black, long, tied back at the nape, and everything else about him was grey: his eyes, his cloak, the long narrow face, the stillness he had stopped in, just inside the door, as if he had walked into a wall. He was younger than the Stark, nineteen or twenty, and taller even than his father, and there were dark circles under his grey eyes as if he had not slept in a week.

"Lorn," said Ulf Stark. "Godfrey."

Lorn Stark looked at the broken letter on the table, and at his father's hand still flat on his chest, and his jaw tightened again on the left side.

Godfrey Greymane was looking only at his father.

"You can't do this," he said.

His voice was low and rough and shaking very slightly, and he did not seem to be able to stop it.

"Father. You can't. You can't just ride down there and... and *give* yourself to them. Like a sack of salt on a scale." His hands had closed into fists at his sides. "I've already lost Mother. I buried her in the autumn, on the ridge, with the frost already in the ground. I'm not burying you in a cask. I'm not..."

He stopped. He could not go on.

Wystan Greymane looked at his son across the room. Something moved in the long grey face that Muken had never seen there, and it was gone almost before he saw it.

"We are the keepers of the wall," Wystan said.

He said it quietly. He said it the way you say a thing to a child at night when they have woken from a bad dream, a thing you have said a thousand times before, the words worn smooth with saying.

"We are the shields of the innocent," he said. "We are the protectors of the realm. We stand on the ridge so that nobody behind us has to. That's what we are. That's all we've ever been. We are the Greymane." He held his son's eyes. "And it's our duty, Godfrey. It was my father's duty, and his father's. And when I'm gone, it'll be yours."

Godfrey said nothing. His fists were shaking.

"The ridge does not move," said Wystan Greymane softly.

His son's fists opened, slowly, a finger at a time.

Lorn Stark had not spoken. He stood just inside the door with the snow melting on his shoulders, looking at his father across the room. Muken saw him look. He saw the young Stark's jaw tighten, and work, and tighten again, and he saw him decide, somewhere in himself, that there was no argument he could make that his father had not already made to himself and answered.

"Why," said Lorn Stark.

It was barely a question. It was barely even a word.

Ulf looked at his son.

"You know why," he said. "You've known since Torvald." He let out a long breath through his nose. "Our generation has to come to an end, lad. It's had its time and it's spent it. Most of it badly. We lost the war, forty years of it, and buried our sons in it, and we held on through every one of those years out of nothing but stubbornness, and stubbornness won't take the north back. Something else will." He did not look at Muken; every man in the room knew where his eyes would have gone. "So we're putting it in your hands. Yours, and Godfrey's. And his." His voice grew rougher. "We entrust him to you, Lorn. Muken. Guide him. Stand beside him. Teach him what he doesn't know, and learn from him what you don't. And see that you win the battle against the Keld."

Lorn Stark was silent while the snow went to water on his shoulders.

Then he put his right hand flat on his chest, over his heart, the way his father had. He held it there. Then he closed it into a fist, slowly, against his breastbone, and pressed it there, hard, once. It was the Stark gesture. Muken had seen Rob do it, and Mark, and every Stark rider on the road to the Foot, before a fight or over a grave. It meant: *here. This. I hold it.*

"By my honour," said Lorn Stark.

---

Muken stood in the middle of the room and did not understand any of it.

He did not understand why they were looking at him. Ulf and Wystan, with their hands on their hearts. Lorn with his fist against his chest. Godfrey, grey-faced, with his fists finally open. All of them looking at him as if he were something, as if he were the answer to a question he had never heard asked. He was nothing. He was a cutting woman's boy from three galleries under Halcrest, with a scrap-iron sword and a sister and a dead token in his coat. He had nothing anybody could swear to. Nothing anybody could put their trust in.

And yet.

The commonfolk had been saying it since he was small. He had never paid it any mind. The old cutters in the Orrel galleries, watching him carry a full load up four levels at nine years old without stopping: *That one's going somewhere.* The women at the stamping floor, after he broke the steward's nose at thirteen: *He'll lead men, that one, you watch.* The Lanternless, in his first season, when he had taken a column through a burning village and brought it out the far side with every man in it: *Born to it, the little bastard. Born to it.* And the old woman with no teeth on the woodpile, four days ago: *Your father must have taught you that.*

He had thought it was just a thing people said. The kind of thing you said about any boy who was big for his age and didn't die.

Now two Warlords of the north were giving him their houses.

Wystan Greymane looked at him, and then at Ulf. Ulf looked at him. For a moment the old men only looked. Then Ulf's mouth moved in his braided beard, into something that was very nearly a smile and was not.

"We're sorry, lad," he said. "Truly. We'd have liked to see it. Where you go. What you become." His voice roughened. "We'd have liked to be there when you reach the top of whatever it is you're climbing. But we won't be."

"You'll have our sons," said Wystan Greymane.

He put his hand on Muken's shoulder one last time, and squeezed it, and let go.

Behind Muken, in the doorway, very quietly, someone drew a breath.

He did not turn round. He had known she was there since the door banged open. He had felt his own breathing slow down the moment the Stark and the Greymane came in out of the snow, the way it always did, without his ever wondering why. She had been standing in the cold of the doorway the whole time with her grey coat buttoned to the throat and her kit under her arm and her face doing nothing at all.

She had heard every word.

### IV · The Road South

They rode out the next morning, before light.

Two old men on two grey horses, with no escort and no banners, down the long road from the Foot into the south, under the last of the aurora: a slow green curtain over the ridge, fringed violet at its hem, that laid a cold sheen on the snow of the road in front of them and went on burning after they had passed out of sight. Ulf Stark had left his wolfhound tied in the gatehouse. It howled for an hour after he was gone, and nobody could make it stop, and in the end Muken went in and sat on the floor beside it with his back against Shigure Tenrai's table and his hand on the great rough head until it stopped. It lay down with its head on his boot. It did not get up again for the rest of the day.

Eleven days later, a crow came up the road from the south, and Rob Stark read the letter it carried, standing in the yard by the woodpile, and then folded it very small and put it inside his coat and went away and did not speak to anybody until the next morning.

Muken never asked to read it. Nobody did.

He went down to the village at the bottom of the ridge that afternoon and chopped wood until it was too dark to see the block, until the snow round him had gone the deep blue of the hour after sunset and the windows of the forty houses showed one by one the yellow of their lamps. The old woman with no teeth came and sat on a stump nearby, wrapped in her three shawls, and watched him, and kept her own counsel. When it was full dark she got up and went home. On her way past, she put her hand on his arm, very lightly, just for a moment, and left it there, and took it away.

### V · The Gall of Them

Nine days after that, a Greymane outrider came up the Kessen road at a gallop with his horse lathered to the hocks and told them what was coming up it behind him.

A party out of the south and east, riding hard, two days behind him on the road, maybe less. He gave the rest of it standing at Shigure's old table with the snow going to water on his shoulders, in the flat short way outriders gave things, as if every word had to be paid for out of his own breath.

"A lady," the outrider said. "Moto, by the veil. Silver on her. Twenty with her, no banners, and a Yukari woman walking barefoot in the snow like it wasn't there.

Muken heard it in the gatehouse, standing at the window where Wystan Greymane had stood, with Ulf Stark's wolfhound lying across his feet.

Something went up through him from the soles of his boots to the roots of his white hair, hot, and black, and very old, and he could not have said where it came from or what its name was.

"The *gall* of them," he said.

He said it low. Rob, and Lorn, and Godfrey Greymane, standing round the table with the outrider, all turned to look at him.

"A Moto lord," Muken said. "Coming *here*. To the Foot. A month after they took the head of the man who took it. And a Yukari with her." He laughed. It was not a good laugh. "Barefoot. As if the Moto couldn't come to us on their own, they've got to walk a Yukari up the road beside them, so we'll look at her bare feet in the snow and open the gate and think it's all been made right somewhere behind our backs while we were burying our dead." His hand had closed on the edge of the window frame until the knuckles went white. "What do they want? What can they possibly want from us that they haven't already *taken*?"

The outrider looked at his boots.

He could feel it rising in him. The warmth, the reinforcement, came down into his arms and legs in a fight; this came from somewhere older than that, and heavier, and it sat behind his left eye like a coal in a grate, and every time he thought of Ulf Stark riding down that road in the dark with his hand flat on his chest, it burned a little brighter. Ulf and Wystan. Torvald before them. The thirty at the breach. The red-haired boy with his spear. The forty houses at the bottom of the ridge breaking up their furniture to burn. Sixteen years of it. Eleven years of the Tenrai on that ridge. Forty years of war.

And now the Moto were coming up the road to make *peace.*

"You can't," said Godfrey Greymane.

Muken turned his head.

Godfrey was standing at the end of the table with his long grey hands flat on it, the way his father used to stand, and his grey eyes were on Muken's face. They had dark circles under them still. They would have them, Muken thought, for years.

"You can't show them that," Godfrey said. "What's in your face. Not when they come." His voice was low and steady and very tired. "They're coming to make peace. Whatever else they are. They're coming to *help*, if the outrider's right about the veil and who's wearing it. You can't meet them at the gate looking like that."

"Help us?"

Muken let go of the window frame. He turned round. The wolfhound lifted its head off his feet.

"*Help* us?" he said. "They've been saying that for sixteen years, Godfrey. They've been saying it my whole life. Every Moto lord who ever put his seal to a requisition in the north said it was to help us. Every tithe. Every closed road. Every granary they took for the house-guard. *For the good of the north. For order. For unity.*" He was not shouting. He had not raised his voice at all. "And the whole time they were saying it, we were dying. Of hunger. Of the cough. Of the cold, with no oil and no wood. The whole time." He took a step toward the table. "They executed your father, Godfrey. Not a month gone. On their floor. Do you not *feel* anything?"

The words hung in the room like breath in the cold.

Rob Stark had gone pale. Lorn Stark, at the other end of the table, had shut his eyes.

Godfrey Greymane looked at Muken. His long grey face did not change at all.

"Of course I feel it," he said.

His voice had dropped almost to nothing.

"I feel angry. I feel so angry I can't sleep. I lie awake every night on that cot in the barracks and I feel it, all night, and when the sun comes up I'm still feeling it." He did not take his hands off the table. "Don't you dare ask me if I feel anything. You didn't know him. You knew him a month. I knew him my whole life."

Muken said nothing.

"It was us," Godfrey said. "The Greymane. The Shieldwardens of the ridge. It was us they put on the front lines against the Zettari for forty years, us and the Starks, because we were northerners and northerners were cheap. We held the passes for the Moto with our own bodies while Gimbzo's lines sat behind us in the warm. We were used like a tool, Muken. Like an axe. Used up, and then thrown away when the handle split." His jaw set. "And then they took my father's head for it. So yes. I feel something."

He let that sit.

"But you have to remember your place," Godfrey Greymane said. "You have to. Because nobody else is going to remember it for you any more."

He straightened up from the table.

"You're a tokenless," he said. "You've no family. No house. No name. Nothing anybody in the south can see, nothing they'd sit across a table from. Even if my father named you lord before he rode out. Even with the Stark and the Greymane sworn to you on a dead man's word." He held Muken's eyes, grey on slate. "When that Moto lady comes up the road, she's going to look at you and see a sixteen-year-old sellsword with a scrap-iron sword and a bad temper. And so is the Yukari. And so is every man in the north who's watching to see what we do next." He paused. "So if you're going to be a king, Muken. If that's what my father died for. If that's what Ulf died for." His voice did not waver. "Then at least act like one."

The wolfhound had laid its head back down on Muken's boot.

Muken stood in the middle of the gatehouse of Kharven's Foot, under the two wolves on the wall, with the black hot thing still burning behind his left eye and a cut across his ribs that would not close, and looked at Godfrey Greymane while the light went red and long across the floor from the broken window.

He knew Godfrey was right.

He hated, more than he had ever hated anything in his life, that Godfrey was right.

He let out a long breath through his nose, slowly. It went out of him like smoke. And the coal behind his eye, very slowly, the way a fire goes down when you stop feeding it, began to dim.

"Two days," he said.

Godfrey said nothing.

"Two days, the outrider said." Muken looked down at the wolfhound on his boot, and then at the broken window, and the long road going down into the south where Ulf Stark had ridden away in the dark. "Then we've two days to mend the gate."

## Author Notes

- **Retcon 2026-10-20 (new-lore questionnaire).** The outrider's report loses the Zettari on the sabre-toothed cat, which is another player's to answer: he now reports a Moto lady by the veil, silver on her, twenty riders without banners and a Yukari woman walking barefoot, and Muken's anger and Godfrey's lines turn on the Yukari instead. Ulf's oath names Torvald as the brother who named him, and his "we lost the last war" is the one forty-year war.

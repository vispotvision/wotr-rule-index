## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Fifteen: The Work of Ruins

---

> *The chroniclers call it the Great War of the North, and date it from the morning on the Skeldmere. The northern clans date it differently. They say it began on the third day, on the river, when the ice brought the Kōkan's sister home.*
>
> *From the* Annals of the North-Forged Crown

---

### I · The Silver Chair

The Shirogane kept their house at Kharven-Seat on the western terrace below the Hall of Verdicts, where the rock fell away in a cliff of grey stone four hundred feet to the frozen river, and the wind came up the face of it and never stopped.

It was a house of silver. Everything in it that could be made of silver was made of silver, and what could not be had been washed with it, so that the long receiving room on the cliff side was a single cold gleam from end to end: the lamps in their silver cages along the walls, the silver thread worked into the grey silk of the hangings, the floor of pale grey stone polished until it held the lamplight like still water. At the far end, on a dais of three low steps, stood a chair of hammered silver with a back as high as a tall man. The lamps had been placed so that whoever sat in it was lit from both sides and from below, and anyone who stood before it was lit from nowhere.

Shizuha Shirogane was not sitting in it.

She stood at the foot of the dais with her hands clasped behind her back. A small woman. Grey-haired, and the grey cut short and severe to the line of the jaw. A narrow, upright body in a gown of silver-grey silk that fell from a high collar to the floor without a crease, worked down the front in silver thread so fine that it looked like frost on a window. She was past sixty. She stood as if she had never once in her life leaned on anything. Her eyes were grey, very pale, and the silver rings in them, the fine concentric lines of the seven lines' blood, were so bright and so closely set that in the lamplight her irises seemed to be made of coins.

Kagenori Tenrai Moto stood a pace behind her right shoulder, in gold-trimmed black, with his narrow face composed and his hands folded in his sleeves.

The doors at the far end opened, and Karogane came in.

He walked the length of the long silver room the way he walked everywhere, every step measured, his plain grey cloak still white at the hem with the road. He had ridden three days without stopping. There was frost in his hair. And behind him, between two of his grey men, they brought her.

They had not been gentle with her on the road. She could not walk. They half carried her and half dragged her the length of the polished floor with her toes trailing and her wrists bound in front of her with rawhide cord, and left a thin smeared line on the grey stone behind her that a servant in grey came forward and knelt and began, silently, to wipe away. Her face was swollen on the left side, the eye closed and purple. Her lip was split. Her sleeves were still rolled to the elbow as they had been on the shore of the Skeldmere, and her forearms were black with old blood to the wrist, some of it the wounded's and some of it her own, and her apron was gone. Her white hair, the Kōkan white, had come loose from its braid and hung across her face.

Karogane stopped at the foot of the dais and went down on one knee on the polished stone.

"Behold," he said.

His soft dry voice filled the silver room.

"Yukazuri," said Karogane. "Of the Moto." He let each word fall like a coin into a bowl. "The daughter of Gimbzo." He lifted his long pale face. "My queen."

The grey men pulled her forward. They did it hard, by the arms, so that her bound wrists jerked up and her knees went out from under her and she came down on them on the cold stone at Shizuha Shirogane's feet. Somebody took a fistful of her white hair and pulled her head back, so that the old woman could see her face.

Shizuha looked down at her.

She looked for a long time. Her hands stayed clasped behind her back. Her face did not change at all. The coin-bright rings in her pale eyes moved over the girl slowly, the way a factor's eyes move down a column of figures, the swollen eye and the split lip and the white hair and the black-blooded forearms, and back to the white hair, and stayed there.

"Ah," said Shizuha Shirogane.

Her voice was small and dry and perfectly clear, and it had in it the faintest edge of satisfaction, like the click of a lock.

"So this is the daughter of Gimbzo." She tilted her head a degree. "She has his hair. She has her mother's face, I think. I only saw the mother once, at a distance. A Byakuya guard. Very upright." Her eyes went back to the girl's. "She will make a worthy sacrifice. For the cause."

Behind her right shoulder, Kagenori Tenrai did not move. But his narrow face, which had been composed as still water since the doors opened, went still in a different way. A little too still. The hands in his sleeves had stopped moving.

"She must," said Shizuha. She did not look round. "You're wondering, Kagenori. I can hear you wondering. Why not keep her? A hostage. A lever. Hold her at the Seat and the boy will come to heel." She shook her head, very slightly. "No. The boy will never come to heel. He told the whole north at his woodpile that he would die for them. He meant it. A man who means that cannot be bought with his sister's life. He can only be broken with her death."

She turned her head a degree, toward Karogane on his knee.

"We have to show Muken how vicious we are," said Shizuha Shirogane. "Not tell him. Show him. We have to reach into his chest and take his heart out, and shatter it into pieces, and send the pieces back to him on the ice." Her voice did not rise at all. "Make him burn. Fill him with rage until there's no room in him for anything else. Revenge. Grief. Hate." She paused. "And that is how we will defeat him. It's simple. It always was."

"Simple," said Kagenori Tenrai, very quietly.

"Simple," said Shizuha. "We use the grief. We use the ambition that grief makes. He will want the Seat for her sake. He will want the Hall for her sake. He will want every one of us under the snow for her sake, and he will take the north apart with both hands to get it." She lifted her chin. "And he will fall, exactly as his father fell. I watched it happen once. Gimbzo was a man of some restraint, once. Before his mother died. Then the Tenrai put grief in his ear every night, and ambition with it, and he drank them both, and twenty years later there was no Kōkan left in the world but himself." Her pale coin-bright eyes moved back to the girl on the floor. "We were among the first reasons that war happened at all. The Shirogane. Did you know that, child? We wanted the war. We made very sure it came."

She smiled, very slightly.

"The Zettari are muck," said Shizuha Shirogane. "We needed the war to keep the Kōkan's eyes on the south, while we did the work in the north. And we still are what we always were. Whatever the Kōkan thought. Whatever your father thought, on his red chair that he never once sat in." The smile did not reach her eyes. "We are the ones who decide what happens. We are the controllers of fate."

Yukazuri lifted her head.

The grey man still had his fist in her hair. She lifted it anyway, against his grip, slowly, until the roots pulled and her one open eye, grey and very steady, came level with the old woman's.

She had not spoken since the shore. Not one word, for three days, on the road, in the black carriage, through everything they had done to her in it. Karogane had remarked on it. *She doesn't scream,* he had said to one of his men, on the second night, in a tone of mild professional interest. *I wonder whether she can't, or won't.*

She looked up at Shizuha Shirogane in her frost-worked silver gown at the foot of the silver chair, and gathered what was in her split mouth, and spat.

It landed on the silver-grey silk at the old woman's breast. Blood and spittle, in a long dark spatter across the frost-work.

Nobody in the long room moved.

"You'd better enjoy this," said Yukazuri.

Her voice was a croak. It was thin and cracked and it had no strength left in it at all, and it carried to the far end of the silver room as clearly as Karogane's had.

"This little bit of quiet," she said. "While it lasts. Enjoy it." Her open eye did not leave the old woman's. "My brother is going to kill every last one of you."

She did not say anything else.

She did not need to. She knelt there on the polished stone, at the foot of the silver dais, with the grey man's fist in her hair and her bound wrists in her lap and her black-blooded forearms bare, thirteen years old, and looked at the Queen of the Clans with one grey eye, and did not look away.

Shizuha Shirogane looked down at the blood on her breast.

She did not touch it. She looked at it for a long moment, the way she had looked at the girl, as if it were a figure in a column. Then she lifted her eyes past the girl to the man on one knee behind her, and inclined her head, a single degree.

Karogane rose.

He did it smoothly, without hurry, in one movement. His long pale hand went inside his grey cloak and came out with a sword. He had carried it all this way and nobody had ever seen it. A short straight blade, plain, without ornament, Tenrai steel with no gold on it anywhere.

Kagenori Tenrai said, "Shizuha..."

It was the first time anyone in that house had ever heard him say her name without her title.

Yukazuri did not look round. She did not close her eye. Her bound hands, in her lap, had moved. The right forefinger was tracing something on the back of the left, slowly, on the skin, over and over. A long down-stroke. A hook. A crossing. A small closed loop at the foot, like a knot in a rope.

Karogane cut once.

---

Yukazuri Moto, daughter of Kasane and Gimbzo, sister of the king, surgeon of the north, died on the third day of the Great War of the North, on the polished stone floor of the Shirogane house at Kharven-Seat, at the foot of a silver chair nobody was sitting in.

The servant in grey came forward with her cloth, and knelt, and began to wipe the floor.

Kagenori Tenrai Moto stood a pace behind his queen's right shoulder, with his narrow face quite white, and his hands in his sleeves, and said nothing at all. And Shizuha Shirogane, who had watched it without moving, without her hands coming from behind her back, looked down once more at the blood on her silk, and then at Karogane.

"The rivers," she said.

---

They sent her home on the ice.

The rivers of Kharven did not flow in winter. They froze through from bank to bank, and the frozen courses became the only roads in the north that never drifted shut, white ribbons winding through the dark forest and the snowfields from the high country down to the Skeldmere, and every sledge and every courier and every army in the north travelled on them from the first hard frost to the thaw. The Marrow ran from the cliff below Kharven-Seat north and east for three days' travel, and passed within sight of the walls of Kharven's Foot, and went on to the lake.

Karogane sent her down the Marrow on a sledge.

A small one, a dog-sledge, with no dogs. He had it pushed out from the foot of the Seat's cliff onto the river ice with a sail rigged on it of plain grey cloth, so that the south wind that came off the Seat's cliff and down the frozen river valley would carry it north. On the sledge he set a pike, upright, lashed to the frame. On the pike he set what he had taken in the silver room. He had wrapped nothing round it. He had left her hair loose, the Kōkan white, so that it would be seen.

The wind took the sledge north down the Marrow. It went for two days and a night, through the forest and out past the frozen meres, past Keld riders straggling home from the Skeldmere who stopped on the bank and stared and did not follow, past a Thornwall pike column marching south who saw it go by and stood in the snow and took off their iron caps. And on the morning of the third day after the battle, the Greymane watch on the river wall at Kharven's Foot saw something small and dark coming up the white ribbon of the Marrow from the south, with a grey sail on it, very slowly, the way a leaf comes down a stream.

---

### II · The Waiting

It was Godfrey Greymane who went down to the river.

He went alone. He did not let anyone come with him. He walked out of the river postern and down the bank onto the ice and stood there in the cold with his grey cloak blowing, and waited while the little grey-sailed sledge came up the frozen river toward him, slowing as the wind failed in the lee of the ridge, until it stopped, at last, of its own accord, twenty paces from where he stood.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he took off his cloak, and walked to the sledge, and did what had to be done with his long grey hands, carefully, so that nothing of her touched the ice, and wrapped her in the grey wool of the Greymane cloak, and carried her back up the bank in his arms.

Muken was at the postern.

Nobody had told him. Nobody had needed to. The Greymane on the river wall had seen the white hair from a mile off, and the word had gone round the Foot in the time it takes to breathe in, and by the time Godfrey came up the bank with the grey bundle in his arms, every soul in the fort who could stand was on the walls or in the yard or at the postern gate, silent, and the king of the north was standing in the gate.

He had come straight from the surgeons' line. He had been there three days, since the shore. He had not left it. He had worked it the way she had worked it, with the two Greymane women who could stitch and the Stark boy who could carry and Kiyome Byakuya, who could close a wound as well as anybody, kneeling in the rows of linen they had brought out from the Foot to replace the rows that had burned. He had not known what he was doing. He had done it anyway. He had held men down while Kiyome took arrowheads out of them, and boiled cloth, and fetched spirit, and sat with the ones who were going, the way she would have, until they went. He had not slept. His own ribs had been strapped by Kiyome on the first night with a long band of linen pulled so tight he could only breathe in the top of his chest, and his forearm sewn, badly, by one of the Greymane women while he held the lamp for her himself. There was blood on his hands that was not his. There was always blood on his hands now.

He stood in the gate and watched Godfrey come up the bank.

Godfrey stopped in front of him. He did not say anything. He did not know what to say. He stood there with the grey bundle in his arms, very straight, with the cold wind off the river taking his long black hair across his face, and held her.

Muken held out his arms.

Godfrey gave her to him.

It was very light. That was what Muken thought, afterward, when he could think anything. That it was so light. She had always been small, smaller than any girl her age in the Cuttings, because there had never been enough, and he had always given her the larger share and she had always found a way to give half of it back to him when he was not looking. And now it was so light he could have carried it in one arm.

He did not unwrap the cloak. He did not need to. A strand of white hair had come out at the top of it, and lay across the grey wool, and stirred in the wind.

He stood in the postern gate with it in his arms for a long time.

Then he turned round and carried her across the yard of Kharven's Foot, through the silent crowd, which drew back from him as he came like water from a stone, to the death-house.

---

The death-house at the Foot was a stone cell built into the north wall where the sun never reached, with a stone shelf along the back of it and no hearth. It was never heated. Every Kharven child knew why. *The cold keeps the dead quiet.*

He laid her on the stone shelf, in the Greymane cloak, and sat down on the floor beside her with his back against the wall.

He did not unwrap her. He did not speak. He sat with his knees drawn up and his strapped ribs grinding with every short breath and his arms across his knees and his bloody hands hanging, and looked at the grey bundle on the shelf, and did not move.

They came, after a while, to sit the Waiting with him.

He did not ask them. Nobody asked. It was not a thing that was asked. Lorn Stark came first, in his father's mail, and sat down on the cold floor of the death-house a little way along the wall from him, and drew up his knees, and said nothing. Then Godfrey, without his cloak, in his shirt in the bitter cold, who sat on Muken's other side. Then Kiyome Byakuya, in her near-white, with her face quite still and her eyes swollen, who knelt at the foot of the stone shelf with her hands folded and the white cord of her house's knot still hanging loose from her sword-hilt. Then Rob and Mark Stark, Mark with his arm strapped across his chest where the arrow had been. Then the two Greymane women from the surgeons' line, and the Stark boy who could carry, and the boy with the red ears from the vanguard, who sat in the doorway because there was no room left inside, and cried without any sound at all.

And then the old woman with no teeth, on two sticks, in her three shawls, who had to be helped over the threshold, and who did not sit, because she could not have got up again, but stood against the wall just inside the door with both hands on her sticks, and looked at the grey bundle on the shelf with her toothless mouth set in a long hard line.

They sat the Waiting.

That was all the Waiting was. Kharven people did not wail for their dead. They did not sing. They did not pray to anyone, because the Sky did not need to be told. They came into the cold room where the dead lay and sat with them in silence, so that the dead would not be alone in the cold, and so that the living would not be alone with the dead. A full night, from dark to dark. Sometimes more. Nobody spoke. Nobody ate. Nobody left, except to relieve themselves, and then they came back.

They sat with her all that day, and all that night, and into the grey of the next morning.

Muken did not speak once.

Not to Lorn, beside him, whose shoulder was pressed against his in the cold. Not to Godfrey on his other side, shivering in his shirt. Not to Kiyome, who at some point in the night reached out one long white hand and laid it on the back of his bloody one where it hung off his knee, and left it there, and he did not move his hand away and did not turn it over to take hers. He sat with his back against the stone and his eyes on the grey bundle on the shelf and his face quite still, and nothing came out of him at all.

Lorn knew that face. He had seen it in the long room, and at the woodpile, and on the Seat road with the salve tin in his fist.

It frightened him worse each time.

### III · The Yard at Night

On the second night he got up.

He did it all at once, in the black hour before the grey, without a word. He stood up from the floor of the death-house, and stood for a moment looking at the grey bundle on the shelf, and then he walked out of the door past the boy with the red ears asleep on the threshold, and out into the yard.

Lorn started to get up after him. Godfrey's hand on his arm stopped him.

"Leave him," said Godfrey quietly. It was the first word anyone had spoken in the death-house in thirty hours.

"He'll do something."

"I know." Godfrey did not take his hand off Lorn's arm. "Let him."

---

He trained.

He did it in the middle of the yard, in the snow, by the light of a single fat-lamp on the woodpile, with the Greymane watch on the walls above him and the wind coming over the curtain wall off the high country. He had no sword. The scrap-iron sword lay in the Foot's smithy in three pieces on the anvil, where the Stark armourer had laid it after Lorn carried the pieces back from the shore. He had looked at it, the first night, and gone away. So he took a practice blade from the rack by the barracks door, a Greymane drill-sword of heavy iron with no edge and a weighted pommel, made to build the arm, twice the weight of a real one. And he went out into the middle of the yard with it.

And he began to cut.

Not drill. Not forms. Not anything Yusif had ever taught him in the back of a worked-out gallery with a stick of ash. He cut the way a man cuts wood when he means to split the whole stack before morning. Down, from the right shoulder, with everything he had. Up, from the left hip. Across. Down again. Over and over, at nothing, at the snow and the dark and the white wind, with the heavy iron going round his head and his strapped ribs screaming at every stroke so that he could only breathe in short snarling gasps through his teeth.

He cut until the stitches in his forearm tore and the blood ran down his wrist inside his sleeve and made the grip slick, and then he wrapped the grip in a strip of linen torn from the bottom of his shirt, and went on cutting.

He cut until the sun came up grey over the curtain wall, and the Greymane watch changed, and the new watch looked down at him from the walls and looked at each other and said nothing.

He cut until noon.

Nobody stopped him. Lorn stood in the door of the barracks with his arms folded and his jaw working on the left side and watched him, hour after hour. Godfrey stood beside him for a while, and went away, and came back. Kiyome did not come out at all. Once, at mid-morning, the old woman with no teeth came out of the barracks on her two sticks and stood by the woodpile and watched him for a long time with her toothless mouth set, and then shook her head, very slowly, and went back in.

He was not getting better. That was what Lorn saw, from the door, and what frightened him most. A man who drills gets better. He finds the line of the stroke and grooves it and the stroke gets cleaner and faster and costs less. Muken was getting worse. Every cut was harder than the last and wilder, more of the shoulder in it and less of the hips, the heavy iron going wide of the line and coming back late, and every time it went wide he cut harder to pull it back, and it went wider. He was not cutting at anything. He was cutting at everything. And every stroke he cut, the gold seam in his left eye, which Lorn could see from twenty paces off now in the grey light, burned a little brighter, and spread a little further across the grey, and the snow round his feet in the middle of the yard had begun, very faintly, to crack.

It was cracking, in fine branching lines, like the glaze on an old pot. Spreading out from where he stood across the trampled snow of the yard toward the woodpile and the barracks wall, a little further with every stroke.

"Godfrey," said Lorn quietly. "Look at the snow."

Godfrey looked. He did not answer.

At noon, the air beside Muken changed.

---

### IV · The Bound Flame, Standing

There was no light. There was no sound. Nobody on the walls saw anything come. But the snow-light in the middle of the yard went warm, the way the air goes warm in a room when someone has opened a stove door, and the cracks spreading across the snow round Muken's feet stopped where they were.

And there was someone standing beside him.

Lorn Stark, in the barracks door, saw a figure. He said so afterward, and Godfrey said so too, and neither of them could ever say more. A tall figure, very tall, taller than the Zettari, standing in the snow a pace to the king's left, made of the same flat grey noon light as everything else in the yard but somehow more of it, so that it was hard to look at directly, like the sun through cloud. They could not see its face. They could not see its feet. They thought, both of them, separately, that it had wings, or had had them once, and that the wings were broken.

Muken did not stop cutting.

"You train with such anger," said Faisal.

The voice was the quietest thing in the yard. It was the voice from the place made of threads, low and warm and very far away and very near. Every man on the walls of Kharven's Foot heard it, and not one of them could have said afterward what language it spoke.

"Such viciousness." The broken light did not move. "Do you think you can win like this?"

The heavy iron went round Muken's head and came down into the snow with a sound like an axe into a stump.

"When you have not yet understood the reason she died," said Faisal. "When you have not yet unlocked the truth of what you are."

Muken stopped.

He stopped with the drill-sword buried a hand's depth in the trampled snow, his bloody hands on the linen-wrapped grip, his strapped ribs heaving, his breath going up off him in short white bursts. He did not lift his head. He stood bent over the sword like a man bent over a plough, and the gold in his left eye burned.

"What do you know?"

It came out of him low and cracked. It was the first thing he had said in two days.

"What do *you* know about it?" He straightened. He turned, with the heavy iron coming up out of the snow in his hands, and looked at the tall broken light beside him with the gold eye blazing. "You're an angel. A sixth of one. Sent down here to fix the world. Something that sits in a place made of threads and thinks it knows everything because it can see the strings." His voice rose. "That's what the Moto think. Did you know that? Every one of them. They think they're the ones who hold the strings. That's what *she* thought, whoever she is in her silver chair, when she had my sister's head cut off and sent down the river on a pike for me to find." He was shouting now. "You're the same! You're exactly the same as the ones who killed her!"

The yard was very quiet.

Faisal put his hands behind his back.

It was a small movement. The broken light simply shifted, at the place where its arms would be, and drew them back, and clasped them there, and stood. And something in the way it stood made Lorn Stark, in the barracks door, go cold all down his back, though he could not have said why, and never knew. He had never been in the Shirogane house at Kharven-Seat. He had never seen Shizuha Shirogane stand at the foot of her silver dais with her hands clasped behind her back, looking down at a kneeling girl.

The Bound Flame looked at the king of the north for a long time.

"Such obsession," said Faisal at last. "Such malice."

The light did not grow brighter. It grew heavier. Every man on the walls felt it, an urge rising in his throat to say a true thing, any true thing, to confess. A Greymane spearman on the north wall found himself whispering the name of a woman in Ridgewall he had never told anyone about.

"You think you can turn this war with it," said Faisal. "You think you can take what you feel and pour it into the Work and ride it like a flood down a river, and drown them all." The bright places where its eyes would be did not leave Muken's face. "Look at the snow under your feet."

Muken did not look.

"Look," said Faisal.

Muken looked.

The snow of the yard round his boots was crazed across like an old pot, in fine branching lines, spreading from where he stood toward the woodpile and the barracks and the curtain wall, and where the lines ran, the snow had not melted or broken. It had cracked, the way glass cracks, in a world where snow did not crack. And at the very edge of the crazing, where it had reached the foot of the woodpile, the pale split ends of the nearest logs had cracked too, in the same fine pattern, though nothing had touched them.

"That is what you are doing," said Faisal. "Without meaning to. Without knowing how. You are taking the flow of the world, the tide of it, the Work that runs through everything, and you are corrupting it. Fouling it at the root. You think you are using it. It is using you." The light did not waver. "Another day of this, and it will be the wall."

Muken stared at the cracked snow.

"So," said Faisal. "You want the power to destroy your enemies."

It was not a question. The light shifted. The hands came out from behind its back.

"Then you have to learn what you truly are first."

---

And the world went white.

It did not fade. It did not dissolve. It was simply gone, between one breath and the next, the yard and the walls and the woodpile and the men in the doorway and the grey noon sky, and in its place there was nothing but white. A white with no edge and no depth and no horizon, like the lifting on the Skeldmere but more complete than any lifting, so white that Muken could not have said whether he was standing in it or floating, or whether there was anything under his feet at all.

The drill-sword was gone from his hands.

Faisal was in front of him. Standing upright, where in the place made of threads he had knelt: tall, the broken light gathered into the shape of a figure with the seam running down it and the ruined wings standing from its shoulders, and for the first time Muken understood how terribly tall it was, and how terribly much of it was missing.

"Hold still," said Faisal.

He put out one hand, open, and laid the flat of it on Muken's chest. Over the strapped ribs. Over the heart.

It did not hurt. It was warm. The warmth went into him through the linen and the skin and the bone like heat going into iron in a forge, steady and low, and it found something in him under the breastbone where the black hot thing lived, and took hold of it, gently, the way a hand takes hold of a bird.

And pulled.

Muken came out of himself.

He felt it happen. He felt himself come loose from his own body the way a man's foot comes loose from a boot stuck in a drift: a long dragging suck, and then a sudden lightness, and then nothing holding him at all. He was standing in the white beside himself. He could see himself. His own body, standing with its bloody hands empty at its sides and its chest strapped under the dead sergeant's coat and its white hair loose and its face, his face, quite still, the gold eye open and staring at nothing.

And then the white began to come apart.

It came apart into threads. The same threads he had seen in the place where Faisal knelt, fine as hair, gold and silver and black, but now they were not running past him and around him in the dark. They were running through him. Through the thing he was now, outside his body, the thing that had come loose. They ran into him from every side of the white, from the whole of the world, more of them than there were snowflakes on the Skeldmere, and every one of them was humming at its own note, and every one of them was beginning, slowly, to dissolve.

They were melting. Unravelling. Coming apart at the ends into fine gold dust that drifted in the white like sparks over a forge. And where they came apart, he could feel heat.

It was the heat a wire gives off when it is bent back and forth in the same place until it breaks: the heat of strain. Of fatigue. Of something carrying more than it was ever made to carry, for longer than it was ever made to carry it, and beginning to fail.

He could feel the cracks.

All of them. Every crack in the world. Every flaw in every stone of every wall of every fortress in the north. Every hairline fault in every blade and every bone. Every place a beam had sagged under a roof, every place a river's ice was thin, every place an oath had been sworn by someone who did not mean it. Every lie. Every betrayal waiting to happen. Every seam in every host where one clan did not trust the next. He could feel them all at once, humming, hot, straining, the whole world crazed across like the glaze on an old pot, holding together only because nothing had yet come to strike it at its note.

And in the middle of it, in the heart of the white, where the threads ran thickest through the thing he was now, he could feel his own crack.

The black hot thing under his breastbone. It was a crack. It always had been. It ran through the middle of him from the cellars at Halcrest to the silver room at Kharven-Seat, and it was humming, louder than any other crack in the world, and the note it was humming was the note of the snow in the yard, and the woodpile, and the wall.

*Hibiware,* he thought. He did not know where the word came from. *The crazing.*

"Yes," said Faisal.

He was standing in the white among the dissolving threads with his hands behind his back once more, and the bright places where his eyes would be were very bright.

"That is Hibiware," he said. "The second waking of the blade your house calls Asami. You touched the first of it on the ice, with the iron hand: one crack, one note, one thing opened. Hibi. This is what lies under it. Every crack at once, and every note, and the whole world ringing." The light dimmed slightly. "You have been touching it these two days in the yard without knowing. That is why the snow broke."

"Asami's in pieces," said Muken. His voice was strange here. It did not seem to come from anywhere. "On an anvil. In the Foot."

"Asami is the word a blade of your house answers to while it sleeps," said Faisal. "The steel only carried it. It sleeps in the pieces now. It will answer when you call it." The broken wing shifted. "Not yet."

He was quiet a moment.

"Hataraki," said Faisal.

The word went through the white like a bell.

"That is the name of what lies under the blade and the eye. Hataraki. The Work. The labour reality performs, through the ones who have become part of its order." The bright places held him. "It is the power to govern the workings of all things. To command every possibility and every likelihood. Every cause and every consequence that comes of it. To reach down to the meaning of a thing, what it is for, why it holds together and why it should not, and change it." He paused. "Your line was made with it, in the beginning. Before the seven. Before the Moto had a name. The Kōkan were given Hataraki by the Head of Fate itself, the one who wrote the Works before there was time to write them in, and you, child, were given more of it than any of them. Since the first." The light pulsed, once. "Your potential is boundless. I have seen the threads that run through you. They do not end."

Muken said nothing.

"And of all the Works," said Faisal, "the one your blood leans toward is the oldest and the hardest. *Hametsu no Gō.* The Work of Ruins. The power to make things end. To shape ruin, and cast it out in your wake, wherever you walk. Every fortress. Every host. Every throne." He let it sit. "You could end the Shirogane. You could end the Seat. You could walk from here to the Hall of Verdicts and leave nothing standing behind you but cracked stone and ash, and I do not think any of them could stop you, in the end, if you were willing to pay what it cost."

The threads hummed.

"However," said Faisal.

The light did not move. But the white round them went, very slightly, cold.

"Your anger is pushing you," he said. "Hear me now. This is the thing you must hear, or nothing else I teach you matters. Every Work has two faces. When a Work runs through recognition, through what the old law calls Attraction, it holds. It keeps. It binds through acknowledgment and lets go when the binding is done. That is ruin as the world needs it: the lawful ending of what can no longer stand. The severing of a rot. The breaking of a false oath. The fall of a house that has become a cage." His voice did not rise. "But when a Work runs through obsession, it does not let go. It clutches. It repeats. It fixes on its object and narrows and narrows until the only thing it can see is the thing it wants to end. Ruin, through obsession, does not stop. It cannot stop. It ends what was rotten, and then it ends what was near the rot, and then it ends what was near that, and it goes on ending until there is nothing left to end but itself."

He looked at the boy.

"Your father," said Faisal, "went down into the cellars at Halcrest with a lamp."

Muken went very still.

"You asked me, in the yard," said the Bound Flame, quite gently, "what I know about the reason she died." The bright places did not leave his face. "I know this much. I know that whoever sent her home to you on that river did not do it because she was dangerous. A girl of thirteen with a needle. She did it because you are. And she did not want you dead. She could have had you dead. She had men on that shore with bows." The light was very still. "She wanted you like this. Standing in the snow for two days, cutting at nothing until the world cracks under your feet. She wanted you to take that grief and that hate into the Work and let it run, and let it run, until you were what your father was, and the north saw it, and turned from you, and you fell. As he fell." A pause. "She told her court as much. I did not hear her say it. I do not need to have heard it. It is written in every thread that ran out of that silver room."

Muken did not move. In the white, outside himself, he found he could not.

"That is the reason she died," said Faisal. "To make you your father. Will you let it?"

For a long time there was no answer.

Then, very low:

"How do you know what she was like? You never knew her."

"No," said Faisal. "But I heard the last thing she said."

The white went quiet.

"I hear oaths," said the Bound Flame. "I told you. Every oath sworn on honour, even now, even as I am. And she swore one. At the end. On her knees on a stone floor, with her hands bound, looking at the woman who had ordered her death." The light warmed, very slightly. "She said: *My brother is going to kill every last one of you.* And while she said it, with the forefinger of one bound hand, on the back of the other, she drew your house's mark. Over and over. Until the end."

Muken made a sound.

It came from somewhere so far down in him that the thing he was, outside his body, in the white, bent over with it as though struck, and the threads running through him shuddered, every one, and somewhere very far away in the yard at Kharven's Foot the crazing in the snow round his empty body jumped a hand's width toward the wall.

"She swore it for you," said Faisal. "She believed it. Every word of it was true, as far as she could know. That is why I heard it." The light did not falter. "But hear me, Muken Moto. She swore what she believed you would do. What you do is yours. She cannot make you keep an oath that you never swore." A pause. "And she was a surgeon. She would have known the difference between cutting out a rot and cutting out a heart."

### V · The First Lesson

"Show me," said Muken.

He was still bent over in the white. He did not straighten. His voice came out of him raw and low and quite steady.

"You said you'd teach me. Teach me. Show me the difference."

Faisal looked at him a long moment. Then the hands came out from behind his back, and he lifted one of them, and the white in front of them changed.

There were things in it now. Floating, at the height of a man's chest, in a long uneven row that ran off into the white further than Muken could see. A stone. A bowl. A blade. A branch with the bark on it. A bone. A rope. A brick from a wall. A cup. Each of them hung alone in the white, and each of them was humming at its own note, and through each of them, if he looked, he could see the threads, and the cracks in them, gold and fine as hair.

Some of the cracks were small. Some were deep. And some of the things in the row, a few of them, scattered along it with no pattern he could see, were rotten. He could feel it. The rope was rotten through, the strands gone grey and sodden at the core, holding nothing. The bowl had a flaw in its clay that ran from the lip to the foot and would split the first time anyone poured hot broth in it and scald whoever held it. The bone was diseased, honeycombed through with something that was eating it from inside. The brick was the one brick in a wall that would give, and bring the wall down on whoever stood under it.

"End the rotten ones," said Faisal. "Only the rotten ones."

Muken looked at the row.

The black hot thing was already up. It had come up the moment he saw the rotten rope, hot and sudden, under his breastbone, behind his left eye, and through it all the cracks in the row went bright at once. Every one. Every flaw in every thing. The rotten ones and the sound ones alike, all humming, all straining, all asking to be broken. He looked at them through the gold and thought of the silver room and a hand in his sister's hair, and he reached.

He did not know how he reached. He had no body here and no blade. He reached with whatever it was in him that read cracks, and found their note, and the black hot thing came up through the reaching like water up a well, and he struck.

The whole row cracked.

All of it. Every stone and bowl and branch and blade, the rotten and the sound, crazed across at once in fine branching lines, and came apart, and fell in pieces into the white with a sound like a cartload of crockery going off a bridge. The rope. The bowl. The branch that had been sound and green. The cup that had had nothing wrong with it at all except one hairline fault near the rim that would have lasted it a hundred years.

And Faisal flickered.

The broken light standing beside him went dim all at once, as if a wind had blown through it, and the long dark seam down the middle of it widened, and one of the ruined wings shuddered, and a little of the warm light came away from its edge and drifted off into the white like sparks off a fire, and did not come back.

Muken stared at it.

"You did that," said Faisal, quite calmly. "You were aiming at the rope."

"I..." Muken stopped.

"I am a crack too," said the Bound Flame. "One sixth of something that was made whole. Everything I am is a seam where the rest of me was cut away." The light steadied, slowly, dimmer than before. "Obsession does not choose what it ends. That is the whole of what I needed you to see. Now you have seen it."

Muken looked at the broken things in the white. At the sound green branch lying in pieces beside the rotten rope. At the dimmed light beside him that had knelt under the weight of the whole world for longer than the north had had a name, and had come down out of the place where the threads run to stand beside him in a yard in the snow, and that he had just hurt.

He thought of Yukazuri.

He did not want to. He tried not to. But she came anyway, her hands before her face: her small sure hands at the surgeons' line under the Foot, with her sleeves rolled up and the lamp beside her, bent over a Greymane boy with a split scalp, while the boy screamed. Her needle going in. Going out. Drawing the edges together. One stitch, and the next. Never more than she needed. Never once cutting what could be closed.

*You don't cut what you can sew,* she had said to him once, at the Veyrath ditch, with blood to her elbows, when a sergeant had wanted to take a man's arm off because it was quicker. *You don't sew what has to come off. You look. You find where it's dead. You take that, and nothing else. And then you close what's left, and let it live.*

He had been fourteen. He had not understood a word of it, and remembered every one.

"Again," said Faisal.

The row was whole again. Every stone, every bowl, every branch and bone and brick, hanging in the white at the height of a man's chest, humming. The rotten rope. The flawed bowl. The diseased bone. The brick that would bring the wall down.

Muken looked at them.

He let the black hot thing come up. He could not stop it. It came. But this time he did not reach with it. He held it, the way you hold a horse that wants to bolt, with all his weight back on the reins, and let it stand there under his breastbone, burning, and did not let it go. And he looked at the row through the gold, and through the burning, and he did what she had done. He looked.

He looked for where it was dead.

The cracks were all there. All of them. Every flaw in every thing, gold and humming. But now, looking, he could see that they were not all the same. The cracks in the sound things were fine and shallow and the note they hummed was steady, a note of strain held and carried, the way a good beam hums under a roof. The cracks in the rotten things were different. They were deep, and they branched in on themselves, and the note they hummed was sour and wavering, a note of something that had stopped carrying and was only pretending. He could hear it. He could hear the difference.

He found the rope's note.

He held it. Only that one. Only the sour wavering note of the rotten rope, and nothing else in the row, and he held it the way she held a needle, with the whole of his attention narrowed to its point. And he did not strike.

He waited.

The rope came apart.

It came apart along its own rot. Quietly. The grey sodden strands at its core simply let go of each other, one after another, the way the iron hand had let go at the rivet, and it fell in two pieces into the white. And beside it, the green branch hung whole and humming, and the cup with its hairline fault near the rim hung whole beside that, and not one crack in either of them had moved.

Muken let out a breath he had not known he was holding.

"Again," said Faisal.

He did the bowl. It took longer. The black hot thing surged when he found the bowl's note, because the bowl was a bowl and his sister had eaten out of bowls like it every day of her life, and for a moment the crazing ran out from the bowl into the stone beside it before he could pull it back. The stone cracked, a hairline across one face. He stopped. He breathed. He found the bowl's note again and held only that, and waited, and the bowl split from its lip to its foot along the old flaw in its clay, and fell.

"Again," said Faisal.

The bone. The brick. He did them both. The bone was the hardest, because the rot in it was everywhere, honeycombed through, and there was no single crack to hold, only a hundred of them, and he had to find the note that was under all of them and hold that. It took him a very long time. But the bone went, at the end, to grey powder that drifted off into the white, and the clean bone hanging beside it did not crack at all.

Then he stood in the white, outside himself, among the broken rotten things and the whole sound ones, and shook.

"That," said Faisal, "is the Work of Ruins."

His light had steadied. It was still dimmer than it had been. It would be, Muken thought, for a long time.

"Through recognition," said the Bound Flame. "Through the law that lets go. You found what was already dead, and you ended that, and nothing else, and you let the rest live. That is lawful ending. That is ruin as the world needs it." The bright places where his eyes would be held the boy. "Do it to a rope, and you are a sailor. Do it to a bone, and you are a surgeon. Do it to a wall, and you are a soldier. Do it to a host, and you are a general." A pause. "Do it to a throne, and you are a king."

Muken said nothing. He was still shaking.

"Your sister knew it," said Faisal. "Long before you did. She could not have told you its name. But she knew." The light warmed, slightly. "It seems the Work runs in more of your blood than the Head of Fate intended."

"Is that all?"

It came out of him in a whisper.

"That is the first day," said Faisal. "There will be others. Hibi you have, now: one crack, one note, one thing opened, and only what is already dead. Hibiware you have touched, and you will not touch it again until you can do what you just did to a rope to every rope in a city at once and leave every sound one standing. That will take you years. Perhaps more." He was quiet a moment. "And there is a third waking under Hibiware, and I will not speak of it. Not to you. Not yet."

"And Hataraki?"

"Hataraki is what you are," said Faisal. "It does not wake. It was never asleep. You have been doing it since you were nine years old, reading the cracks in a factor's ledger at the top of a gallery shaft." The light shifted. "What wakes is you."

The white began to grey at the edges.

"Go back," said Faisal. "They are frightened for you in the yard. The Stark has not moved from that door in a day and a half. And your sister is still lying in the cold, and the Waiting is not finished." The light dimmed further. "I will come again. Not every day. When you need it, and when I can. One sixth of me does not go far." A pause. "Practise on rope. Not on men. Not yet."

"Faisal," said Muken.

The broken light stopped dimming.

"I'm sorry," said Muken. "For what I said. In the yard." He swallowed, in a throat he did not have. "And for the wing."

The Bound Flame looked at him for a long time across the greying white.

"Swear by your honour," said Faisal, "as the Stark taught you. And keep it. I will mend."

And the white went away.

### VI · The Yard at Noon

He was standing in the middle of the yard of Kharven's Foot with the drill-sword in his hands, buried a hand's depth in the snow.

The grey noon light. The wind off the high country. The woodpile and the barracks wall and the Greymane watch on the curtain wall above, staring down at him. Lorn Stark in the barracks door with his arms unfolded now and his whole body leaning forward like a man about to run. Godfrey beside him.

No time had passed. The sun had not moved.

Muken looked down at the snow round his feet.

The crazing was still there. The fine branching cracks across the trampled snow, spreading from where he stood to the foot of the woodpile, where the ends of the nearest logs were cracked in the same pattern. But it had stopped. It was not spreading any more. And as he looked at it, he found he could hear it: the sour, wavering, unhappy note of snow that had been made to crack in a world where snow did not crack, humming faintly, waiting.

He found the note. He held it. He did not strike.

And very slowly, the way frost goes off a window when the sun comes round to it, the cracks in the snow closed. The logs stayed cracked. But the snow of the yard, round his feet, drew itself back together along its fine branching lines, one after another, from the edges inward, until it was only snow again, trampled and grey and whole, and the note went quiet.

On the wall above him, a Greymane spearman said, very softly, "Sky above."

Muken pulled the drill-sword out of the snow.

He looked at it a moment, the heavy iron with no edge, the linen on the grip gone black with his blood. Then he turned and walked across the yard to the barracks door, and stopped in front of Lorn and Godfrey, and held it out, hilt first, to Godfrey.

Godfrey took it.

"Lorn," said Muken.

His voice was hoarse. It was the first thing he had said to either of them in two days, and it was very quiet, and it was his own.

Lorn Stark did not say anything. He could not.

"Wake the smith," said Muken. "Tell him I need the pieces of the sword. All three. And a coil of rope." He paused. "Old rope. The rottenest he's got."

Lorn stared at him.

"Then come and sit with me," said Muken. "Both of you." He turned his head toward the death-house in the north wall, where the door stood open on the cold and the grey bundle lay on the stone shelf with the old woman standing guard over it on her two sticks. "She's been alone in there long enough. The Waiting's not done."

He walked back across the yard toward the death-house, slowly, with his strapped ribs grinding and his torn forearm bleeding into his sleeve, and the wolfhound came out of the shadow of the woodpile where it had lain for two days watching him, and fell in at his side.

Lorn Stark watched him go. Then he looked at Godfrey.

"What," he said, "in the Sky's name was that?"

Godfrey Greymane looked down at the drill-sword in his hands. Then at the snow in the middle of the yard, whole and grey and trampled, where an hour ago it had been crazed across like old glaze.

"I don't know," he said. "But my father told me once that the Kōkan kings could make the world hold its breath." He handed the drill-sword to the nearest Greymane on the barracks step, and turned toward the death-house. "I think they can teach it to breathe out as well."

He went after the king. After a moment, Lorn went with him.

And in the cold of the death-house, through the rest of that day and all the long night after, they sat the Waiting out to its end, the three of them, side by side, against the stone, with the old woman on her sticks by the door, and the king of the north did not speak again until morning. But his hands, lying open on his knees in the cold, were very still, and the gold in his left eye had gone quiet as a banked fire.

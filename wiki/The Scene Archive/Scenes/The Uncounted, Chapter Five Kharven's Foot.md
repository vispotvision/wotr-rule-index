## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Five: Kharven's Foot

---

The night before the assault, the two old men sat up late in the command tent with a jug between them and the lamp turned low, and talked about the boy.

The tent was Greymane, and it smelled of it: oiled canvas and wet wolfskin and the iron-and-tallow stink of armour that had been greased against the cold and worn for a week. A brazier of charcoal stood in the middle of it, ticking as it cooled. A map was pinned to a trestle under the lamp, weighted at the corners with a dagger, a helm, a horn cup and a stone, and on it in charcoal someone had drawn the long road climbing out of the low country, and the ridge, and the squat square shape at the top of the ridge where the road went through a gate. Beside the shape, in Wystan Greymane's cramped hand, three words: *Kharven's Foot. Two hundred.*

Wystan sat with his long legs stretched out toward the brazier and his boots off, flexing his toes in their wool. He was sixty-one, and long, and grey all through: grey hair cropped short, grey beard cut close to the jaw, grey eyes set deep in a long grey Greymane face that had been carved, it seemed, for weathering and nothing else. He had the stillness that the Ridgewall men had, the stillness of men who spent their lives standing on a wall looking west for something that might come, and he had a way of not moving at all for a long time and then moving very fast. On the breast of the surcoat that hung over the back of his chair, worked in darker thread, was the wolf's head of his house. On the table at his elbow, folded small, was a scrap of parchment on a cord, with a number on it. It had been round his brother Aldwin's neck when the Tenrai sent him home in the salt cart. Wystan had not let it out of his reach since.

"So," he said. "The boy."

Ulf grunted. He was sitting on a camp chair that creaked every time he breathed, with a cup in his maimed hand, looking at the map.

"What do you make of him?" Wystan said. "Truly. Now that you've had him under your eye for a week." He turned the cup slowly on his knee. "Is he one of them? One of the Kōkan?"

Ulf did not answer at once. He looked at the charcoal shape of the fort for a long while. When he spoke, his voice was low, and very certain.

"There's no doubt of it," he said. "None at all. He's Gimbzo's son."

The brazier ticked.

"You've seen it yourself," Ulf said. "Don't tell me you haven't. The way he stands. The way he goes quiet before he moves, as if he's already moved and he's only waiting for his body to catch up. The way a room changes when he walks into it, and he hasn't the first idea it's changing." He set the cup down. "I rode beside Gimbzo for two summers when I was young and stupid enough to think he'd save us. I know what that man felt like at ten paces. I felt it again in the factor's hall at the river post, from a sixteen-year-old sitting on a stool by my fire with his sister's stitches in his face." He shook his head slowly. "And he doesn't know. He doesn't know any of it."

"Yusif knew."

"Yusif knew." Ulf's mouth went tight in the braided beard. "Yusif was there the night of the cellars in Halcrest. That's the whole of why he stepped off. You know that. He watched Gimbzo go down into those cellars with a lamp after his own line, every Kōkan he'd left breathing, his own blood, for no reason any man in the north ever got out of him. And Yusif turned his horse round the next morning and never rode with him again." He was quiet for a moment. "And there was Kasane. The Byakuya girl who took the Yukari name, Gimbzo's own sworn guard, the one who stood at his shoulder in every hall he ever walked into. The way the north tells it, he took her. The way Yusif told it, she walked out of that cellar on her own feet with the boy under her coat, and never once said his name after." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on his knee and did not drink from it. "Then Yusif spent the rest of his life going down into the salt every season to make sure that boy, and the girl who came after him, grew up knowing which end of a sword goes into the other man. Right up to the winter they caught up with him, and he gave them nothing. And never once told either of the children why."

Wystan let out a long breath through his nose.

"And now we've got him," he said.

"And now we've got him." Ulf looked down at his hands, the whole and the maimed. "I don't like it, Wystan. I'll say it to you, since I can't say it to anyone else. It sits wrong with me. Every time I look at the lad I feel like a man selling a horse with a cracked hoof and a blanket over it." He rubbed his face. "He's a Moto. He's the last of the Kōkan and Gimbzo's own blood, and he's about to ride into a Moto fort tomorrow at the head of our vanguard to kill Moto for us, and he doesn't know what he is. It feels like theft. It feels like we're stealing something off him."

Wystan was silent for a while.

"Don't take this wrong, Ulf," he said at last. "But you're soft on him."

"I'm not..."

"You are. It's the uncle in you. Yusif's blood." Wystan held up a long grey hand before Ulf could argue. "And I'll tell you why you're wrong, and then you can hit me if you like, but you'll not tell him." He leaned forward. "Think it through. You tell that boy tomorrow, or the day after, or any day this year, that he's Gimbzo's son and the last of the Kōkan and a Moto by blood, and what happens? He stops knowing whose he is. He stands in front of a Tenrai gate with his sword up and he looks at the men on the other side and he thinks, *those are mine.* He thinks, *that's my blood up on that wall.* And he hesitates. And one hesitation in a breach is the whole of a man's life."

Ulf said nothing.

"He'll learn it," Wystan said. "He'll learn it on his own. He's halfway there already, I'd wager, him and that sister of his. And when he does learn it, he'll have learned it the hard way, the way every man who ever led anything worth following learned what he was. With his own hands." His voice went lower. "If we give him all the answers, Ulf, we take the war away from him. We take the pain of it. And it's the pain that makes them. You know that. You've buried enough of them who were made too soft."

The brazier ticked. Outside, somewhere in the lines, a horse stamped and blew.

"Besides," Wystan said, and a dry, crooked smile came onto his long grey face for the first time. "Who'd have thought it? At our age? That we'd live to see the rise of another great warlord of the north. Another one of the old kind." He chuckled, low in his chest. "I thought I'd be in the ground long before I saw that again."

Ulf looked at him for a long moment. Then, slowly, he picked his cup up again.

"You think he'll do it, then," he said. "Tomorrow. You think he'll take the Foot."

Wystan nodded.

"No doubt," he said. "No doubt at all."

He reached for the jug and filled both cups, and they drank without speaking. On the trestle the lamp burned low over the map, over the charcoal road and the charcoal ridge and the squat charcoal square at the top of it with its two hundred, and over a second scrap of parchment lying folded by Ulf's elbow, sealed in grey wax. It had gone south that morning in a copy, by crow, through the Mahuo post at Sum-gol, and from there by another crow east into the Yukari valleys. It said, in Ulf Stark's square northern hand: *The Foot will be in our keeping by the new moon. The Kessen road will stay open. Tell the Lord Naiser the north has not forgotten who stood with it.*

---

Kharven's Foot stood at the top of the long ridge where the Kessen road came up out of the low country and went through the mountains toward the north.

It had been there longer than anyone could say. The old northern clans had built the first of it, a ring of dry stone round a well, back when the land was still grass and the riders still rode; then somebody had built over that, and somebody over them, and eleven years back, with the war already thirty years old and the north going badly in it, the Tenrai had come and rebuilt all of it in their own square grey stone, with a gatehouse and two towers and a curtain wall eight men high, and put a garrison in it, and a toll-post at the gate, and a Tenrai banner on the tallest tower that you could see in clear weather from three days down the road. Whoever held the Foot held the road. Whoever held the road held the north by the throat. In two months, when Kagenori Tenrai Moto laid his martial law on the Kessen road, the Foot was where the door would be.

So the north had come to take the door off its hinges first.

They had come up through the dark, six hundred of them, Stark and Greymane and a scatter of lesser houses and a great many men who belonged to no house at all, and they stood now in the dead hour before dawn in the snow under the ridge, below the line of sight of the walls, in their ranks. Their breath went up white in the cold and hung over them in a cloud. Nobody spoke. Somewhere far above them, on the wall, a Tenrai sentry was singing to himself to stay awake, a thin small sound in the enormous dark, and every man in the six hundred could hear it.

The vanguard stood at the front.

There were a hundred and twenty of them, and Muken had walked down their lines twice in the dark already, and both times he had felt his stomach go cold. Ulf had not lied to him. They were boys. Not all of them; there were older men among them too, here and there, grey-bearded veterans with no sons left, who had asked for the vanguard because the vanguard was where you went when you had nothing left to keep yourself alive for. But most of them were boys. Sixteen. Seventeen. A few of them younger, and lying about it, with spears they had to hold two-handed and helmets stuffed with rags to keep them on, and the soft blurred faces of children who had not yet decided what shape they were going to be.

Like him. They looked like him.

He stood at the front of them in a mail shirt Ulf had given him that was too long in the arms and too short in the body, with the scrap-iron sword on his back and the stitched seam on his cheek gone tight and itching in the cold, and the dead token inside his coat against his chest, and looked up at the black line of the ridge, and the black line of the wall on top of it, and tried not to think about anything at all.

Ulf and Wystan came down the line toward him through the snow.

The Lord of Hollenmark was in his war-mail, with a great axe across his shoulder and his breath going up white through his braided beard, and men straightened as he passed them without knowing they did it. Wystan walked beside him with the long Greymane stride, in a grey surcoat and a grey cloak, bareheaded, with his sword at his hip and a hunting horn at his belt. They stopped in front of the vanguard, and turned, and looked at it.

Ulf did not raise his voice. He did not need to. In the silence under the ridge it carried to every man in the hundred and twenty and some way past them.

"This is ours," he said. "This battle. This morning. This is ours, lads, and for no one else. Not for the Seven. Not for me. For the people. For your mothers in the long houses with nothing in the pot and the snow at the door. For your sisters. For your little brothers who'll be stood where you're stood in three years if we don't do this now." He let it sit. "For every one of you who's buried his father this winter. This is for them."

The breath of a hundred and twenty boys hung in the air over them.

"Some of you will have noticed," Ulf said, "that I've put a stranger at your front."

Nobody laughed. A few of them looked at Muken. Most of them already were.

"I'll tell you about him," Ulf said, "because you've a right to know who you're following up that hill. He's got no house. He's got no family name. He's tokenless. He's never sworn to me nor to Lord Greymane nor to any lord in the north, and there's no contract on him anywhere in the world that would hold him to this ground for one breath longer than he chose to stand on it." He looked down the line. "He owes nobody anything. He could have taken his pay at the river post and gone south with his sister and never thought about any of you again."

He put his maimed hand on Muken's shoulder.

"And he's here," Ulf said. "He came. Nobody made him. He's here because he wants to fight for you. Because he thinks the people of the north are worth keeping safe, and he's no more reason to think so than any of you has. That's why the house of Stark and the house of Greymane have named him your lieutenant, as of this morning. And that's why I want you to trust him." He paused. "Rely on him. He'll lead you into more battles than this one, lads. A great many more. Some of you'll be old men telling your grandchildren you were stood in the snow under the Foot the morning he first drew his sword for the north."

He took his hand away.

Wystan Greymane stepped forward. He looked down at Muken, from his great grey height, with something in his deep-set grey eyes that Muken could not read, and put his long hand on Muken's other shoulder, and squeezed it once.

"You're up, lad," he said quietly.

Then he stepped back, and Muken was alone at the front of them.

---

He did not know what to say.

He stood in the snow and looked at them, the hundred and twenty, and they looked back at him, and the silence went on, and he could feel it stretching and going thin like ice over a river, and he could not find a single word. He had never made a speech in his life. Sergeants did not make speeches. Sergeants said *move* and *hold* and *on me* and *get your shield up, you stupid bastard,* and that was the whole of it. He looked at the soft faces of the boys under their rag-stuffed helmets, and the hard grey faces of the old men among them, and he thought: *They're waiting for me. They're waiting for me to tell them why.*

And he did not know why. Not in words.

Then, behind the vanguard, past the end of its ranks, where the surgeons had set up their line in the lee of a fallen pine with their kits open in the snow and their braziers lit for the irons, he saw a small figure in a grey coat with a white braid pinned up off her neck. She was standing very still. She was not looking at Ulf or Wystan or the ridge or the wall. She was looking at him.

She did not nod. She did not smile. She did not do anything at all. She only stood there and looked at him, steadily, the way she looked at a man on a cart when she had decided he was going to live.

He felt his breathing slow down.

And then it came, all at once, the way his body moved in a fight before he decided to, and he opened his mouth and let it.

"We're going to free ourselves," Muken said.

His voice came out rough and too loud and cracked on the last word. He did not care.

"That's what this is. That's all it is. We're going up that hill to take back what's ours, from the people who think it's theirs." He pointed up at the black line of the wall with his whole arm. "From the ones up there. From the ones who sent them. From every Moto lord who's ever sat in a warm hall in the south and looked at a map of the north and decided what's going to happen to us." He took a breath. "They don't get to do that any more. They don't get to decide our lives. They don't get to arrange us. Like stones on a board. Like sacks in a cart. Like *numbers* in a drainage book."

The hundred and twenty were very quiet.

"They'll tell you it's for unity," Muken said. "They'll tell you it's for order. They'll say the roads have to be closed and the grain has to be taken and the salt has to be counted, because it's for the good of everybody, and they understand what the north needs better than the north does." He felt his face go hot under the stitches. "They don't understand anything. They've never once lived up here. They've never had to break the ice on a bucket to wash. They've never sat up all night by a sick child with nothing to put in its mouth. They've never buried their father in ground so frozen they had to light a fire on it for a day to dig. They don't know the first thing about what it takes to stay alive in this frozen wasteland, and they think they've the right to tell *us* how to do it."

He was not shouting any more. He did not need to. They were leaning toward him, the whole front rank of them, the way men lean toward a fire.

"So this is for the north," he said. "For the people in it. That's all. That's all I've got to give you, I've nothing else." He looked down the line, at the boys and the old men, at the Stark wolves and the Greymane wolves on the breasts of some of them and the bare cloth on the breasts of most of them. "I don't care if you're Stark. I don't care if you're Greymane. I don't care if you're some house nobody's ever heard of, from some valley nobody's ever been to. I don't care if you're nothing at all. If you're tokenless. Like me." He swallowed. "We're neighbours. That's what we are. Every one of us. We're the man next door. And you don't let the man next door go hungry, and you don't let anybody burn his house, and you don't let him walk up a hill on his own."

He reached up over his shoulder, and drew the scrap-iron sword.

It came out of the scabbard with its dry rasp in the silence, black and pitted and ugly in the grey dark before the dawn, and he raised it up over his head, as high as his arm would go, toward the sky.

"So we fight for each other," Muken said. "Every one of us. For every one of us. And we rage together." He held the sword up. "Because our fury is one."

For a moment nothing happened at all.

Then a boy in the front rank, a thin red-haired boy of perhaps fifteen with a spear twice his height, lifted the spear up over his head in both hands, the way Muken had lifted the sword. And the boy next to him did. And the old grey-bearded veteran behind them did, without a word, his axe going up into the dark. And then it went down the line like fire through dry grass, spears and axes and swords and bare fists going up, a hundred and twenty of them, all at once, and from somewhere in the middle of them a sound came up, too close under the wall for cheering: a low, hard, rising growl, from a hundred and twenty throats, like wolves.

Wystan Greymane turned his head and looked at Ulf Stark.

He was smiling. It was not a large smile. It was the dry crooked smile of an old man who has been right about something and is enjoying it more than he will ever admit.

*For someone so young,* the smile said. *Who taught him to talk like that?*

Ulf did not smile back. He was looking at Muken. His pale northern eyes were wet, and it was not the cold.

Up on the wall, the Tenrai sentry had stopped singing.

Thirty of the hundred and twenty would not come down off the ridge again. None of them knew that yet. Most of them would not have cared.

---

Wystan blew the horn.

They went up the ridge in the grey of the first light, in the snow, at a run.

The Foot woke above them. Muken heard it wake: a shout on the wall, and then another, and then a bell, iron and frantic, hammering out across the dawn, and the scrape and clatter of men running on stone. Then the first arrows came down out of the grey, and he heard the sound they made, the long thin hiss in the air and then the flat wet thump, and a boy two places to his left made a small surprised sound, and sat down in the snow with a shaft through his throat, and did not get up.

"*Shields!*" Muken roared. "*Shields up, get your shields up, keep moving, don't stop, don't stop!*"

They kept moving. That was the only rule. You did not stop under a wall. A man who stopped under a wall was a man who had decided to die there. The snow on the ridge was knee-deep and crusted, and every step broke through it and caught, and the slope was steeper than it had looked in the dark, and the arrows came down and came down, and men fell, and the men behind them went round them and kept going.

The plan was the gate. It had always been the gate. Wystan's men had the ram, a great iron-capped pine trunk slung on chains from a timber frame, and they were dragging it up the road behind the vanguard, and the vanguard's work was to get to the foot of the gate-towers and hold the ground there long enough for the ram to come up. Ladders on the curtain either side, to split the defenders. Then the gate, and the yard, and the keep. Simple. Every plan was simple until the first arrow.

Halfway up the ridge, Muken looked at the wall, and saw it.

He did not know how he saw it. He would never know, not for years. One moment the curtain wall was a long blank face of square grey Tenrai stone in the grey dawn, eight men high, featureless, with heads and bows moving along the top of it. The next, there was a line in it. A fault. A long jagged seam running down through the stone from the battlements to the footing, thirty paces left of the gatehouse, where the Tenrai had built their new grey wall over the old dry-stone ring of the clans, and the frost had got in between the two, year after year after year, and pushed. Nobody had seen it. Nobody could have. It was something you *felt,* the way you feel the one stair in a staircase that is going to give under your weight: a place where the whole of the wall's load was going down through stone that could not carry it, and had not been able to for years, and was only waiting.

The heat came up behind his left eye, sharp, like a cinder.

"*Left!*" he roared. "*Ladders left! On me! Thirty paces left of the gate, on me, ON ME!*"

They came with him. He would remember that, later, more than anything else about the morning. He turned away from the gate and ran for a blank piece of wall where the plan had said nothing at all, and he did not look back, and they came with him. Forty of them, fifty, with the two ladders. The thin red-haired boy with the spear. The old grey-bearded veteran with the axe.

The ladders went up against the seam. The Tenrai on the wall saw where they were going and ran along the battlements to meet them, and the stones and the boiling pitch came down, and the first ladder went over backward with six men on it, and Muken heard them hit the slope. The second ladder held. He went up it. He did not remember deciding to. The warmth came down out of his chest into his legs and arms and back, as it always did, and he went up the rungs three at a time with the scrap-iron sword in one hand, and at the top of the ladder a Tenrai spearman leaned out over the battlement to thrust down at his face, and Muken caught the haft below the head with his free hand and pulled, and the man came over the wall headfirst past him and was gone.

He came over the battlement onto the wall-walk.

And the wall moved under him.

It was a small movement. A shiver, a settling, like an old horse shifting its weight. But he felt it through his boots, and the men on the walk felt it, Tenrai and northern both, and every one of them stopped, for one breath, and looked down. And then the seam he had seen from halfway down the ridge opened along its whole length with a sound like a tree splitting in a hard frost, and thirty feet of the Tenrai curtain wall leaned slowly outward over the slope, and kept leaning, and went down.

He was not on it. He had come over the wall six paces to the right of the seam, by luck or by something that was not luck, and he stood on the wall-walk and watched the whole section beside him go over in a long roar of grey stone and snow and dust and screaming men, down the ridge, and when the dust began to thin there was a gap in the curtain of Kharven's Foot that a cart could have driven through.

The hundred and twenty came through it.

---

After that, for a long time, it was the yard.

He would remember very little of the yard afterward, and he would be glad of it. It was the kind of fighting that has no shape. The Tenrai garrison came down out of the towers and the barracks and the keep to plug the breach, and the northerners came up through it to widen it, and they met in the snow of the courtyard between the walls, and it became a crush, two hundred men and more in a space built for forty, pressed so close that half of them could not lift their arms. Men died there without knowing who killed them. Men were killed by their own side. Men drowned in the trampled slush with their faces pushed into it and a dozen pairs of boots on their backs.

He fought. He did not remember most of it. He remembered the warmth in his body, and the scrap-iron sword going in and coming out, and the shock of it up his wrists every time it met bone. He remembered that the warmth was thinner than it had been on the Vessant road, and going faster, and that his arms were growing heavy. He remembered the red-haired boy with the spear, beside him for a long time, and then not beside him, and when he looked round for him, the boy was lying on his back in the snow by the well with his spear still in his hands and his eyes open and a Tenrai sword through his belly, and there was nothing to do about it and no time to do it. He remembered the old grey-bearded veteran with the axe going into a knot of Tenrai spearmen by the barracks door, roaring, alone, and the knot closing over him, and not opening again.

He remembered, somewhere in the middle of it, hearing the ram hit the gate. And then hit it again. And then a great splintering crash, and Wystan's horn, and the roar of the Greymanes coming in through the gatehouse behind the Tenrai, and the crush in the yard breaking all at once, the way ice breaks on a river in spring.

The Tenrai fell back toward the keep.

There was a broad stone stair up to the keep door, and they went up it in a body, the last of the garrison, fifty or sixty of them, and turned at the top of it with their shields locked, and held. And the northerners came across the yard after them, and stopped at the foot of the stair, and the whole of the fighting stopped for a moment, the way a wave stops at the top of a beach before it goes back. In the sudden quiet you could hear men breathing, and men crying, and somewhere a horse screaming over and over in the burning stables.

Muken stood at the foot of the stair, with blood running off the end of the scrap-iron sword into the snow, and his chest heaving, and the stitches in his cheek torn open again and bleeding down his neck into the collar of the borrowed mail. The warmth was nearly gone out of him. His legs were shaking. He looked up at the shield wall at the top of the stair and thought, quite clearly and calmly: *I don't have another one of these in me.*

The shield wall opened.

---

It opened, deliberately, in the middle, two shields swinging back like a door, and a single figure came out through the gap and down three steps of the stair, and stopped.

And every man at the foot of it, northern and Tenrai alike, went still.

Muken felt it before he understood what it was. It came down the stair ahead of the figure like the heat ahead of an opened kiln, and it went into him through his chest and his face and the backs of his eyes, and the warmth that was left in his body shrank down from it the way a snail shrinks from salt. It was pressure. Something enormous pushing against the air of the yard from the inside, the way water pushes on a dam, and every man in the yard felt his knees want to bend under it. Behind him, he heard a boy of the vanguard begin to vomit. He heard somebody else drop a spear.

The figure on the stair was a woman.

She was perhaps forty. She was tall, and lean as a blade, and she wore the war-dress of the Tenrai: black lamellar laced with gold, a gold-trimmed surcoat over it, and on the breast of it not a boss but the banner of her line, the Tenrai heraldry, worked in gold thread. She carried no shield. She held a long, slightly curved sword loosely at her side in one hand, with the point resting on the stone of the stair, and there was no blood on it at all. Her hair was black, threaded with grey, bound up high on her head with a gold cord. Her face was long and narrow and pale and very calm, and at her brow and her temples and the hollow of her throat, the skin caught the light of the burning stables with a faint metallic sheen, like old brass.

Her eyes were the worst of it. There were rings in them. Fine bright lines, turning slowly in the dark of the iris, like the rings that spread on a pond after a stone has gone in, round and round, never stopping.

She looked down the stair at the northern army in the yard below her, at the dead in the snow and the burning stables and the broken gate and the gap in her wall, and nothing at all moved in her face.

Then she looked at Muken.

And something did.

He saw it. He would have sworn afterward that he saw it, though there was nothing to see, and nobody else in the yard ever said they had. The turning rings in her eyes stopped. All at once, as if a hand had closed on them. And her face, her long calm narrow face, went perfectly white, white as the snow, white as the face of the Zettari surveyor at the Marrow when Yukazuri showed him the mark on the frosted glass.

"You," she said.

It was barely a whisper. It carried across the whole of the silent yard.

Muken did not answer. He did not know what he could have said.

The Moto commander came down the last steps of the stair, slowly, one at a time, and stopped on the bottom one, so that her eyes were level with his, an arm's length and a sword's length away. The pressure coming off her was so great now that he could hardly breathe in it. His eyes were watering. The heat behind his left eye had come up, sharp, like a cinder, like a coal, so bright that for a moment the whole yard went red at the edges.

She was looking into his face. Into his eyes. She looked at him the way a woman looks at a grave she has been told was empty, and has just seen move.

"Where did you get that face?" she said.

"I was born with it."

"No." Her voice was very soft. "No. I knew that face. I stood behind that face for twenty years. I watched it in the war." The sword-point lifted a hand's width off the stone. "I watched it burn the Akagane granaries at Hiroe with the children still inside. I watched it on the night of the Kōkan cellars." Her lips had gone thin and white. "And I watched it on the field at Shimane when the Zettari boy put it in the mud. *I was there.* He's dead. He's *dead.*"

"I don't know what you're talking about," said Muken.

He did not. He did not understand one word of it. But something in him did. Something underneath the cold and the exhaustion and the blood running down his neck, something that had always been there, at the back, in the worked-out gallery where he did not go: it heard her, and it understood, and it went very still and very cold and very heavy, like a stone settling to the bottom of a well.

The Moto commander looked at him for one breath longer. Then the rings in her eyes began to turn again, faster now, much faster, bright as struck steel.

"My name," she said, and her voice had changed, and was the voice of her line, flat and formal and enormous, so that it filled the whole of the yard and came back off the walls, "is Shigure Tenrai Moto. Captain of the Foot, by the hand of Kagenori Tenrai. Daughter of the Gold Banner. Blade of the seven lines and the one blood."

She brought the long curved sword up from the stair into both hands.

"And whatever you are, boy," said Shigure Tenrai Moto, "I am going to find out what's inside you."

Behind Muken, across the yard, at the end of the surgeons' line by the broken gate, a small figure in a grey coat had stopped with a roll of bandage in her hands, and was looking at him over the heads of the army.

He did not see her. He did not see anything but the woman on the stair.

He lifted the scrap-iron sword.

The rings in her eyes flared gold.

She came down off the last step.

## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Fourteen: The Seat Road

---

### I · The Note of Everything

It was as if the world came apart into pieces, and every piece was singing.

The ice sang. He heard it under his knee, through the trampled snow, a note so low he felt it as a weight, the whole frozen Skeldmere humming over the drowned lake beneath it like the string of a great bow drawn and held. The white of the lifting sang, high and thin and wavering, a thousand points of frost hanging in the air each at its own pitch. His own body sang, the broken ribs a ragged discord on his left side, his heart under them a drum, the scrap-iron sword in his hand a long clear ringing note that went up his arm into his teeth and did not stop.

And Aksel Keld sang.

All of him. The short dense body in its two coats and its horn lamellar sewn between them, every plate of it at its own pitch. The leather straps. The bone in his forearm. The birch handle of the axe in his left hand. And the iron fist, drawn back at the top of its arc against the white, black and heavy, singing a high thin wiry note of a thousand blows struck and taken, and in the middle of that note, at the base of the second knuckle where the rivet went through, a flaw in the song. A small sour place. A crack, humming.

It was a swirl. It was a weave. It was a harmony so vast that his mind could not hold the shape of it, only the parts, the way a man in a storm can hold a single raindrop and not the rain. And for one breath, kneeling on the ice with his blood running warm under his mail, Muken understood that it had always been there. Every fight. Every ditch. Every gallery under Halcrest. The world had always been singing, and he had always been reading the cracks in the song, and he had never once known it was a song.

*Find the note. Hold it.*

The fist came down.

Muken stepped off its line.

He did not decide to. His feet did it, the way Yusif had always told him they would if he stopped thinking. He came up off his knee and turned his body a quarter, left shoulder back, and the iron fist went past his face so close he felt the wind of it on his cheek and heard it, singing, a hand's width from his ear. And as it went past, as the black iron hand dropped through the place where his head had been, he laid the flat of the ringing scrap-iron blade against it.

He only touched it, the way you lay a finger on a bell.

The note of the blade went into the iron hand.

It found the sour place at the base of the second knuckle as if it had been looking for it all its life. For half a breath nothing happened. The hand went on past, Aksel's whole weight behind it, carrying him forward and down. And then the iron sang back. It sang the note of the scrap-iron blade, high and clear, louder and louder, the sound of it climbing out of the black metal into the white air like a kettle coming to the boil, and the crack at the rivet heard it.

The crack ran.

It ran faster than an eye could follow. It went out from the second knuckle along the line it had been waiting a thousand blows to follow, through the riveted plate across the back of the hand and down into the finger-joints and up into the wrist-cuff, branching, branching again, and every place it went the iron opened along its own grain as if it had been sawn. The rivets sheared. The walrus-tusk inlay split with a sound like a dry log in a fire. The sinew cords that worked the fingers, drawn tight to the shoulder, parted one after another, snapping back up the sleeve, and the great black articulated fist that had killed more men than any spear on the tundra came apart in the air in seven pieces and fell around Aksel Keld's feet in the snow.

Aksel stared at the stump of his arm.

The leather cuff was still strapped to it. The stump inside it, the old wrist-end a Tenrai sword had made when he was thirteen, was bare and pale and ridged with old scar in the cold air, and the cuff's end was ragged where the iron had been. Nothing else was left. He stared at it with his narrow black eyes gone wide, and for one breath the Iron Fist of the Keld looked exactly as young as he was.

Muken swept the scrap-iron sword forward, low, at his belly.

It was a long flat cut, the whole length of the blade, and Aksel saw it coming and threw himself back from it. He was fast. Even now, with his iron hand lying in pieces in the snow, he was fast. The edge went past his gut by a finger's width, and opened the outer sealskin coat across the belly in a long gaping lip, and the horn plates of the lamellar under it showed through the cut, pale and curved and stitched in rows like the scales of a fish. Aksel staggered back two paces on the trampled snow, his short legs going wide for balance, the axe coming up in his left hand.

Muken did not stop there.

He went after him. He came in off his back foot as hard and fast as he had ever moved in his life, his broken ribs screaming and the blood running down his side and the white of the lifting going grey at the edges, and he did not lift the sword. The axe was coming up between them, and the sword would have had to go round it, and there was no time to go round anything. So he dropped his head and drove the crown of it, forehead first, the whole weight of his body behind it, straight into Aksel Keld's face.

He felt the nose go under his forehead. The bridge of it, the thin bones between the eyes. He felt it give and spread like a crushed egg. Blood burst across his brow and into his eyes and down his face, hot, salt, and the sound Aksel made was a thick wet grunt, as if all the air in him had been punched out through his face. The back of the Keld's head snapped back. His eyes rolled up white for an instant.

Muken's own head rang like a struck pot. The world swung. For a moment he did not know where the ice was.

Aksel fell.

He went over backward with his short legs folding under him and landed flat on his back on the trampled snow, hard, with a sound like a sack of wet grain dropped off a cart. The axe went out of his left hand and skittered away across the ice. His arms went wide. His face was a mask of blood from the brow down, the nose spread flat and the lips split, and he lay with his narrow black eyes open and blinking up at the white sky, and did not get up.

Muken stood over him.

He reversed the scrap-iron sword in his hand. He did not think about it. He took it by the blade, below the guard, with the edge biting into his palm through the old callus, and he lifted it over his head like a hammer, and he brought the pommel down on Aksel Keld's chest.

The blade was still ringing.

The pommel hit the lamellar over the breastbone, through the cut sealskin, and the ring went into the horn plates as it had gone into the iron hand, and found every old crack in every plate at once. He heard them go. A crackle like ice breaking on a puddle under a boot, spreading out from the place he had struck in a ring, plate after plate. And under the plates, through them, the blow itself went on, the weight of his whole body behind the pommel, into the bone beneath. He felt the sternum give. The ribs either side of it, at the front, where they joined the breastbone by their cords of gristle, popped away from it one after another like buttons off a coat.

He lifted the sword and brought it down again.

"*This is for the people!*"

He did not know he was going to shout it. It came up out of him from somewhere under the broken ribs and the salt and the stair and the long room, in a voice that was not the gallery voice at all, raw and cracked and enormous.

The pommel came down. The horn plates broke.

"*For Harrowmere!*" The pommel came down. "*For Tullock! For Brannick ford!*" Down. "*For every child in every long house who ate bark because of Tenrai seed grain!*" Down. "*For the carts! For the levies! For the salt! For her lungs!*" Down. "*For everything!*"

Aksel Keld did not fight back.

He did not lift his arm. He did not try to roll away. He lay on his back on the ice with his ruined face turned up to the white and his stump lying in the snow at his side, and took it, every blow, and his narrow black eyes were open the whole time, looking up at the boy with the white hair and the blood on his brow standing over him with the sword reversed in his hands. There was no fear in them. Something else was there, something that might, in another face, at another time, have been recognition.

Muken roared.

It had stopped being a word. It was the sound the wolfhound had made in the long room, low and continuous, coming up out of the bottom of his chest and going on and on, and the adrenaline was going through him like fire through dry grass, so that he could not feel his ribs or his split palm or the blood in his eyes, only the hammer-weight of the sword in his hands going up and coming down, up and down, and the singing of the world all round him so loud that it drowned out everything.

Then he stopped.

He did not know why. He did not decide to. He stood over the Iron Fist of the Keld with the sword lifted for another blow and the pommel slick with blood and fragments of horn, and the roar ran out of him like water out of a broken pot, and his arms would not bring the sword down again.

*He's not fighting back,* he thought. *He stopped fighting back the moment the hand came apart. He's lying there and letting me.*

*He always stopped,* said something in him, in a voice he did not know. *A beaten clan was governed. Not humiliated.*

He did not know where it came from. He let the sword down slowly until the point touched the snow.

Aksel Keld's chest was moving. Badly. A short, shallow, catching breath, with a wet sound in it at the end, and a bubble of pink froth at the corner of his split mouth that grew and burst and grew again. The front of the lamellar over his chest had caved in like the lid of a crushed box. His eyes were still open. They were still on Muken's face.

"So," said Aksel Keld.

It came out of him as a whisper, thick with blood.

"That's... what you'd have offered." The split lips moved in something that was nearly a smile. "At my fire."

Muken stood over him and breathed. In through the nose, until his own broken ribs ground together. Out through the mouth. The world was still singing round him, but more quietly now, the harmony drawing back to the edges like a tide going out, and the ringing in the sword had dropped to a hum.

He turned the sword round in his hand. He wiped the pommel on his thigh. He sheathed it.

The blade went into the scabbard with a sound he had never heard it make before, a grinding catch halfway down, as if something in the steel had shifted. He did not look at it.

All around them, out in the white, the Keld were scattering. He could hear it. The thousand riders of the wedge, the ones who had gone on past toward the Stark ring, had turned somewhere out in the lifting when the iron hand came apart, as though they had felt it go, and he could hear their hooves going away in every direction across the frozen lake, east and north and west, the drumming of the ice under them fading as they went. *Take the head,* every Keld captain was taught, *and the herd scatters.*

The herd was scattering.

Muken stood in the little cleared ring of trampled snow with the Iron Fist of the Keld broken at his feet, and the wolfhound came up beside him with blood on its muzzle that was not its own, and pressed its great grey shoulder against his leg, and for the space of three breaths, in the white, in the silence after the singing, he thought it was over.

Then he heard the horse.

### II · The Shore

It was Mark Stark.

He came out of the white from the west, from the shore, on a lathered Stark bay that was going lame in the near fore, and he was riding with one arm. The other hung at his side with a black-glass arrow standing out of the meat of the upper arm, through the hide-coat and the mail, the shaft snapped off short. There was blood all down that side of him. His face was the colour of the snow.

He pulled the bay up so hard it nearly went down, and looked at Muken, and looked at the body on the ice, and did not seem to see it.

"*They've taken her,*" said Mark Stark.

Muken did not move.

"Yukazuri," said Mark. His voice cracked on it. "On the shore. The surgeons' line. They came out of the lifting, grey men, a score of them, more, on southern horses. We never saw them till they were in among the carts." He swayed in the saddle and caught himself on the pommel with his good hand. "They killed the Greymane guard. All of them. Set fire to the carts. Took her and put her in a carriage, a closed thing on runners, Tenrai black and gold, it was waiting on the shore road the whole time, Sky knows how long, and they're going south with her. On the Seat road." He breathed. "I tried. I'm sorry. I tried."

"Where," said Muken.

It came out of him very quietly. Mark Stark stared at him.

"*Where is she?*"

That was not quiet. That was the roar again, all of it, coming up out of the bottom of him and tearing his throat, and the wolfhound beside him flinched from it, and Mark Stark's lame bay threw up its head and shied. The world came back. The singing came back, all at once, everything humming and wailing and ringing in the white, and every note in it was wrong.

*She was at the Foot. She was at the Foot behind the Greymane wall with her rows of linen and her salve tins, counting. She was at the Foot.*

He knew why she had not stayed at the Foot. He knew it before Mark finished. He knew it the way he knew the shape of his own hand. The arrows had come out of the white and the Starks had started falling, forty, fifty, and a man with a black-glass arrowhead in his gut or a leaf-headed lance through his chest did not live long enough to be carried two hours up the Kessen road to a surgeons' tent. So she had brought the tent to them. She had loaded her linen and her gut and her needles into the carts and come down the road with the Greymane guard Godfrey would have given her, twenty men, because Godfrey could never in his life have refused her, and she had set up her line on the shore of the Skeldmere behind the Stark ring, in the snow, close enough to hear the screaming, so that the men who fell could be carried to her in minutes and not hours.

*Of course she did. Of course she did. That's who she is.*

And Karogane had known she would.

Muken understood that too, standing there on the ice with the roar still in his throat. He understood it the way he had understood the gold lines in the Keld host. The Tenrai agent had never come to the Skeldmere to break the boy with a sentence. The carter had told him in the war-lodge at dawn that the sentence was spent, and Karogane had said *it changes nothing,* and he had been right, because the sentence had never been the plan. It had only been the first plan. The second plan had been sitting on the shore road in a closed carriage on runners since before the grey came up, waiting for the lifting, waiting for the king of the north to be busy somewhere out on the ice, waiting for the one person in the world he would turn his whole army round for.

*The red line's last. Both of us. Not one.*

*He doesn't need to kill me. He only needs her.*

Muken was already moving.

He went past Mark Stark at a run, across the trampled snow, and caught the reins of the Hollenmark bay with the one white foot where it stood waiting, and hauled himself up into the saddle with his broken ribs grinding like millstones and a sound coming out of his mouth he did not hear. The wolfhound was running before he had turned the horse. He put his heels in. The bay went.

Behind him, on the ice, the Iron Fist of the Keld lay on his back in the snow with his chest caved in, breathing his short pink-frothed breaths, and watched him go.

### III · The Grey Men

He came out of the lifting onto the shore of the Skeldmere and the world was burning.

The surgeons' carts were burning. Six of them, drawn up in a half-ring on the snow above the ice with their shafts down, the way Yukazuri had always drawn up her line, a wall against the wind, and every one of them was alight from end to end. The linen was burning, the clean linen in its rows, and the boiled cloth in its tubs had boiled dry and the tubs were burning too, and the spirit had gone up in a blue roar that had blackened the snow for ten paces round. The smoke went up thick and greasy into the white and lay along the shore. The smell was burning cloth and burning wood and burning spirit and, under it, something else.

The Greymane guard lay in the snow among the carts.

Twenty men. Muken did not count them. He did not need to. They lay where they had stood, in their grey cloaks, in a rough ring round the place where the surgeons' tent had been, most of them on their backs, most of them with their spears still in their hands. Some had been cut down from horseback, the long downward strokes of a cavalry sword that open a man from the collarbone to the breastbone. Some had been shot. One, near the tent, lay on his face with his own spear through him from behind, the point standing out between his shoulder blades, and his arms folded under him as though he had been holding them that way when he died.

Muken knew that man. He had seen him at the woodpile. A Greymane spearman near the gate, with his arms folded and his face like a shut door, who had stood when the yard knelt.

*He let them in,* Muken thought. *And they paid him the way the Tenrai pay.*

The wounded from the Stark ring lay where they had been carried, in a row in the lee of the carts. Some of them were still alive. Some of them were screaming. A Stark boy with no left hand was sitting up in the snow holding the stump against his chest and rocking, and looked up as Muken came past, and his mouth opened, and nothing came out of it.

Among the burning carts, the grey men were waiting.

There were eight of them. They had been left. Muken saw it at once, the way he saw the gold lines: a rear-guard, set across the mouth of the shore road where it left the Skeldmere and went south through the low drifts toward the Seat, to hold it long enough. Long enough for what, he could see over their heads. A dark shape on the road, half a mile south and going away, a long black box on runners drawn by four horses at the gallop, with riders round it, going into the white.

*There.*

The grey men saw him coming. They spread out across the road. They did it well, with no word spoken, the way Karogane moved, every step measured. They were dismounted, their horses held behind them by a ninth man, and they had cavalry swords in their hands, long and slightly curved, Tenrai steel with the gold worked so fine into the guards that you could only see it in the firelight. Plain grey cloaks. Hoods up. No faces.

"*Where is she?*" Muken roared at them.

They did not answer. They came at him.

The first two came together, from either side of the bay's head, the way a pair of hounds come at a stag. He was off the horse before they reached it. He did not remember getting off. He was on the snow with the scrap-iron sword out of its scabbard in his hand, and it came out with that grinding catch again, halfway, as if the steel had swollen. He did not care. The first grey man was coming in from the left with his sword up for a cut at the head, and Muken did not parry it. He stepped in under it, inside the arc, close enough to smell the man's breath through the hood, and brought the scrap-iron sword round flat and low and up, two-handed, with his hips behind it, into the side of the man's neck below the hood.

The edge went in at the side of the throat, through the muscle there, the long cord of it that turns the head, and on. The blade was dull and pitted and worn thin, and it should not have gone through. It went through. It was still humming, very faintly, and wherever it went the flesh and the bone seemed to part ahead of it along lines that were already there. It went through the windpipe and the spine at the neck with a short hard grinding crunch, and out the other side, and the hood fell one way and the body fell the other.

The second grey man was already swinging. Muken turned on the ball of his foot with the dead man's blood still in the air between them and took the swing on the scrap-iron blade, edge to edge, and felt it bite into his own blade, deep, a notch the width of a finger, and he did not care about that either. He shoved. The grey man's sword slid off his. And Muken cut back across, high, at the place where the hood met the shoulder, and the head came off that one too.

It came off clean. It went up and turned once in the air with the grey hood still on it and came down on the burning cart behind, and lay there.

"*You will pay!*"

He was roaring it at the cart, at the dark shape on the road, at the white, at all of them.

"*You will pay for this! Every one of you!*"

The other six came.

He met them in the smoke among the burning carts, and afterward he did not remember most of it. He remembered pieces. A sword coming at his face out of the smoke, and the scrap-iron blade coming up under it, and the sound the two met with, a high ugly shriek, and the pain in his hands. A grey man going down with his knee opened. The wolfhound with its jaws locked on a grey man's sword-arm, shaking him like a rat while he screamed, until the arm broke and the scream changed. Heat on his left side from a burning cart. Blood in his eyes again, someone's, his own. A cut along his right forearm he did not feel until later, through the coat and the skin to the meat, a long clean opening the length of his hand.

And the scrap-iron sword, failing.

He felt it go. It had been singing since the ice, since *Fure*, and every time it met a Tenrai blade the song in it went more sour. He had held its note too long. It had carried the cracks in the iron fist and the horn plates and the grey men's necks, and somewhere in all of that carrying it had found its own. He could hear it. A crack in the scrap-iron, near the guard, where the old blade had been forged and reforged a hundred times by men who did not care what it was. It was humming. It was running.

A grey man came in at him from the right with a lunge at his belly. Muken knocked it aside with the scrap-iron sword, and the scrap-iron sword broke.

It came apart along its own line, the way the iron fist had, the crack running up from the guard through the black pitted blade in a long jagged branching seam, and two-thirds of it fell off into the snow, and he was holding the hilt and a hand's length of broken steel.

He threw it at the grey man's face. The man flinched.

Muken went past him.

There was a Keld lance lying in the snow beside one of the burning carts. Birch, with a long leaf-shaped head, dropped by some Keld rider who had chased a Stark wounded this far and died for it. He snatched it up on the run. He turned with it. The grey man who had lunged was coming after him, and another behind him, and Muken did not set the lance or brace it. He ran at them with it held low in both hands like a hay-fork and drove it into the first grey man's belly under the ribs, through the cloak and the coat and whatever was under the coat, with all his weight and all his rage behind it, so far that the leaf head came out of the man's back and the birch shaft went in after it to the length of a hand, and the man folded over it and went down.

He let go of the lance.

The second grey man was on him. Muken caught the sword-wrist in his left hand as it came down, and held it, and the grey man was strong and the broken ribs on Muken's left side were grinding and his arm shook. His right hand went out by itself to the grey man's belt. There was a knife there. A long thin Tenrai dagger in a sheath of black lacquer. He drew it.

He drove it into the side of the grey man's head.

It went in above the ear, through the hood and the hair and the thin flat bone of the temple, to the hilt. The grey man's eyes went wide inside the hood. His sword hand opened. He stood there a moment, held up by Muken's grip on his wrist, with the black lacquer hilt standing out of the side of his head, and then Muken let go, and he went down in the snow beside the burning cart, and did not move.

### IV · The Salve Tin

Muken stood among the dead and the fire.

He stood there with his chest heaving and the breath going up off him in a great white plume into the greasy smoke, and his hands hanging at his sides, empty, and the blood all over him. In his hair. In his eyes. Down the front of the dead sergeant's coat and Ulf Stark's mail, so much of it that the brown wool had gone black and stiff with it and the rings of the mail were clotted. Some of it was his. Most of it was not. His broken ribs were a red roaring furnace on his left side and every breath went in short and came out with a catch, and his right forearm was laid open and dripping off his fingers into the snow, and his split palm, where he had held the sword by the blade, had begun at last to hurt.

The carts burned round him. The wounded in the lee of the carts had gone quiet, or most of them. The wolfhound stood a little way off over the grey man whose arm it had broken, with its head low, and the grey man was not moving either.

He looked down.

There was something in the snow at his feet, among the trampled footprints and the blood, half under the edge of the burned-out cart nearest the road. Small and round and dull. He knew what it was before he bent for it. He would have known it anywhere.

Her salve tin.

He picked it up. It was dented. The lid had been half pulled off, as though she had had it in her hand when they took her and held on to it until someone tore her fingers off it. The salve inside had gone hard and grey in the cold. And on the lid, scratched into the tin, freshly, with the point of something sharp, a needle, the scratches still bright against the dull metal, there was a mark.

The long down-stroke. The hook. The crossing. The small closed loop at the foot like a knot in a rope.

And under it, smaller, scratched hard and quick and crooked, an arrow. Pointing south.

She had left him a sign. She had stood on the shore of the Skeldmere with her carts burning round her and the Greymane guard dying in the snow and grey men coming for her out of the smoke, and she had taken the needle out of her apron pocket and scratched their name and the way they were taking her into the lid of her salve tin, and dropped it where he would find it. Because she had known he would come. Because she had never in her life doubted that he would come.

Muken closed his hand on the tin until the dented edge of it cut into the split palm.

He looked up.

Half a mile south, on the shore road, the long black box on runners was still visible against the white, going away. It had slowed. The road climbed there, out of the low drifts round the Skeldmere toward the first of the hills, and the four horses were labouring on the slope. The riders round it had slowed too. And among them, at the back, on a tall grey southern horse that stood a hand higher than any of the others, one rider had turned in his saddle, and was looking back at the burning shore.

Karogane.

Muken could not see his face at that distance. He did not need to. He could see the shape of him against the white, tall and lean, with the hood down, sitting very still, looking back. Looking at him.

"*You think you can run?*"

The roar came out of him with everything he had left. It tore his throat. It went out across the burning shore and the trampled snow and the dead, out across the half-mile of white to the shore road, and he saw the tall grey rider hear it. He saw the head lift.

"*You think you can run from me?*"

The grey rider did not move.

"*I will not stand for this!*" Muken was walking now, then running, out past the burning carts onto the shore road with the salve tin clenched in his bleeding hand and no weapon at all. "*Do you hear me, Tenrai? I will not stand for it! If you do anything to her, anything, if you touch one hair on her head, you will regret it! You will regret it for the rest of your life, and I will make it a very short one!*"

Out on the hill, the tall grey rider turned his horse away, unhurried. He turned his back on the burning shore and the boy running on the road, and touched his heels to the tall grey horse, and rode on up the slope after the black carriage, and did not look back again.

Muken ran after him.

He ran for twenty paces on the frozen road, with his broken ribs grinding and the blood from his forearm spattering on the snow and the wolfhound bounding at his side, before Lorn Stark caught him.

### V · The Battle Is Won

Lorn came up on him from behind at a gallop and threw himself off his horse before it had stopped and caught Muken round the chest with both arms, from behind, and held on.

He had to hold on hard. Muken fought him. He did not know it was Lorn. He did not know anything. He twisted in the Stark's grip and drove his elbow back into Lorn's ribs and tried to throw him, roaring, and Lorn took the elbow with a grunt and did not let go, and set his feet on the frozen road the way his father had taught him, weight straight down through both heels, and held.

"*Muken!*"

The broken ribs ground together under Lorn's arms. Muken screamed. It went through the rage and came out the other side, and for a moment he went slack in the Stark's grip, and Lorn shifted his hold, and did not let go.

"*Muken. Stop.*" Lorn's voice was rough and very close to his ear. "*Stop. We can't. Not like this.*"

"*She's on that road...*"

"*I know she's on that road.*" Lorn's arms were shaking. There was blood on his face from the cut that had nearly taken his ear, and more of it on the arms locked round Muken's chest. "*I know. I saw the carts. I saw the Greymanes.*" He breathed. "*But look at you. Look at yourself. You've no sword. You've no horse under you. You've two ribs stove in and you're bleeding out of your arm like a stuck pig, and they've thirty men round that carriage and an hour's start, and if you run up that road after them on your feet, you'll die in the snow on it before noon. And then she's got nobody.*"

Muken said nothing. He was breathing in short, catching gasps. The salve tin was still in his fist.

"We've won," said Lorn Stark.

He said it more quietly. He did not loosen his arms.

"We've already won the battle. Do you hear me? It's over. The Keld are running. All of them, every way there is off the ice. Thornwall's got the north side and Ashmore's got the south and the Sennet clan's turned on the Haldorssons and they're killing each other over the Teeth pastures like their grandfathers did." His breath was hot on Muken's neck. "The Iron Fist's lying on his back on the Skeldmere with his chest stove in, and his herd's scattered, and there's four thousand Keld riders who'll be telling their children for fifty years about the boy who broke the iron hand with a song." He swallowed. "You did that. We won. It's done."

"*She's not done.*"

It came out of Muken in a whisper. It was barely a voice at all.

"No," said Lorn. "She's not." He was quiet a moment. "She's my king's sister. She closed the cut on my brother Rob's leg at the Foot with her own hands while he screamed at her, and then she sat with him till he slept." His arms tightened. "And I swear to you, by the sky that covers us, by my father's death and my honour, we'll get her back. Every Stark rider on this ice will ride for it. But not like this. Not on foot, bleeding, with no sword, after an hour's start, into the white, with the Tenrai waiting at the end of it." He breathed. "That's what they want. That's the whole of why they took her. Don't give it them."

Out on the hill, the long black carriage reached the top of the slope, and went over it, and was gone.

The riders round it went over after it, one by one, small and dark against the white. The tall grey horse went last. And then there was nothing on the shore road but the empty white of the lifting, and the snow, and the long double furrow of the carriage runners going south over the hill toward Kharven-Seat and the Hall of Verdicts and whatever was waiting there.

Muken stood on the road in Lorn Stark's arms and watched the place where it had gone.

He was not roaring any more. He was not fighting. He stood quite still, with the blood running down off his fingers onto the snow and the dented salve tin in his fist and the breath going up off him in short white plumes, and his face, under the blood, had gone very quiet.

Lorn knew that face. He had seen it in the long room, the morning the Moto came, in the three minutes after Kiyome spoke. He had seen it at the woodpile.

It frightened him more than the roaring had.

"Let go of me, Lorn," said Muken.

His voice had come back. It was the gallery voice, flat and even, every word set down like a foot on a bad ledge.

Lorn did not let go.

"I'm not going up the road," said Muken. "Not now. You're right." He did not take his eyes off the hill. "Let go of me."

Lorn let go, slowly, and stepped back, and stood with his hands open at his sides, ready.

Muken did not move.

Behind them, along the burning shore, riders were coming. He could hear them. The thorn-horn of Thornhaven, sounding somewhere out on the ice as the pikes came in off the lake. Ashmore hoofbeats on the shore road from the west. A woman's voice, low and clear as a struck bowl, calling his name. Kiyome. And under all of it, under the horns and the horses and the crackle of the burning carts and the moaning of the wounded in the snow, the world was still singing. Very quietly now. He could still hear it. He thought perhaps he would always hear it.

And in all that singing, somewhere far to the south, over the hill, on the Seat road, there was one note he would have known anywhere. Small and clear and fierce, and quite unbroken.

*Still alive,* he thought. *She's still alive. I can hear her.*

He opened his hand and looked at the salve tin in it. The mark. The crooked arrow pointing south.

Then he closed his hand on it again, and turned round, and walked back along the shore road through the trampled snow toward the burning carts and the wounded and the dead, to see who of his could still be saved.

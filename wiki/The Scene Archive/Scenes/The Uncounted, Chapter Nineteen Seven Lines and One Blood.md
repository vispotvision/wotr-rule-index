## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Nineteen: Seven Lines and One Blood

---

> *There was peace in the north that spring. The chroniclers who came after would count it: the first spring in forty years that no levy went south, the first in twenty that no Kōkan was hunted, the first in a thousand that the seven lines sat at one table under one name. They would call it the Spring of the Red Line, and write it in gold. The northern clans did not call it anything. They only said, when they spoke of it at all, that it was the year the boy came home from the ice with nobody.*
>
> *From the* Annals of the North-Forged Crown

---

### I · The Empty Palace

The Tenrai had kept the Kōkan's palace as a treasury.

It stood on the crown of the rock beside the Hall of Verdicts, older than the Hall and lower, a long gray house of the old Kōkan building with deep eaves and dark beams and floors of black stone worn smooth by a thousand years of feet. For three hundred years, since the Tenrai took the Seat, nobody had lived in it. They had filled its rooms with chests. Grain-tallies and tax-ledgers and the deeds to half the north, stacked to the beams in the old Kōkan bedchambers, and the iron-bound strongboxes of the Tenrai treasury in the great hall where the Kōkan kings had once sat to give judgment. They had put a lock on every door. They had never once lit a fire in it.

Muken lit one.

He did it himself, on the first night, in the small room at the back of the house that the Hallenfeld clerks, going through the chests, told him had been the Kōkan kings' own: a plain square room with a low ceiling and a single narrow window looking north, over the frozen river and the white country and the long dark line of the hills toward the Foot. There was a hearth in it, cold for three centuries. He cleaned it out with his own hands. Old ash, and a dead bird, and a nest of something that had been mice once. He carried up wood from the Hall kitchens. He built the fire the way his mother had taught him to build one in the galleries, small and close and economical, so that it would burn all night on very little, and lit it, and sat down in front of it on the black stone floor.

He was still in the red harness. He had not taken it off since the gatehouse. Nobody had dared suggest it.

The fire took. The small room filled slowly with warmth and the smell of pine and old dust burning. Somewhere outside, in the Hall square, Stark riders were singing round a brazier, low and rough and a little drunk, an old Kharven song about a woodpile. Somewhere further off, on the river, a horse was neighing. The draw-hum had come back at dusk, when Ilse Hallenfeld sent word to her draw-masters that the main could be mended, and it lay under everything again now, low and steady, under the rock and the ice and the walls, the way it had for three hundred years. Through the narrow window he could see the Seat come back to light: the worked lamps along the cliff stair and the terraces kindling again one by one in their brass cages, amber climbing the dark rock from the river to the crown like a slow tide coming in, until the whole fist of the Seat hung lit over the frozen Marrow again, and the aurora came out above it, green and violet, rippling. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. He looked at it for a while and found he could not feel anything about it at all.

He sat in front of the fire and listened to all of it.

And under all of it, the singing of the world. He could still hear it. He thought he always would. The stone of the old Kōkan house, low and patient and very old. The fire. The frozen river. The ridge, far off, three days to the north, and the cairns on the ridge, and the last cairn, with the small stone on top no bigger than a hen's egg.

He did not hear her note.

He had not heard it since the road out. Since he opened his hand in the place where the threads run and let it go. He had known he would not. He listened for it anyway. He sat in front of the fire in the Kōkan kings' own room on the first night of his reign over the whole of the north, in his dead mother's armor, and listened for the note of a thirteen-year-old girl with a needle, among all the notes of the world, and it was not there.

He had nobody.

He sat and understood it, slowly, the way a man understands that the cold has got into his feet. His mother was in the salt under Halcrest. Yusif was dead three years, somewhere on a road he had never been told the name of. His father he had never known, and was dead in the mud at Shimane, and was a monster. And his sister was on the ridge under the stones.

He had Lorn. He had Godfrey. He had Kiyome, who was his mother's sister, and who loved him, and whom he did not know. He had three thousand people who would die for him, and the whole of the north knelt at his feet, and the seven lines of the Moto waiting in the Hall of Verdicts in the morning to be told what he had decided they would become.

And he had nobody.

There was no one in all the world who had known him when he was nine years old at the top of a gallery shaft. No one who had slept on a pallet beside him in the cold so that neither of them froze. No one who had given him back half her share when he was not looking. No one who would give him an earful.

He sat in front of the fire with his hands open on his knees, and did not weep. He had done that, in the place where the threads run. It was done. He only sat, and let it go through him, into the black stone floor of the Kōkan house, into the ground, the way the Bound Flame had taught him, and held none of it longer than it needed to be held.

It needed to be held a very long time.

Toward midnight, there was a knock on the door.

He did not answer. The door opened anyway, very quietly, and Lorn Stark came in with a blanket over one arm and a bowl in his hands, and set the bowl down on the stone floor beside him without a word. Bone broth, with the fat still floating on it. His hands were clean now. Somebody had made him wash them. His brother was lying in the Tenrai keep on a table under a Stark cloak, with Mark sitting beside him, and the Waiting would begin at dawn, and Lorn had come here first, with a bowl. Then he sat down on the floor a little way off, against the wall, with the blanket in his lap, and drew up his knees, and said nothing.

After a while, Godfrey came in too, and sat down on Muken's other side.

After a while longer, Kiyome.

They did not speak. It was not the Waiting. Nobody had died. But it was something like it. They sat on the black stone floor of the Kōkan kings' room in front of the small fire, the four of them, and nobody said anything at all, and the Stark riders went on singing in the square about the woodpile, and the fire burned down, and Lorn got up once and fed it, and sat down again.

Muken drank the broth.

It was cold by then. He drank it anyway, all of it, and set the bowl down, and sat looking at the fire.

"The first bowl," said Lorn Stark quietly, after a long time, "goes to the one who can't fill it himself."

Muken did not answer.

But some time before dawn, he fell asleep, sitting up, with his back against the wall and his head against Godfrey Greymane's shoulder, and Godfrey did not move until morning.

### II · The Hall of Verdicts

The Hall of Verdicts was a long black room under a long black roof on the crown of the rock, and it had been built for one purpose, and it had served that purpose for a thousand years.

The floor was black stone, polished, and it fell away from the doors toward the far end in three wide shallow steps, so that whoever stood at the back of the hall looked down on whoever stood at the front. The walls were black stone. The beams were black oak, so old they had gone hard as iron. There were no windows. The only light came from the lamps, a hundred of them, worked lamps on draw in cages of black iron hung from the beams on long chains, each one burning a clear steady gold that laid the polish of the floor with a hundred small suns, and from a single round opening in the roof directly over the place at the bottom of the hall where those who came to be judged were made to stand, so that they stood in a shaft of gray daylight and everyone else stood in the dark.

At the far end, on a low dais of black stone, there were seven chairs.

Six of them stood in a long shallow arc, each in its line's colors. Silver for the Shirogane. Gold for the Tenrai. White for the Byakuya. Copper for the Akagane. Indigo for the Amagiri. Black, with the crimson hidden in it, for the Kurenai. And in the middle of the arc, a little forward of the others, a little higher, the seventh.

The red chair.

It was not grand. It was smaller than the others, if anything. A plain high-backed chair of red lacquer over dark wood, very old, with no ornament on it at all except, worked into the top of the back in fine black lines, a mark. A long down-stroke. A hook. A crossing. A small closed loop at the foot of it like a knot in a rope.

Nobody had sat in it in twenty years. Gimbzo never had. And before Gimbzo, for three hundred years, the Kōkan kings had sat in it only on sufferance, under the Tenrai, in a hall the Tenrai held.

Muken stood in front of it for a long moment, in the morning, before the others came in.

Then he sat down.

---

They came in.

He watched them come, from the red chair, in the lamplight. All of them. Down the three shallow steps of the black floor and into the dark of the hall, and found their places, and stood.

The Banner Houses first. Lorn Stark in his father's mail, with the Stark wolf on his breast and his father's axe across his back, who stood at the front on the left with his weight even on both feet and his jaw working on the left side. Godfrey Greymane in his new gray cloak, very straight, beside him. Hisae of Thornwall, small and upright in gray felt, with her Byakuya eyes. Edric Ashmore in brown and russet with the boar on his breast and his broken nose and his look of faint amazement. Ilse Hallenfeld in her dark coat lined with wolf, with her ledger under her arm and ink on her gloves.

And the Keld.

They had brought Aksel Keld in on a litter. Four of his own captains carried it, the ones who had come in off the tundra under a white cloth when they heard the Seat had fallen, to ask what the king of the north meant to do with their warlord. He lay on the litter under a heap of reindeer hides with his chest strapped and his ruined face swollen and purple from the brow down, the nose set crooked, the lips split and scabbed. His right arm lay on top of the hides. The stump of it was bare, the old wrist-end, ridged with scar. There was no iron hand. There would never be another. Borin Ironheart had been asked, and had said that some things were better left unmade.

His narrow black eyes were open, and on the red chair.

Then the seven lines.

Kiyome Byakuya, in her near-white, with the glass beads of her veil pinned back and her sword at her hip without its knot, went to the white chair and stood beside it. She did not sit. Tetsugen Akagane, broad as a door, in his copper coat, went to the copper chair and stood beside it. Hayato Amagiri went to the indigo chair. His uncle had not come. His uncle had been on the ice of the Marrow with the other Tenrai lords who ran, and had knelt to Edric Ashmore, and was in a room in the Tenrai keep with a Stark rider at the door. Hayato stood beside the indigo chair with his restless fingers folding and unfolding the corner of his rain-cape, and the juniper still caught in the toggle.

Sumie Kurenai came last of the Moto lords who had a chair to come to.

She was a tall dark woman of fifty in black and ash, with the crimson of her line hidden in the lining of her sleeves, and she walked down the black floor of the Hall of Verdicts the way she had walked down it a month ago to carry the Kurenai blade to a block in the square where Ulf Stark and Wystan Greymane knelt. She did not look at Lorn or Godfrey as she passed them. She went to the black chair and stood beside it. And then she turned, and looked at the boy in the red chair, and went down on one knee on the black stone.

She did not say anything. The Kurenai never did. They were the executioners' line. They had carried out the verdicts of the Hall for a thousand years, whatever the verdicts were, and asked no questions, and been hated for it, and borne it.

She knelt there with her head bowed. And then she stood, and stood beside the black chair, and waited.

Two chairs were empty. The silver. The gold.

And then the doors at the top of the hall opened again, and two more people came in, and the whole Hall turned to look.

### III · The Ones Who Were Chosen

The first was a young woman of perhaps twenty-five, in silver-gray.

She came down the black floor slowly, alone, with her hands clasped in front of her. She was small, like her sister. Gray-eyed, like her sister, with the same coin-bright rings in the gray. But her hair was not gray. It was black, long, braided down her back, and her face was not hard as a hatchet. It was a young face, pale, set very carefully, and it had been crying, and had stopped.

Nanami Shirogane.

Shizuha's sister. Thirty-six years her junior, the last child of a mother who had not expected another. She had been at the white house in the Shiraki hills for the last ten years, sent there by her sister to be raised by the Byakuya, because the Shirogane and the Byakuya had kept a fostering between them for three hundred years, each sending a child to the other, so that each house would always have someone inside the other's walls. She had not been at the Seat when the girl came up the stair. She had not known. Kiyome had sent for her, three days ago, before the Seat fell, and told her.

She stopped beside the silver chair, and did not sit, and looked at the boy in the red chair with her sister's eyes, and her face did not change.

The second was a man of forty in plain gray.

He was tall and lean, with a long pale face and very pale eyes, and the whole Hall stirred when it saw him, because for a moment, in the lamplight, he was Karogane. Then he came closer and he was not. He was older. Grayer. His face was lined in places Karogane's was not, round the eyes and the mouth, the lines of a man who had laughed more in his life than his brother. And he walked down the black floor of the Hall of Verdicts the way a man walks who has been told he is going to his own hanging and has decided to do it with his back straight.

Shiro Tenrai.

Karogane's elder brother. The Tenrai keep's steward of records for twenty years, who had kept Kagenori's ledgers and never once been asked to put his hand to anything else. Hayato had found him in the keep on the afternoon the Seat fell, sitting at his desk in the record-room with the door open and his hands folded on a closed ledger, waiting. *I knew one of you would come,* he had said. *I've been keeping the books for this house for twenty years. I know what it owes.*

He stopped beside the gold chair.

And beside him, holding his hand, there was a boy.

Ten, perhaps. Small and thin and pale, in gray like his father, with the long Tenrai face and the very pale Tenrai eyes, and the faint ring-lines of the seven lines' blood in them. He held his father's hand and looked round the Hall of Verdicts with those very pale eyes, at the lamps and the black beams and the seven chairs and the boy in red in the middle of them. He did not look frightened. He looked as though he were counting something.

Nobody in the Hall knew his name, then. Shiro had not given it. It would be many years before anyone thought to ask.

---

Muken looked at the two of them for a long time from the red chair.

He had chosen them. He and Kiyome, in the long room at the Foot, on the night before they marched, with the map of the Seat on the table and the Dawi asleep on the floor. *When it falls,* Kiyome had said, *the silver and the gold will need someone to sit in them. You can leave them empty. Or you can burn them. Or you can fill them.* She had looked at him a long time. *If you leave them empty, someone will come for them. If you burn them, someone will come for you. If you fill them...* She had stopped.

*If I fill them,* he had said, *with someone who has every reason to hate me, and nothing to gain by it.*

He looked at them now, in the lamplight, at the foot of the dais. Nanami Shirogane, with her sister's eyes and her black braid and her careful face, standing beside the silver chair. Shiro Tenrai, with his brother's face and his own lines in it, standing beside the gold, with his son's hand in his.

They looked back at him.

They did not look happy. They looked like what they were: two people who had been brought into the Hall of Verdicts on the morning their brother and their sister were to be judged in it, and told they were to sit in the chairs their brother and their sister had stood behind, and understood exactly what that meant. That the chair was not a reward. It was a sentence of its own. That they would sit in it for the rest of their lives under the eyes of a boy in red who had every reason to watch them, and that every Shirogane and every Tenrai who had ever hated the red line would come to them now, and whisper, and they would have to send them away. Over and over. Forever.

They looked disappointed. They looked afraid. And they knew. Their fates had been spelled out for them the moment they walked through the doors, and there was nothing in the world they could do about it but stand there, beside their chairs, and let it be done.

Muken understood that look. He had worn it himself, in the long room, the morning Kiyome drew the mark in the dust.

### IV · The Verdict

They brought in Shizuha and Karogane together.

The Kurenai brought them. That was the old law. Four Kurenai in black and ash, two to each, walking them down the black floor of the Hall from the doors to the bottom of the shallow steps, to the place under the round opening in the roof where the shaft of gray daylight came down. And left them standing in it, in the light, with everyone else in the dark.

Shizuha Shirogane stood very upright in her silver-gray gown, cleaned, with the frost-work down the front and her short gray hair perfectly in place. Her hands were bound in front of her with white cord. She did not look at the boy in the red chair. She looked at the silver chair beside him, and at her sister standing beside it, for a long time. Nanami did not look away. Neither of them said anything.

Karogane stood beside her in his plain gray, with his hands bound, and his long pale face quite still again. Whatever had cracked in it on the stair had been mended. He looked at the gold chair, and at his brother standing beside it, and at the boy holding his brother's hand. Something moved, very slightly, at the corner of his mouth. Then it was gone.

The Hall was silent.

Muken stood up from the red chair.

He came down off the dais, slowly, in the red harness, with Tsugigane at his hip, down the three low steps of black stone, and stood at the edge of the shaft of gray light, so that they were in the light and he was in the dark, and looked at them.

He did not speak for a long time.

He was listening. He could hear them both. Every note. Karogane's, flat and still and tired, the note of a knife that had just found out it was only a knife. Shizuha's, small and dry and clear and very hard, and under it, very far down, a thousand years old, the long sour note of the wound. He could hear it going out of her into the Hall, toward the silver chair, toward her sister.

*End the woman, if you must,* the Bound Flame had said. *But end the wound with her. Close it.*

"Shizuha Shirogane," said Muken. "Karogane Tenrai."

His voice was quiet. It filled the Hall anyway, the way a stone fills a pool when you drop it in, every word set down with that weight in it that Kiyome had heard in the long room, and the hundred lamps in their iron cages on their long chains swung very slightly, all together, as though a wind had gone through.

"For your treason," said Muken, "against Kharven, and against its people."

He let it sit.

"Against the long houses, whose grain you sent south while their children ate bark. Against the Banner Houses, whose fathers you put on the block in the square outside this door. Against the seven lines, whom you set against each other for a thousand years so that one of you could sit in a silver chair." His voice did not rise. "And against a surgeon of thirteen, with a needle in her apron, who had never in her life hurt anyone who did not need closing. Whom you had dragged into a silver room on her knees, and killed, and sent home to her brother down the river on a pike."

Nanami Shirogane closed her eyes.

"You will be executed," said Muken.

The Hall did not move.

"Tomorrow, at the first bell. In the square outside this door, where Ulf Stark and Wystan Greymane knelt." He looked from one of them to the other. "And all your possessions, your lands and your houses and your people, will come under the crown of the north. Under my hand." He paused. "Not to be kept. To be given back. To whoever sits in the chairs you stood behind, when they have shown me they can be trusted to hold them."

He turned his head, very slightly, toward the silver chair, and the gold.

"And the unification of the Moto," said Muken, "and all its territories, the seven lines and the Banner Houses and every clan and every fire under the Sky between the Polar Reach and the southern border, is to be ratified. Here. Today. In front of all of you." He turned back. "Seven lines, and one blood. Under one crown."

Shizuha Shirogane looked at him at last.

The coin-bright eyes, in the shaft of gray daylight, fixed on the boy in red in the dark at the edge of it. And for a long moment she said nothing at all.

"You'll do it yourself?" she said. "Tomorrow."

"Yes," said Muken.

"Not the Kurenai."

"No."

"Why?" said Shizuha Shirogane.

Muken looked at her.

"Because I passed the verdict," he said. "And I'll not put it in someone else's hand and stand back and watch. That's what you did. In your silver room." He was quiet a moment. "I'll not be like you. Not in that. Not in anything."

Something went across the small hard face in the shaft of light. Very fast. Gone.

"And her," said Shizuha. She did not look round. "My sister. In my chair."

"Nanami Shirogane," said Muken, "will sit the silver chair. If she will." He did not look at Nanami. "And Shiro Tenrai the gold. If he will. And they'll sit in them under my eye for the rest of their lives, and every Shirogane and every Tenrai who comes to them in the night and whispers that the red line must be ended will be sent away. By them. In their own voices." He held the old woman's eyes. "Every one. For as long as they live. Until there is nobody left in either line who remembers why the whispering started."

He stepped forward, one pace, into the edge of the shaft of gray light.

"That's what I'm going to do with your wound," said Muken. "The one you were born in. The one your mother was born in, and hers. The one you meant to pass on to your sister with your chair." He looked at her. "I'm going to close it. And I'm going to make her hold the edges together for the rest of her life, until it heals."

Shizuha Shirogane stared at him.

And then, for the first time since the silver room, since the girl on her knees, the small hard upright woman in the silver-gray gown did something nobody in that Hall had ever seen her do.

She looked away.

### V · The Ratification

They did it in the afternoon, in the Hall of Verdicts, with the lamps lit and the doors open and the whole square outside full of people, Starks and Greymanes and Thornwall pikes and Ashmore riders and the women from the long houses who had walked down the Marrow behind the army, standing in the snow in the cold to hear it.

The Hallenfeld clerks wrote it down.

Ilse Hallenfeld wrote it herself, at a small table at the side of the Hall, in her ledger, in her fine cold hand, word for word as it was spoken. She did not let anyone else touch the pen. *A debt is a debt,* she said, when Hayato asked her why. *This is the biggest one I've ever seen recorded. I'll not have a clerk smudge it.*

They came forward one at a time to the foot of the dais, where the boy in red sat in the red chair, and swore.

Lorn Stark first. For the Starks of Hollenmark. He came down the shallow steps of the black floor and stood at the foot of the dais in his father's mail and put his right hand flat on his chest, and closed it into a fist against his breastbone, and pressed it there once, hard, and held it.

"By the sky that covers us," said Lorn Stark. "House Stark. To the red chair, and to the man in it." His voice was rough. "My father would have. And Rob."

Godfrey Greymane, for the Greymanes of Ridgewall. "The ridge does not move," he said, very quietly. "We'll hold it. For you, and for whoever comes after." He looked up at Muken. "My father would have, too."

Hisae of Thornwall, small and upright, Byakuya-born. She swore for Thornhaven and the Thornwood and the corridors, and when she had finished she did something none of the Banner lords had done. She turned, and looked at Kiyome Byakuya standing beside the white chair, and the two of them, the old woman and the not-so-old one, both born in the white house in the Shiraki hills, both with the same gray eyes, said together, very softly, so that only the front of the Hall could hear it:

"*And the red line before the white. Until the end of the red line.*"

Edric Ashmore, for the Ashmore marches. He swore with his hand on the black stone of the dais, as he had sworn with his hand on the junction stone below Ashbury, and looked faintly amazed at himself all the way through.

Ilse Hallenfeld, for the junction and the counting-house. She did not come forward. She swore from her small table at the side of the Hall, without looking up from her ledger, and wrote it down as she said it.

And then the Keld.

They carried Aksel Keld down the shallow steps on his litter, the four captains, and set him down at the foot of the dais. And the Iron Fist of the Keld lay on his back under the reindeer hides with his chest strapped and his ruined face swollen and purple and the bare stump of his right arm lying on top of the hides, and looked up at the boy in the red chair with his narrow black eyes.

He did not try to rise. He could not have.

"The Keld will swear," said Aksel Keld.

His voice was a thick wet rasp through the broken nose and the split lips. It carried anyway.

"To you," he said. "Not to the red chair. Not to the seven lines. Not to the Seat." The narrow black eyes held Muken's. "To you. The boy on the ice. Who broke the iron hand with a song, and stood over me with his sword up, and stopped." He breathed, a short catching breath. "We'll swear to that man. On one condition."

The Hall was very quiet.

"Stay worth following," said Aksel Keld.

Muken looked down at him a long moment from the red chair.

"That's an honest condition," he said. "I accept it."

Something moved in the ruined face on the litter. It might have been a smile. It hurt him to do it, whatever it was. He did it anyway.

"Then the Keld are yours," said the Iron Fist. "While you're worth it."

---

The seven lines came last.

Kiyome Byakuya, for the white line. She came to the foot of the dais and went down on both knees on the black stone, and laid her sword across them with its hilt toward him, without its knot, the way she had in the long room, and bowed her head.

"Purity before power," she said. "Peace before the blade. And the red line before the white." She lifted her head. "I've said it. You know the rest."

Tetsugen Akagane, for the copper line. He did not kneel. He had said he would not. He stood at the foot of the dais, broad as a door, and put his scarred hand flat on his chest, the Stark way, and held it there.

"The Akagane never swore to the red," he said, in his flat iron voice. "We only ever obeyed it. I'll not obey you, boy." His heavy face did not change. "I'll follow you. It's a different thing. You'll find out how different the first time you're wrong."

"I'm counting on it," said Muken.

Hayato Amagiri, for the indigo line, in his uncle's place. He swore in the plain working cadence he used for distances, quickly, the words tumbling over each other, as though slowing down would lose him the end of it, and at the end of it, he looked up at the boy in the red chair and said, "Two days and a road, sire. Always. Whatever you need."

Sumie Kurenai, for the black line. She came forward and knelt, and said the oath of her house in a low dark voice that nobody in the Hall had ever heard her use, the old oath of the executioners' line, that they would carry the verdicts of the red chair wherever the red chair sent them, and ask no questions. And when she had finished, she stayed on her knees, and lifted her head, and looked at the boy in red.

"You'll not need us tomorrow," she said. "At the first bell."

"No," said Muken.

The Kurenai woman looked at him a long moment.

"No one's ever said that to us," said Sumie Kurenai. "In a thousand years." She got up, slowly. "I'm not sure what we're for, now."

"You'll find out," said Muken. "I think it'll be closing things."

She did not understand him. He could see that. But she bowed, very low, lower than she had ever bowed in her life, and went back to the black chair.

And then the silver, and the gold.

Nanami Shirogane came forward to the foot of the dais. She stood there, small and pale, with her black braid and her sister's eyes, and her hands clasped very tightly in front of her, and looked up at him. She did not kneel.

"I'll sit it," she said. Her voice shook, once, and steadied. "The silver chair. If that's what you've decided."

"It's what I've decided," said Muken. "If you'll have it."

"I don't want it."

"I know."

"She's my sister." Nanami's voice did not shake this time. "Tomorrow you're going to..." She stopped. "And I'm going to sit in her chair. Under your eye. For the rest of my life."

"Yes."

Nanami Shirogane looked at him for a very long time.

"I'll sit it," she said at last. "And I'll send them away. Every one who comes to me in the night. In my own voice." She lifted her chin, a degree, exactly as her sister did. "And one day, when you're dead, I'll have done it so long that I'll have forgotten how to do anything else. That's what you want, isn't it."

"That's what I want," said Muken.

She swore. The oath of the silver line, in a voice like her sister's, small and dry and clear. And at the end of it, she went to the silver chair, and sat down in it, and folded her hands in her lap, and looked straight ahead, and did not look at anyone, and did not cry.

Shiro Tenrai came forward with his son's hand in his.

He swore for the gold line without being asked, steadily, the long pale face quite composed, the lines round his eyes very deep in the lamplight. At the end of it he said only, "I've kept the books for twenty years, Your Grace. I know what the Tenrai owe. I'll see it paid." And he went to the gold chair and sat down in it, and the boy stood beside him with his hand still in his father's and his very pale eyes going round the Hall, counting.

And it was done.

Ilse Hallenfeld laid down her pen.

"Ratified," she said, into the silence. "Witnessed. Recorded." She blotted the page, very carefully. "Seven lines. Six Banner Houses. One crown." She looked up at the boy in the red chair with her eyes like gray ice. "It's the largest single entry the Hallenfeld counting-house has ever made, Your Grace. I'll want it noted that I made it."

---

### VI · The First Bell

He did it himself, at the first bell, in the square outside the Hall of Verdicts, where Ulf Stark and Wystan Greymane had knelt.

He did not make a speech. Nobody made a speech. The square was full, and silent, and cold, and the breath of three thousand people went up over it in a single white cloud. The Kurenai brought them out. Shizuha first, in her silver-gray, and then Karogane, in his plain gray. Neither of them said anything. Shizuha looked once, at the end, at the doors of the Hall, where her sister stood in the doorway in silver with her hands clasped in front of her. Then she looked away.

Muken drew Tsugigane.

It sang in his hand. Clear and high and sweet, with the silver thread running through it.

He did not use Hibi. He did not need to. There was nothing dead to find. He did it with the edge and the weight and the long clean line of the cut that Yusif Stark had taught him in the back of a worked-out gallery with a stick of ash, the way a thing is done that must be done, once each, cleanly, and then is over.

He did not look away from either of them.

When it was done, he wiped the blade on a cloth Kiyome handed him, and looked at it. The gray steel. The bright seam of silver by the guard. There was no mark on it. There never would be. He sheathed it, and turned, and walked back up the steps of the Hall of Verdicts past Nanami Shirogane in the doorway, who did not look at him, and did not move, and stood there in the cold in her silver until the Kurenai had finished, and the square had emptied, and the snow had begun, very softly, to fall.

---

### VII · The Spring

There was peace in the north.

It came the way the thaw came, slowly, and then all at once. The draw-hum came back under the Seat and stayed there, and the Hallenfeld clerks went through the Tenrai treasury chest by chest and sent the grain-tallies back to the long houses they had been taken from, and the grain after them, up the Marrow on sledges and down Hayato's indigo roads on mules, with a Yukari witness at each end counting the sacks. The levies did not go south. Not that spring, nor the next. The Stark riders went home to Hollenmark, carrying Rob Stark with them on a sledge under a cairn of his own shields, and the Greymanes to Ridgewall and the Thornwall pikes to Thornhaven, and the Ashmore horse to Ashbury, and the Keld to their fires on the tundra with their warlord on a litter, and in every long house in the north that winter, when the wood went on the fire, someone told the story of the boy who came home from the ice.

The thaw, when it came, was green. Nobody alive had seen the north green. The moss came up first along the river in the lee of the rock, a soft brilliant green like spilled paint against the last gray ice, and then, on the south face of the Seat's cliff where the sun lay longest, small white flowers nobody could name, thousands of them, in the cracks of the stone. The old woman with no teeth, who had come down the Marrow behind the army on a sledge and refused to go home, sat on a bench in the Hall square in the sun and looked at them for a whole afternoon. *When the grass comes,* she said, to nobody, and laughed, a cracked old sound like a hinge, and wiped her eyes.

They built a woodpile in the Hall square at the Seat. Nobody ordered it. Somebody started it, the boy with the red ears, the day after the first bell, splitting pine from the Tenrai stables, and the others joined him, and by the end of the week it ran the whole length of the Hall's south wall as high as a man's shoulder, every split end of it pale in the light. Nobody burned from it. It was not for burning. It was only there.

And at the end of the row of cairns on the ridge above Kharven's Foot, someone kept a fire.

Nobody knew who. Nobody ever saw them. But every night that winter, and the next, a small fire burned at the foot of the last cairn, the one with the stone no bigger than a hen's egg on top, small and close and economical, the way a fire is built in a salt gallery to last all night on very little. And every morning it had burned down to ash, and every evening it was lit again.

The king of the north lived alone in the old Kōkan house on the crown of the rock, in the small square room at the back with the single window looking north, and slept on the floor in front of the hearth, and practiced on rope.

He was sixteen.

He had a crown he had never asked for, and a red chair, and a sword with a surgeon in it, and seven lines and six Banner Houses at his feet, and the whole of the north between the Polar Reach and the southern border under his hand.

He had nobody.

He sat in the red chair in the Hall of Verdicts and listened to the seven lines argue, and let them say all of it, and said very little, and when he did speak, the hundred lamps on their iron chains swung very slightly, all together. And sometimes, when the arguing was done and the Hall was empty, he sat on in the red chair alone in the lamplight, and listened to the singing of the world, the long low humming of everything, and the fine bright notes of the seven lines in their seven chairs, all different, all held together for the first time in a thousand years.

And among them, very faint, very far down, two notes he listened for every day.

One was silver. Small and dry and very careful. A young woman in a silver chair, sending someone away in the night in her own voice. Over and over.

The other was gold. Very pale. A boy of ten with the long Tenrai face, standing beside his father's chair with his hand in his father's, looking round the Hall of Verdicts with his very pale eyes. Counting.

Muken could not hear what the boy was counting. Knowledge revealed what a thing was. It did not tell you what it would become. That was Temporality's labor, and Temporality did not show you a road you had not yet walked.

But he listened to it. Every day.

There was peace in the north.

He did not know how long it would last.

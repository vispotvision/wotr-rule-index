## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Seven: Crows

---

The word went out of Kharven's Foot the way word always went in the north: faster than horses.

It went out in a teamster's mouth to the Marrow crossing, and in a courier's satchel to Hollenmark, and in a frightened Tenrai deserter's to the first village down the southern road, where he drank himself senseless in the common room and told everyone who would listen about the wall that fell down on its own and the boy with the gold in his eye. It went out by crow. Ulf Stark's crows went west to the Ridgewall and north to the Keld fires, and two of them went south and east, one to the Mahuo post at Sum-gol and one past it, over the low country and the river and the long climb of cedar forest, to a valley where the trees stood so close and so tall that the light came down between them in thin pale bars.

And it went out on a horse, south down the Kessen road, with a woman on it in black-and-gold Tenrai lamellar with her left arm strapped to her chest and her palms bound in bloody linen, who did not stop to sleep for four days.

By the end of the first week, everyone who mattered in the Inner World knew that Kharven's Foot had fallen.

By the end of the second, a few of them knew who had taken it.

### II · The Hall of Verdicts

Kagenori Tenrai Moto did not shout. He had not shouted in thirty years. He sat in the gold chair in the Hall of Verdicts, under the Tenrai banner, with his narrow face perfectly still and his mouth closed like a purse, and the whole of his rage came off him in a slow, steady pressure that made the lamps in the great iron ring overhead burn low and blue.

"Commoners," he said.

He said it softly. It went round the hall and came back down out of the dome.

"We lost," said Kagenori Tenrai, "to commoners."

Four of the chairs were filled. Shizuha Shirogane sat in silver at his right, upright and grey and hatchet-faced, and had not spoken since the hall was opened. Sumie Kurenai sat in black across from him, very pale. The Akagane chair, the Amagiri chair and the Byakuya chair were empty. So was the seventh, the red one, under its dust, with its blind enamel lion. Nobody looked at it. Everybody knew exactly where it was.

In the centre of the circle of script, where prisoners knelt, there was a stool, and on the stool sat Shigure Tenrai Moto.

She had ridden four days to get here and had not changed her armour. The black lamellar was stiff with dried blood down the whole left side, where the cut at the top of her shoulder had bled and bled and been bound and bled again. Her left arm was strapped across her chest. Her palms were wrapped in linen gone brown. Her face was grey with exhaustion and loss of blood, and the rings in her eyes turned very slowly, dimly, like the last ripples on a pond long after the stone has gone in. She had told her story once, from the beginning to the end, in a flat level voice, without being asked a single question. Now she sat and waited.

"They took our fort," Kagenori said. "The Foot. The gate on the Kessen road. Two hundred of our house-guard on a ridge that has never fallen to anybody, in a fortress our own grandfathers built. And they took it in a morning. Stark and Greymane, with their old men and their boys." He did not move. "Led by a tokenless. A *tokenless*. Do you understand what that does? Do you understand how that makes us look? Every village between the Thresh and the shield is going to hear it before the month's out. The great Moto, the seven lines and the one blood, the house that reads the world. Beaten off its own wall by a boy who can't sign for a sack of meal."

He let it lie.

"And the boy is sixteen," he said. "Sixteen years old." His eyes moved, slowly, toward Sumie Kurenai. "Isn't that a coincidence."

Sumie did not look away.

"It isn't a coincidence," she said.

Her voice was very quiet. She had not slept, either, by the look of her. She sat in the black chair with her thin hands closed in her lap and her ink-dark eyes on the woman on the stool.

"Think about it, Kagenori," Sumie said. "Think about it properly, for once, instead of how it looks. Sixteen years. That's when it would have been. If one of them got away. If the Stark was right, at the end, kneeling there with his blood on the script." She looked at the stool. "And then think about who he fought. Shigure is the best blade the Tenrai have put in the field in a generation. She was Gimbzo's right hand for six years. There aren't ten people in the Inner World who could put her on her knees in a straight fight, and every one of them is a name we know." She drew a breath. "Nobody beats Shigure that easily. Nobody nameless. It's the Kōkan child. It has to be."

"He didn't beat me *easily*," said Shigure Tenrai Moto.

They all looked at her.

"He beat me," she said. "I'll not dress it. He put me on my knees and he put my own blade into my shoulder. But not easily. Not by being stronger. He isn't. He's barely woken. He's running on a reinforcement a first-year Tenrai cadet would be beaten for." Her voice was flat and grey and level. "He beat me because I couldn't read him. I looked at him, and there was nothing. Not a line. Not a thread. Not one next moment. In twenty years of fighting I have never once looked at anything that lives and seen nothing at all." She turned her dim turning eyes on Sumie. "Your register couldn't read them. Neither could I."

Sumie Kurenai had gone very white.

"And then the eye opened," said Shigure.

The lamps in the great iron ring overhead dipped, all at once, very slightly.

"I saw it," Shigure said. "The left eye. In the middle of the fight. Gold came up through the iris, like a crack running through slate." She was quiet for a moment. "I've seen that eye before, Kagenori. Once. In a cellar in Halcrest, sixteen years ago, on a woman with no name the clerks would take, the night Gimbzo went down with a lamp to see what he'd caught." She held his gaze. "The boy has that eye. And he has Gimbzo's face. I'd know that face if I were dead. I stood behind it for six years."

Nobody spoke.

Shizuha Shirogane, in the silver chair, turned her head very slowly and looked for the first time at the empty red chair with its blind lion.

"Then we have to be rid of them," Kagenori said. "Both of them. Quickly."

His voice had not risen. But the pressure coming off him had, and the blue lamps flattened in their bowls, and Shigure on her stool closed her eyes for a moment against it.

"If he's what you say he is," Kagenori said, "if he really is Gimbzo's get on a Kōkan woman, with the Kōkan eye in his head, then he's going to *grow.* Every fight he walks into is going to wake more of it. Every one. He woke the eye on the stair of the Foot. What does he wake in the next one? And the one after that?" He leaned forward, very slightly, in the gold chair. "He isn't a boy. He's a Kōkan who doesn't know he's a Kōkan yet. And the day he finds out, he'll walk into this hall and sit in that chair, and every one of us will have to kneel to him on our own script."

He sat back.

"Damn the Starks," he said, very softly. "Damn the Greymanes. We should have ended both those houses before the war, root and branch, and every cousin who could sit a horse. I said so at the time. Gimbzo wouldn't hear it. He wanted them for his front line." His mouth thinned. "And now look."

He was quiet for a moment, looking at nothing. Then something moved in his narrow face, and his eyes narrowed with it.

"The Ridgewall," he said.

Shizuha Shirogane turned her head back toward him.

"It all makes sense now," Kagenori said. "Why the Ridgewall's been left to fall. Three years, and the Greymanes have pulled their men back off the western wall a few at a time, and we've all said it was because they had no men left to hold it with." His fingers tapped once on the arm of the gold chair. "They had men. They had men enough to put three hundred on the Foot. They've been letting the wall go on purpose. Letting Undaar-Keth come through the passes in the west and raid down into our own lands, so that we'd have to send the house-guard west to meet them. So that when the north rose, we'd be fighting on two fronts. Three." He looked at Shizuha. "They've been opening the back door for three years and we've been thanking them for closing it."

"It's what I would have done," said Shizuha Shirogane.

It was the first thing she had said. Kagenori looked at her for a long moment, and then, very slightly, inclined his head.

"Then we do what they won't expect," he said. "We don't fight the north. Not yet. We buy it."

He turned to the Tenrai steward standing at the edge of the hall.

"Send to Ashmore," he said. "To Thornwall. To Hallenfeld. And to the Keld fires, if any crow of ours can find them. Bring their lords here. Under the house's protection, with the house's gifts, and every courtesy the seven lines can show." His voice was very soft. "Ashmore wants to sell its grain to whoever pays. We'll pay. Thornwall wants the Ore Council off its back. We'll take it off. Hallenfeld wants its tariff-master back out of our cells. They can have him." He paused. "And the Keld want to be left alone. We'll promise to leave them alone. For ever. In writing."

"And then?" said Sumie Kurenai quietly.

"And then the north has two houses. Stark and Greymane. Against everyone." Kagenori looked at the empty chairs. "And then we call the whole council. All seven lines. All of them." His eyes went to the Akagane chair, the Amagiri chair, the Byakuya chair, standing empty in their copper and their indigo and their near-white. "Every one of them. In this hall. Whether they wish to come or not."

He stood.

"Shigure," he said.

She opened her eyes.

"You let him live," said Kagenori Tenrai Moto.

It was not a question. Shigure did not pretend it was.

"I did," she said.

"Why?"

She looked up at him from the stool, grey-faced, with her ruined hands in her lap and her strapped arm across the gold banner on her breast.

"Because you'd have wanted to see him first," she said. "Before you killed him. You'd have wanted to know for certain." Her dim eyes held his. "And because I did."

Kagenori looked at her for a long time.

Then he walked out of the hall, and did not look back, and the blue lamps in the great iron ring slowly, slowly, came up to their ordinary gold again behind him.

### III · The White House

The Byakuya kept their seat in the old city below the Moto hold, in a house of pale stone with a garden of white gravel and glass, and Kiyome Byakuya had not left it in eleven days.

She had not been to the Hall of Verdicts since the night of Torvald Stark. She had not answered the summons that came the morning after the Foot fell, or the one that came the morning after that. She sat in the long pale room at the back of her house that her grandmother had used for prayer, with the shutters open on the garden and the glass beads of the veil laid on the floor beside her, and did not eat very much, and waited.

When Tetsugen Akagane and Hayato Amagiri came to her, they came at night, by the garden gate, without servants, in plain cloaks over their house colours, the way men come to a house when they do not want it known that they have come.

She received them in the prayer room. There were three cushions on the floor and a single lamp, and a pot of tea that nobody touched. Tetsugen lowered his great bulk onto his cushion with a grunt, like a bull lying down, and sat with his huge copper-ringed hands on his knees. Hayato did not sit at all at first. He stood by the open shutters with his restless fingers moving at his sides and looked out into the dark garden, and only sat when Kiyome looked at him, and then on the very edge of the cushion, as if he expected to have to leave in a hurry.

"You heard," Kiyome said.

"The whole city's heard." Tetsugen's voice was a low rumble. "Shigure rode into the outer court four days off the Kessen road with her arm hanging and her hands cut to the bone, and told it to the gate guard before she told it to Kagenori. Half the Tenrai house-guard will have it in every tavern from here to the river by morning."

"A Kōkan," Hayato said quietly. "A living Kōkan. With the eye."

Kiyome closed her eyes.

They did not know. Neither of them knew, not really. They were of the Akagane and the Amagiri, and to them the Kōkan were a story the house told itself about a chair. But the Byakuya had been something else to the Kōkan, once, a very long time ago, before the Recension and the long diminishment and the closing of the book. The near-white line had stood behind the red one. Its veils and its glass and its halos had been the Kōkan's shield, in the oldest days, the ones nobody wrote down any more, and the oaths of that protection had been spoken over every Byakuya child for a thousand years after there was nobody left to protect, because nobody had ever thought to stop. Kiyome had said them herself, at nine years old, kneeling on this floor, in this room, with her grandmother's hand on her head. *Purity before power. Peace before the blade. And the red line before the white, until the end of the red line.*

She had never once in her life imagined that the end of the red line might not have come.

"I'll not go to the council," she said.

Hayato's head came round.

"Kiyome."

"I'll not go. Not to this one. Not to any of them." She opened her eyes. "You know what he's going to do in there. You know what he's going to ask for. He's going to bring the northern lords to the hall with gifts in their hands and buy them out from under the Starks one by one. And then he's going to call the whole house together and stand up in front of the seven chairs and ask us to vote to hunt down a sixteen-year-old boy, and his sister, and kill them both, and close the book again." Her hands had tightened on her knees. "I sat in that hall and watched him take a man's head on the script for telling us the truth. I'll not sit there and watch him do it again."

Tetsugen was silent. Hayato looked at the floor.

"Then where?" Tetsugen said at last. "If not the hall. Where do you mean to go?"

Kiyome looked at him.

"East," she said. "To the Yukari."

Hayato let out a breath. "Kiyome. You can't be serious."

"I'm entirely serious."

"The Zettari are there." His fingers had begun moving again. "The Sovereign of Kushara has been sitting at Naiser Yukari's table for a week, by every report. Making peace. Making marriages. Three Moto lords riding into the Yukari enclave while the Zettari are still in it?" He shook his head. "They'll think it's an embassy. Or an assassination. Or a defection. And they'll be right about one of them."

"The Zettari may still be there," Kiyome agreed. "And they may not. Either way, Hayato, it's the only door left in the Inner World that isn't Kagenori's." She leaned forward. "The Yukari keep the spirit side. They've always known more about the Kōkan than they've ever said. And if a Kōkan child has come back into the world, they'll know it before anyone. They may know it already."

Tetsugen rubbed his bald head slowly with one enormous hand.

"And what do we say to them," he said, "when we get there? Three Moto lords. Our house just lost its war with the Zettari and took the head of a Stark on the floor of its own hall. What do we tell Naiser Yukari, who's just beaten the Seikai into the dirt, that's going to make him trust anything that comes out of a Moto mouth?"

Kiyome was quiet for a long moment.

"The truth," she said. "That we were wrong."

Neither of them answered.

"You know what Kagenori's doing isn't right," she said. "Both of you. You've known it since before Torvald. You voted for his head anyway." She looked from one to the other, steadily, without anger. "And you knew what Gimbzo was doing was wrong. All of us did. The granaries at Hiroe. The cellars. The burnings. Every one of us sat in those chairs and knew, and voted, and went home and slept." Her voice did not rise. "And we didn't stop it. Not one of us. We told ourselves it was the house's will and the house's hand. And now there's a boy in the north with Gimbzo's face and a Kōkan's eye, and the only reason he's alive at all is that nobody in the seven lines ever had the courage to stop the man who made him."

The lamp burned between them. In the garden, the glass hanging in the white trees chimed very faintly in a breath of wind.

"I'm going east," Kiyome Byakuya said. "Before Kagenori's council sits. I'd rather you came with me. I'd rather have the Akagane and the Amagiri beside me at that gate than go alone. But I'll go alone if I have to."

Tetsugen Akagane looked at her for a long time.

Then he looked down at his great copper-ringed hands on his knees, and opened them, and looked at the palms, as if there were something written on them.

"I didn't hang him," he said.

Kiyome frowned. "Who?"

"Ren. My soldier. The one who wouldn't burn the granaries at Tōmi." Tetsugen did not look up. "Kagenori told me to dispose of him. I disposed of him. I put him on a horse in the dark with a purse and the name of a man at the Marrow crossing and told him if I ever saw his face again I'd kill him myself." His jaw worked. "I've not told anyone that. I'm telling you."

He lifted his head.

"I'll come," he said heavily. "Sky help me. I'll come."

Kiyome turned to Hayato.

The young lord of the Amagiri sat on the edge of his cushion with his restless fingers gone still at last, looking at the open shutters, at the dark garden and the glass chiming in the white trees, and beyond them the black shape of the Moto hold on its rock against the stars, where the lamps of the Hall of Verdicts were still burning.

"I've been mapping the roads out for a year," he said. His voice was very quiet. "Every one of them. Every pass, every ford, every way out of the western shield that a man could ride by night without being seen from the hold. I told myself it was the Amagiri's office. Route-reading. Escape." He laughed, softly, without any humour in it at all. "I think I was just getting ready to run."

He looked at Kiyome.

"Well," said Hayato Amagiri. "Now I know which way."

## Author Notes

**What this is.** Isaac's passage expanded into Chapter Seven, in three movements. Every beat is kept. The word spreads to the Moto council and to Naiser. Naiser reads that the Starks and Greymanes took Kharven's Foot, and goes to find Shaka-Al before he leaves for Kushara. Kagenori rages to Sumie that they lost to commoners led by a tokenless, and asks whether the age of sixteen is a coincidence. Sumie answers that it is the Kōkan child, because nobody beats Shigure that easily. Kagenori says they must be rid of them quickly, because a Moto will unlock more power as he fights, and damns the Starks and the Greymane, who should have been wiped out before the war. He realises the Ridgewall has been left to fall on purpose so the Moto would be attacked from every side. He summons the Ashmore, Thornwall, Hallenfeld and Keld lords and calls the whole Moto council. Kiyome hears a Kōkan lives; the Byakuya once led the Kōkan's protection and stand for peace and purification, and she won't attend. Tetsugen and Hayato sit with her and ask whether they should really talk to the Yukari with the Zettari still there. Her answer: what Kagenori is doing isn't right, what Gimbzo did was wrong, and they didn't stop it. Past tense, third person, a different close POV in each movement. About 4,800 words.

**Fitting the timeline.** These are now concurrent, as your passage makes them. To square that:
- The **Great Spirit War** of Titans and Archons ended twenty-three years ago. Kasane came down into the Cuttings that year, and Muken was born seven years later.
- The **four-year war** that just ended, the Yukari against the Seikai and the Zettari against the Moto, is a later war. Naiser's father held the seam through it, Gimbzo died in it (killed by Dougou), and Torvald's council came right after it.
- Muken took the Foot about a week after Naiser's talks with Shaka-Al. Shaka-Al is still at the enclave the morning the crow arrives, about to leave for Kushara.
- If you have a name for the four-year war, it should replace "the last war" and "the four years."

**One of your lines read for meaning.** Kiyome's "You know what Kagenori is doing is right" I took as *isn't* right, given everything around it.

**Shaka-Al stays off the page.** Movement I ends as Naiser walks toward the south gate where Kushara's party is saddling. Shaka-Al and Dougou get no lines, actions or thoughts.

**Originated here (yours to keep or strike):**
- **Ulf's crow** to Naiser, with its afterthought: *The breach was made by a lieutenant of mine. Sixteen years old. Tokenless. He will be worth your knowing.* Plus the Mahuo courier's report from the Marrow: the gold eye, the broken stair.
- **Naevra's deduction.** A Nalūn seal can't take hold of a man the Wellspring can't see, so the Yukari's own register was wrong. Telling Shaka-Al now protects the peace, because he would learn it anyway.
- **Shigure at the Hall of Verdicts.** She tells it straight: Muken beat her because she couldn't read him, and she saw the eye. She also saw it *sixteen years ago, in a cellar in Halcrest, on a woman with no name the clerks would take, the night Gimbzo went down with a lamp*. That makes her a witness to Kasane's taking. Asked why she let Muken live: *Because you'd have wanted to see him first... And because I did.*
- **Kagenori's plan.** Don't fight the north, buy it. Ashmore's grain, Thornwall free of the Ore Council, Hallenfeld's tariff-master released from the cells, and the Keld promised in writing to be left alone. Then he calls all seven lines, "whether they wish to come or not."
- **The Ridgewall gambit.** The Greymanes pulled men off the western wall so Undaar-Keth would raid through the western passes into Moto lands. That would force the Moto to fight on two or three fronts. Shizuha: *It's what I would have done.*
- **The Byakuya oath** to the Kōkan, spoken over every Byakuya child: *Purity before power. Peace before the blade. And the red line before the white, until the end of the red line.*
- **Tetsugen spared Ren**, the Akagane soldier from *The Verdict of Six*. He sent him off on a horse with a purse and a contact at the Marrow crossing.
- **Hayato's escape roads** turn out to be the way east.
- **The breakaway.** Byakuya, Akagane and Amagiri ride east to the Yukari enclave before Kagenori's council sits. That puts three Moto lords on course for the cedar enclave, possibly while Kushara's party is still there.

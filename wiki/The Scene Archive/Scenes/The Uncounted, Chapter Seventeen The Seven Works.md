## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Seventeen: The Seven Works

---

> *Before measured time, before chronology, and before the later unfolding of the epochs, Bara created the Works of the World within the Plane of Fate. These Works were not made as techniques or schools of sorcery, but as primal functions: the first operative laws by which existence could act, divide, endure, unravel, and complete itself.*
>
> *From* Hataraki: The Works of the Plane of Fate

---

### I · The Place Where the Threads Run

He sat down on the stone shelf at the back of the death-house, where she had lain, and put his back against the cold wall, and closed his eyes.

He did not know how to go. Faisal had never told him. The first time, on the ice, he had been falling, and the second time, in the yard, the Bound Flame had put a hand on his chest and pulled. Now there was nobody to fall for and nobody to pull, only the cold and the dark and the bar down on the door and the faint dark stain on the stone under his hand where she had lain for two days in Godfrey's cloak.

So he did what Yusif had taught him in the back of a worked-out gallery before every fight. He breathed. In through the nose, slowly, until the strapped ribs on his left side ground together and the pain went up white behind his eyes. Out through the mouth, long, until there was nothing left in him at all. And again. And again. The Kharven breath.

And on the seventh breath, or the seventieth, he heard it.

The singing. Faint, at first, under the silence of the death-house, the way you hear the sea under a storm. The note of the stone shelf under him, low and patient and very old. The note of the wall at his back. The note of the bar across the door. The note of the snow in the yard outside, and the woodpile, and the curtain wall, and the ridge, and the cairn on the ridge with its pale stones. And under all of them, the long low humming of the world.

He listened to it. He did not reach for it. He did not try to hold any of it. He only listened, the way she had listened to a man's chest with her ear against his ribs, until the listening was all there was.

And then the dark of the death-house was full of threads.

They came up out of the stone shelf under him and down out of the dark above, gold and silver and black, fine as hair, more of them than there were stars, running away in every direction and humming each at its own note. The gold ones burned. The silver ones shone cold and clear, and the black ones had a sheen on them like a raven's wing, green and violet where the light slid along them. Where they crossed, they flared, small and bright, and the whole dark was strung with those crossings, near and far, deeper than the sky over the tundra on a winter night, as if someone had hung every aurora the north had ever seen on a loom and set it humming. He was not sitting on the shelf any more. He was not anywhere. He was standing, or floating, in the place made of threads, and his ribs did not hurt, and his breath did not go up in a plume.

Faisal was kneeling in front of him.

He was as he had been the first time, on the ice: bowed, one knee down and the other drawn up, with the whole weight of the threads coming down on his back and shoulders and running through him and out of him into the dark below, and holding it. The great wings folded low around him under the weight. The warm heavy light, gold and white, that made Muken want to say every true thing he knew.

But the light was dimmer than it had been. And at the edge of one of the great wings, where Muken in the white space had cracked the whole row of things in his rage, there was a place where the light did not reach any more. A small place. A missing piece.

"You came on your own," said Faisal.

"You said you'd come when I needed it." Muken looked at the dark place on the wing. "I didn't want to wait."

"No." The bright places where eyes would be did not move from him. "No, you never have." The bowed head inclined, very slightly. "Sit."

Muken sat. There was nothing to sit on. He sat anyway, cross-legged, in the place made of threads, the way the Keld had sat at their fires on the bare packed snow, in front of the kneeling Bound Flame, and the threads ran through him and past him and hummed.

"Your Dawi has begun," said Faisal. "I can hear the iron arguing with him from here." The light shifted, as if something under it had smiled. "He will win. He usually does. You have until he finishes."

"How long is that, here?"

"Here," said Faisal, "there is no how long. The threads do not run in hours. Temporality is one Work among seven, and this is the place where the Works were written, before there was time to write them in." He was quiet a moment. "You may stay as long as you need. A day will pass at the Foot. It may be a great deal longer for you."

"How much longer?"

"As long as it takes," said the Bound Flame, "for you to learn what I can teach you, and for your bones to knit, and for you to be ready." The light did not waver. "Or for you to break. One of those."

Muken looked at him.

"Then teach me," he said.

### II · The Work of Ruins

They began with what he already knew.

Faisal lifted one hand from the weight he carried, and the threads in front of them parted, and in the space between them there was the row again: the stone, the bowl, the blade, the branch, the bone, the rope, the brick, the cup, hanging in the dark at the height of a seated man's eyes, each humming at its own note, each with its cracks.

"Again," said Faisal.

Muken did it. Rope first. He found the sour wavering note of the rot in it and held only that, and waited, and the rope let go of itself along its own dead core and fell in two pieces into the dark. The bowl. The bone. The brick. It went faster than it had in the white space, and cleaner, and the black hot thing under his breastbone came up only once, at the bowl, and he held it there with his weight back on the reins and did not let it go.

"Again," said Faisal.

The row was longer. There were twenty things in it now. Then fifty. Then a hundred, running off into the dark further than he could see, stones and blades and bones and ropes and cups and things he had no names for, and among them, scattered without any pattern, the rotten ones. He found them one after another. He held their notes. He let them go.

"Again," said Faisal.

A thousand.

He lost count of the days. There were no days. There was only the row, and the notes, and the sour wavering hum of the dead things among the steady hum of the living ones, and his own attention narrowed to a needle's point, finding them, holding them, letting them go. Over and over and over. Sometimes the black hot thing came up and he lost his hold and the crazing ran out sideways into a sound thing beside the dead one, and the sound thing cracked, and Faisal said nothing, only lifted his hand, and the row was whole again, and he began from the beginning.

He did not count how many times he began from the beginning.

And then, at some point he could not have named, the row was not things any more.

It was a wall. A wall of gray stone, rough-coursed, the kind of wall a Tenrai mason would build round a fortress, with a gatehouse and a gate. He knew it was the Seat. He did not know how he knew. And it was full of cracks, all humming: the fine steady ones of stone carrying its own weight, the way a sound wall carries it, and here and there, deep, branching, sour, the ones that had stopped carrying and were only pretending. A course where the mortar had rotted. A lintel over a postern that had been cracked by frost forty winters ago and patched and never mended. A ward-stone set into the gatehouse foundation, a worked thing tied into the Seat's draw, humming with Tenrai gold and glowing in the threads like a coal, whose binding had been laid by a man who had not meant the oath he laid it with.

"Again," said Faisal.

Muken looked at the wall.

He found the ward-stone. He held its note, the sour lie at the heart of it. He waited. And the ward-stone let go, along the line of the oath its maker had not meant, and cracked through, and the gold went out of it like a lamp going out. And the gatehouse wall round it stood. Every sound stone in it stood.

"That," said the Bound Flame, "is what you will do at the Seat."

"Only that?"

"Only what is dead," said Faisal. "Only what is already broken and only pretending to stand. Everything else, the men will do. The Stark and the Greymane and the Byakuya and the Dawi's iron. You will not crack one living thing that does not need cracking." The light held him. "That is the oath I am asking of you. Not now. When you are ready to give it."

Muken said nothing. He looked at the wall until he could hear every stone in it.

"I'll give it," he said.

### III · The Work of Purity

His ribs were the first thing.

Faisal did it without warning. There was no row any more and no wall. There was only the place made of threads, and the Bound Flame kneeling, and Muken sitting cross-legged before him, and Faisal said, "Look at yourself," and Muken looked.

And he could see his own body, apart from the thing he was here among the threads: the one lying on the stone shelf in the death-house at the Foot with its back against the wall and its eyes closed and its chest rising and falling, very slowly, in the cold. He could see it the way he saw everything here, as threads and notes and cracks, and it was full of cracks. The seventh rib and the eighth on the left side, broken clean through, the ends grinding against each other at every breath. The old cut along the ribs, torn open twice and sewn three times, weeping. The long slash down the right forearm, sewn badly. The split palm. The bruise on the lung under the broken ribs where Aksel Keld's iron fist had driven them in. And everywhere, under all of it, fainter, older, the cracks of sixteen years: a wrist broken at eleven in the stamping floor at Halcrest and set crooked; a hairline in a shin from the Veyrath ditch; the knuckles of both hands, each one cracked and healed and cracked again.

"Ruin ends what is dead," said Faisal. "You know that now. But ending is only half of a Work. Something has to remain, after." The light pulsed, softly. "*Junketsu no Gō.* The Work of Purity. It establishes. Hear me, that is a larger thing than healing. It finds what ought to stand, the proper shape of a thing, what it was made to be before the world bent it, and it holds the thing to that shape until the thing remembers."

"My ribs," said Muken.

"Your ribs," said Faisal. "Find what they ought to be."

He tried. It was harder than the rope. Harder than the wall. A crack in a rope was a crack, and it hummed sour, and you found it and let it go. But a broken rib was not a crack to be let go. It was a crack to be closed, and to close it he had to hear, under the ragged discord of the break, the note the rib had sung before it broke, and hold that, and wait for the bone to remember.

He could not hear it.

He listened until the threads round him dimmed with his tiredness, and he could not hear it. Only the break. Only the grinding discord where the ends met. The black hot thing came up and told him that he was broken, that he had always been broken, that the break was what he was, and there was no other note to find.

"You are listening to the wrong thing," said Faisal.

"I'm listening to the bone."

"You are listening to the break," said the Bound Flame. "Of course you are. You have spent your whole life listening to breaks. You hear cracks the way other men hear their own names." The light dimmed, very slightly, and steadied. "Listen to her."

Muken went still.

"You know how she closed a wound," said Faisal. "You watched her do it a thousand times. Did she look at the wound?"

Muken thought of the surgeons' line under the Foot. The lamp, and her sleeves rolled to the elbow, and a Greymane boy screaming. Her small sure hands. Her eyes.

She had never looked at the wound. Not once. She had looked at the edges of it. At the whole skin round it, on either side, the living skin that had not been cut. She had looked at where it should meet. And then she had drawn it there, one stitch after another, and let it meet.

He let go of the break.

He listened to the rib on either side of it. The whole bone, above the break and below it, the living bone that had not been broken, humming its own steady note, the note of a rib that carried a man's breath in and out. He heard it. He held it. And he waited.

And very slowly, in the cold of the death-house at the Foot, on the stone shelf, under the strapped linen, the two ends of the seventh rib on Muken's left side moved toward each other, a hair's breadth, and met, and began to remember. In the place made of threads he saw it as a seam of pale gold closing along the bone, warm as lamplight through a window.

---

He did the eighth rib after. Then the cut along his side, which was worse, because the edges had been torn and sewn so many times they no longer knew where they should meet, and he had to find, under the stitching and the swelling and the old scar of the Vessant road, the note of skin that had never been cut at all. Then his forearm. Then his palm. It took a long time. Each one took longer than the last, because each one was more tired, and the black hot thing kept coming up in the middle of it and telling him that the break was what he was.

He kept letting go of the break. He kept listening to the edges. He kept waiting.

And at the end, sitting cross-legged in the place made of threads, he could see his own body lying on the shelf at the Foot, and it was not whole, because nothing ever is. The old crooked wrist was still crooked. The hairline in his shin was still there. The knuckles of both hands were still cracked and healed and cracked. But the ribs were knit, and the cut along them had closed to a long pale seam, and the forearm and the palm were only scars. And his chest, rising and falling on the shelf, was rising all the way.

"Do not do that to a man," said Faisal quietly, "who has not asked you to."

Muken looked at him.

"Purity, through recognition, restores," said the Bound Flame. "Through obsession, it purges. It finds what ought to stand and holds everything to it, and what will not be held, it burns." The bright places where eyes would be did not move. "There is a woman in a silver chair at the Seat who believes she knows what the north ought to be. She holds everything to it. That is why your sister is on the ridge."

Muken thought of the silver room, and of the ridge, and was quiet.

"I won't," he said.

---

### IV · The Work of Spirit

"There is a thing I must show you," said Faisal, "that you will not want to see."

The threads round them shifted.

This time they turned. All of them, all at once, the whole of the place made of threads turning slowly round the two of them like the sky turns round the pole star, and as they turned, the gold in them paled toward silver and the silver toward a soft violet-blue, the color of snow in the hour after sunset, and the notes changed. Lower. Softer. The humming of the living fell away somewhere behind them, and what was left was something else. Fainter. Further off. A sound like many people breathing very quietly in a cold room, a long way off. Like the Waiting.

"*Seirei no Gō,*" said Faisal. "The Work of Spirit. It governs what passes between. Between life and death. Between the body and what comes after. It keeps the unseen world from becoming disorderly." He was quiet a moment. "Everyone who dies passes through it. Everyone. It is the road out."

Muken did not move.

"You heard her," said the Bound Flame. "On the Seat road. Among all the notes of the world, you heard her note, small and clear, far to the south. Alive." The light dimmed, very slightly. "You have been listening for it ever since. In the death-house. In the yard. On the ridge, when you drew the mark. You are listening for it now."

"Yes," said Muken.

"It is still here," said Faisal. "Faintly. On the road out. She has not finished passing. Most do not, for some days. They linger, a little, near the ones they loved." The bright places held him. "And you are holding her."

Muken stared at him.

"You do not know you are doing it," said Faisal. "But you are a Kōkan, and your blood leans toward this as much as toward Ruin. You heard her note on the Seat road, and you have held on to it since, with everything in you, the way you held her hand on the stair at Halcrest when she was small. And every moment you hold it, she cannot go." The light did not waver. "Spirit, through recognition, lets the dead pass, and keeps them in the memory of the living, and is at peace. Spirit, through obsession, will not let go. It hoards. It holds the dead by the wrist on the road out, and they cannot go forward and they cannot come back, and they wait there, in the cold, forever." A pause. "Listen."

Muken listened.

And there it was. Among all the faint far breathing of the road out, one note he would have known anywhere. Small and clear and fierce. Quite unbroken. But no longer coming from the south. Coming from very near. From his own hand. From the place where his own fingers were closed, here, in the place made of threads, round something he had not known he was holding.

He looked down at his hand.

There was a thread in it. A single thread, fine as hair, pale, almost white. Humming. He was holding it so tightly that his knuckles had gone white, and the thread ran out of his fist and away into the faint far dark of the road out, taut, taut as a bowstring, and at the end of it, very far away, something was pulling gently against his grip. Not hard. Just pulling. The way a child pulls on your hand when she wants to go and look at something and you are not letting her.

"Yukazuri," said Muken.

It came out of him in a whisper.

"Let her go," said Faisal.

"I can't."

"You can," said the Bound Flame. "You must. For her sake, more than yours." He was quiet. "She is not finished passing. She cannot finish while you hold her. She will wait on the road out in the cold for as long as you hold on, because she loves you, and because she would never in her life let go of your hand first." The light warmed, very slightly. "She never did, did she."

Muken shook his head. He could not speak.

He looked at the pale thread running out of his fist into the dark. At his own white knuckles. He thought about the stair at Halcrest. The room over the tea-house. The salve tin in the snow with the mark scratched on its lid and the crooked arrow pointing south. *Because she had known he would come. Because she had never in her life doubted that he would come.*

*Please be careful.*

*Try harder than your best.*

*If you come back through this gate on a door with your guts in your lap, I'll put them back in, and I'll sew you up, and then I'll kill you myself.*

He opened his hand.

The thread did not go at once. It lay across his open palm a moment longer, humming, pale, small and clear and fierce. And then it lifted. Slowly. The way a strand of hair lifts in the wind. It lifted off his palm and drew away into the faint far dark of the road out, shining as it went, frost-pale and then faintly rose, and its note went with it, further and further, softer and softer, until he could not hear it any more over the breathing of the dead.

And just before it went, at the very last, he thought he heard it change. Only a little. The way her voice used to change when she was pretending to be cross with him and could not keep it up.

Then it was gone.

Muken sat in the place made of threads with his open hand on his knee and his face turned toward the dark, and wept.

He had not wept on the ridge. He had not wept in the death-house, or at the postern, or on the shore of the Skeldmere with the salve tin in his fist. He had not wept since he was nine years old, at the top of a gallery shaft, when the factor told his mother she owed more salt. He wept now. All of it. Without any sound at all, the way the boy with the red ears had wept in the doorway of the death-house, with his shoulders shaking and his open hand on his knee and the threads of the world running through him, and humming, and the Bound Flame kneeling before him under the weight of everything, saying nothing, holding it.

It went on for a very long time.

When it was over, Faisal said only, "Thank you."

Muken did not ask him what for.

### V · The Work of Temporality

"Now you will see," said Faisal, "why this happened."

The threads turned again. Back. The faint far breathing of the road out fell away and the humming of the living came up, and with it, something new. A direction. Muken felt it before he saw it, the way you feel a current in a river before you see the water move: all the threads in that place, all of them, were not only running. They were running *from* somewhere, and *to* somewhere. They had a before and an after.

"*Jikan no Shigoto,*" said Faisal. "The Work of Temporality. It arranges what comes before and what comes after. Cause, and what the cause makes. Without it the world would be worse than chaos. It would be unreadable." The bright places shifted. "Follow her thread back."

Muken did not understand. And then he did.

He looked for the place in the threads where her pale one had been. The thread was gone, and the place remained: a gap, where it had run. And he followed the gap back, the way you follow a dry streambed up a hill to where the spring was, and as he followed it, the threads round it lit up, one after another, running away from him into the past like lamps being lit down a long street.

He saw the silver room. He did not want to. He saw it: the polished floor and the caged lamps and the silver dais and a small gray woman with her hands behind her back, and he saw the thread running out of her, silver gone sour and tarnished, toward the girl on her knees. And he followed that thread back, from the woman, and it went into a room full of letters with two seals on every one, silver on the left. And back from there, into a lodge of reindeer hide on the tundra, and a Keld boy with an iron hand saying *the queen's will is absolute*. And back. A council in a hall of black stone, six lords of the seven lines round a table, and a man in gold-trimmed black with a narrow face saying *search every road, every register, every salt pan.* And back. A cellar under Halcrest. A lamp. A man with white hair going down the stair after his own blood. And back, and back, and back, through twenty years and forty and a hundred, through wars he had never heard of and kings whose names were dust, through the silver line and the red line standing side by side in the Hall of Verdicts for a thousand years with their hands on their swords.

"It is all one thread," said Muken slowly.

"It is all one thread," said Faisal. "Your sister did not die because a woman in a silver chair hated her. She died because a thousand years ago the silver line was told it would always stand second, and believed it, and could not let it go. And every Shirogane since has been born into that one wound, and lived in it, and died in it, and passed it on." The light did not move. "Temporality, through recognition, lets things move on. Cause, and consequence, and then the next thing. Through obsession, it loops. The same wound. The same vow. The same moment, over and over, forever, because nobody will let it end."

He let the threads fall still.

"That is the reason she died," said the Bound Flame. "I told you in the yard that it was to make you your father. That is true. But under that, this. She died because a wound a thousand years old was still open, and the woman in the silver chair was living inside it." He looked at the boy. "If you go to the Seat and kill her for it, and that is all you do, you will have reopened it. The wound will pass to her sister, or her niece, or whoever sits the silver chair after. And in a thousand years, some Shirogane child will be told why it must hate the red line, and the reason will be you."

Muken said nothing while the lit threads faded, one by one, back into the dark.

"Then what do I do?" he said at last. "Let her live?"

"I did not say that," said Faisal. "She has killed. She will answer for it, in whatever way your law decides. That is lawful ending." The light shifted. "I said, do not let it be the only thing you do. End the woman, if you must. But end the wound with her. Close it. The way your sister would have."

---

### VI · The Work of Knowledge

The fifth Work, Faisal taught him quickly, because he already had the beginnings of it.

"*Chishiki no Shigoto,*" said the Bound Flame. "The Work of Knowledge. It lets the world be known instead of only suffered. It reveals what a thing is. Its true name. Its true use." The bright places where eyes would be held him. "You have done it all your life. The eye. The cracks. You looked at a host of four thousand Keld and saw where two clans hated each other over a pasture. You looked at a Greymane spearman in a crowd and saw that he would betray you." A pause. "You did not know you saw that. But you did. It is why you looked at him."

"I didn't stop him."

"No," said Faisal. "Knowledge reveals. It does not act. That is the other Works' labor." The light was quiet. "But you will not miss it again. Look."

And Muken looked, and he saw the Seat.

This time it was the people in it. Every lord of the seven lines who had stood in the Hall of Verdicts and voted for the Kurenai blade on Ulf Stark's neck. Every Tenrai factor and Shirogane steward. Every captain of the house-guard. He could see the threads running out of each of them, Tenrai gold and Shirogane silver and the many colors of the lesser houses, the whole lamplit city on its rock strung with them like a harp, and he could hear what they were humming, and most of them were humming fear. Fear of Kagenori. Fear of the silver chair. Fear of the boy in the north who had broken the Iron Fist with a song. A few were humming greed, and a few were humming nothing at all, the flat dead note of men who had stopped feeling anything years ago.

And one was humming grief.

It was very faint. A thread of gold, running out of a man in gold-trimmed black who stood alone in the silver room at the Seat at the foot of an empty dais, with his narrow face quite white, looking at the place on the polished floor where a servant in gray had wiped up a girl's blood.

"Kagenori," said Muken.

"Yes," said Faisal.

"He's grieving." Muken stared at the thread. "For her. For Yukazuri."

"For what was done in front of him," said the Bound Flame. "For what he has become, standing behind the silver chair for twenty years, building it for the woman in it, and then watching her use it to do that." The light did not waver. "He loves her. He has loved her his whole life. And he looked at what she did, and something in him broke, and it has not stopped breaking."

Muken was silent a long moment.

"What will he do?"

"Knowledge does not tell you that," said Faisal. "It tells you what is. What will be is Temporality's, and Temporality does not show you a road you have not yet walked." He paused. "But you know now that he is broken. You know where. When you come to the Seat, you will not need to look for it."

---

### VII · The Work of Balance

The sixth Work was the one Faisal had been kneeling under since the world was young.

"*Baransu no Shigoto,*" he said. "The Work of Balance. It keeps opposites from tearing the world apart. Ruin and Purity. Life and death. Law and freedom. Motion and stillness." One great wing moved slightly under its burden. "It does not make them the same. It gives each its proper weight, so the world can carry the strain and not break."

He was quiet a moment.

"Come here," said the Bound Flame.

Muken got up. How he did it in that place he never knew; he did it, and came and stood beside the kneeling figure, close, closer than he had ever been, so that the warm heavy light of it lay over him like the warmth off a banked fire, and he wanted to tell it everything.

"Kneel," said Faisal.

Muken knelt.

He went down beside him, one knee down and the other drawn up, the way the Bound Flame knelt, with his head bowed. And the weight came down on him.

He had not known what it would be. He had seen the threads coming down on Faisal's back and shoulders from the whole of the dark above and running through him and out of him into the dark below, and he had thought he understood. He had not understood. A little of it came onto him now, a very little, a hair's breadth of it, and it was the heaviest thing he had ever felt. Heavier than the salt. Heavier than the gallery roof. Heavier than his mother dying and his sister dead. It came down on his back and his shoulders and pressed him toward the dark below, and every thread in it was humming, all at once, the whole song of the world in his bones, and his knee went down further, and his back bowed further, and he knew that if he let it, it would press him all the way down into the dark and through it and there would be nothing left.

"Do not fight it," said Faisal, beside him. His voice was very close. "If you fight it, it will break you. Everyone who has ever fought it, it has broken."

"Then what do I..."

"Carry it," said the Bound Flame. "Do not push it up. Do not let it push you down. Find where it wants to go, and let it go there, through you, and out of you, and do not hold any of it longer than it needs to be held." The light was very steady. "That is Balance. It is knowing where the weight goes."

Muken knelt under it.

He found where it wanted to go. It took him a long time. The weight came down on his back, and he did not push against it and he did not let it push him, and he felt for where it was going: down, through him, into the dark below. Into the ground. The way the weight of a roof goes down through a beam and a post and into the earth. The way the weight of a man goes down through his legs into the ice. The way Lorn Stark stood, with his weight going straight down through both heels, the way his father had taught him. He let the weight go through him. Down. Out. He held none of it. He only let it pass.

It got lighter. There was as much of it as ever; it was moving now.

"Yes," said Faisal softly.

They knelt together a long time, the Bound Flame and the boy, under the weight of the threads, in the place where the Works were written.

And at some point, Muken noticed that the dark place on the edge of Faisal's wing, the small missing piece where the light no longer reached, was not as dark as it had been. A little of the warm light was coming back into it, gold seeping into the gap the way dawn seeps into a valley. Very little. A hair's breadth.

He kept it to himself, and was glad.

"*Shigoto no Kabe,*" said Faisal, at last. "That is what your grandfathers' grandfathers called it. *The Work of Bearing Load.* When you stand, someday, in front of something that means to come down on everyone behind you, and you will not move, this is what will hold you there." The light pulsed, once. "The weight, going through you, into the ground. You will feel it in your bones for days afterward. It will crack them, a little, every time. But it will hold."

---

### VIII · The Seventh

When he got up from under the weight, at last, Muken stood in the place made of threads and looked at the Bound Flame, and asked the question he had been carrying since the white space.

"There are seven," he said. "You said. Seven Works. Ruins, and Purity, and Spirit, and Temporality, and Knowledge, and Balance." He counted them on his fingers. "That's six."

Faisal did not answer.

"The seventh," said Muken. "You said in the white space that there's a third waking under Hibiware, and you'd not speak of it. Is it that?" He looked at the kneeling figure. "What is it?"

The Bound Flame was silent, and the threads hummed on round them, gold and silver and black.

"*Zentai-sei no Hataraki,*" he said at last. "The Work of Totality."

The threads round them went very quiet.

"It is the crown of the others," said Faisal. "It is what makes all of them belong to one world. Ruin ends and Purity establishes and Spirit passes and Temporality orders and Knowledge reveals and Balance reconciles, and Totality is what lets all of that be one thing instead of six things tearing each other apart. It is the first permission for the world to be whole." The light gathered, and for a breath every thread in that place shone at once, white-gold, so bright and so whole that Muken could not look at it and could not look away. "It is your house's. The Kōkan's. It was the mark on your banner before your banner was blank. The crowned serpent. The raven king. *Kamigan.* Every Kōkan king who ever sat the red chair reached for it, and some of them touched it."

"Teach me," said Muken.

"No."

The word was very quiet. It went through the place made of threads like a stone dropped into a well, and every thread stopped humming for the space of a breath.

"Not yet," said Faisal. "It is not time. It is not ready. And you are not ready for it." The bright places where his eyes would be held the boy. "Hear me. You sat in your own yard with your hands on a table and told three lords of the seven lines that you would bind the seven lines into one blood. You stood on the Skeldmere and told a Tenrai knife that every Moto line would give up its lands into your hand." The light dimmed, very slightly. "Do you know what Totality is, through obsession?"

Muken did not answer.

"It is the hunger to gather everything into one hand," said the Bound Flame. "Every distinction. Every line. Every house and every people and every difference between them, gathered up and pressed together into one shape until nothing is left that is not you." He was quiet a moment. "Enforced oneness. Dominion over everything. It is the oldest corruption there is, and the worst, and every Kōkan who ever reached for Totality before he was ready fell into it. Your father reached for it. That was what the cellars were. He wanted the red line to be one thing. Only one. Himself."

Muken stood very still.

"You will unite the seven lines," said Faisal. "I believe that. I have seen the threads. But if you do it with Totality in your hands before you understand what it is, you will not unite them. You will swallow them. And in twenty years someone will go down into a cellar with a lamp." The light warmed, slightly. "When you can stand in front of seven lines and a dozen Banner Houses and a Zettari prince and want every one of them to stay exactly what they are, and still be one thing together, then I will teach you the seventh. Not before."

"How long?"

"Years," said Faisal. "Many. Perhaps your whole life." A pause. "Perhaps it will be someone else's, after you."

Muken thought about that, and about a cellar under Halcrest, and a lamp.

"All right," he said at last.

The Bound Flame looked at him.

"You're right," said Muken. "I said it on the ice. *One blood.* I meant it." He looked down at his own hands. "And I was angry when I said it. I didn't know the difference yet." He lifted his eyes. "I'll wait."

The light warmed.

"Then you are readier than you think," said Faisal.

---

### IX · Renewed

He did not know how long he had been in the place where the threads run. He could not have said. When he tried, afterward, to count it for Lorn or Godfrey, it came out as a day, and as a season, and as a very long time, and none of them were wrong.

At the end of it, Faisal lifted his hand from the weight he carried one last time, and laid it, open, on Muken's chest. Over the knit ribs. Over the heart. Over the black hot thing under the breastbone, which was still there, and would always be there, but which lay quiet now, banked, like a coal under ash.

"You have six," said the Bound Flame. "Not mastered. You will spend your life on them. But you have them. Ruin, and Purity, and Spirit, and Temporality, and Knowledge, and Balance." The light pulsed, softly. "And Hibi, for the blade. Hibiware you have felt, and you will not touch again until you can do to a city what you did to a rope. And the seventh, waiting." He paused. "Your Dawi has nearly finished. I can hear the iron giving up."

"Will I see you again?"

"When you need it," said Faisal, "and when I can. What kneels at the edge does not go far from it." The light shifted, as if something under it had smiled. "But I think you will not need me so often now."

"I'll swear it," said Muken. "The oath you asked for. For the Seat."

The Bound Flame waited.

"By my honor," said Muken, "I'll crack nothing at the Seat that is not already dead. No living thing that does not need cracking. Not wall, not ward, not man." He breathed. "And I'll close what I can. The way she would have."

The warm heavy light went through him like heat through iron.

"I heard it," said Faisal. "Every word of it was true."

The hand on his chest pressed, once, very gently. And pushed.

---

He opened his eyes on the stone shelf in the death-house at the Foot.

The cold. The dark. The bar across the door. The faint dark stain on the stone under his hand. A thin blue dawn light coming under the door from the yard, and beyond the door, faint, the sound of a man breathing in his sleep, sitting against the wood: Lorn Stark, keeping the door.

He sat up.

He breathed in. All the way down, through the nose, until his lungs were full, and his ribs did not grind, and nothing went white behind his eyes. He breathed out. He looked down at himself. The strapped linen round his chest had come loose in the night and lay in a heap on the shelf beside him. Under it his side was whole, a long pale seam of new scar where the old cut had been. His right forearm was a scar. His left palm was a scar. His wrist was still crooked. His knuckles were still cracked.

He flexed his hands. They were steady.

He could hear the world singing. All of it. The stone and the snow and the wall and the woodpile and the ridge and the cairn and the long low humming of everything. He could hear it, and it did not drown him. It was only there, the way the wind is there, or the sea under a pier.

He got up off the shelf, and went to the door, and lifted the bar.

## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Six: The Captain of the Foot

---

She came down off the last step, and the first cut was already in the air before her foot touched the snow.

Muken never saw it start. He saw the end of it: a long bright line of curved steel coming across at the height of his neck, flat and level, faster than anything had ever come at him in his life. His body moved before he did. The scrap-iron sword came up of its own accord, the flat of it, both hands, and the curved blade struck it a hand's width from the hilt with a sound like a hammer on a bell.

The shock went through his wrists and his elbows and his shoulders and into the roots of his teeth. His arms went numb to the shoulder. His feet went backward in the snow without his asking them to, one step, two, sliding, and he would have gone down on his back if the dead man behind him had not been there for his heel to catch on.

He kept his feet. He did not know how.

Shigure Tenrai Moto had not moved after the cut. She stood where she had landed, at the foot of the stair, with the long curved sword held out to the side at the end of its swing and her eyes on his face. She had not even shifted her weight. The morning was coming up hard and blue behind the keep, and it caught on her: black lamellar laced in gold, the Tenrai heraldry worked in gold thread on the banner at her breast, a gold cord at the hilt of the sword. She was the only bright thing on the stair that was not on fire. She had cut at him the way a woman reaches out to snuff a candle, and the candle had flinched, and she was looking at it with mild interest.

"Hm," she said.

Behind Muken, across the yard, somebody of the vanguard started forward with a spear. Shigure did not look at him. She lifted her free hand a little from her side, palm down, the gesture of a woman settling a dog, and the pressure that was coming off her, the great slow weight against the inside of the air, *pushed*. The spearman's knees went. He went down on them in the slush with a sound like a man who has been punched in the stomach, and the spear fell out of his hands, and he knelt there with his mouth open and could not get up.

"No," said Shigure Tenrai Moto. "Not you." She did not raise her voice. "Him."

She looked back at Muken.

"Again," she said, and came.

---

The second exchange was three cuts long, and he lived through it because of the stair.

She came in low and from the left, a rising cut toward his hip, and he got the scrap-iron down across it, and the shock jarred him again to the shoulder. She was already turning off it before the steel had finished ringing, the curved blade circling up over her head and coming down from the right toward his collarbone, and he got under that one too, barely, the edge sliding off his blade and down past his ear so close that he felt the wind of it in the torn stitches of his cheek. The third came straight down from above, a cut to split his skull, and there was nothing he could do about it at all. He was too slow. He knew it the way you know you've missed a step in the dark. His sword was in the wrong place and his weight was in the wrong place and the steel was coming down.

She cut short, by a finger's width. The curved edge came down and stopped in the air above his face, and turned, and went back up, and she stepped back onto the bottom stair with her blade at her shoulder and her eyes on him, and he stood there in the snow with his sword in the wrong place and his mouth open and his heart going like a hammer.

He did not understand it. He did not understand it for a full breath. Then he looked at her eyes.

The rings in them had stopped turning.

Every time. He saw it, now that he was looking, as he had seen the gait of the big creature on the Vessant road and the seam in the wall. The fine bright rings in the dark of her eyes turned and turned while she moved, slowly, like ripples on a pond, gold on black, and then, the instant before each cut, they stopped. They stopped dead and went bright, as a marksman's eyes fix when he takes aim. And on the last cut, the one that should have split his skull, they had stopped and gone bright, and then they had flickered, and gone out, and come back on, like a lamp in a draught.

And she had pulled the cut.

She was frowning. It was the first expression he had seen on her long pale face that was not calm. She was looking at him the way a scribe looks at a column of figures that will not add up.

"You don't *read*," she said.

He had no idea what she meant. He had no breath to ask.

"Everything reads," said Shigure Tenrai Moto. She said it softly, almost to herself. "Everything that lives has its next moment in front of it, and every next moment has a line, and every line can be seen if you know how to look. A man's sword arm. A horse's foot. An arrow. A stone on a slope. I look at a man, and I see where he will be when my blade gets there, and I put my blade there." She tilted her head. "I have been doing it since I was nine years old. I have never once, in all that time, looked at anything that lives and seen nothing."

She lifted the curved sword off her shoulder.

"I look at you," she said, "and there's nothing there. It's like reading a page with no ink on it."

Her lips thinned.

"Gimbzo read. Gimbzo read like a forest fire. I could see him coming three moves out." The rings in her eyes began to turn again, faster. "So what are you?"

---

He did not answer, because he did not know, and because at that moment the heat came up behind his left eye.

It came up hard. This time it came as a brand. It came up out of somewhere at the back of his skull, out of the worked-out gallery where he never went, and it went into the eye like a nail, and for one breath he could not see out of it at all for the pain. He staggered. He felt something wet run down out of the eye onto his cheek, into the torn stitches, and did not know if it was tears or blood.

And then he could see again. And the world had changed.

It was still the yard. The snow, trampled black and red, and beyond the trampling a field of it glittering untouched under the walls. The burning stables, orange and roaring, throwing sparks up into a sky gone the hard clean blue of a northern morning. The dead. The Tenrai on the stair with their shields locked, and the northerners at the foot of it, and Wystan's gray men coming across from the broken gate. The woman in front of him in black and gold, with her long curved sword. But it was all of it laid open now, the way a frozen river is laid open when the ice is clear, so that you can see the current moving under it. He could see where everything was carrying its weight. The stair. The walls. The shields. The bodies of the living and the dead. Every one of them was a set of loads going down through bones and stones and timber into the ground, and every load went through some place that was carrying more than it could hold. He could see those places. They stood out to his left eye like cracks of light in a dark wall, a fine hot gold, the color of a forge seen through a chink.

He could see them in her.

The Tenrai captain stood on the bottom step of the keep stair with her weight on her left foot and her right foot a little forward, and her whole tall lean body balanced on that left foot like a blade balanced on a finger, beautifully, perfectly. And under the left foot, through the boot and the snow, he could see the step. The bottom step of the keep stair. Old stone, older than the Tenrai, older than the dry-stone ring of the clans, worn hollow in the middle by a thousand years of feet, with the frost got into a hairline crack along its face from the left end to the middle. The same crack. The same frost. The same thing that had taken thirty feet of curtain wall off the ridge an hour ago, smaller and quieter and only waiting.

The rings in Shigure Tenrai Moto's eyes had stopped turning.

She was staring at his left eye.

"*Shingan,*" she whispered.

Her face had gone white again, paper-white, to the lips.

"Seven lines and one blood," she said. "Oh, no. Not *that.* Not the eye. The eye went into the dust with the rest of them." She was staring at it as if it might go out if she looked away. "Any Moto brat can see where a wall wants to fall. That's Shingan. The gold in it isn't." Her voice cracked on it. "I haven't seen gold in an eye since the cellars. I stood beside Gimbzo twenty years and never once read the red line in his eyes, in a fight or out of one, even the night he went down into the cellars after it. That eye is *Kōkan.* He thought he'd burned the last of it."

He did not know what any of the words meant. He did not care. He was looking at the step.

---

She came at him. She came fast, and hard, and furious, and frightened, and that was her mistake, though neither of them knew it yet.

The first cut came high, and he did not block it. He went down under it. He dropped onto one knee in the slush so suddenly that the curved blade went over his head and took a lock of his white hair with it, and from his knee, with both hands, with every last thread of warmth that was left in his body poured down into his arms and his back and his hips in one burning rush, he brought the scrap-iron sword round in a flat, low arc, aimed at the step and nowhere near her.

The pommel hit the old stone at the left end of the crack, where the frost was thickest.

He felt it give. He felt it through the iron and through his wrists and into his chest, the way he had felt the wall move under his boots: a shiver, a settling, a long low *crack* like river ice in the spring. The face of the bottom step split off along its whole length and slid forward into the snow in a single slab. And Shigure Tenrai Moto, balanced on it on her left foot like a blade on a finger, beautifully, perfectly, had nothing under her.

She went down.

She did not fall far. She was too good to fall far. She twisted in the air as the stone went out from under her and came down on her right knee and her left hand in the snow, with the curved sword still up in her right, and her face turned up toward him, and the rings in her eyes turning so fast now that they blurred into a single bright golden disc.

He was already up. He was already moving. He did not decide to. His body moved before he did, the way it always had, and the scrap-iron sword came round and down in both hands, and she got her curved blade up across it.

Steel on steel. The shock went through both of them.

But she was on one knee in the snow, with her weight wrong and her footing gone, and he was standing over her, and the cut had his whole body behind it falling forward. Her blade held. Her arm did not. The scrap-iron sword drove her own curved edge back and down into her, and her own edge bit into the top of her left shoulder where the black lamellar left the collarbone bare beneath the gorget, and went in, and stuck against the bone. Muken felt it go in through two swords. Black-gold lacing parted. Blood came up between the plates, very bright, steaming in the cold.

She made a sound, a long hiss through her teeth, like water on a hot stone.

And then she did a thing he had not seen anyone do, and would not see again for many winters.

She let go of her sword.

She let it go, the long curved beautiful thing, and it fell out from between them into the snow, and with her free hands she took hold of the scrap-iron blade where it lay on her shoulder, both hands, bare fingers round the black pitted edge, and *pulled* it, down and to her right, past her, out of the cut, into the snow. Her palms opened on the edge. He saw her blood run down the iron. She did not seem to feel it. And as the blade went past her and down, his whole weight went with it, forward and down, off balance, and her knee came up into his belly under the mail, and all the breath went out of him at once, and he folded over it and went down in the snow on his side.

He lay there. He could not breathe. He could not get his arms under him. The warmth was gone out of him entirely, all of it, all at once, and there was nothing left in his body but cold and a great grinding emptiness, as if someone had scooped him out with a spoon.

Above him, Shigure Tenrai Moto got slowly to her feet.

She was bleeding badly. The cut at the top of her shoulder was deep, to the bone, and the blood was coming out of it through the black plates in a steady pulse and running down the gold of the banner on her breast, and her left arm hung down at her side and did not move when she moved. Her palms were laid open across both of them where she had taken the blade in her hands. She stood over him in the snow, swaying, with the turning rings in her eyes beginning to slow and dim, and looked down at him while the blood ran off her fingers and pocked the snow red.

Her own sword lay in the snow beside his head. She did not pick it up.

"You read nothing," she said. Her voice was very quiet, and shaking, and very sure. "And you have his face. And you have *the eye.*" She breathed. "Do you understand what you are? Do you have any idea? Do they know? The old men who brought you here, the Stark, the Greymane, do they know what they've got?"

He could not answer. He could not get the air into him to answer.

"No," said Shigure. "No, you don't." Something moved in her face that might almost have been pity. "Whoever's had the raising of you hasn't told you a thing."

From across the yard, Wystan Greymane's horn sounded, very close. There were boots on the stone, a great many of them, coming fast. Men shouting in northern voices. The Tenrai shield wall on the stair above them was breaking up; Muken heard it go, the clatter of dropped shields and the scrape of retreat.

"Captain!" someone on the stair was shouting. "*Captain!* The postern! The postern's open, captain, we can still..."

Shigure Tenrai Moto looked down at Muken for one breath more.

She could have killed him. He knew it. She knew he knew it. Her sword was a hand's width from her foot, and she had one good arm, and he could not lift his own. One stroke. And the face that had come down out of Gimbzo, and the eye that had come up out of the dust, would have gone back into the snow of Kharven's Foot where nobody would ever see them again.

She looked at him, at his face more than at the eye, and the rings in her eyes slowed and stopped, and something passed across her long white face that he would not understand for years.

Then she bent, with a grunt of pain, and picked up her sword left-handed by the blade, careless of her open palm, and straightened.

"Kagenori will want you," she said. "Alive, if he can have you. Dead, if he can't. And so will every Moto in the seven lines, the moment they hear what I've seen." She took a step back. "I'll be the one who tells them. Remember that, boy. When they come for you. Remember who told them."

She turned. Two of her men came down the broken stair for her, and she put her good arm round the neck of one of them, and the three of them went up the steps and in through the keep door, fast, without looking back, and the door slammed behind them, and a bar came down across it on the inside with a sound like a coffin lid.

A moment later, from somewhere round the far side of the keep, he heard the sound of horses on stone, going away fast down a road toward the south.

Then nothing. Only boots, and northern shouting, and the burning stables, and the long low cold sound of the wind coming over the broken wall and lifting the snow off the rubble in glittering skeins.

---

He lay on his side in the snow and watched the world come back.

It came back slowly. The cold first. Then the pain, in his belly where her knee had gone in, and in his left eye, which was weeping steadily now, something warm and thin down his cheek, and in his chest under the borrowed mail where he had not felt it until now: a long burning line from his left armpit down across his ribs. One of the cuts in the second exchange. One of the ones he had thought he blocked. The mail had turned the edge, mostly. Not all of it. He could feel the wet of it soaking into his shirt.

Somebody was kneeling beside him. Many people. Boots, and voices, and a hand on his shoulder, very big, with two fingers missing from it.

"*Muken.* Muken, lad, look at me. Look at me." Ulf Stark. His breath was going white over Muken's face, fast and hoarse. "Sky above. Sky above, lad, you put a Tenrai captain on her knees. With that. With *that.*" He was looking at the scrap-iron sword in the snow. "Wystan. Wystan, come and see this. Come and see what he did to the step."

Another face. Long and gray. Wystan Greymane was not looking at the step. He was looking at Muken's left eye, very closely, from a hand's width away, and his face had set like ground taking the frost, in a way that Muken would remember long afterward.

"Ulf," said Wystan Greymane quietly. "Look at his eye."

Ulf looked.

Neither man spoke. Behind them the stable roof fell in, and a column of sparks went up gold into the blue.

Muken could not see his own eye. He would not see it for days, until he found a scrap of polished steel in the fort's armory and looked into it, and saw what they had seen: a crack in the slate-gray of the left iris, low, fine as a hair, running out from the amber fleck that had always been there toward the edge, bright as a seam of gold in a stone. It would fade, over the next week, until he could barely find it. It would never go entirely.

"The keep," said Wystan, and stood up, and his voice was ordinary again, an old soldier's voice giving orders. "She's gone out the postern with what's left of them. Ten, maybe twelve. Let her go. We've no horses to catch her with and no men to spare." He looked round the yard, at the dead. "Get the gate up. Get the stables out before it takes the barracks with it. Get a count."

"Wystan."

"Later," said Wystan Greymane. He did not look at Muken's eye again. "Later, Ulf."

---

The count came in at noon.

The Tenrai garrison of Kharven's Foot had been two hundred. Sixty-one of them were dead in the yard and on the walls and in the rubble of the breach. Eighty more had thrown down their arms in the barracks when the gate went and were sitting now in the snow of the yard with their hands on their heads, under the spears of gray-bearded Greymane men. The rest had gone out through the postern with their captain, or over the north wall on ropes, or nobody knew where.

The north had come up the ridge six hundred strong. Fifty-two of them would not go down it again.

Thirty of those fifty-two had been in the vanguard.

They laid them in a row along the inside of the curtain wall, by the breach, where the light came in, with their faces to the sky. The noon sun came through the gap in a long white bar, and the frost on their mail and in their hair caught it and glittered, so that the row of them shone as if someone had dressed them for a feast. Thirty of them. Most of them were boys. The thin red-haired one with the spear was third from the end. Somebody had closed his eyes and crossed his hands on the spear-shaft, and somebody else had taken the rags out of his helmet and folded them and put them under his head, as if that would make the ground softer.

Muken walked down the row.

He walked it slowly, because he could not walk it any other way, with his left arm strapped across his chest and his ribs bound under his shirt and a pad of wool tied over his left eye. He stopped at every one. He did not know most of their names. He made himself learn them. He asked the men who were standing nearest, at each one, *What was he called,* and they told him, and he said the name aloud, once, and went on to the next.

At the end of the row, someone was waiting for him.

She was sitting on a fallen block of the breach with her kit open on her knees and her gray coat buttoned to the throat, and her hands were red to the wrists, and there was blood dried brown on her face and in the white of her braid. She had been working since the gate went down. She looked about a hundred years old. She looked about nine.

She looked up at him, at the strapped arm and the bound ribs and the pad over his eye, and her small grave face did not change at all.

"Sit," said Yukazuri.

"I'm all right."

"You're not. You've opened your face again and your ribs are bleeding through the binding and you're walking like Rob Stark." She pointed at the block of stone beside her. "*Sit.*"

He sat.

She reached up without a word and untied the pad from over his left eye, gently, and looked into the eye, close and unhurried, as she looked into wounds. He felt her breath on his face. He felt his own breathing slow down, the way it always did, and the grinding emptiness in his chest ease, a little, for the first time since the stair.

She did not say anything about the crack in his iris. She looked at it, and he saw her see it, and he saw her decide not to say anything, and he loved her for it more than he had ever loved anyone.

Then she tied the pad back on, and took a curved needle out of the kit on her knees, and threaded it, and turned his face toward the light with two red fingers under his jaw.

"Hold still," said Yukazuri. "You can dislike me when it closes."

## Author Notes

- **Retcon 2026-10-20 (new-lore questionnaire).** Shigure's line on the eye is rewritten: the Shingan is any Moto's, the gold in it is the Kōkan sign she has not seen since the cellars, and she never read the red line in Gimbzo's eyes in twenty years at his side; he thought he had burned the last of it. Her spared stroke now rests on his face, the face she loved, and the "he would not understand for years" beat stands.

**What this is.** Chapter Six: the duel at the foot of the keep stair, carried through to the fort's fall and the count. Past tense, third person, close on Muken. About 4,000 words.

**How the fight decides (Stage gap, the read, what's spent, the ground):**
- **Stage gap.** Shigure is far above Muken. Her Pressure drops a spearman to his knees with a gesture, and her first cut nearly takes Muken off his feet. On a straight exchange she wins every time, and the second exchange is three cuts he only half survives.
- **The read, her side.** She's a Moto, and she cuts where she has read the next moment will be. *You don't read.* Muken is uncounted, so her fate-sight returns nothing on him, "like reading a page with no ink on it." This is the Recension's unreadable mark from the Long Reading, working in a fight.
- **The read, his side.** He sees her tell twice before he uses it: the rings in her eyes stop and go bright just before each cut, and on him they flicker. Then the Shingan opens, the first ignition from his card, with Fractura's stress-reading. He sees the loads in everything, and the frost-crack in the old bottom step under her planted left foot, the same flaw that took the wall.
- **What's spent.** He pours the last of his reinforcement into one pommel strike on the step, not on her. The step's face splits, she goes down on one knee, and his falling cut drives her own blade into her shoulder above the collarbone, to the bone.
- **The way out.** She lets go of her sword, grabs his blade bare-handed and pulls it past her, then knees him in the gut. He goes down empty, and she has the kill.
- **Why she doesn't take it.** She loved Gimbzo and sees him in the boy's face; she still reports to Kagenori. *Kagenori will want you... I'll be the one who tells them. Remember who told them.* She escapes through the postern. That's a live thread: the Moto will now know the Kōkan eye has come back. She names it from the opponent's chair: *Shingan*, which any Moto may carry, and the gold in it, which is the Kōkan sign and the start of the Kamigan (*That eye is Kōkan. He thought he'd burned the last of it*). That's the opponent-voice explanation, contempt as instruction.

**The cost (for the Ledger):**
- **Muken.** Cut from the left armpit across the ribs, mostly turned by the mail. Blunt trauma to the gut from the knee. Cheek stitches torn open again, left arm strapped, total reinforcement exhaustion. A hairline gold crack in the left iris running out from the amber fleck, which fades over a week but never fully goes.
- **Shigure.** Her own blade driven into the top of her left shoulder to the bone, with the arm hanging useless after. Both palms laid open from grabbing the scrap-iron edge.
- **The north.** 52 dead of 600, 30 of them in the vanguard, laid in a row by the breach. Muken walks the row and learns every name.
- **The Tenrai.** 61 dead, 80 captured, Shigure and ten or twelve escaped south. Kharven's Foot and the Kessen road are taken.

**Witnesses to the eye:** Shigure, who will carry it to Kagenori. Ulf and Wystan, who see it and say nothing ("Later, Ulf"). Yukazuri, who sees it and decides not to mention it.

**Closing line:** Yukazuri's canon voice line from her card, *Hold still. You can dislike me when it closes.*

**Rule ids that most constrained it:** R71-7 (the deciding fact, the cracked step, shown in the read before the blow), R71-8 (a findable way out, both directions), R71-23 (a tell shown twice and used on the third), R71-19 (the ground on its own row: frost in old stone), R70-69 (Pressure in the room, then the bodies), R71-6 and the gore floor (shoulder to the bone, palms opened, the dead laid out), R70-114 (the warm bond at the close).

**Revision 2026-10-24.** Lifted the color (Tenrai black and gold on Shigure, the gold of the stress-cracks, the blue northern morning, glittering frost on the laid-out dead and the blowing snow) and kept magitech absent at the frontier fort; trimmed "for a long time", "very/quite still" and the chained "the way" similes. Every beat, line and figure of the duel, the count and the close is kept.

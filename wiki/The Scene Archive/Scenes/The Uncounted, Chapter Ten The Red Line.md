## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Ten: The Red Line

---

### I · The Chair Before the King

The fire in the great hearth had been built of Tenrai pine, split from the stable timbers, and it burned too fast and spat, orange at the heart and a clean gold along the split faces where the resin ran. Every few breaths a knot in it gave with a crack like a knuckle, and a spark went up the flue, and the shadows of the people round the split table jumped on the stone and settled again. The room smelled of pitch and wet wool and the wolfhound, and under all of it, faintly, of the seal-fat lamp somebody had left burning on the sill of the far window because it was the only lamp the Tenrai had not taken. Outside, in the yard, a child was still splitting wood. Three blows for every split. The sound came in through the narrow windows small and steady, like a clock that nobody had wound.

Muken let the prince finish.

He let him say all of it. The chair. The coat. The sword. *Younger than I expected.* He let him walk round the room with his eyes and come back, and talk about the Keld and the Iron Fist and how entertaining it would be, and say *I don't particularly like the Moto,* and say *not today.* He sat through every word of it with his back to the hearth and his hands flat on the rough pine on either side of the scrap-iron sword, the way Godfrey had told him, and he did not move. Godfrey had said *let them say all of it.* Muken had not expected the first of them to take him at his word.

When the prince stepped aside at last, and the space in front of the table stood open, Muken looked at it for a moment. Then he looked at the man who had made it.

"You're right about the chair," he said.

His voice was quiet. It had the flat salt-gallery evenness it always had when he was holding something down, every word laid down flat and separate, like props in a soft gallery.

"They built the table first," Muken said. "Took the doors off the Tenrai mess and laid them on trestles so the wounded had somewhere to lie off the floor. Then they built the gate. Then they mended the wall. The chair was last. That's the right order, I think." He glanced down at the battered pine under his hands. "If they'd built me a throne before they'd mended the wall, I'd have had them burn it for the fires."

He looked back up.

"And you're right that they chose. I'll give you that as well. Nobody handed me this." He lifted one hand off the table, an inch, and set it down again. "But you've got the wrong idea of what they chose. They didn't choose me, Prince. They chose to stop kneeling. To the Tenrai, and their tax-men, and their grain-wagons going south full while the long houses ate bark. They'd been choosing it for twenty years and never had a morning to do it on. Then they had one. I happened to be standing in the yard when it came." His mouth moved, very slightly. "Somebody had to be. It might as easily have been Lorn. It ought to have been his father."

Against the wall, Lorn Stark's jaw tightened on the left side and was still.

"As for curiosity and friendship," Muken said, "I wasn't going to mistake one for the other. I've sold my sword since I was fourteen. A man who looks at you the way you looked at me just now, all the way down and back up, isn't wondering whether he likes you. He's wondering how you'd go down." He held the prince's eyes. "I've had that look from sergeants and from paymasters and from men across a ditch. I know what it costs and what it doesn't. You can keep looking. It's free."

The wolfhound, standing at his chair with its hackles still raised, made a sound in its chest like a door on a bad hinge. Muken put his left hand down on its great gray head without looking at it, and the sound stopped.

"And yes," he said. "The Keld."

He did not look at Godfrey, and Godfrey Greymane spoke as if he had been waiting for his place in the line, and perhaps he had.

"The Keld march in two days, sire," Godfrey said. "From their fires at the Grey Teeth. Our outriders have them at something under four thousand. Riders, mostly, on the short northern horses that don't founder in the drift, and every man of them can sleep in the snow in his coat and wake up and fight." His long gray hands lay flat and unmoving on the pine. "Aksel Keld leads them. The Iron Fist. You've heard he's never lost. That's true. He's eighteen, and he's fought eleven times, and he's won eleven times, and three of those were against forces greater than his by twice and more." He paused. "My father fought beside his grandfather once, on the ice, against a Shiogane raid. He said Old Harald Keld was the only man he'd ever seen who could hear a whiteout coming before the sky changed. He said the grandson would be worse."

"Worse," Muken said.

"Better, sire. For the Keld. Worse for whoever's standing in front of him."

Lorn Stark spoke from the wall, and his voice was rough and low and did not carry quite as far as he meant it to.

"You said entertaining, Prince."

He had not moved from where Godfrey had put him. His hands were still at his sides. But his chin had come up, and his eyes, Stark-blue and bloodshot from two days without sleep, had settled on the big man in black and gold and stayed there.

"There'll be three hundred Stark riders on that ice," said Lorn, "the morning after next. My father's men. Rob and Mark among them. Half the boys who sewed that banner on the gate." He let that sit. "I'll grant you it may be worth watching, from where you'll be standing. I'd only ask you to remember it's my people who'll be doing the bleeding while you watch."

He said it evenly, with his weight even on both feet, the way his father had taught him. Then he was quiet again, and looked at the floor three feet in front of him, as a Warlord of the north did when he had said what he had to say in front of a king and did not mean to say any more.

Muken let the silence after it run its length. Then he spoke again, to the prince.

"Your war or mine," he said. "You're right. It's mine. And you can put your mind at rest. I didn't ask you for an army, and I'll not ask. If the Zettari came north under arms there isn't a Moto in the Hall of Verdicts who wouldn't call it an invasion before the first horse cleared the pass, and they'd be right to, and every Stark and Greymane on that ice would be a Zettari dog in their mouths by nightfall." He tilted his head a fraction. "I'd sooner lose to the Keld on my own than win with your father's spears behind me and lose the north for it. So keep your army. And keep your hands off the Iron Fist. He's spoken for."

Tetsugen Akagane made a sound in his chest that might, in another man, have been a laugh.

"As for your witnessing," Muken said. "Good. I hope you do it." He looked down the table at the two men in copper and indigo, standing in the doorway where they had stopped when Kiyome stopped. "You'll want Lord Hayato for the roads. And Lorn will give you a rider who knows the long houses between here and the Kessen ford. The ones the Tenrai left standing. Go into one of them in the Thin Weeks, Prince. Count the bowls on the shelf and then count the children. That's the only sum anyone up here needs to see done."

He sat back, very slightly, in Shigure Tenrai's carved chair.

"You said you were born a prince," he said. "And that I wasn't raised one. And that this is something I have over you." He considered it. "I don't know what you think I have. I was raised three galleries down under Halcrest, cutting salt with my mother, sleeping on a pallet with my sister so neither of us froze. Everything I own I've taken off a dead man or been given by a live one. The coat was a sergeant's who died at the Veyrath ditch. The mail was Ulf Stark's. The sword I took out of a scrap-heap at the Orrel stamping floor when I was eleven, and it's been sharpened so many times there's hardly any blade left on it." He looked at the prince. "If that's what I have over you, you can have it. I'd have traded it at any age for one winter with enough to eat."

Then, very quietly, he added:

"But I'll tell you one thing I do have. When those men knelt out there by the woodpile, nobody had told them to. That's the only reason I'm sitting here. If they ever stop, I'll get up." He put both hands flat on the table again. "I don't think anyone ever made you that promise. I don't think you've ever had to make it."

The fire cracked. A spark went up.

"And you said you don't like the Moto," said Muken.

### II · No Silver Spoon

He said the next part carefully, because it was the part he had been holding behind his teeth since the prince first spoke it.

"You are speaking," said Muken, "as if I claim to be a Moto."

The room shifted. It was a small thing: a weight changing on a foot, a breath taken and held. Kiyome Byakuya stood exactly as she had stood since the door, and yet the glass beads at her temples, which had hung silent and full of small firelit sparks since she came in, touched each other once with a sound like rain starting on a slate roof.

Muken did not notice. He was looking at the prince.

"As if I was born with a silver spoon in my mouth," he said. "As if I'm sat here in my grandfather's chair with my grandfather's sword and you've come to look at the last of something." He lifted one hand, palm up, at the room: the split pine, the barrel, the stools, the white banner beyond the window. "Look at it. Look at me. Do I look like a lord of the seven lines to you?" His voice did not rise. "I've never set foot in the Seat. I've never seen a Wellspring with the Guild's lead on it. I've never held a token that hummed. Every Moto I ever met before this month was a Tenrai factor at the top of a gallery shaft with a ledger, telling my mother she owed more salt this season than last."

He turned his head, then, for the first time since they had come in, and looked down the table at the three of them.

"No offense, Lady Kiyome," he said. "Lord Hayato. Lord Tetsugen."

He gave each of them the name, exactly, one after another, as Lorn had at the gate.

"I'm sure you've come a long way, and I'm sure you've a great deal to say, and I'll hear it. Godfrey's told me I'm to hear all of it, and Godfrey's generally right." He held Kiyome's eyes. "But I'll tell you now, before you start, so nobody wastes their breath. I don't care what you people think. Of me, or of the north, or of who has a right to what. I only care what the people think. The ones out there." He jerked his chin at the window, at the yard, at the steady three-blows-to-a-split of the child at the woodpile. "And what they want. What they've wanted for twenty years. Their ambitions, if you like, though they'd never call them that. A roof that doesn't fall in. A pot with something in it. A boy who goes south on a levy and comes back. Those are the only ambitions I've got any time for."

Tetsugen Akagane had come a step into the room. He was a heavy man, broad as a door, bald as an egg, with the copper of his house gone dark at the seams of his coat and burning red-bronze everywhere else in the firelight, and the horn handle of a meat knife at his belt that was the only steel he had brought in. He gave Muken a long weighing look from under thick gray brows, like a man looking at a horse he had been told was unbreakable.

"Good," Tetsugen said.

Muken's eyes moved to him.

"Good," said the Akagane lord again, in his flat iron voice. "You should care what they think. None of us did. Not for forty years." He set his scarred hands on his belt. "I'd not give you a copper for a king up here who cared what the Hall of Verdicts thought of him. We've had twenty years of one who did."

Muken looked at him a breath longer. Then he looked away, to the window, and his face changed.

"You came up the Kessen road," he said. "All of you. You'll have passed Harrowmere."

Nobody answered.

"There were eighty people at Harrowmere when the Tenrai took the levy in the autumn," said Muken. "I know because I took a convoy through it in the summer, and the headman tried to sell me his daughter for a sack of oats, and I gave him the oats and told him to keep her. When we came back through the week before last, there were twenty-six. The rest are in the pit behind the long house, because the ground's too hard to dig separate graves and nobody's got the strength to carry them to a death-house." He looked back down the table at the three Moto, one after another, and held each of them. "And that's one. There's Tullock. There's Brannick ford. There's Low Esk, where the Tenrai factor fenced the well and sold the water by the cup. There's every long house between here and the Marrow crossing with a woodpile you could carry off under one arm."

His voice was very quiet now.

"So you'll forgive me if I don't much care for the Moto. You've never once in my life given me a reason to."

Hayato Amagiri was the one who answered him.

He had been standing half a step behind Tetsugen with his narrow face drawn and his restless fingers for once at rest, folded in the wet indigo of his rain-cape, which the fire turned the deep blue of a lake at dusk. A sprig of blue-black juniper had caught in the toggle at his throat somewhere on the northern road and stayed there. When he spoke it was in the plain working cadence of a man discussing distances, and it was all the more terrible for that.

"Harrowmere was a hundred and four in my grandfather's survey," he said. "Seventy-nine when the war ended. Twenty-six, you say, and I believe you, because I passed it two days ago and counted eleven chimneys with smoke." He drew a breath through his long nose. "Tullock is nine. Brannick ford is gone; the Tenrai burned it in the summer for harboring a Stark rider. Low Esk I don't know. I'll learn." He looked straight at Muken. "I keep the roads for my house, sire. I've walked every one of them north of the Seat. And I'll tell you what no one in the Hall of Verdicts would tell you, because it's the only thing I'm good for. Every one of those numbers is in a Tenrai ledger somewhere, in a clerk's good hand, with the date it was taken down. They know. They have always known." His mouth tightened. "They simply never thought it was a sum worth doing."

Muken stared at the three Moto leaders.

He stared at them the way he had stared at the Tenrai factor at the top of the shaft when he was nine, with his mother's ledger-marks in the clerk's book and her cough coming up the gallery behind him. There was nothing hot in it. It was the old cold look, the gallery look, the one that came up from three hundred feet under the salt and settled behind his eyes and did not go away. The gold seam in his left iris, the hair-fine crack he had carried out of the gatehouse at the Foot, caught the firelight and held it.

"It is fine," said Muken at last.

He said it very quietly.

"It is fine. I don't need the Tenrai's ledgers. I don't need the Hall's leave, or its chairs, or its tokens." He laid his right hand flat on the black pitted blade of the scrap-iron sword. "I'll clear it. All of it. The muck that's spread across the inner world since before I was born, the poison in the springs and in the granaries and in the Seat. I'll dig it out with my hands if I have to, the way we dug salt." His fingers closed on the blade, not hard. "And then I'll give the people the power they deserve. To keep what they grow. To eat what they keep. To choose who stands at the front of them, and to tell him to sit down when he's done."

The fire cracked. The wolfhound, under his hand, had gone quiet, and its great ribs barely moved.

### III · The White and the Red

Kiyome Byakuya looked at her nephew.

She had not stopped looking at him since the door. She had looked at him while the prince spoke and while the boy answered, and through Godfrey's report of the Keld and Lorn Stark's quiet hard words from the wall, and through Harrowmere and Tullock and Low Esk. She had looked at the shape of his jaw, which was Kasane's, and the set of his mouth, which was not, and the white hair tied back with a strip of leather the way her sister had tied her own hair back on the training ground at the white house when they were girls. And she had looked at his left eye, at the hair-fine crack of gold across the gray, and her own eyes had stung so hard that the silver rings in them had turned once, very slowly, against her will.

*So much hatred,* she thought. *Seven lines forgive us, child. So much of it. And every word of it earned.*

That was the worst of it. He had not said one thing that was not true. He had been raised under the salt by a woman the Moto had driven there, and fed on bark-bread and the Tenrai's arithmetic, and sent to cut salt at nine and to sell his sword at fourteen, and every Moto he had ever laid eyes on had been a man at the top of a shaft with a ledger. He had seen the worst of them. He had seen nothing else. He had never sat in the white house at the hour of the evening bell and heard the Byakuya children say the oath over the lamp. He had never seen an Akagane granary thrown open in a famine year, as Tetsugen's grandfather had thrown his open, or ridden the Amagiri roads with Hayato's people carrying medicine to villages that had no name on any map. He had never known that there were Moto who had wept, privately, for twenty years, in their own houses with the doors shut, for everything their own blood was doing.

He had only seen ten bad things and been told they were all there was.

And he was right that they were all he had been given.

"You've seen the worst of us," Kiyome said.

It was the first thing she had said since the gate. Her voice was low and level, the voice of the white line, that had kept the peace in rooms worse than this one. But Muken heard something under it, and his eyes came to her and stayed.

"I won't tell you there's been better," she said. "Not today. You'd be right not to believe me." She folded her hands in front of her. "But there has been. Once. And there could be again."

"Maybe," said Muken.

He held her eyes while the pine spat twice in the hearth. Then he looked away, at the white wool of the banner beyond the window, and the next words came out of him slowly, as if they had been lying a long way down.

"I never asked to be a king," he said. "I want you to know that. All of you. I never asked for it, and I never wanted it. A month ago I wanted a full purse and a dry bed and my sister out of the Cuttings, and that was the whole of what I wanted out of the world." He turned his eyes back to the room. "I've got none of those. I've got this instead. And I don't know yet whether I'm any good at it." He lifted his chin very slightly. "So I'll not make you speeches about what kind of king I'll be. I'll let my actions speak for themselves. You can judge me by them. So can the people. They're the ones who'll have to live with them."

Kiyome closed her eyes.

She held them closed for the space of a breath. She was thinking, Hayato knew, because he knew her, of the white knot at her hip, and the oath that went with it, and the boy's mother, and a letter of four lines in a hand she had not seen for sixteen years. She was thinking of Karogane on the tundra, going from fire to fire with Tenrai silver, and of what the Iron Fist's riders would hear shouted between the lines the morning after next if the boy did not hear it first, here, from her.

*They'll put him on a post for it,* that was what Kagenori was counting on. *His own people will do it for us.*

*No,* thought Kiyome Byakuya. *Not from you. From me. In his own hall, with his own men round him, while he can still choose what to do with it.*

She opened her eyes.

"Muken," she said.

It was the first time she had said his name.

She came forward then, all the way down the long room, past the barrel and the stools and the bench from the stables, with the hearth-light going rose and gold through the white of her robes, until she stood at the foot of his split pine table, close enough to touch the scrap-iron sword. The wolfhound's hackles went up again. Muken did not take his hand off its head. Kiyome did not look at it.

"You said you don't claim to be a Moto," she said. "And you don't. You've never claimed anything in your life, have you? You've only ever taken what you could carry." Her gray eyes held his. "So I'll claim it for you. Because somebody has to, before somebody else does it worse."

She put out one long white hand and laid her forefinger on the rough pine of the table, in the space between them.

And she drew.

It was a mark. One mark, drawn slowly, with a single finger, in the fine gray dust of the Tenrai mess where the boys had not swept. It was not a letter. It was not any letter in the Concord hand or the scribe's hand or the trader's ciphers. It had a long down-stroke and a hook, and a crossing, and a small closed loop at the foot of it like a knot in a rope, and when she lifted her finger off the end of it, it lay on the table between them in the dust, plain as a footprint.

Muken looked at it.

His face did not change. Nothing on him moved at all. But Godfrey Greymane, sitting at his right hand, saw his knuckles go white on the edge of the table, and the gold seam in his left eye flare, once, like a struck match.

He knew it. He had always known it. His mother had drawn it on the salt of the gallery wall with the burned end of a stick, when Yukazuri was very small, and pointed at it and said *that's ours. That's our name. Don't ever show it to the clerks.* Yukazuri could still draw it. She had drawn it on the frost of the window in the room over the tea-house at Halcrest and on the bottom of her salve tin and once, at the surgeons' line under the Foot, in the blood on her own palm, to steady her hands. Muken had tried a hundred times. His hand had never once been able to make it come out right.

He had never seen anyone else draw it in his life.

"Where," he said. His voice was barely there. "Where did you learn that."

"From my sister," said Kiyome. "The night she was sworn."

She did not take her eyes off his.

"The truth is, Muken, you aren't a tokenless. You never have been." Her voice was very low and very even, and each word was set down exactly where it would bear the weight. "The seals in the south don't sing for you because the registers can't read you. Every token in the inner world wants a house name to sit beneath, and the name of your house was never written in any hand a register knows. It was written in that." She did not look down at the mark. "The Kōkan wrote their names in it for a thousand years, so that no clerk and no Guild and no Measurewright could ever count them. That's why the token at your belt is dead in your hand. It's looking straight at you and can't find the column. You weren't left out of the count, child. You were the one name the whole count was built underneath."

The fire cracked. Nobody moved.

"Your mother," said Kiyome, "Kasane, is my sister."

She stopped. She had not meant to say *is.* She did not take it back.

"Kasane Byakuya. My elder, by twelve years. She heard the Telling in the Yukari valley when she was fourteen and took their name, and answered to it to the end. And when the time came for a Byakuya to stand at the Kōkan king's shoulder in the old way, the white line before the red, it was Kasane who swore." She drew a breath. "And she married him. In the white house, before our grandmother's lamp, with no clerk and no witness but me, because he asked her to, and because she loved him, and because she thought she could hold him. She married Gimbzo Kōkan Moto."

Hayato Amagiri shut his eyes.

"You have never been tokenless," said Kiyome. "Not for one day of your life. You are Gimbzo's son, and Kasane's, and the last of the red line. You are the true heir to the north." Her voice did not shake. "And to the red chair in the Hall of Verdicts, that no Kōkan has sat in for twenty years. And to everything my house was sworn, a thousand years ago, to keep for you."

### IV · Three Minutes

For a while there was only the fire.

The room went quiet in the way a room only goes quiet when something has been broken in it and everyone is waiting to see how badly. The fire went on burning. The pine spat. Outside, in the yard, the child at the woodpile had stopped splitting wood, and nobody inside could have said when. The silence came in through the narrow windows instead, enormous and white, the silence of the whole snow-bound north lying glittering to the edge of the sky.

Tetsugen Akagane stood rooted in the middle of the room, with his scarred hands on his belt and the firelight red on his copper.

Hayato had opened his eyes again. He was looking at the floor.

At the wall, Lorn Stark had not moved. He stood with his weight even on both feet and his hands still at his sides, exactly as his father had taught him, and looked at the floor three feet in front of him, and his face was the color of the wall.

Godfrey Greymane did not look at the floor. He looked at Muken.

They had known. Both of them. They had known since the gatehouse, since the night before their fathers rode south down the Kessen road to the Seat and the Hall of Verdicts and the Kurenai blade. Ulf Stark had taken Lorn up onto the wall-walk in the dark and told him all of it, plainly, in twelve sentences, and then made him swear by the sky that covers us that he would carry it until it could be carried by someone with a better right. And Wystan Greymane had sat with his son by the woodpile in the cold with a cup of the skin going sour in his hand and told him the same, in rather more words, and at the end of it had said: *He'll learn it one day, Godfrey. Better from a friend's mouth than an enemy's. But not from yours. You swear it. You hold the ridge.*

And Godfrey had held it. Every day since, at the boy's right hand, through the Foot's mending and the white banner and the kneeling by the woodpile, through *sire* and *yes, sire,* he had held the ridge. He had looked into that gray eye with its gold crack every morning and said nothing. He had found it the hardest thing he had ever done in his life.

He was not sure, now, sitting very straight on the bench from the stables, whether he was glad it was over.

Muken was silent.

He sat in Shigure Tenrai's carved chair with his hand on the wolfhound's head and his eyes on the mark in the dust, and kept his silence so long that the shadows of the people round the table, thrown long by the fire, crept a finger's width across the floor while they watched. A minute. Then two. The seal-fat lamp on the far sill guttered and steadied. A log fell in the hearth, and nobody looked at it. Somewhere outside a horse blew on the lines. Three minutes, by a fire's measure, and perhaps more. The longest anyone in that room had ever heard a man stay silent who had something to say.

What went through him in those minutes, nobody in the room could see. He did not let them.

It went through him like water under ice: fast, and black, and a long way down.

*Shigure,* he thought. *On the stair at the Foot, with her sword at my throat. I knew that face. I watched it on the night of the Kōkan cellars.* *Every tavern in Halcrest knew that story. The king who went down into the cellars with a lamp after his own line. Every Kōkan he'd left breathing. His own blood.*

*Sixteen years ago. The same year she came down into the Cuttings with me in her arms and no name the clerks would take.*

*And Yukazuri, three years after. His too.*

*And the factor at the top of the shaft. And her lungs. And the last year, when she couldn't get up off the pallet and I cut her quota as well as mine, and she'd lie with her face to the wall and I thought it was the salt. I thought it was the salt that was killing her. Every day. For a year.*

*And the morning the crier came down the galleries. The king is fallen at Shimane. And she turned her face to the wall and never turned it back.*

*It wasn't the salt.*

His hand had closed, very slowly, in the gray fur of the wolfhound's neck. The great dog bore it without a sound.

*Yusif,* he thought. *Coming down four galleries every season with salt-meat and wool. Looking at her like something in a shrine. Teaching me the stick. Teaching me to protect the weak.*

*Protect them from what, Yusif? From him?*

*From me?*

The gold seam in his eye had spread. It was very faint, a hair's breadth, nothing anyone but Godfrey and Kiyome would have seen. But it had spread, and the edges of the room had gone gold for him, the way they had gone gold on the stair at the Foot, and in the heart of all that gold, very far down, something black and hot turned over once like a sleeper and was still.

He let it lie.

He took a breath. A long one, all the way down, the way Yusif had taught him to breathe before a fight in the back of a worked-out gallery: in through the nose until the ribs ached, out through the mouth until there was nothing left. His cut side pulled under the mail. He let it.

Then he lifted his eyes from the mark in the dust, and looked at his mother's sister.

"I may be a Moto," said Muken.

His voice was quite steady. It was low, and rough at the edges, as if it had been a long way off and come back. But it did not shake.

"I may be. If you say it, I suppose I am. You'd know." He looked down, once, at the mark, and then back up. "But I don't hold the sentiments of my father. Or his ambitions. His foolish ambitions." The word came out of him like a stone out of a sling. "I don't know what he wanted. I don't care. Whatever it was, it burned Hiroe with children in it, and it emptied Harrowmere, and it took a man down into the cellars of my own town with a lamp to kill his own blood. If that's what the red line is, then it can end with me, and the north will be better for it."

He stopped. His jaw worked.

"I don't keep his memory," he said. "I keep hers."

He was quiet for a breath.

"I keep my mother's," said Muken. "And I'll tell you what I remember, Lady Kiyome, since you've come all this way to give me the rest." His hand had come off the wolfhound's head, and lay flat on the table beside the mark. "I remember her cutting salt with her lungs going, three galleries down, so Yukazuri and I could eat. I remember her coughing so hard at night she'd go out into the dead gallery so we wouldn't hear it, and we heard it anyway. I remember her drawing that." He touched the edge of the mark with one fingertip and did not smudge it. "And I remember the morning the crier came down the shafts calling that the king was fallen at Shimane. I didn't know who the king was to us. I didn't know anything. I only saw her face." His voice went very low. "I saw her heart break. I didn't know what it was. I thought she'd taken a turn. I sat with her all that day and I didn't know what I was looking at."

The pine spat in the hearth. Nobody so much as shifted a foot.

"She'd been going for a long time before that," he said. "I know that now. I watched her wither, a little more every season, and I never knew what of. I thought it was the salt." He looked at Kiyome. "It was him, wasn't it. It was always him."

Kiyome Byakuya did not answer. Her face, which had been calm and composed as still water since the gate, had broken again, and this time she did not mend it.

"Yukazuri and I sat with her," said Muken. "The two of us, in the last gallery, with the lamp, until the next morning. Nobody else came. There was nobody else to come." He breathed. "And then we put her in the salt, because there wasn't anywhere else, and we came up the shaft, and we never went down it again."

He sat back in the carved chair.

"So no," he said. "I've never been given a choice. You say I'm the heir to the north. Maybe I am. But I've never had the choice of not surviving, not once: at nine, at the top of the shaft; at fourteen, with a sword I'd taken out of a scrap-heap; on the Vessant road or the stair at the Foot." His eyes moved from Kiyome to Tetsugen to Hayato, and back. "If I hadn't tried to survive, every day, every one of those days, I'd surely have died. And my sister with me. That's the whole of what I am. That's my inheritance. It's the only one I've ever had."

He looked at the mark in the dust.

"If there's another one," he said quietly, "then it'll have to wait its turn."

### V · The Ridge

It was Godfrey who spoke first. Of course it was.

"Sire."

Muken turned his head and only looked at Godfrey Greymane, sitting at his right hand on the bench from the stables, very straight, with his long gray hands flat on the pine and the dark circles under his gray eyes.

"We knew," said Godfrey. "Lorn and I. Both of us. Our fathers told us the night before they rode south." He did not look away. "Mine made me swear it on the ridge. I swore. I'd swear it again, and I'd keep it again, every day of it. I'll not ask your pardon for that." His voice did not change. "But I'll tell you that I'm sorry for it. Every morning I said *sire* to you, I was sorry."

Muken looked at him, and went on looking.

"You held the ridge," he said at last.

"I held the ridge, sire."

"All this while. With me sat next to you." Something moved at the corner of Muken's mouth. It was nowhere near a smile. But it was nowhere near the black hot thing either. "Your father would have been proud of you. I'd have hit you, in his place. But he'd have been proud."

Godfrey's long gray hands did not move on the table. But his throat did, once.

"He told me a lord in a hurry to end a meeting leaves it knowing less," he said. "I think today he'd have said a king in a hurry to hate somebody hates the wrong man." He met Muken's eyes. "You didn't, sire. Just now. You could have. You didn't."

At the wall, Lorn Stark had lifted his eyes off the floor.

"My father said to tell you a thing," he said, roughly. "If it ever came out. He said I wasn't to say it before." He swallowed. "He said: *Tell him Yusif never once in sixteen years called him Gimbzo's boy. Tell him Yusif called him Kasane's. Every time.*" Lorn's jaw worked on the left side. "That's all. That's what he told me to say."

Muken did not answer him. He looked down at his hands on the table. After a moment he put his right one flat on his chest, over his heart, the Stark way, and then closed it into a fist against his breastbone, and pressed, hard, once.

Lorn did the same, from the wall.

Hayato Amagiri cleared his throat.

"Sire," he said. It was the first time he had used the word. He seemed to notice it as he said it, and decided to let it stand. "Forgive me. I'm the one who keeps the roads, and there's a road you need to know about before you take any of the others." He came a step into the room. "Kagenori Tenrai knows what Lady Kiyome has just told you. He's known since the Hall of Verdicts. He has a man on the tundra named Karogane, who's been going from Keld fire to Keld fire for six weeks with silver in one hand and seed grain in the other." His restless fingers had started moving again, folding and unfolding a corner of his wet cape. "When the Keld meet you on the ice the morning after next, Karogane will be standing between the lines before either side draws a blade. And he will tell your army, in a voice every rider in it can hear, whose son you are."

The room was very quiet.

"He thinks they'll put you on a post for it," said Hayato. "Every Stark and every Greymane. He thinks the word *Gimbzo* will do what four thousand Keld riders can't." He looked at Muken. "I came up four hundred miles of road to tell you that before it happened. Lady Kiyome came up the same road to make sure you heard the rest from her and not from him."

"They'll hear it from me," said Muken.

He said it at once, without any heat at all.

"Tonight. In the yard. By the woodpile, where they knelt." He looked at Lorn, and then at Godfrey. "Every Stark and every Greymane in the Foot. The boys who sewed the banner. The old woman by the barracks door with no teeth. All of them." His hand moved, once, over the mark in the dust, without touching it. "If they want to put me on a post for it, they can do it here, in front of me, tonight, and save the Keld the trouble. And if they don't." He stopped. "If they don't, then Karogane can stand between the lines and shout whatever he likes at the ice. There'll be nobody on our side of it who hasn't heard it already."

Tetsugen Akagane gave the boy in the Tenrai chair the same weighing look as before, from under his thick gray brows, and held it longer. Then he turned, slowly, to Kiyome.

"That," he said, in his flat iron voice, "is not his father."

"No," said Kiyome Byakuya. Her eyes were wet and she did not trouble to hide it. "No. That is my sister."

Muken did not look at either of them. He was looking past them, past the barrel and the stools and the bench, to where the Crown Prince of Kushara had stepped aside and stood beside the table with his great arms folded into his sleeves. He had heard all of it. Every word. And Muken found, somewhat to his surprise, that he did not mind.

"You said you don't particularly like the Moto, Prince," he said.

His voice was quiet. It had come back from wherever it had been, all the way, and it was his own again: the flat salt-gallery voice, every word set down like a foot on a bad ledge.

"It seems we've that much in common." He touched the dead bronze token on his belt, once, with two fingers. "And you said one day. When I'm not surrounded by all this." He looked at the prince steadily, without any heat, and without looking away. "I'd hold you to that. If we're both still breathing when the snow goes."

Then he turned back to the table, to his aunt and the two lords of the seven lines, and put both hands flat on the rough pine on either side of the mark in the dust.

"Now," said King Muken. "You've told me who my father was. Tell me who the Keld are bringing. And tell me what the Tenrai have promised them. All of it." He looked up. "I march in two days. I'd like to know what I'm marching into."

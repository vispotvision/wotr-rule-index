## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Thirteen: The Skeldmere

---

> *The Keld have a word for the hour before a whiteout, when the light goes flat and the horizon lifts off the ice and floats, and a man cannot tell any more whether the white in front of him is ground or sky. They call it* the lifting. *A Keld child is taught to stop walking in the lifting, and sit down where he is, and wait. A Keld rider is taught the opposite.*
>
> *From* Customs of the Polar Reach, *a Hallenfeld quartermaster's notebook*

---

### I · The Carter

The carter came in out of the dark an hour before dawn, half dead, on foot, with frost in his beard and both his hands gone white to the wrist.

The Keld outriders found him on the Skeldmere a mile from the fires, walking in a slow wandering line that had already bent twice back on itself, the way men walked when the cold had got into their heads. They put him across a saddle and brought him in, and laid him by the fat-lamps in the war-lodge on a reindeer hide, in the deep butter-yellow of their light, and a Keld woman with a face like a walnut rubbed snow into his hands, hard, until he screamed. Then they gave him the skin, the sour fermented milk in its sealskin, and he drank it and was sick and drank it again, and when he could speak, he spoke.

Aksel Keld sat on the bare packed snow of the lodge floor and listened to him.

He listened to all of it. The torches in the yard and the six hundred people in the breath-cloud. The woodpile. The boy standing at the foot of it with his white hair in the torchlight. *I am Muken Moto. First of my name.* The three lords of the seven lines coming out of the crowd one after another and giving up their land, all of it, in the snow, in front of everyone. *Son of Kasane, and son of Gimbzo.* The girl with the blood on her hand. The old woman who started the chant.

"They knelt?" Aksel said.

The carter's lips were cracked and bleeding. He nodded.

"Knowing."

"Knowing." The carter's voice was a wet rasp. "He told them. Every word of it. Then he told them they could walk out, and nobody would stop them. I walked." He coughed, and something came up that the walnut-faced woman wiped off his chin with a fistful of snow. "Twenty of us did. Thirty. Out of six hundred."

The lodge was very quiet. The fat-lamps hissed.

Behind Aksel's left shoulder, a pace back, in plain gray with his hood down, Karogane had not moved. Aksel knew what his face was doing without turning to look. He had sat across a lamp from that face for six weeks. It was a long pale face with very pale eyes, and it did nothing, ever, and Aksel had learned to read the nothing the way he read a sky.

It was doing a great deal of nothing now.

"So," said Aksel Keld.

He turned the horn cup on his knee a quarter-turn with his left hand, slowly, and looked into the milk.

"So your sentence is spent, Tenrai." He did not turn his head. "You meant to stand between the lines and tell them whose son he was, and watch them put him on a post. The boy said it first. In his own yard. By his own wood." He drank, and set the cup down. "It's a strange thing to learn about an enemy the night before you fight him. That he's not afraid of the truth."

"It changes nothing," said Karogane.

His voice was soft and dry and perfectly level. It was the first thing he had said since the carter came in.

"It changes the morning," said Aksel. "A man who's been told he's following Gimbzo's son and kneels anyway won't break when you shout it at him across the ice. He'll laugh at you." He looked, at last, over his shoulder, and his narrow black eyes held the Tenrai agent's pale ones. "I had six hundred riders in my count who'd have turned their horses round on the ice when they heard it. Starks with Stark grievances. Greymanes who buried brothers at Hiroe. Now I've none. Now I've four hundred of Lorn Stark's best in front of me, who know exactly whose son they're following, and chose him anyway." He turned back to the lamp. "I'd sooner fight four thousand men who don't know why they're there than four hundred who do."

"You have four thousand," said Karogane.

"I do," said Aksel. "And I'll use them." He lifted his iron hand off his knee and let it lie on his thigh, black and heavy, with the cord running up into the sleeve. "But don't tell me nothing's changed. Something always changes. A Keld who can't see it when it does is a Keld who walks out into the lifting and never comes back."

He got to his feet.

He did it all at once, the way the Keld rose from their fires, with no hand on the floor, his short powerful legs taking all of him straight up off the packed snow. The walrus-ivory rings in his braids clicked. Around the lodge the Keld captains rose with him, one for each of his fights, men he had broken in and men who had broken in beside him, in their reindeer coats with their spears and their short recurved bows of horn and sinew, and the old Keld war-chant they never sang aloud but only hummed, low in the chest, began somewhere at the back of the lodge.

"Wake the fires," said Aksel Keld. "We ride at the gray."

### II · We March

The Keld did not form ranks. They had never formed ranks. A rank was a southern thing, a wall of men who stood in a line because they could not trust themselves to stand anywhere else. The Keld came out of their snow-shelters in the black hour before the gray and found their horses by smell, and mounted, and drifted together across the ice in loose knots of twenty and thirty that moved like a herd of reindeer moves, each knot with its own captain and its own reason, and the knots drifted together in the dark until they were four thousand riders spread over a mile of the Skeldmere, breathing.

Six hundred men breathing in the cold made a sound like a tide coming in under a pier. Four thousand made a sound like the sea.

They were short men on short horses, the northern kind that did not founder in the drift and could live a week on lichen and their own fat. Every rider had two coats, the inner of reindeer hair turned in and the outer of sealskin turned out, and a hood of wolverine fur that did not take frost. Every rider had his bow in a case of sealskin at his knee and thirty arrows in the quiver at his back, the heads of black glass and bone because iron cracked in the deep cold. Every rider had a lance of birch with a long leaf head, and a short axe, and a knife. And every rider had, tied at his saddle-bow, a twist of white cloth the length of his arm, which he would pull over his face and his horse's face in the lifting, so that a Keld rider in a whiteout was a white thing on a white thing in a white world, and could not be seen until he was close enough to touch.

Aksel Keld rode out in front of them as the gray came up, and with it, low in the east behind the Grey Teeth, a band of rose and pale gold no wider than a hand.

He sat his horse, a stocky dun with a black stripe down its back, at the edge of the great loose herd of them, and looked out east over the ice to where the long dark line of the Grey Teeth stood up out of the white, and then south, toward the Foot. Toward the boy.

Karogane rode up beside him on a tall gray southern horse that stood a hand higher than any Keld mount and looked absurd for it, and stopped, and said nothing.

Aksel gave him one look, and no more.

Then he turned his horse to face the Keld, all four thousand of them, spread over the ice in the early light with their breath going up in a single white mist that the low sun turned pink along its top, and he did not raise his voice. The Keld did not raise their voices. The wind took anything that was not said plainly.

"We march," said Aksel Keld.

The mist moved.

"For the safety of our people," he said. "For the herds, and the fires, and the children in the snow-houses who'll eat this spring because of Tenrai seed grain and not because of anybody's crown." He let it sit. "We march for ours. For the ice. For the Keld." His narrow black eyes went over them, slowly, from one knot to the next, and every captain he looked at lifted his chin. "And we ride over all who'd stand in the way of it. Moto or Stark or Greymane or the Sky itself. Whatever's in front of us when the lifting comes, we ride over it, and we don't look back to see what it was."

He lifted his right hand.

The iron hand. Black iron and walrus tusk and sinew, the heavy articulated fingers closing slowly as he drew the cord at his shoulder, until it was a fist the size of a man's skull, held up black against the rose of the eastern sky over the Skeldmere where every Keld rider on the ice could see it.

"The Iron Fist of the Keld," said Aksel.

He said nothing after it. Four thousand throats took it up, low, a hum in the chest that went across the ice like a wind coming, and the horses stamped and blew, and the frozen ground rang under their hooves and came up through the horses into the riders' knees.

Beside him, on his absurd tall gray horse, Karogane watched the Keld warlord with his very pale eyes, and his long pale face did nothing at all.

*For now,* Aksel Keld thought again, as he had thought in the lodge with his fist on his heart, and lowered his hand.

### III · The Earful

At the Foot, Yukazuri was waiting at the gate.

She had been up all night. Muken could see it in the set of her shoulders under her shawl and the color of the skin under her eyes. She had been up all night at the surgeons' line with the two Greymane women who could stitch and the Stark boy who could carry, laying out everything she had on clean linen in rows: the gut and the needles, the salve tins, the spirit, the long iron probes for arrowheads and the short curved knife for what the probes could not reach, the splints, the straps, the boiled cloth in its wooden tubs with the steam still coming off it. He knew because he had come to the tent door at midnight and seen her at it, kneeling between the rows with her hands moving and her lips moving, counting, and had gone away again without saying anything because there was nothing he could say that she had not already counted.

She stood at the gate now in the first light, with the frost on the new gate-timbers glittering and the white banner hanging over her head, blank as new snow, and her sleeves already rolled to the elbow in the cold.

Behind her the yard was full of horses. Four hundred of them. Lorn Stark's vanguard, the best of the Stark riders and the boys who had sewn the banner, in their hide-coats and mail with their axes across their backs and their round shields of limewood and hide on their arms, mounted and waiting, their breath going up in a single low cloud. The Greymane foot stood along the walls above them in their long gray file, spears grounded, watching. They were not riding. Godfrey Greymane had argued against it for an hour in the long room and lost, and then argued for it, quietly, to his own men, for another hour, and won. *Somebody holds the Foot,* he had said, finally, standing at the end of the split pine table with his long gray hands flat on the wood. *If the Keld come round us in the snow and we've left the gate with nobody on it, we'll have no Foot to come home to. The Greymanes hold. It's what we do.*

He stood at the gatehouse now, a little apart from his men, very straight, in gray, and did not look at the yard.

Muken stopped in front of his sister.

He looked at her. She looked back at him, with her small sharp face set like a closed fist and her gray eyes very steady, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the four hundred horses blew and stamped behind them, and somewhere on the wall a Greymane spearman coughed.

"Please be careful," said Yukazuri.

That was all. Her voice was very quiet and quite level. She did not reach for him. Her hands stayed at her sides with her sleeves rolled up, red at the knuckles from the boiled cloth.

Muken looked at her a moment longer.

Then he put his hand on her head.

It was the old thing. It was what Yusif had done, once or twice, in the gallery, when he was going away and did not want to say so. It was what Muken had done on the convoy road at the Marrow crossing and in the room over the tea-house and in the dark at the back of the surgeons' tent two nights ago, when he had told her whose mark it was. The whole weight of his hand, flat and warm through her hair, and not ruffling it, and not pressing down. Just there.

"I know," he said. "If I'm not, you'll give me an earful."

The corner of her mouth moved. It was nowhere near a smile.

"So I'll try my best," said Muken.

"Try harder than your best," said Yukazuri. "Your best got you a cut across the ribs I had to close twice."

"Then I'll try harder than that."

"Good." She did not move from under his hand. "Because if you come back through this gate on a door with your guts in your lap, I'll put them back in, and I'll sew you up, and then I'll kill you myself."

Somebody in the front rank of the vanguard laughed. It was the boy with the red ears. He stopped at once, and went red to the collar, and then, because nobody told him off, he laughed again, quietly, and the boy beside him did too.

Muken took his hand off her head.

He did not say goodbye. Neither of them did. He turned and went to his horse, a rangy Stark bay with one white foot that Lorn had given him out of the Hollenmark string, and mounted, and the wolfhound came out of the gatehouse at a lope and fell in beside its stirrup without being called. He looked once at the gatehouse, at Godfrey. Godfrey Greymane looked back at him, and put his right hand flat on his chest, and held it there.

Then Muken rode out of the gate of Kharven's Foot at the head of four hundred Stark riders, under the white banner, onto the Kessen road, and did not look back.

Yukazuri stood in the gate and watched them go until the last of them had passed under the arch and the road had taken them and the snow they had kicked up had settled again.

Then she turned round and walked back across the yard to the surgeons' line and knelt down between her rows of linen, and began counting again from the beginning.

### IV · The Ice

The Skeldmere was a lake, once.

Nobody living had seen it as one. It had frozen through in the century the grass went and never thawed, and the snow had come down on it and been packed hard by the wind and come down again, year on year, until it was a plain of white so flat and so wide that a man standing in the middle of it could see the curve of the world. Under the snow, if you dug, there was ice, black and clear, and under the ice, a long way down, the old lake was still there in the dark, and sometimes on a still night the ice moved over it and boomed like a great drum struck very far away, and the Keld said it was the drowned grass trying to come up.

The vanguard came out onto it at the second hour of the morning, from the Kessen road, and stopped at the shore. The low sun lay along the Skeldmere and gilded every wind-ridge on it, and the hollows between the ridges were blue as a jay's wing.

Muken sat his bay with Lorn Stark at his right hand and looked across the ice.

He had known the number. Godfrey had told him in the long room. The outriders had told him every night for a week. He had said it to the yard himself, at the woodpile. *Four thousand riders.* He had thought he understood what it meant.

He had not.

They covered the ice. From the dark line of the Grey Teeth in the east to the white blur of the far shore in the north, the Keld were spread over the Skeldmere in their loose drifting knots like a herd that went on to the edge of seeing, and the edge of seeing went on further than it should have, because the light was already going flat. Four thousand riders, and every one of them a short dark shape on a short dark horse, and over all of them their breath standing in a mist that the low gray sky came down to meet. They were not moving. They sat their horses on the ice the way the ice sat on the lake, as if they had always been there and always would.

"Four thousand," said Lorn Stark quietly.

"Four thousand," said Muken.

"There's really four thousand of them."

"There really are."

Lorn said nothing else. Behind them, four hundred Stark riders sat their horses on the shore of the Skeldmere and looked out at the same thing, and none of them said anything either, and the only sound was the horses and the wind and, very faint, very far out on the ice, a deep low boom that came up through the frozen ground into the horses' legs.

Muken's eyes moved over the Keld.

He did not decide to do it. It happened the way it had happened on the stair at the Foot, and on the Vessant road, and in every ditch he had ever stood in: the Shingan opened behind his left eye like a hand unclosing, and the gold seam in his iris spread a hair, and the world changed.

The Keld were still there. The ice was still there. But now, over all of it, like frost on a window, there were lines.

Thin lines. Hair-fine. Gold. They ran through the Keld host the way cracks run through old glaze, between one knot of riders and the next, along the seams where one captain's men ended and another's began, branching and joining and branching again. Most of them were faint, hardly there. A few were brighter. And one, out on the left of the Keld host, toward the Grey Teeth, between a big knot of riders on dark horses and a smaller one on grays, was bright enough that it seemed to hum.

*There,* thought Muken. *That's where it's tired.*

He did not know how he knew. He had never known. He only knew that it had never once been wrong.

"Lorn," he said. "The grays. On their left, by the mountains."

Lorn looked.

"I see them."

"They don't like the ones next to them," said Muken. "The big knot, on the dark horses. Something between them. Old." He did not take his eyes off the gold line. "If it breaks anywhere, it breaks there."

Lorn Stark gave him a long sideways look. Then he looked back at the ice, and his jaw tightened on the left side, and he nodded once.

"The grays are the Sennet clan," he said. "The dark horses are the Haldorssons. Sennet and Haldorsson have been killing each other over the Teeth pastures since my grandfather's time. Aksel Keld broke them both in and made them ride together." He was quiet a moment. "You couldn't have known that."

"I didn't," said Muken.

Out on the ice, a knot of riders had detached itself from the front of the Keld host and was coming toward them across the snow at a walk.

### V · Between the Lines

They met in the middle of the ice, halfway between the two hosts, where the snow lay untouched and very white.

Twelve Keld riders came out, in a loose ring, with Aksel Keld at the front of them on his dun with the black stripe, and the tall gray southern horse beside him. Muken took Lorn and six Stark riders, and the wolfhound, and nobody else. He had thought of taking Kiyome and her forty. He had thought better of it. *If the Tenrai see Byakuya white beside me at a parley,* he had said to her at dawn, *they'll know what you've done before you're ready for them to know it.* She had looked at him a long moment and then gone to her riders, and they had ridden off north along the shore before light, toward Thornhaven, and he had not seen them since.

Hayato had gone two nights ago. South and west, on his own roads, toward Ashbury, with his twenty. *Give me two days,* he had said, mounting in the dark of the yard with the juniper still in his toggle. *Two days and a road. That's all I've ever needed.*

It had been two days.

Muken looked across the snow at the Iron Fist of the Keld.

He was younger than Muken had expected, and older. Eighteen, Godfrey had said, and he had the face of eighteen, broad and flat and wind-burned red-brown across the cheekbones, with the three thin blue lines down his chin and the fourth beside them. But he sat his horse like a man of forty, low and still and settled, as if the horse were part of the ice and he were part of the horse, and the iron hand lay on his thigh, black and heavy, and did not move. The walrus-ivory rings in his braids, one for each fight, clicked faintly when the wind took them.

Aksel Keld looked back at him with narrow black eyes and said nothing at all.

It was the man beside him who dismounted.

He swung down off the tall gray horse in one smooth movement, plain gray cloak swirling, and stood on the snow, tall and lean with his hood thrown back and a long pale face and very pale eyes. He carried no sword that Muken could see. He did not need one. He moved the way Shigure Tenrai had moved on the stair, with nothing wasted and nothing hurried, as though every step had been measured out for him in advance and he was only walking the measure.

*Karogane,* thought Muken. *So that's what a knife looks like.*

The Tenrai agent ignored him. He looked past him, at the young Stark at his right hand, and his thin mouth moved very slightly at one corner.

"Lorn Stark," said Karogane. His voice was soft and dry and carried perfectly across the still air. "Son of Ulf. Warlord of Hollenmark." He let each title fall like a coin into a bowl. "And you would stand beside a tokenless."

Lorn Stark looked down at him from the saddle and let the silence have its say first.

Then he shook his head. Slowly. Once to each side.

"Watch your tone," said Lorn, "when you speak of the Rightful King of the Clans. And of the Banner Houses." His voice was rough and low and did not rise. "You are nothing more than a lord's man, Tenrai. You don't even have a chair. You'll address him properly, or you'll not address him."

The Keld riders in the ring stirred. Aksel Keld did not.

Karogane's pale eyes moved, at last, to Muken.

Muken looked back at him.

He looked at the long pale face and the plain gray cloak and the hands, which were long and pale too, and motionless at his sides, and very clean. He looked at the Tenrai gold thread worked so fine into the hem of the gray that you could only see it when the light caught it. He thought about the stair at the Foot, and Shigure's sword at his throat, and the Tenrai factor at the top of the shaft with his ledger. He thought about Kagenori in the Hall of Verdicts, and the letters with two seals, and the silver always on the left.

"So this is what my family has become," said Muken.

He said it quietly, almost to himself, and it carried anyway.

"A muck," he said. "A muck of disgust. Clerks and knives and men who send other men's sons to die on the ice for a chair they'll never sit in." He did not look away from Karogane's face. "My father was a monster. I'll not argue it. But he stood in the mud at Shimane himself, at the end. He didn't send a man in gray to stand there for him."

Something moved in Karogane's face.

It was the first time Muken had seen it move. It was very small, a tightening at the corners of the pale eyes, a whitening at the nostrils. But it was there. And when Karogane spoke again, his voice was no longer quite so soft.

"You are not even supposed to be alive," he said.

The words came out of him like something that had been held a long time.

"Do you understand that? You should have died in the cellars at Halcrest with the rest of your line, sixteen years ago. Your mother should never have walked out of that door. Every breath you've drawn since is a clerk's error." His long pale hands had closed at his sides. "And now you sit on a borrowed horse in a dead man's coat and think you can win this? With your little four hundred?" He swept one hand back at the Keld, at the four thousand on the ice behind him. "You think you can protect those people in your long houses? Your woodpile? Your old women and your boys with needles?" His voice dropped. "You're dooming them. Every one. When the Keld are done with you, the Tenrai will come to the Foot, and the Hall after them, and there'll be nothing north of the Kessen road but ash and crows. And it'll be your name they curse for it."

The ice boomed, far off, under the snow.

Muken looked at Aksel Keld.

He did it slowly, on purpose. He turned his head away from the Tenrai agent and looked at the Iron Fist on his dun horse, and held his narrow black eyes, unhurried, as if Karogane had stopped speaking some time ago and was no longer there. Aksel looked back at him. Neither of them said anything.

Then Muken turned his head back to Karogane.

"Dooming," he said. "No."

He let it sit.

"I'm not afraid," said Muken. "Of you. Of them." He moved his chin, very slightly, at the Keld host. "I've been doomed since I was born. You just said so. A clerk's error." His mouth moved at the corner. "I've lived sixteen years as a clerk's error. I can manage a morning more."

"Nobody," said Karogane, "will take the north except Shizuha."

He had mastered himself again. The voice was soft and level and dry, and the hands had opened.

"Shizuha Shirogane," he said, "is the Queen of the Clans. The silver line was always the rightful second of the red. Now the red is ended, whatever a boy in a sergeant's coat tells his woodpile, and the silver stands first." He lifted his chin. "The Hall will confirm it before the thaw. The Keld have already sworn. Thornwall and Ashmore have taken her silver. By the spring there won't be a lord in the north who hasn't knelt to her, and those who haven't will be under the snow."

Muken shook his head.

"No," he said. "You'll submit to me, Tenrai."

Karogane stared at him.

"You," said Muken. "And Kagenori. And Shizuha Shirogane in her silver chair, and every line in the Hall that ever sent a levy south. All of you." His voice did not rise. "You'll give up your lands, the way the Byakuya and the Akagane and the Amagiri gave theirs. Into one hand. And I'll bind the seven lines back into one blood, the way they were before any of you learned to count." He held the pale eyes. "Seven lines and one blood. Isn't that what they teach you in the Seat? I'll make it true."

For a moment the ice was silent.

Then Karogane laughed.

It was a short, dry, genuine laugh, and it went across the untouched snow and made two of the Keld riders glance at each other.

"Do you understand," said Karogane, "what you are saying? Do you have the faintest idea?" He spread his long pale hands. "You're telling me you'll make war on the whole of the Moto house. Every line. Every seat. Every Wellspring under Guild lead from here to the southern sea. With four hundred riders and a dog."

Muken understood.

He understood it very well. He had understood it in the long room, with his hands pressed flat on the pine to stop them shaking, and he had understood it at the woodpile, in the torchlight, with the breath-cloud over him. He understood it now, on the ice, with four thousand Keld at the Tenrai's back and the light going flat and the gold lines running through the host behind Aksel Keld like cracks in old glaze.

"I understand," said Muken. "Everyone who stands against my claim will be destroyed."

He said it quite plainly, without any heat at all.

"Everyone," he said. "That's what it means. I'll not pretend otherwise. I'd sooner they didn't. I'd sooner every lord in the north came in under the white banner tomorrow and nobody had to bleed for it." He held Karogane's eyes. "But I swore at the woodpile that I'd fight for those people. Die for them. Put everything on the line. And anyone who stands between me and them is standing on the line." He gathered his reins. "So they'll be destroyed. You'll be destroyed. And I'll not look back to see what you were."

He turned his horse.

There was nothing more to say, and everyone on the ice knew it. Lorn turned his horse after him, and the six Stark riders after him, and the wolfhound, and they rode back across the untouched snow toward the white banner at a walk, without hurrying, with their backs to the Keld.

Behind them, Karogane was shouting.

He was shouting it at last, the thing he had come four hundred miles across the tundra to shout. He had climbed back into his saddle on the tall gray horse and stood in the stirrups, facing the Stark line, and his soft dry voice had found a carrying edge Muken would not have believed it had.

"*Gimbzo's son!*" shouted Karogane. "*You are following Gimbzo's son! The butcher of Hiroe! The cellar king! His blood is in that boy and you are following it onto the ice!*"

The Stark line was silent for a moment.

Then, from somewhere in the middle of it, a voice called back. It was Rob Stark's. Muken knew it at once, rough and cheerful and a little hoarse from the cold.

"*We know!*"

And then, from the front rank, the boy with the red ears, high and cracking:

"*He told us himself, you daft bastard! Two nights back! At the woodpile!*"

And then the whole Stark line was laughing. Four hundred riders on the shore of the Skeldmere in the flattening light, laughing at a Tenrai agent standing in his stirrups on a horse too tall for the ice, and the laughter went out across the snow and came back off the Grey Teeth, and Karogane stood in the stirrups with his mouth still open and his long pale face, for the first time in anyone's memory, going a dull and blotched red.

Aksel Keld did not laugh.

He sat his dun horse beside the Tenrai and watched the boy with the white hair ride back to his line, and his narrow black eyes did not move, and his iron hand lay on his thigh. Then he lifted his left hand, once, and lowered it.

Behind him, four thousand Keld riders pulled the white cloth up over their faces.

### VI · The Lifting

The light went flat.

It went all at once, the way the Keld said it did, the way the Hallenfeld quartermasters wrote in their notebooks and nobody south of the Kessen road believed. One moment there was a horizon, the dark line of the Grey Teeth and the white blur of the far shore and the gray sky over both. The next there was nothing. The ice and the sky became one white, and the white had no edge and no depth, and a man looking into it could not tell whether the thing in front of him was a mile off or a hand's width, or whether it was there at all. The horizon lifted off the Skeldmere and floated. The sky came down. The world went blind.

And out of the blindness came the Keld.

A southern host charged in a wall, with a sound. The Keld came out of the white in silence, in their loose drifting knots, white riders on white-hooded horses, and the first thing the Stark line knew of them was the arrows.

Black glass and bone, out of nowhere, with no sound until they arrived. They came down on the Stark line in long slanting flights from three directions at once, and the Starks raised their limewood shields and took them, and some of them did not. A rider two places down from Muken took one through the throat above the mail collar, a short black shaft standing out of the side of his neck under the jaw, and he put his hand up to it as though to brush away a fly, and then the blood came, bright and fast, the carotid on the left side opened, and it came down the front of his hide-coat in a sheet and steamed in the cold, and he looked at Muken with an expression of great surprise and slid sideways off his horse into the snow. He was dead before he reached it. He could not have lived another twenty breaths with that vessel open, and he did not get twenty.

Another took one in the thigh, high, in the meat of the quadriceps, through the hide and the wool, and screamed and kept his saddle. Another took two in his horse, and the horse went down screaming and rolled on him, and the sound his leg made under it was like a green branch breaking.

Then the Keld were among them.

They came out of the white close enough to touch, as the old saying went, white shapes that resolved into men only at a lance's length, and the long leaf-headed birch lances came in low and fast at the Stark horses' chests and the Stark riders' bellies. A Stark rider on Muken's left took a lance through the side under the arm where the mail gapped, and the leaf head went in between his ribs and out of his back, and the Keld who held it let go of the shaft and was gone back into the white before the Stark had finished falling. Another Keld came in from the right, low on his horse's neck, and swung a short axe into the knee of a Stark bay as he passed, and the horse folded and its rider went over its head into the snow, and two more Keld rode over him before he could rise.

They did not stay. That was the terror of it. They did not stand and fight. They came out of the white and struck and were gone, and came out of it again somewhere else, and struck, and were gone, and the Stark line could not close with them because there was nothing to close with, only white, and arrows coming out of the white, and lances, and the sound of men screaming in a blindness that had no edges.

"*Close up!*" Lorn Stark was roaring. "*Close up! Shields! Shields together!*"

They closed up. They did it well. Lorn Stark's vanguard were the best riders in Hollenmark and they had been drilled since they were boys, and they pulled in round the white banner in a tight ring with their shields locked and their axes out and their horses' heads turned outward, and the Keld arrows rattled off the limewood like hail on a roof. But the ring was a target, and the Keld knew it. They circled it in the white, unseen, and shot into it, and every time a Stark rider went down, the ring had to close again, smaller.

Muken sat his bay at the heart of it with the scrap-iron sword in his hand and the wolfhound pressed against his stirrup, snarling at nothing, and looked into the white.

He could see the lines.

That was the thing. The Shingan did not care about the lifting. The Keld were invisible to his right eye and his left eye both, and still the gold lines ran through the host, and the host was there whether he could see it or not. He could see the gold running through the white like veins through marble. He could see where the knots were, by the seams between them. He could see them moving, circling the Stark ring in the blindness, drifting in and striking and drifting out. And he could see the bright line out on the left, the humming one, between the Sennet grays and the Haldorsson dark horses, still there, still bright, still tired.

But he could not reach it. It was half a mile off across the ice, behind a thousand Keld in a white he could not ride through without losing his way inside forty paces. And between him and it, the Stark ring was getting smaller.

"*Lorn,*" he said. "How many?"

Lorn Stark's face was white and his helmet was gone and there was blood on his cheek from a cut that had nearly taken his ear. He did not look round.

"Forty down," he said. "Fifty. I don't know. They're picking us apart."

"I know."

"We can't stay here."

"I know."

"Then what in the Sky's name do we do?"

Muken did not answer.

Out in the white, on the right, the gold lines had begun to move in a way he had not seen before. They were drawing together. Knot after knot of Keld, drifting in from their wide circle round the Stark ring, closing on one place, east of it, toward the Grey Teeth, gathering. The lines between them were going thick and bright where they had been thin, the seams knitting shut, the whole host on that side pulling into a single dense weave like a fist closing.

*He's done picking,* thought Muken. *He's going to hit us.*

He could see where. He could see the shape of it in the gold, the way you could see the shape of a hand inside a mitten. A wedge. A great dense wedge of a thousand riders, more, forming in the blindness on the right, with its point aimed at the eastern face of the Stark ring, and at the point of it, at the very tip of the gold, one knot brighter and denser than all the others, with a single line running through the heart of it so thick and so bright that it did not look like a crack at all. It looked like a bar of iron.

*Aksel.*

The wedge began to move.

### VII · Thornhaven and Ashbury

The horns came from the north.

Three long notes, low and harsh, from somewhere out on the white beyond the far shore of the Skeldmere, where there should have been nothing but ice and the empty tundra. The Keld did not use horns, and these were no Stark horns either. Lorn Stark's head came round and his bloody face went blank with astonishment.

"That's Thornwall," he said. "That's the thorn-horn. That's *Thornwall.*"

Muken was not listening to him. He was looking at the gold.

Out on the north side of the Keld host, at its very edge, something had come into the weave that had not been there before. A new pattern. Dense and square and rigid, utterly unlike the loose drifting knots of the Keld, with lines in it so straight they might have been ruled with a rod. It was coming out of the white from the north at a steady walk. And at its front, small and bright, there was something else, a short line of pale gold he had seen once before, in the cedar hall, in the long room, in the yard. Kiyome's line. The white line.

*She went to Thornhaven,* he thought. *She said she had cousins there. She didn't say she'd bring them.*

They came out of the lifting like a wall walking.

Thornwall pikes. Twelve hundred of them, Lorn said afterward, and he had counted the banners, each with its dark red thorn-sprig standing out of the white like blood on a sleeve: the men of Thornhaven, in their heavy gray felt and their iron caps, in a solid block forty men wide and thirty deep, with their eighteen-foot ash pikes leveled before them in a hedge so thick that a horse could not have pushed its nose through it. They did not hurry. They came down out of the north onto the Keld flank at a walk, in step, to the slow beat of a drum, with the thorn-horn sounding behind them every hundred paces, and at their head, on a white horse, in near-white robes with her loose white hair blown back and the glass beads at her temples catching what light there was and throwing it back in small sparks of every color, rode Kiyome Byakuya, with forty Byakuya riders at her back and a woman beside her in Thornwall gray whom Muken had never seen.

The Keld on the north side of the host did not know what had hit them.

They had been circling the Stark ring, drifting in and out of the white, picking at it. They had their backs to the north. They had been riding in the lifting all morning and the lifting had been theirs, their blindness, their advantage, and now something had come out of it at their backs that they could not ride round or through or past, a moving hedge of iron points with twelve hundred men behind it, and their short horses ran straight into it in the white before their riders saw it.

The sound of horses on pikes was a sound Muken never forgot.

And then, before the Keld on the north side had understood what was happening to them, the second thing came.

It came from the south-west, out of the Kessen road, behind the Stark ring, along the shore. Riders. Not many, five or six hundred, in the plain brown and russet of the Ashmore marches, the russet bright as autumn bracken against the snow, on big southern horses, with lances and long swords and the Ashmore boar on their pennants, at the gallop, in a long ragged line. And at the front of them, on a horse too small for the company it was keeping, with an indigo rain-cape streaming out behind him and a sprig of juniper still caught in the toggle at his throat, rode Hayato Amagiri.

He had his sword out. Muken had never seen him with his sword out. He held it badly, like a man who carried it for show, and he rode badly too, too far forward, all elbows. But he was at the front.

"*Two days!*" Hayato was yelling, as he came past the Stark ring at the gallop with the Ashmore riders streaming after him. "*Two days and a road, sire! Two days and a road!*"

And the Ashmore horse went into the Keld on the south side of the host at full tilt.

---

They told it afterward round the fires, the ones who lived, and the telling got bigger every time, the way tellings do. But this much was true, and Lorn Stark swore to it, and so did Hayato.

Kiyome Byakuya had not gone to Thornhaven on the night of the woodpile. She had sent a crow before it. She had sent it the night she arrived at the Foot, before she had even told Muken who he was, because she had known that whatever he chose, the Keld were coming, and four hundred riders could not hold the Skeldmere against four thousand. The crow had gone to the Lady of Thornwall at Thornhaven. And the Lady of Thornwall was a Byakuya. She had been born in the white house in the Shiraki hills, a cousin of Kiyome's mother, and married north forty years ago into the Thornwall line to seal a peace nobody now remembered, and she had sat in Thornhaven ever since under Tenrai taxes and Tenrai factors and Tenrai silver, and smiled, and paid, and kept the oath of her house over the lamp every night with the doors shut. *Purity before power. Peace before the blade. And the red line before the white.*

Kiyome's crow had said four words. *The red line lives.*

The Lady of Thornwall had called out her pikes the next morning. The Tenrai factor at Thornhaven had objected. She had had him put in the cellar. The pikes had marched two days without stopping across the frozen tundra north of the Skeldmere, through country nobody marched through in winter, and come down on the Keld out of the lifting because a Byakuya rider who had grown up on that ice had led them through the whiteout by the sound of the lake booming under it.

Ashmore was simpler, and Hayato told it himself, plainly, the way he told distances. Ashmore had taken Tenrai silver because Ashmore had no choice. The Ashmore marches lay on the junction of three roads, the Kessen and the Marrow and the salt road from the south, and whoever held the roads held Ashmore's grain, and for twenty years the Tenrai had held them. Hayato had ridden into Ashbury two nights ago with twenty riders and a map. He had laid the map on Lord Edric Ashmore's table. Every road into the Ashmore marches that the Tenrai knew, he had drawn in black. Every road they did not know, his roads, the Amagiri tracks, the herders' shoulders and the deer paths and the smugglers' fords, he had drawn in indigo. There had been more indigo than black.

*Your grain comes up the black roads,* Hayato had said, *at the Tenrai's price. Swear to the king of the north, and from this spring it comes up the indigo, at no price at all, and no Tenrai factor will ever find the way.*

Edric Ashmore had looked at the map under the low hum of the one worked lamp in his hall, the only licensed draw in the marches, for as long as it took his steward to bring wine and carry it away again untouched. Then he had sworn, at the junction stone at the crossroads below Ashbury, with his hand on the stone, to Hayato Amagiri in the name of the king. And he had called out his horse.

---

On the ice, the Keld host began to come apart.

Muken saw it in the gold before he saw it with his eyes. The north side of the host, where the Thornwall pikes had come in, had gone into a chaos of broken lines and knots spinning off into the white. The south side, where the Ashmore horse had struck, was buckling, the knots pulling back on each other, the seams between them glowing hot. And out on the left, by the Grey Teeth, the bright humming line between the Sennet grays and the Haldorsson dark horses had begun, at last, to run.

He watched it go. It went like a crack runs across ice when the weight comes down: all at once, faster than the eye, along its whole length. The Sennet grays turned their horses, and turned them on the Haldorssons beside them, in the white, with their lances down, because whatever old thing lay between those two clans over the Teeth pastures had been waiting for exactly this, for the Iron Fist to be busy somewhere else, and the Iron Fist was busy.

*There,* thought Muken. *I told you. That's where it was tired.*

But the wedge was still coming.

The great dense wedge on the east side, the thousand riders gathered behind the bar of iron. The Thornwall pikes had not reached it. The Ashmore horse had not reached it. It was coming out of the white straight at the eastern face of the Stark ring at a gallop, the frozen ground ringing under it like a drum, the white cloths over the riders' faces streaming back, and at its very point, on a dun horse with a black stripe down its back, with his right arm drawn back and his iron fist closed, rode Aksel Keld.

He had seen the pikes. He had seen the Ashmore horse. He had seen the Sennet turn on the Haldorssons. He had understood, in the time it took the thorn-horn to sound twice, that the battle on the Skeldmere was lost, and that there was only one thing left on the ice that could win it back.

The boy under the white banner.

*Take the head,* every Keld captain was taught, *and the herd scatters.*

"*Lorn,*" said Muken. "*Hold the ring.*"

And he put his heels to the Stark bay and rode out of it, alone, with the wolfhound running at his stirrup, straight at the point of the wedge.

---

### VIII · The Iron Fist

They met on the open ice, on the eastern face, a hundred paces out from the Stark ring, in the white.

Aksel saw him coming. Muken saw him see it. The narrow black eyes above the white cloth widened a fraction and then narrowed again, and the Iron Fist of the Keld did something no southern rider would have done: he pulled out of the point of his own wedge, swerving his dun hard to the right, and let the thousand riders behind him go on past toward the Stark ring without him. They went past like a river round a stone. And then it was the two of them, on the open ice, in a pocket of the lifting that the passing wedge had churned clear, a ring of trampled snow perhaps forty paces across with the white standing round it on every side like a wall.

Aksel Keld turned his horse to face him.

Muken pulled up the bay.

They looked at each other across the snow.

The wolfhound had stopped at the bay's shoulder with its hackles up and its head low, and it did not make a sound.

Aksel pulled the white cloth down off his face with his left hand. His breath went up in a plume. His broad flat face was red with cold and wet with sweat at the temples under the wolverine hood, and there was blood on the side of his neck, not his own, and his black eyes were very calm.

"So," said Aksel Keld. "The boy who isn't afraid."

"So," said Muken. "The Iron Fist."

"Your Byakuya brought Thornwall."

"She did."

"And your Amagiri brought Ashmore."

"He did."

"I'd have liked to know that," said Aksel, "two days ago." He sounded faintly interested, the way a man sounds when he has lost a game of stones to a move he did not see. "The Tenrai told me Thornwall and Ashmore were bought. He told me they'd taken the silver."

"They did take it," said Muken. "Then somebody offered them something they wanted more."

Aksel looked at him a long moment.

"The Keld were bought too," he said. "You know that. No tithe on the ice. Seed grain. No Moto boot on Keld snow." He shifted his weight in the saddle. "What would you have offered, Moto, if you'd come to my fire first?"

"The same," said Muken. "And one thing more."

"What?"

"That I'd still be offering it after I'd won."

Something went across Aksel Keld's face, very fast, like a fox crossing snow. Then it was gone.

"Get down," said Aksel.

He swung off the dun himself, in one movement, and stood on the trampled snow with his short powerful legs planted and his iron fist hanging at his side, and drew the short axe from his belt with his left hand.

"I'll not fight a man on a horse that isn't his," he said. "Get down, and we'll settle it on the ice, the way the Keld settle things. If I win, the Starks go home and you go into the death-house, and the Tenrai can do what they like with the rest. If you win." He shrugged, one heavy shoulder. "The herd scatters."

Muken got down.

He gave the bay's reins to nothing, let them drop, and the bay stood. He stepped forward onto the trampled snow with the scrap-iron sword in his right hand, black and pitted and worn thin from a thousand sharpenings, and the wolfhound came with him a pace and then stopped, because he put his left hand down flat in front of its nose without looking at it, and it understood.

They circled.

Muken's eyes were on the iron fist. The Shingan was wide open now, wider than it had ever been, and the gold seam in his left iris had spread to a thread, and through it Aksel Keld was a man made of lines. Most of them were faint. The Iron Fist was not tired anywhere. His stance was low and wide and balanced, his weight going straight down through his short legs into the ice, and the lines through his body ran clean and even and strong. There was no crack in him that Muken could see.

Except the hand.

The iron hand was full of lines. Black iron and walrus tusk and sinew, the articulated fingers riveted at every joint, the gauntlet strapped to the stump and the forearm, the cord running up the sleeve. And through all of it, through every rivet and every strap and every finger-joint, fine gold lines running, branching, the way the lines ran through old glaze. It had been hit a thousand times. It had hit a thousand things. Every one of them had left something in it.

*There,* thought Muken. *If anywhere. There.*

Aksel came in.

He was fast. Faster than Muken had believed a man built like that could be. He came off his back foot low and hard with the axe in his left hand leading, a short chopping stroke at Muken's sword-arm, and Muken took it on the flat of the scrap-iron blade and felt it go all the way up his arm into his shoulder, and stepped back, and the iron fist came round behind the axe in a short flat hook at his ribs.

The left side. The cut side. The one Yukazuri had closed twice.

He saw it coming. He saw it in the gold before it moved, the line through Aksel's hips and shoulder lighting up an instant before the fist did. He saw it, and he moved, and he was not fast enough.

The iron fist hit him under the left arm, on the ribs, through Ulf Stark's mail and the dead sergeant's coat.

It was like being kicked by a horse. It was like the gallery roof coming down. The mail held and the rings did not part, and the force went through them anyway, through the wool and the skin and the muscle, into the bone, and he heard the ribs go. Two of them. The seventh and the eighth, at the side, where the old cut was, with a sound like green sticks breaking inside a sack. The breath went out of him all at once, and his left lung, on that side, under the broken ribs, did not want to take it back in. Something hot and wet opened under the mail, the old cut tearing, and he felt the blood start, warm, down his side under the wool.

He went down on one knee on the ice.

The scrap-iron sword was still in his hand. He did not know how. The wolfhound was roaring somewhere behind him. Aksel Keld was standing over him with the iron fist drawn back again, black against the white, and his narrow black eyes were quite calm, and Muken looked up at the fist and saw the gold in it, all the fine lines running through the iron and the tusk and the sinew, and one of them, at the base of the second knuckle, where the rivet went through, very slightly brighter than the rest.

He could not breathe.

The white closed in round the edges of his eyes.

And the world went away.

### IX · The Bound Flame

He was standing in a place made of threads.

He knew it was a place because he was standing in it, and he knew he was standing because his feet were on something, though when he looked down there was nothing there but more threads, running away from under his boots in every direction, crossing and crossing again, fine as hair, gold and silver and black, rose and sea-green and the violet of the northern sky, and colors he had no names for. They ran up into the dark above him and down into the dark below and out to every side, so many that the dark between them was only a kind of absence, and every one of them was humming. Very faintly. Each at its own note. The sound of it was like the sound of six hundred people breathing in the cold, or four thousand, or every person who had ever lived, all at once, very far away.

His ribs did not hurt.

He put his hand to his side, under the coat. There was no blood. There was no coat. He was wearing nothing he could name, and he was cold and not cold, and he could breathe again, all the way down, and the breath went out of him and did not go up in a plume.

*I'm dead,* he thought. *Aksel Keld's hit me again and I'm dead.*

"No," said a voice. "Not yet."

It came from in front of him. Far away, and near. It was the quietest thing he had ever heard. But every thread in that place stopped humming for the length of a breath when it spoke, and then started again, a little lower, as though they were listening.

He looked up.

There was a figure among the threads.

It was kneeling. That was the first thing he understood about it, and for some while it was the only thing. It knelt the way a man kneels under a load too heavy to carry standing, with one knee down and the other drawn up and its head bowed and its shoulders set, and the load was the threads. They ran into it and out of it. They came down on its bowed back and its shoulders from the whole of the dark above, more of them than Muken could count, more than there were stars, and they ran down through it and out of it into the dark below, and it held them. It held all of them. It knelt there in the middle of the place made of threads with the whole weight of them on its back, and did not move, and did not fall.

It was burning. A heavy warm light that came off it the way warmth comes off a banked fire, steady and low, gold and white, and as Muken looked at it he found that he wanted, more than he had ever wanted anything, to tell it the truth. Every truth. Every lie he had ever told, to a sergeant or a paymaster or a factor at the top of a shaft or his sister, rose up in his throat like water in a well, and he had to close his teeth on them.

It had wings.

Two great curved shapes of the same warm light rose from its shoulders and folded down around it to the ground, held close and low the way a man holds his arms in against a load, and every long feather of them was bent, very slightly, under something Muken could not see. And the figure itself, now that he looked, was bowed under that same weight from the crown of its head to the knee on the ground, and steady, and not moving at all.

*It's holding something up,* thought Muken. *Something enormous. And it has been holding it a long time.*

"Six," said the voice. "Into six. This is one."

The bowed head lifted.

It did not have a face. It had where a face would be, and in that place there was only the warm light, steady and low, and two places in it that were brighter, where eyes would be. They looked at him. He felt them look at him. He felt them go into him the way the Shingan went into other men, and find every line, and every crack, and every place where he was tired.

"You are the boy with the eye," it said. "The crack-reader. The red line's last."

"Who are you?"

"I am called Faisal," said the kneeling figure. "The Grand Angel of Servitude. The Bound Flame. He who kneels at the edge of creation." The light moved very slightly, as if something under it had smiled. "I kneel here, in the place where the threads run, under the weight the Bell can no longer hold, and I do not get up. It is a small kingdom. It is the one I chose."

"Why am I here?"

"Because you were falling," said Faisal. "And I do not let things fall. It is the only thing I was ever made to do." The bright places where eyes would be did not move from him. "And because you swore an oath, two nights ago, on your honor, by a woodpile, in front of six hundred people. I heard it. I hear every oath that is sworn on honor, even here, even under this. I heard it, and it was true. Every word of it was true." The light warmed. "Do you know how rarely I hear that? A king, swearing, and every word of it true?"

Muken did not answer.

"I will teach you one thing," said Faisal. "Only one. There is not time for more, and you could not carry more. Listen."

The threads hummed.

"You read cracks," said the kneeling figure. "You have always read them. You think it is a gift of your blood. Every bearer learns it first, because a crack is where the weight shows. Everything that carries a load carries it imperfectly, and the imperfection is where the load collects. In stone. In iron. In bone. In a host of riders on the ice. In a man." One great wing moved slightly under the weight. "You see where the weight has gathered, and you have learned to strike there. That is a mercenary's use of it. It is a good use. It has kept you alive."

"But," said Muken.

"But you have only ever looked," said Faisal. "Listen, now. Every made thing has a note. Every thread in this place is humming at its own. So is every stone and every blade and every bone in your body and his. You have heard it all your life, and never known you were hearing it. When you strike a thing, you are only knocking on its door. If you hold its note instead. If you sing it back to it, through the steel, the way a voice holding the note of a glass will break the glass, then the weight that has gathered in its crack will carry the crack forward for you, faster than any eye, and the thing will open along its own flaw. It will break itself, because you have shown it where it was already broken."

The light dimmed and steadied.

"Hear me," said Faisal. "Your father thought it was strength. Your father struck everything he met as hard as he could, and he broke a great deal, and in the end he broke himself, and your mother with him. This is listening. You find the note of a thing, and you hold it, and you wait, and the thing tells you where it will open." The bowed head inclined, slowly. "The word for it in your house's old tongue is *shake*. *Fure*. Your grandfathers' grandfathers knew it. It was taken from your line in the cellars at Halcrest with everything else. I am giving it back."

"Why?"

The question came out of him before he could stop it. In that warm light, under those eyes, he could not have stopped it.

"Why give it to me? You don't know me. I'm Gimbzo's son."

The kneeling figure did not answer at once. The threads hummed. Far away, very faint, Muken thought he heard something that might have been ice booming, or a dog roaring, or a girl's voice saying *please be careful.*

"I kneel," said Faisal at last, "because I must never fall. That is what I am. Kneeling, under the weight, so that the weight does not come down on what is beneath it." The light pulsed, once, very softly. "You knelt your whole life, child. Under the salt. Under the factor's ledger. Under your mother's dying and your sister's hunger. You carried it, and you did not fall, and you never once asked anybody to carry it for you." The bright places where its eyes would be held him. "Gimbzo never did that. That is yours."

The threads began to tremble.

"Go back," said Faisal. "He is about to strike again. Find the note. Hold it. And when you swear, swear by your honor, as the Stark taught you." The light began to dim, and the place made of threads began to dim with it, going gray at the edges, going white. "I will be listening."

"Will I see you again?"

"If you live," said the Bound Flame, from very far away, "and if you keep your word. I kneel a very long way from anywhere. But I do not forget."

And the threads went white.

---

He was on the ice.

He was on one knee in the trampled snow with the scrap-iron sword in his hand and blood running warm down his left side under Ulf Stark's mail, and his broken ribs grinding when he tried to breathe, and the white of the lifting standing round the little cleared ring like a wall. Aksel Keld was standing over him. The iron fist was drawn back, black against the white, and coming down.

Hardly any time had passed. The fist was still at the top of its arc. He saw it, and through the Shingan he saw the gold in it, every fine line through the iron and the tusk and the sinew, and the one at the base of the second knuckle, where the rivet went through, brighter than the rest.

And now, for the first time in his life, he could hear it.

A note. Very high, very thin. The note of that black iron hand, of all the thousand blows it had struck and taken, humming in it like a wire in the wind. It had always been there. He had always heard it. He had never once known that he was hearing.

Muken drew in breath. In through the nose, until the broken ribs screamed. Out through the mouth, long and slow, until there was nothing left. The Kharven breath. Yusif's breath, in the back of a worked-out gallery with a stick of ash in his hands.

The scrap-iron sword, in his hand, began to ring.

"*Fure,*" said Muken.

## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Three: The Tokenless

---

They lost five men at the head of the column before the horn stopped blowing, and three more on the Marrow crossing two days after that, when the ice under the lead wagon gave without a sound and took a team, two riders and the young driver everyone called Pell's Nephew down into the black water together. They never found any of them. On the ninth day the road came out of the timber at last onto a long bare shoulder of hill above a river, and at the bottom of it, behind a stockade of split logs gone grey with weather, stood the Vessant post.

Eleven wagons had left the Halcrest gate. Nine came in. Muken rode beside the lead team with his cheek stitched shut in a long black seam from the jaw to the corner of his eye, and his sister's stitches pulling every time he talked, and he did not talk much.

The factor at the post counted the wagons in against his manifest twice. Then he counted the men, which took less time. Then he paid, in stamped Vessant bars, full weight, on a trestle by the gate, and every bar was there to the last, and the death-price for the eight who had not come back was there too, in a separate stack, sealed in oiled cloth and labelled in a neat clerk's hand with the names Muken gave him. He did not haggle. He did not ask a single question about the road. He looked at Muken's face, at the seam in it, and then he looked away and wrote something down in his book that Muken could not read upside down.

That was the first thing that was wrong.

The second was the road itself. Muken had walked it again in his head every night since the granite shoulder, lying awake under the wagon with his face throbbing, and he could not make it come out right. Draycott had given him a route on the morning they left the gate, drawn on a slip of paper in the agent's own hand, and he had followed it, because that was what the paper was for. It had taken them through every narrow place in the timber. Every ford with no bridge. Every pass where the sightings on Yukazuri's map lay thickest. There had been a shorter road, two days shorter, along the river valley to the south, with a Vessant way-station on it and a garrison at the halfway mark. He had found it on the map on the fourth night and asked the drivers, and the drivers had looked at each other and said the house had told them it was closed.

It was not closed. They had passed the end of it on the sixth morning. There was fresh dung on it.

Somebody had wanted this convoy to take the worst road in the north. Somebody had paid twice the going rate for an escort to take it, and paid the death-price without blinking, and put Muken's name on the contract in particular, when there were three bigger companies in Halcrest that would have taken the work for half.

He did not know who. He did not know why. He knew what it felt like, though. It felt like the first time the Orrel steward had made him carry a full load up four galleries and stood at the top with his watch in his hand.

It felt like a test.

---

Rob Stark found him at the water trough by the stockade gate, with his coat off and his sleeves pushed up, washing the last of the road off his forearms.

The big northerner was limping. The bite above his knee had gone bad on the fifth day, the way bites did, hot and swollen and stinking under the bandage, and Yukazuri had opened it again on a wagon tailboard and cleaned it out with spirit while Rob bit down on a strap and did not make a sound. He walked now with a stick cut from a spruce sapling. Mark was riding in the last wagon with his arm splinted to the shoulder and a fever that had broken only that morning. Neither of them had complained. The Starks never did.

"Sarge," Rob said.

Muken looked up. Water was running off his elbows into the snow.

"There's a man wants to see you," Rob said. "Inside. In the factor's hall." He shifted his weight off the bad leg. "Just you."

Muken straightened slowly. He reached for his coat and shook it out, and as he did it he glanced, without turning his head, along the stockade to where the company's wagons were drawn up in a row, and his sister was sitting on the tailboard of the last one beside Mark Stark, with her kit open on her knees, changing the dressing on the boy's arm. She was not looking his way. She did not need to be. He saw her hands stop moving for a moment, then start again.

"What man," Muken said.

Rob met his eyes. His face did what Stark faces did, which was close up like a door. "He'll tell you that himself."

"He one of yours?"

"He's all of ours," said Rob Stark.

He turned and limped off toward the factor's hall without waiting to see whether Muken followed. After a moment, Muken put his coat on, settled his sword-belt, and did.

---

The factor's hall was the biggest building in the post: a long, low log house with a turf roof and a smoke-hole, and one end of it partitioned off with a wall of planed boards and a door with a real iron lock. Rob led him through the outer room, where a half-dozen Vessant clerks and teamsters were eating at a long table and fell quiet as they passed, and opened the door in the partition, and stood aside.

Muken went in. Behind him, he heard the door close. He heard the key turn in the lock from the outside.

It was a small room and very warm. There was a stone fireplace at the far end with a fire of whole split logs going in it, and the heat came off it in a wall, and the only light was the fire's. It smelled of woodsmoke and old leather and wet dog. A wolfhound the size of a pony lay asleep on the hearthstone with its legs twitching. Beside it, in a great high-backed chair that had certainly not been made for a factor's hall, a chair of dark oak and iron and old wolf-pelt that looked as if it had been hauled down from some hall in the north on the back of a cart for this one meeting, sat a man.

He was big. He was the kind of big that Rob Stark was going to be in twenty years, if Rob Stark lived that long: broad as a doorway, with a belly gone heavy under the mail and the arms gone heavier, and a great beard that had been rust-red once and was now salt-and-rust, braided at the chin with a ring of black iron. His hair was cut short, almost to the scalp, and the scalp was scarred white in three places. His left hand rested on the arm of the chair, and two fingers were missing from it, the small and the ring, cut away clean at the knuckle a long time ago. His face was broad and weathered and red with the fire, and his eyes in it were pale, northern blue, and very much awake.

He wore no crown. He did not need one. He sat in the chair the way a mountain sits on a plain.

Muken knew who it was before the man opened his mouth. He did not know how he knew. Everyone north of the salt basins knew the name, the way you knew the name of a winter that had killed your grandfather's cattle. He had just never imagined he would be in a room with it.

*Ulf Stark.* Lord of Hollenmark. Master of the eastern March. One of the Seven Warlords of the north, and by most counts the worst of them to have against you.

"Ah," said Ulf Stark. "So there he is."

His voice was deep and rough and amused, and the vowels in it were flattened out like hammered nails, the way the Starks talked. He looked Muken up and down, slowly, from his boots to the seam in his face, and back again.

"Muken," he said. "The Tokenless."

Muken bowed his head. It was not a good bow. He had never been taught one, and it came out the way the Lanternless bowed to a paymaster: short, stiff, a little sullen at the neck. But he held it, and he meant it, which was more than he could have said for most of the bows he had given in his life.

"You called for me, Lord Stark?"

Ulf laughed.

It was a big laugh, from the belly, and the wolfhound on the hearth woke at the sound of it and lifted its head and looked round and lay down again.

"*Lord Stark*," Ulf said. "Listen to him. I heard you weren't half so formal with the last man I sent you." He was grinning. "I heard you told my representative in Halcrest that the keeper of a tea-house was a liar, and your captain was a liar, and you'd go anywhere for enough money, and then you doubled his price and made him shake your hand on it because you hadn't a token for him to seal to." He wiped his eye with the back of his maimed hand. "Draycott's not been the same since. He writes me letters about you. Long ones."

Muken stood very still.

"Draycott," he said. "Draycott's Vessant."

"Draycott's Vessant," Ulf agreed. "And Vessant's oldest contract is a supply line into the eastern timber that runs, every mile of it, through Stark country. Thirty-one years that contract's been sound. You know why?" He did not wait. "Because every one of those thirty-one years, the Vessant house has paid Hollenmark a tithe for the road. And because every one of those years, when the house of Stark has wanted a thing done quiet in the south, the house of Vessant has done it. That's what a contract is, lad. It's not the paper. It's the thing both sides know that nobody wrote down."

The fire cracked. A log settled.

"The road," Muken said slowly. "You picked the road."

Ulf's grin widened.

"I picked the road."

"Three of my men are at the bottom of the Marrow. Five more are on the granite shoulder under a cairn. Rob Stark's going to walk with a stick till the spring." Muken did not raise his voice. He found, a little to his own surprise, that he did not need to. "You picked the worst road in the north for us on purpose."

"I did." Ulf did not look away. "And you brought nine wagons out of eleven down it, and every man but eight, and both my nephews, alive. Through the thickest country on that pretty Zettari map your sister bought off a scribe in Ironlink." He let that land. "Yes, I know about the map. I know about the room over the tea-house. I know you bought her number out of the Orrel drainage book with your first season's pay and counted it twice. I know a great many things about you, Muken. I've been knowing them for some time."

He leaned back in the great chair and looked at Muken over his steepled fingers, the whole and the maimed.

"Now," he said. "Rob."

Muken heard the key in the lock behind him turn again, and the door open a hand's width.

"Go and stand outside, lad," Ulf said, past him. "Outside the hall, not outside the door. If anybody comes within ten paces of this wall, I want to hear about it before they do."

"Aye," said Rob, behind him.

The door closed. The key turned. Muken heard the stick and the uneven step go away through the outer room, and the outer door bang, and then nothing but the fire.

---

"Sit," said Ulf Stark.

There was a stool by the hearth. Muken looked at it, and then at the man in the chair, and then, after a moment, he sat. The wolfhound lifted its head and looked at him with yellow eyes and sniffed once, delicately, at his knee, and put its head down again on its paws.

"She likes you," Ulf said. "She doesn't like anyone. Bit a Tenrai tax-collector's hand off at the wrist last winter. Well. Most of it."

"You didn't bring me in here to talk about your dog."

"No." Ulf's grin went away. Without it his face looked older, and very tired, and harder than any face Muken had ever seen. "No, I didn't."

He was quiet for a moment, looking into the fire.

"You've proven yourself," he said. "I'll give you that plainly, because you've earned it plainly, and because I'm too old to dance. Any company in Halcrest could have taken that convoy. Most of them would have lost it. A few would have brought it through and lost two men in three doing it, and called it a good season. You lost eight, and you kept your column together the whole way down, and you killed two of the big ones yourself with a scrap-iron sword and a cut-down reinforcement a first-year apprentice would be ashamed of." He looked at Muken sideways. "Oh, yes. Rob told me. He told me about the knee. He's never in his life told me about anyone."

Muken said nothing.

"So I'll tell you why you're here," Ulf said. "I want to recruit you."

"Recruit me."

"Into my service. Into the north's service." He turned the iron ring on his beard-braid with his thumb. "There's a threat coming down on us, Muken. A bigger one than anything you've seen on that road. Bigger than anything the north saw in forty years of the war, and the war's only a month done."

Muken looked at him for a long time.

"Lord Stark," he said. "I'm a nobody."

He said it flatly, without any bitterness in it, the way he would have said the sky was grey. It was simply the truth, and he had been carrying it his whole life, and it was as familiar to him as the weight of his own sword.

"I'm a commoner. Less than. I'm tokenless. In the chain-cities a tokenless man can't sign for a sack of meal. I was born in a cutting gallery under Halcrest and I've been selling my arm to whoever pays since I was fourteen." He spread his hands, the cracked white knuckles, the scar across the back of the right one. "I've got a sister, a sword three smiths wouldn't price, and a reputation for not dying. What in the name of the Sky can a man like me do for one of the Seven?"

Ulf shook his head slowly.

"No common man," he said, "brings a column down that road. Let alone a boy of sixteen with his little sister in the carriage. No common man looks at the map on the fourth night and sees the river road and asks the drivers why it's closed. No common man walks into Draycott's offer and comes out of it with double the money and the death-price on top and his sister's place fixed in the terms." He leaned forward in the great chair, and the oak creaked under him. "You don't know what you are, lad. You've never had anyone to tell you. You've got the making of a general in you. A commander of men. I've seen two in my life with what you've got, and one of them's dead and the other's sitting in this chair."

The fire burned. The wolfhound sighed in its sleep.

"I want you in my army," Ulf said. "I want you to join the fight. To protect the north. The people in it. The ones who can't fill their own bowl." His voice had gone low and rough. "And to make sure that whatever the south thinks it's going to take control of up here, it doesn't."

Muken sat on the stool and did not answer.

"You'll be wondering," Ulf went on, "why all this. The locked door. Rob in the yard. The dog." He made a short sound that was nearly a laugh and was not. "It's because if anyone outside this room ever learned what I've just said to you, lad, the Moto lines would have you dead within the month. You and your sister both. They'd not even trouble to make it look like an accident." He paused. "This very thing I'm talking to you about has already cost me my brother."

Muken's head came up.

"Torvald," Ulf said. "You'll have heard."

He had. Everyone had. It had come down the road ahead of the convoy, the way news did in the north, faster than horses: in a tavern at the Marrow crossing, from a teamster who had it from a courier who had it from a man who had been in the Moto seat on the night. The Stark chief, taken at Kessen ford. Brought before the council of six in the Hall of Verdicts. Beheaded on the floor of it, by the Kurenai's own hand, with his blood running round the old script in a ring.

"They say he laughed at them," Muken said slowly. "Right at the end. They say he said something that turned them all white, and then he laughed."

"He'd have laughed," Ulf said. "He always did, when it was going badly." He looked into the fire. "They gave the Greymane back to the Ridgewall for his weight in salt. They put the Hallenfeld tariff-man in the cells for his ledger. They hanged my brother's riders on the post in the yard at dawn, both of them, my own cousins' boys, and then they sent me my brother's head in a cask of brine with a note in Kagenori Tenrai's own hand." He did not move. "It said: *The north is reminded.*"

The log in the fire broke in two with a soft crack and fell in on itself in a burst of sparks.

Muken sat down further on the stool. He had not meant to. His legs had simply stopped holding him up quite so straight.

He had known it was bad. Everybody in the north knew it was bad. But there was knowing a thing the way you knew there was weather in the next valley, and there was sitting in a hot room with a man whose brother's head had come to him in a cask, and they were not the same.

---

"Here's what's coming," Ulf said.

He had pulled himself back from wherever he had gone, and his voice was steady again, and flat, the voice of a man reading out the strength of an enemy.

"They're going to starve us," he said. "The Moto. That's the plan, and it's a good plan, I'll give them that. They don't need to march an army up here. They don't need to win a single battle. All they need is to stop the north getting the things it needs to live through a winter. Salt. Iron. Lamp oil. Medicine. Seed grain for the spring. Every one of those comes up out of the south on the roads, and the Moto have been closing the roads one at a time since the war went bad, four years back, and faster since Shimane. Two winters more of it and there'll be ten thousand dead in the north from hunger and the cold, and nobody'll have to swing a sword to do it." He let a breath out through his nose. "Only the Seven can stop it. The seven great families of the north. That's what we're for. That's what we've always been for."

"Seven," Muken said.

"Seven." Ulf looked at him oddly for a moment, and something moved behind the pale northern eyes and was gone. "We've always said seven. We've been saying it for a very long time." He turned the iron ring on his beard again. "But we haven't won anything yet. Not one great battle. Torvald burned their granaries at Kessen and they took his head for it. We've raided. We've hidden. We've held." His jaw set. "Haven't *won* yet. That's the word, lad. *Yet.*"

He leaned forward, and dropped his voice, though there was no one in the room but the two of them and the dog.

"Two months from now," he said, "Kagenori Tenrai Moto means to lay martial law on the Kessen road."

Muken frowned. He knew the name of the road. Everybody did. It ran south and east from the Kessen ford, through the low country, all the way down to Sum-gol and beyond, past the foot of the Yukari valleys.

"That's the last road," he said.

"That's the last road." Ulf nodded. "Everything the north still gets from the east comes up it. The Mahuo's salt. The Yukari's medicine. The Sum-gol iron. Close it, and you've closed the north's throat. Put Tenrai troops on every bridge and every ford on it, and a Tenrai seal on every cart, and in a winter you'll have the north on its knees and the Yukari cut off from every friend they've got in the world." His mouth thinned. "Kagenori's not stupid. He's choking two birds with one hand."

"And you mean to stop him."

"I mean to stop him." Ulf's maimed hand closed on the arm of the chair. "Me, and Wystan Greymane."

"Greymane." Muken's mind went to the ridge, the long wall in the west, the iron-haired men with the wolf on their breasts. "The Lord of the Ridgewall?"

"The same. His brother Aldwin came home to him in a salt-cart with a ransom tag on his collar. Wystan's not a man who forgets that sort of thing."

Muken was quiet. He was thinking. He found that he could not stop himself: the way he could not stop himself looking at a road and seeing where it narrowed, the way he could not stop himself reading a big creature's gait for the leg it favoured. The north was a map in his head now, the way Yukazuri's had been, laid out in brown ink and red.

"Two houses," he said. "Out of seven." He looked up. "What about the rest of them? Ashmore. Thornwall. The Keld. Hallenfeld."

Ulf cackled.

It was an ugly sound, high and cracked, and it made the dog lift its head.

"Ashmore," he said, "has the grain. All of it. Every sack of it between the Greenhollow and the treeline, and they've worked out that a man who owns the grain in a famine doesn't need to fight anybody. They'll sell to the Moto. They'll sell to us. They'll sell to whoever's standing at the gate with silver when the snow comes, and they'll call it neutrality." He spat into the fire. "Thornwall's no better. Thornwall's got the iron corridor and the Ore Council on their doorstep, and they're so busy reading the Ore Council's moods that they've forgotten there's a north behind them. They'll not come to terms with us. They're too frightened of what they'll lose."

"And the Keld?"

"The Keld can be brought round." Ulf's face changed, a little. "Maybe. They're out past the treeline on the tundra, and they don't care much what happens on the roads, because they've never needed roads. But they're hungry this winter, same as everybody, and they've got a long memory and no love for anyone who's tried to tax a reindeer." He paused. "It'd take somebody going out to them in person. Sitting with them. Eating their food. Sleeping in their shelters. They don't swear to a letter. They swear to a man."

"Hallenfeld?"

"Hallenfeld are clerks and couriers. They'll carry anybody's letters." Ulf shrugged. "Their tariff-master's in a Moto cell for keeping honest books. That might put a little iron in them. It might not." He sat back. "So. Stark. Greymane. And maybe the Keld, if somebody's fool enough to walk out onto the tundra in the dead of winter and ask them nicely. That's what the north has to put against six Moto lines."

He spread his hands.

"That's all we've got."

---

Muken sat on the stool and looked at the fire for a long time.

There was a question in him. It had been in him since Rob found him at the trough, and it had grown, the whole time he had been in this room, until there was no room left in him for anything else.

"Why me?" he said.

Ulf did not answer.

"I mean it," Muken said. "Why me? You're one of the Seven. You've got your own riders, your own household, your own blood. You could've hired any company in Halcrest for this. The Grey Lamps. Fennick's. Any run-of-the-mill sellsword who knows which end of a spear goes in the other man. Why go to all this trouble?" He gestured at the room, the lock, the dog, the chair hauled down out of the north. "Why pick the worst road in the world and send me down it, just to see if I'd come out the other end?"

Ulf Stark looked at him for a long moment. Then he chuckled. It was a quieter sound than the others, and warmer, and a little sad.

"Very perceptive," he said. "Aren't you."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Don't call me perceptive and then not answer."

Ulf's mouth twitched in the braided beard.

"I'm afraid," he said, "I can't tell you that. Not all of it. Not yet." He held up his maimed hand before Muken could speak. "It's not that I don't want to. It's to protect you. There are things that, once a man knows them, he can't stop knowing. And there are people who can read a man for what he knows, lad, better than you'd ever believe. People who'd only have to look at you once, the way you're looking at me now, to see it on you. If you don't carry it, they can't find it."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Ulf agreed. "It's not. But it's the one I've got."

He leaned forward in the great chair. The firelight moved across his face, and the old white scars on his scalp, and the black iron in his beard.

"I'll give you this much," he said, "because you've earned something, and because I think you'd go mad without it. You know what the Moto are doing is wrong. You don't need me to tell you that. You've known it since you were a boy." He paused. "You were raised by a man I knew very well."

Muken went still.

"Yusif Stark," Ulf said.

The name went into the room and stayed there.

"One of the greatest warlords this family ever put in the field," Ulf said. "My uncle. My father's younger brother. He rode with Gimbzo in the war, in the long middle of it, when the north still thought the Moto might be the ones to set things right. He was the best of them. The best of us." He looked down at his hands. "And then one day he looked at where Gimbzo's road was going, and he saw the end of it, and he stepped off. Just like that. Turned his horse round and rode away from the whole of it. From his rank and his name and his place in the Seven. Everyone in this family thought he'd gone mad." He was quiet a moment. "He hadn't. He'd gone to raise you. You and your sister both."

Muken could not speak.

He remembered. He remembered a big quiet man with a grey beard, who came down into the Cuttings once a season with salt-meat and wool and never stayed the night. Who had taught a boy of eight to hold a stick like a sword in the back of a worked-out gallery, and a girl of five to hold a needle. Who had never told them his name except the short one, *Yusif*, and had never once in all those years explained why a man like him would climb down four galleries under Halcrest to visit a cutting woman and her two uncounted children. Who had told Muken, over and over, until he could have said it in his sleep: *You protect the weak. You defend the man next door. You keep the north quiet so the children can sleep in it. Everything else is just weather.*

Who had stopped coming, the winter Muken was thirteen. Who had never come again.

"Why," Muken said. His voice came out hoarse. "Why would a man like that..."

He stopped. He did not know how to finish it.

"That," said Ulf Stark gently, "is the part I can't tell you." He held Muken's eyes. "But I'll tell you this. Yusif Stark did not throw away everything he was for a nobody. He knew exactly what he was doing and who he was doing it for. That makes you special, lad. In ways you don't even begin to understand."

---

Muken sat on the stool by the fire with his elbows on his knees and his head down, and looked at the floor between his boots for a long time.

He thought about the road. About Big Bertha going down onto her broken knee in the snow. About Mark Stark sobbing with a short sword in his hand, holding the cub still. About the ice going out from under the lead wagon without a sound.

He thought about the steward at Orrel asking a child of the Cuttings *what's your oldest?* and laughing, every time, because the answer was always nothing.

He thought about ten thousand dead in the north from the cold, and nobody having to swing a sword to do it.

And under all of it, the way it was always under everything, he thought about a big quiet man in the back of a worked-out gallery, holding his small hands round the end of a stick. *You protect the weak.*

"If it's really as bad as you say," he said at last.

He did not look up.

"Then you've not left me much choice, have you." He let out a long breath through his nose. "My sister would never let me live it down if I walked away. Never. I'd hear about it every day till I died." Something that was almost a smile touched the stitched side of his face, and pulled at the stitches, and went away. "And I don't think I could. In good conscience. Not after the road." He lifted his head and met the pale northern eyes. "Not after what you've told me about him."

Ulf Stark looked at him for a long moment, and did not smile.

Then he reached inside his coat and took something out, and held it out across the hearth, on the flat of his maimed palm.

It was a seal-token. A small bronze disc on a short chain, the size of a large coin, stamped on one face with the wolf of Hollenmark and on the other with the Nalūn chain-mark. Muken had seen ten thousand of them in his life, on ten thousand merchants' belts. He had never once been handed one.

"A contract," said Ulf Stark. "Between the house of Stark and you. Sealed under the Chain Light in Ironlink, a month ago, by my factor there. Your service to the north, and the north's protection to you and yours, for as long as you live." He held it out further. "Take it. You've an oldest now, Muken. Show it to the next man who asks."

Muken took it.

He held it in his cracked white palm, and looked at it, and waited.

Every token he had ever seen hummed. That was the whole of what they were. A sound contract hummed in the bronze, a faint steady note you could hear in a quiet room and feel a little behind the eyes, for as long as the contract was sound. Draycott's had hummed. The tea-house keeper's had hummed. Every merchant on every belt in Halcrest had walked about humming like a hive.

The token in Muken's palm lay perfectly still, and cold, and silent.

He looked up.

Ulf Stark was watching the token. He was not watching Muken's face at all. He was watching the little bronze disc, in the firelight, in the cracked palm, with an expression on his own broad weathered face that Muken could not read, and had never seen on anyone. It was the face of a man who has been told something he did not want to believe, and has just watched it turn out to be true.

"It's dead," Muken said. He turned it over. "Your factor's cheated you. It's dead."

"No," said Ulf Stark quietly. "It's not."

He sat back slowly in the great chair, and let his maimed hand fall onto the arm of it, and looked at Muken over the sleeping dog, and the fire, and the dead bronze, for a long time.

"Keep it anyway," he said at last. "Keep it on your belt where people can see it. And Muken." His voice had gone very low. "Don't ever let anybody see you hold it in your hand."

## Author Notes

- **Retcon 2026-10-20 (new-lore questionnaire).** Yusif rode with Gimbzo in the long middle of the forty-year war rather than its first years, and Ulf now says he went to raise both children; Ulf's "since the war" lines are re-timed to a war a month over.

## Book One of the Kōkan · Chapter Twelve: The Woodpile

---

### I · The Yard After Dark

They lit the yard with everything the Foot had left to burn.

Torches of split pine soaked in seal fat, jammed into the cracks of the curtain wall every ten paces. Two iron braziers dragged out of the Tenrai guardroom and filled with the last of the stable timber, so that the charred ends of the old stalls stood up out of the flames like the ribs of something that had died there. The blubber lamps from the barracks, every one, set in a long row along the top of the woodpile, spitting and stinking of fish-oil and hot tallow, their light coming up yellow off the pale ends of the split wood. And over all of it, very high, very faint, the green of the northern sky moving behind the cloud like something turning over in its sleep.

It was cold enough that the snow squeaked underfoot. The breath of six hundred people stood over the yard in a single low white cloud, and the torchlight went into it and stayed there, so that the whole of Kharven's Foot seemed to be lit from inside a lamp of frost.

They had all come. Nobody had been ordered.

Lorn had sent the word round the barracks at dusk, plainly, in four words, *the king will speak,* and then he had stood at the door of the keep and watched them come out of every door in the fort. The Stark riders first, in their hide-coats and mail, with their axes across their backs out of habit, because a Stark did not go to hear a thing said without something to hold. The Greymane foot behind them, grey-cloaked and quiet, in their long patient file. The vanguard boys, who had sewn the white banner, in a knot at the front with their elbows in each other's ribs. Then the others. The farriers and the cooks and the carters, the two Greymane masons with stone-dust still in their beards, the wounded from the surgeons' line on their crutches and their stretchers, carried out and set down along the barracks wall in a row. The women from the long houses down the Kessen road who had walked up after the Foot fell to cook and carry and nurse. Their children, wrapped to the eyes. And the old woman with no teeth, on her stump by the barracks door, in her three shawls, who had not moved from that stump in four days and did not move from it now, because the yard had come to her.

They stood in the snow in the torchlight and the breath-cloud, six hundred of them, and waited, and the only sound was the braziers and the spitting of the lamps and, somewhere at the back, a child asking a question and being hushed.

Muken stood at the foot of the woodpile.

He had chosen it. Godfrey had wanted the gatehouse steps, where he could be seen from the whole yard, and Lorn had wanted the wall-walk over the gate, under the banner. Muken had said no to both, quietly, and walked out of the keep in the dusk and stood where he stood now, with his back to the long pale wall of split wood that ran down the barracks side as high as a man's shoulder. The same place they had knelt, the morning after the crow. *If they're going to put me on a post,* he had said, *I'd sooner they didn't have to walk far to find the wood.*

Nobody had laughed.

He stood there now in the dead sergeant's coat with Ulf Stark's mail under it and the scrap-iron sword at his hip and the dead bronze token on his belt, bareheaded, with his white hair tied back and the torchlight on it. Ulf Stark's wolfhound sat at his left knee. Lorn Stark stood at his right hand, a pace back, in his father's mail with his father's axe; Godfrey Greymane at his left, in grey, with his long hands folded in front of him. Neither of them moved.

Kiyome Byakuya, Hayato Amagiri and Tetsugen Akagane stood in the crowd.

Muken had asked that of them too. *Not with me,* he had said, in the long room, as the light went. *Down there. Among them. Where they can see you.* Kiyome had looked at him a long moment and then nodded, once, and gone out into the yard ahead of everyone and stood in the snow at the front, a little to one side, in her near-white robes with the white cord of her house's knot still hanging loose from her sword-hilt, untied. Tetsugen had grunted and gone and stood beside a Stark farrier who was nearly as broad as he was. Hayato had found a place by the braziers with his wet indigo cape steaming in the heat and the juniper still caught in its toggle. Everyone in the yard knew who they were. Everyone in the yard had seen them ride in that morning. The crowd had left a little space round each of them, the way water leaves a space round a stone, and in each of those spaces the snow was trampled flat and nobody stood.

Down at the front, between the vanguard boys and the row of stretchers, stood Yukazuri.

She had come out of the surgeons' line with her sleeves still rolled to the elbow and the salve tin still in her apron pocket. Muken had found her there an hour ago, after the long room, bent over a Greymane boy with a split scalp, and had stood in the doorway until she finished the stitch and looked up. And then he had told her. All of it. In the dark at the back of the surgeons' tent, in the smell of boiled gut and spirit and blood, with the lamp turned low and her hands still red to the wrist, kneeling in front of her so their faces were level, the way Yusif used to kneel.

She had listened to every word without moving.

When he finished she had been quiet for a long moment. Then she had taken her red hand out of her lap and drawn the mark on the canvas of the tent wall beside her, in the blood on her fingers, the long down-stroke and the hook and the crossing and the small closed loop like a knot in a rope. Perfectly, the way she always drew it. And she had looked at it, and then at him.

*So that's whose it was,* she had said.

That was all. She had not cried. She had wiped her hand on her apron and gone back to the Greymane boy and finished closing his scalp, and Muken had stood in the doorway and watched her hands, which did not shake at all, and thought that of the two of them it had never once been him who was the strong one.

She stood at the front of the yard now with her sleeves rolled up in the cold and her breath going up white, and looked at her brother, and waited.

### II · The Name

"I have to tell you something," said Muken.

He did not raise his voice. He did not need to. The yard was so quiet that the words went out over it like a stone over still water, and the breath-cloud moved when he spoke, a little, and settled again.

"Something I learned this morning," he said. "From these three." He did not point. He did not need to. Six hundred heads turned, very slightly, toward the three trampled places in the snow, and turned back. "I'll tell you all of it. And when I've told you, I want you to know that if it changes who I am to you, you don't owe me anything. Not one step. You knelt here because you chose to. If you want to choose different, you can do it here, tonight, by the same wood, and no man of mine will lift a hand to stop you."

He paused. The braziers cracked.

"I am Muken," he said. "That's the name my mother gave me, and the only one I've answered to." He drew a breath, and the breath went up white. "And I am Muken Moto. First of my name."

The sound that went through the yard was very small: six hundred people taking a breath at the same time and not letting it go.

"Rightful King of the Clans," said Muken. He did not stop now. He said it the way he had walked the last ledge in the galleries as a boy, every foot set down and no looking back. "And of the Banner Houses. True Heir of the North." His jaw worked once. "Son of Kasane Moto, who cut salt under Halcrest for sixteen years to keep me fed. And son of Gimbzo."

The yard did not breathe out.

At the front, one of the vanguard boys, the youngest, the one with the red ears who had held the needle while the others argued over what to put on the banner, said "*No,*" very quietly, the way a child says it when a thing has been dropped and broken. Nobody hushed him.

Further back, a Stark rider with a grey braided beard turned his head aside and spat into the snow. It hissed.

By the barracks door, on her stump, the old woman with no teeth had not moved. But her hands, folded in her lap in her three shawls, had closed on each other.

Muken let it all happen. He did not look away from any of it. He stood at the foot of the woodpile with his hands at his sides and let six hundred people look at him, at his white hair and his grey eyes and the thin gold seam in the left one that the torchlight caught, and he let them see, at last, what they were looking at. The red line. The Kōkan. The last of the blood that had burned Hiroe and emptied the cellars at Halcrest and taken their fathers south on the levies and sent them home in carts.

"Yes," he said quietly. "That one."

Then he lifted his voice, for the first time.

"But I don't want you to follow me because of my name," said Muken. "Hear me. Not because I'm a Moto. Not because I'm his. Not because three lords of the seven lines rode four hundred miles to tell me what blood I've got in me." His eyes went over the yard, slowly, from the stretchers to the vanguard boys to the grey-bearded Stark who had spat. "That blood never did one thing for any of you except take. If that's all I am, then put me on a post, and burn me with this wood, and I'll not blame you."

He took one step forward, off the foot of the woodpile, into the trampled snow.

"I want you to follow me because I'll fight for you," he said. "That's all. That's the only reason worth anything. I'll fight for you. Every one of you. I'll die for you, if it comes to that, and I think it may." His voice did not shake. "And I'll put everything I have on the line. Everything. This name too, if that's what it costs. I'll put it on the line first."

The breath-cloud moved over the yard and settled.

Nobody said anything. Nobody moved.

And then Muken turned his head, and looked at the three trampled places in the snow, and the three people standing in them, and the yard turned its head with him.

He had to do this. He had known it since the long room. He had known it, perhaps, since Kiyome knelt on the cold stone with her sword across her knee and said *it doesn't bind you to anything at all.* The north would not take his word for it. The north had taken Moto words for forty years and been paid in carts. They had to see it. Here, in their own yard, by their own wood, with their own eyes.

### III · The Three Oaths

"Kiyome Byakuya," said Muken.

She came forward out of her trampled space into the light of the nearest brazier. She did not hurry. The crowd drew back from her a little further, without meaning to, and then held.

"Do you, Kiyome Byakuya," said Muken, "of the White Line, commit yourself to the sanctity of the people? And give up your land, and your power, to me? The true King of the Clans. And Leader of the Banner Houses."

Kiyome looked at him for a long moment, in the torchlight, with the frost standing on the shoulders of her robes.

Then she bowed. The white line bowed to a lord of the seven by the finger's width, small and exact; she bent from the waist until her loose white hair fell forward past her face, and held it there, in front of six hundred people in the snow.

"I do," said Kiyome Byakuya.

Her voice carried. It was the voice that had kept the peace in rooms worse than this, and it went over the yard clear as a struck bowl.

"Everything I control is now yours," she said, still bowed. "The white house in the Shiraki hills, and its land, and its forty riders, and the granary under its floor that the Tenrai have never found. My sword. My name." She straightened, slowly, and looked at him. "And my oath, which was yours before you were born. I've only now been able to give it to the one it belonged to."

She stepped back into the light by the brazier and stood there, with the white cord hanging loose from her hilt.

"Hayato Amagiri," said Muken.

Hayato came forward from beside the other brazier, steaming, his narrow face drawn tight in the heat.

"Do you, Hayato Amagiri," said Muken, "of the Indigo Mist Line, commit yourself to the sanctity of the people? And give up your land, and your power, to me? The true King of the Clans. And Leader of the Banner Houses."

Hayato's restless fingers had gone still in the folds of his cape. He looked at Muken, and then, for a moment, round the yard. At the farriers and the masons and the women from the long houses. At the row of stretchers along the barracks wall.

"I do," said Hayato.

He said it plainly, without any ceremony at all, the way he said distances.

"I'll tell you all what that's worth, since it's not much," he said to the yard, in the same plain voice. "I don't hold the Amagiri land. My uncle does, and he sits at Kagenori Tenrai's left hand, and he'll curse my name in the Hall of Verdicts before the week's out." A few heads turned toward him. "What I've got is twenty riders, and every road north of the Seat that the Tenrai don't know is a road. Every pass, every ford, every herders' hut with a woodpile in it between here and the southern border." He looked back at Muken. "They're yours. The first grain that comes up them goes to your long houses. Not his." He jerked his chin south, at nothing, at the whole of the dark. "Yours."

"Tetsugen Akagane," said Muken.

The great bald man did not come forward. He stood where he was, beside the Stark farrier, broad as a door, with the copper of his coat gone black in the cold and his scarred hands on his belt, and let the yard come to him the way the boy had let the room come to him that morning.

"Do you, Tetsugen Akagane," said Muken, "Leader of the Copper Line, of the Blood and Heart Arts, commit yourself to the sanctity of the people? And give up your land, and your power, to me? The true King of the Clans."

Tetsugen looked at him from under his thick grey brows for a long time. Long enough that somebody in the crowd shifted their feet on the squeaking snow.

"I do," said Tetsugen Akagane.

Then, because he was who he was, he did not leave it there.

He turned, slowly, on the spot, and looked at the yard. At the Starks in their hide-coats and the Greymane foot in their grey. At the grey-bearded rider who had spat. He looked at them the way a man looks at a field he once rode over at the head of a column.

"Some of you will know my name," he said, in his flat iron voice. "If your fathers went south in the Sennen campaign, they ate from my granaries on the way. I signed for every sack. I knew where the columns were going and what they'd do when they got there, and I signed." He let that sit in the breath-cloud. "I'll not ask you to forgive it. I've given the boy the books. Every name. Mine's at the top." He turned back to Muken. "My valley's yours. The Kurogane granaries. My levies, every one, and they'll answer nobody's muster but yours. And my own neck, if you find a use for it."

He did not bow. The Akagane had never sworn to the red, and he had said so that morning. But he took his right hand off his belt and laid it flat on his chest, over his heart, the Stark way, in front of three hundred Starks. And held it there.

### IV · What Is Ours

Muken turned back to the yard.

"Now you've seen it," he said.

His voice had come up. It carried to the back wall and the stretchers and the old woman on her stump, and the breath-cloud moved with it.

"You've seen it with your own eyes," said Muken. "These are Moto. All three. The white line, and the copper, and the indigo. Lords of the seven. The same houses that sent the levies and the tax-men and the grain-carts going south." He swept one hand toward the three of them standing in the firelight. "And they've just given it up. All of it. Land and riders and granaries and roads. In front of you. By your woodpile." He let the hand fall. "They didn't have to. They could have stayed under the Tenrai, where everything was safe and warm and fed. Kagenori would have kept them in their chairs for the rest of their lives if they'd only kept their mouths shut. They came here instead."

He looked out over them.

"So they're not our enemies," he said. "Hear me. These three aren't. They've put themselves on the side of the people, the same as you did when you knelt here. They've given up their livelihoods for it. And they've done it knowing the Hall will call them traitors for it by the end of the month." His jaw worked. "That's the line, from tonight, and it always was: the ones who take and the ones who are taken from. And I know which side of it I was born on." He touched the dead token at his belt. "Three galleries down under Halcrest. Whatever my blood is."

He took a breath.

"And there's another thing you'll carry home," he said. "Every one of you. To every long house that ever buried a son on a southern field." He looked straight out at the grey-bearded rider who had spat. "Under my rule, no Moto will ever march on the Zettari again. Or anyone else's land. Not a levy. Not a column. Not one of your boys sent south to die in somebody else's war. We'll hold the north, and we'll hold it well, and we'll reach for nothing past it." His voice hardened. "I swore it this morning. By my honour. You can hold me to it."

The grey-bearded rider looked back at him. He did not spit again.

"But we have to fight for what's ours," said Muken.

And now his voice rose all the way, for the first time in his life, and the gallery had gone out of it. It came up out of somewhere deeper, out of the salt and the stair and the long room, and the torches seemed to lean toward it.

"Nobody will give it to us," he said. "The Tenrai won't. The Hall won't. They've had forty years to give it and they've given us carts. So we'll take it." He turned his head, very slightly, north and east, toward the dark beyond the wall where the Keld fires were. "The Keld march in two days. Four thousand riders, bought with Tenrai seed grain, and an Iron Fist at the front of them who's never once lost. We'll meet them on the ice. And we'll break them." He turned back. "And then the Banner Houses who still stand in our way. Ashmore. Thornwall. Whoever else has taken Tenrai silver to keep their own people on their knees. We'll bring them in. Under the white banner, or under the snow." He drew breath. "And when the north is ours again, all of it, every fire and every stack, then we'll see who sits in the Hall of Verdicts and calls himself our master."

He stopped.

He stood at the foot of the woodpile in the torchlight with the breath going up off him in a white plume, and the scrap-iron sword at his hip, and the wolfhound risen to its feet beside him, and waited.

### V · Hail

For a long moment, nothing.

The braziers cracked. The lamps spat along the top of the woodpile. Six hundred people stood in the snow and looked at the boy who had just told them he was Gimbzo's son, and nobody moved, and the silence went on so long that Godfrey Greymane, at Muken's left hand, felt his own heart counting it.

Then Yukazuri stepped forward.

She was the smallest person in the front rank. She walked out of it, between the vanguard boys and the stretchers, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow in the cold and her hands still faintly red from the surgeons' line, across the trampled snow to her brother. She did not say anything. She turned round, so that she was facing the yard, and stood beside him, at his right knee, opposite the wolfhound. And she held up her right hand, palm out, so that six hundred people could see it in the torchlight.

There was a mark drawn on it. In blood that had gone dark. The long down-stroke and the hook and the crossing and the small closed loop at the foot.

She held it there.

At the front, the vanguard boy with the red ears made a sound in his throat. Then he went down on one knee in the snow.

The boy beside him went down after him. And the one beside him. And then the whole knot of them, the twenty-odd who had sewn the banner, all at once, the way they had done it the first time, with their elbows in each other's ribs.

Rob Stark went down. Then Mark beside him. Then the grey-bearded rider who had spat, slowly, with a grunt, one knee and then the other, in his frozen hide-coat that creaked at the elbow like a door.

And then it went through the yard the way fire goes through dry grass, or the way it did in the old days when there was still grass: from the front to the back, from the Starks to the Greymane foot to the farriers and the masons and the women from the long houses with their children on their hips. The men on the stretchers along the barracks wall could not kneel, and so they lifted their fists instead, and laid them on their chests.

Not all of them.

Muken saw it, because he was looking for it. Twenty men, perhaps thirty, scattered through the crowd, who stood. A Greymane spearman near the gate who stood with his arms folded and his face like a shut door. Three Stark riders together at the back who looked at each other and did not move. A carter who turned round, as the yard went down around him, and walked away through the kneeling bodies toward the postern gate without looking back, and pulled it open, and went out into the dark.

Lorn Stark's hand moved toward his axe.

"Let him go," said Muken quietly.

Lorn looked at him.

"Let him go," said Muken again. "I told them they could. Every one of them who walks out of that gate tonight saves Karogane the shouting." He did not look at the postern. "And every one who stays has heard it from me."

Lorn's hand came off the axe.

And then, from the side of the yard, from the old woman on her stump by the barracks door, came a sound.

She had not knelt. She could not. She had sat on that stump in her three shawls for four days with her toothless mouth set in a long hard line, watching the boy with the white hair, and she had not said a single word to anyone. Now she lifted her head and opened her mouth, and what came out of it was cracked and thin and old as the stones of the Seat, and it carried over the whole yard.

"*Hail,*" said the old woman.

Six hundred heads turned.

"*Hail to the king,*" she said. She was shaking. She did not stop. "*Hail. Hail to the true king of the north.*"

The vanguard boy with the red ears said it after her. Then the boys round him. Then Rob, and Mark, and the farrier beside Tetsugen Akagane, and the women from the long houses, and the Greymane foot in their long grey file, all of them, from their knees, in the snow, in the torchlight, in the cold that took the words out of their mouths in white plumes and lifted them into the breath-cloud over the yard until the whole of it seemed to be made of them.

"*Hail to the true king of the north. Hail to the true king of the north.*"

It went on. It went up. It came back off the curtain wall and the pale seam of the mend and the raw pine of the new gate and the blank white banner hanging over it, and down again, louder, until the torches shook in their cracks and the wolfhound threw back its head beside Muken's knee and howled with it, long and low and enormous, and nobody laughed at that either.

Muken stood in the middle of it.

He did not lift his hands. He did not smile. He stood at the foot of the woodpile with his sister at his right knee holding up her bloody mark, and the dog howling at his left, and Lorn Stark and Godfrey Greymane a pace behind him with their fists on their chests, and he let it come.

Down at the front, in the light of the brazier, Kiyome Byakuya had her eyes closed and her lips moving. Tetsugen Akagane had not knelt, and would not, but he had his hand on his chest still and his heavy jaw set like a stone. And by the other brazier, Hayato Amagiri was looking past all of it, past the yard and the kneeling crowd and the boy at the woodpile, at the postern gate standing a little open in the wall and the black cold beyond it, where somewhere out on the snow a single carter was walking south and east toward the Keld fires with the whole of it in his mouth.

*Two days,* thought Hayato. *He'll be there in one.*

He did not say it. Nobody would have heard him.

Over the yard, high up, behind the cloud, the green of the northern sky turned over once more in its sleep. And the chant went on, and on, and the woodpile stood behind the king of the north as high as a man's shoulder, every split end of it pale in the torchlight, the fullest stack in Kharven.

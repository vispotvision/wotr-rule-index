## The Moto Seat · After Gimbzo

---

There were seven chairs in the Hall of Verdicts, and six of them were filled.

The hall was the oldest room in the Moto seat, and the seat was the oldest thing in the western shield, and there were scholars in the Holy Sea who argued that the hall was older than the Moto themselves and that the house had only moved into it, the way a hermit crab moves into a shell. It was round. Its walls were a dark grey stone so close-grained that it took a polish like iron, and they rose without a single window to a dome that was lost in shadow, and from somewhere up in that shadow, on a chain too long to see the end of, hung a great ring of black iron set with worked lamps in iron cages, each with a Guild seal stamped in brass on its collar, that burned a steady gold without smoke or smell. They drew from the great main in the rock under the hold, and the draw came up through the walls with it, a low hum felt in the teeth and the breastbone more than heard, so that the whole round room seemed to be holding one long note. The floor was the same stone as the walls, and the Guild had laid heated lines under it, so that even in the dead of the northern winter it was warm to kneel on. Cut into it, in a circle that ran just inside the line of the chairs, was a band of script no living scholar could read, deep and clean, as fresh as the day it was carved. Prisoners were made to kneel inside that circle. The house said that the script listened.

The seven chairs stood round the outside of it, facing in.

They were not thrones. The Moto had always been very careful about thrones. Each was a plain high-backed seat of black wood on a low stone dais, and each was dressed in the colours of its line, so that a man brought in to kneel at the centre of the hall could turn his head and know, without a word spoken, exactly who was going to decide whether he lived.

Bright hard silver for the Shirogane. Copper and raw red-bronze for the Akagane. Unrelieved black and ash for the Kurenai, with the crimson kept to a single hidden thread. Near-white for the Byakuya, pale as a church. Gold and banner-heraldry for the Tenrai, the banner behind the chair worked so thick with gold thread that it threw the lamplight back like a shield. Indigo and mist-grey for the Amagiri, deep as a lake at dusk.

And red enamel over near-black steel, with a lion's head in a boss at the crest, for the Kōkan: a red so deep and live under the gold lamps that it looked wet.

The Kōkan chair was empty. The seventh chair had not been sat in since the war began. Gimbzo had ruled from a saddle and a tent and left it to the dust, so that every lord at the ring would look at it and know he could walk in any day and take it. Nobody had moved it in all that time, and nobody had ever had it taken out, and there was a fine grey skin of dust on its arms that the servants who swept the hall had been told, forty years ago, never to touch. The lion on its crest looked down at the centre of the floor with blind enamel eyes. Under it, on the night of the verdict, Torvald Stark knelt in chains.

---

He had been made to kneel, and he had not made it easy.

It had taken four of the Tenrai house-guard to put him down, and one of them was still in the corner with a cloth pressed to his face where Torvald's forehead had met it. They had put chains on his wrists and his ankles and a third round his waist that ran down to a ring set in the stone, inside the circle of script, and the chains were worked steel with Shirogane silver in the links, and he could feel the silver drinking at him through his skin like a leech. They had beaten him before they brought him in. They had beaten him the night before as well, and the night before that. His left eye was swollen shut. Two of his teeth were gone and a third was loose, and the blood from his mouth had run down into his beard and dried there and run again, so that the great rust-red beard of the Stark chief hung clotted and black on his chest.

He was a big man, even kneeling. Broad through the back and the shoulders the way the northern men were broad, from a lifetime of cutting wood and hauling it, with a neck like a bull's and forearms covered in old white scars where hounds and wolves and men had all at one time or another tried to take them off. His shirt had been torn half off him. Under it his skin was a map of bruises gone every colour from yellow to black, and on his left side, low, the ribs moved wrong when he breathed. He was fifty years old. He had been leading the north against the Tenrai for three of them.

He lifted his head and looked up at the Moto.

Every child in the north was taught, before it was taught anything else, never to meet a Moto's eye. The Moto read through the eyes. A man who met them had opened his door. Torvald had been taught it as a boy at his grandmother's knee, and he had taught it to his own sons, and he lifted his one good eye now and looked straight into the faces of the six most powerful people in the Inner World, one after another, slowly, the way a man looks over horses at a fair.

Kagenori Tenrai sat in the gold chair. He was a lean man of middle years, narrow-faced and motionless as a heron, with grey at his temples and a mouth like a closed purse, and the Moto blood showed on him plainly: the faint metallic sheen at the brow and the temple, catching the lamplight like old brass, and the ring-lines in the iris that sharpened when he looked at something he meant to keep. He wore gold-trimmed black, and behind his chair hung the Tenrai banner, and he was the one who had called the council. Everyone in the hall knew he was the one who meant to keep it.

Shizuha Shirogane sat in silver at his right. She was older than him, and very upright, a small grey-haired woman with a face like a hatchet and silver at her collar and her wrists and in the pins in her hair, and she had not looked at the prisoner once since he was brought in. Tetsugen Akagane, in copper and red-bronze that glowed like a banked forge under the lamps, was the biggest of the six: thick-necked, heavy-handed, bald as a stone, with a jaw that worked continually as though he were chewing on something he did not like the taste of. Sumie Kurenai sat in black. She was perhaps forty, pale, thin as a rope, with her hair cut short at the jaw and ink-dark eyes that did not seem to blink, and her hands lay folded in her lap, and there was nothing of crimson about her anywhere that Torvald could see. Hayato Amagiri, in indigo and grey, was the youngest of them, a slight sharp man with restless fingers who sat a little forward in his chair and kept glancing, without seeming to know he did it, at the hall's single door.

And Kiyome Byakuya, in white.

She was the only one of them who looked as though she had not slept. She was perhaps thirty, tall and pale and very straight, in a robe so near to white that under the lamps it seemed to give off a faint light of its own, with a veil of fine glass beads pinned back from her face and a halo-ring of pale metal at the crown of her head. The sheen at her temples was silver, faint as frost. She was sitting forward in her chair, both hands on its arms, and she was the only one of the six who was looking at Torvald as if he were a man and not a problem.

Behind him, kneeling in a row along the edge of the circle, chained the same way, were the others.

---

There were five of them.

Two were his own. Hakon and Sten, riders of his household, cousins to each other, taken with him at the ford when the Tenrai came over the hill. Hakon had lost an ear. Sten could no longer hold his head up and had to be propped against his cousin's shoulder, and his breathing was the bad wet kind that Torvald had heard before in men who did not live to see the next morning.

Beside them knelt a man in the remains of a grey surcoat with a wolf's head worked on the breast in darker thread. Aldwin Greymane. Younger brother of the Lord of the Ridgewall, and the man the Greymanes had sent north with forty riders a year ago to stand with the Starks when the Tenrai first began closing the passes. Thirty-eight of the forty were dead. He was forty-four, iron-haired, with the long grey Greymane face and a soldier's stillness, and he had been beaten as badly as Torvald and showed it less. He knelt with his back perfectly straight and his chained hands resting on his thighs, and he was looking at the empty Kōkan chair.

Next to him knelt Osbert Lambert, and Osbert did not look like a man who should have been in chains at all.

He was the tariff-master of the Hallenfeld river corridor. Fifty-odd, round-shouldered, soft in the belly, with thinning sandy hair and small square spectacles that were miraculously still on his face, one lens cracked across. He had ink on the second finger of his right hand that no amount of beating had got off. He was not a fighting man and he had never pretended to be one. What he had done was keep books. For eleven years he had sat at the Hallenfeld junction with his tally board and read the carts in against their manifests, and when the Tenrai began moving grain off the eastern routes and calling it the requisition of the realm, he had read those carts in too, and written down what was in them, and where they went, and who signed for them, and how much of the grain that left the corridor marked for the starving provinces arrived anywhere at all. Then he had made a fair copy of it and sent it north.

He had a son. The son was seven, and was at home in the corridor with his mother, and Osbert had not seen either of them in four months. He was thinking about the boy now; Torvald could see it on him, the way a man's face goes inward when he is trying to remember something exactly.

And last, at the end of the row, a Moto.

A common soldier of the Akagane house-guard, in copper-buckled harness with the buckles cut off. Young, perhaps twenty-five, square-faced, with the faint sheen of the blood at his wrists that every Moto foot-soldier had, and a look on his face of a man who still could not quite believe where he was kneeling. His name, Torvald had learned in the cells, was Ren. He had been ordered, three weeks before, to put the granaries at Tōmi to the torch, so that the village would have nothing left to give the Starks. He had refused. He had stood in front of the granary door with his spear held crossways and said that his grandmother lived in Tōmi.

Tetsugen Akagane had not looked at him once, either.

---

"We shouldn't be so hasty," Kiyome Byakuya said, "about this decision. We still haven't fully agreed."

Her voice was clear and very calm, and it went round the hall and came back from the dome, and it was the first thing anyone had said since the prisoners were brought in.

"We have the testimony of the Tenrai captains," she went on, "and we have the Stark's own riders, and we have a confession that was got out of a man after four nights in a cell. We do not have the council's consent. Not all of it." She looked along the line of chairs. "There are seven lines and one blood. We have always said so. The verdict of this hall is the verdict of the house, or it's the verdict of a man in a gold chair, and I would like to know, before we shed a drop of anyone's, which of those this is going to be."

Kagenori Tenrai turned his head and looked at her.

He did it slowly. The ring-lines in his eyes sharpened. When he spoke his voice was perfectly pleasant, and very soft, and it carried to every corner of the hall without his raising it at all.

"Do you think," he said, "that we can sit here delegating, Lady Byakuya, while they seek to poison our people with lies?"

He did not wait for her to answer.

"The Stark has been riding the northern provinces for three years with a story," he said. "That the house is starving its own. That the grain the house takes in requisition goes into the house's own granaries and never comes out. That the house could feed every child between the Thresh and the shield if it chose to and has simply decided not to." He spread his hands, very slightly. "It is a good story. It is the kind of story that makes a man pick up a scythe and walk toward a wall. There are eleven thousand people in the north who have picked up scythes because of it. Every day we spend discussing whether it is lawful to stop the man telling it, another hundred of them join him."

"Some of the story is true," Kiyome said.

The hall went quiet around the hum.

Kagenori did not look away from her. "All the best lies are," he said.

Across the circle, Tetsugen Akagane grunted, a heavy sound low in the chest, and shifted his great weight in the copper chair.

"I agree," he said.

They all looked at him. He did not seem to enjoy it.

"I don't like you, Kagenori," he said. "I want that said, here, where the script can hear it. I've never liked you, I don't like what you've done with the roads, and I don't like how quick you are to put the word *house* in front of whatever it is you happen to want." He rubbed his jaw with one enormous copper-ringed hand. "But you're right about this. He's right," he said, to Kiyome. "We can't let them think they've got the upper hand. Not now, with Gimbzo dead and every line in the house looking at the others to see who flinches first. We let the Stark walk out of here, or we let him sit in a cell for a year while we argue about it, and every man with a scythe in the north will know we were afraid to finish it."

He did not look at the soldier at the end of the row as he said it. He very carefully did not look.

---

Torvald laughed.

It hurt. It hurt badly, the broken ribs grinding on his left side, and the laugh came out wet and turned into a cough halfway through, and he bent over his chained hands and spat a mouthful of blood onto the band of script in the floor, very deliberately, right in the middle of a word.

Several of the house-guard took a step forward. Kagenori lifted one finger and they stopped.

Torvald raised his head again. Blood was running from the corner of his mouth into his beard.

"You," he said, and looked at Kiyome with his one good eye. His voice was hoarse and thick and very northern, the vowels flattened out like a hammered nail. "You're the only one in here with any sense, lass, and you're still too shallow to see it." He jerked his chin at the five other chairs. "These fucking idiots made up their minds two weeks ago. Before they ever caught me. Before the ford. They had the post set in the yard before I ever saw your walls." He grinned at her, a ruin of a grin, with the gaps in it. "You're not arguing for my life. You're arguing for the right to say you argued."

Kiyome did not answer him. But her hands had tightened on the arms of the white chair.

At the end of the line of seats, Hayato Amagiri leaned forward, his restless fingers going still for once, and looked down at the Stark with something on his narrow face that was very nearly regret.

"And to think," he said quietly, "that I once counted you among the few men in this capital with any honour."

Torvald turned his head to him.

"Honour," he said.

He laughed again, softer this time, and it did not turn into a cough.

"You think my life's some precious thing to me?" he said. "You think I came south to bargain for it?" He shook his head slowly, and the chains rattled on his wrists. "I'm fifty years old, Amagiri. I've buried a wife and two sons and every brother I had but the one, and he'll bury me, and then he'll come for you. I've buried more of my own riders in three years than you've ever had in your house-guard. My life's an old coat. I'd have thrown it in a ditch a long time ago if there'd been anybody else to wear it."

He looked round the circle of chairs.

"And you think me dying here will change the truth?" he said. "You think you put me on that post in your yard, and the grain comes back out of your granaries by itself? The children in Tōmi stop being hungry because the man who told them why they were hungry is dead?" He spat again, onto the script. "The truth doesn't care what you do to me. It was true before I said it. It'll be true when I'm in the ground. You can kill the man who carries the lamp. The thing he was carrying it toward is still standing there, plain as day, for anybody else who walks by."

Kagenori said nothing. His face had not changed at all.

Torvald leaned forward against the chain at his waist, as far as it would let him.

"And here's another truth for you, while I've breath to give it," he said. "It's only a matter of time. Only a matter of time before the world finds out." He let it hang there. "That there's two left. Two children of the Kōkan line, still breathing, somewhere you haven't looked."

---

The hall changed.

Torvald felt it. Every man and woman kneeling in the circle felt it. The lamps in the great iron ring overhead held steady, and yet their light seemed to draw in, all at once, a little closer to their cages, as if something enormous had taken a breath, and the draw-hum in the walls dropped half a tone. The warm floor stayed warm. The script cut into it under his knees grew cold, the way iron is cold at thirty below, so that it burned through the cloth at his knees and into the bone. The silver in his chains bit harder.

Six heads had turned toward him. Six pairs of eyes were looking at him, and in every one of them the ring-lines had sharpened, and in some of them the iris had begun to break up into fine patterns like frost on a pane, shards and lines and turning rings of light, and Torvald understood that six of the oldest bloodlines in the world were reading him at once. He felt it like hands. Like six sets of fingers going through him, through his face and his chest and the back of his skull, turning him over, looking for the lie.

He let them look. He grinned at them while they did it.

Sumie Kurenai had gone white.

She was the only one of them who had moved. She had not stood up, or spoken, or so much as lifted a hand from her lap. But she had gone white to the lips, and the ink-dark eyes in her thin face had gone wide, and she was not looking at Torvald any longer. She was looking at the empty Kōkan chair.

Torvald saw it. Aldwin Greymane, kneeling three places down, saw it too. Torvald heard the Greymane's breath go in, very softly, through his nose.

"Where," Kagenori said.

It was the first time his voice had risen. It was not much. It was enough.

"Where are they?"

"Go and look," Torvald said.

"*Where.*"

"I don't know." Torvald shrugged, as far as the chains would let him, and the broken ribs ground together and he grunted and kept grinning. "That's the beauty of it. I don't know, and you can read me inside out all night and you'll find I don't know. I heard it the way you hear anything in the north. From a man who heard it from a woman who heard it from somebody who was there." He tilted his head. "But I know it's true. You know it's true. Look at her." He nodded at Sumie Kurenai. "*She* knows it's true. She's the one whose line was meant to make sure it couldn't be."

Every head in the hall turned to the black chair.

Sumie Kurenai did not speak. She drew a breath, slowly, and her hands in her lap closed into fists, and the colour did not come back into her face.

"Kurenai," said Shizuha Shirogane.

It was the first word the old woman had spoken. Her voice was dry and thin and sharp as a needle.

"You told this council," she said, "that the register was closed. You told us the line was ended. You told us there was nothing left of them but a chair." She did not look at the chair. "You signed for it."

"I signed for what I was shown," Sumie Kurenai said. Her voice was low. "The register was closed. Every name that could be read was read out of it and every name that was read was ended. That is the truth. I would swear it before the script." She looked up, and her ink-dark eyes found the Shirogane's. "But there were marks in that register, Shizuha, that could not be read, by me or by anyone. Names written in a hand the instrument would not take." She swallowed. "We closed the book on what we could see. What we couldn't see, we called nothing. Because it gave us no reading. Because a thing that gives no reading is a thing that isn't there."

Torvald had spent half his life at fords and toll-gates listening to men explain themselves to him, and he knew the shape of what she had just done. Every word of it was true. She had laid them down one after another, carefully, the way a man lays flat stones over the mouth of a well, so that you could walk across them all day and never once hear the water underneath.

The six chairs held their silence, silver and copper and gold, black and white and indigo, round the red one.

Up in the dark of the dome, very faintly, something in the great iron ring creaked on its chain.

---

Torvald began to laugh again, and this time he could not stop it.

It went on a long time. It went on until the tears ran out of his good eye and down into his beard with the blood, and the broken ribs ground, and he had to bend almost double over his chains to get his breath. The house-guard shifted. Kagenori did not lift his finger this time. He sat in his gold chair and waited, with his mouth closed like a purse, until it was done.

"Oh," Torvald said at last, wheezing. "Oh, Sky above. Look at you. Look at all of you." He wiped his mouth on his bound wrist. "The keepers of fate. The house that reads the world. And you couldn't read two names in your own book."

He straightened up on his knees.

"And you think," he said, and his voice had gone very quiet, and very steady, and there was no laughter left in it at all, "that by killing me you'll stop my family going to war."

He looked at Kagenori.

"My sons are dead," he said. "But I've a brother living, and he's had the wolf off me since before ever I rode for Kessen, sealed and witnessed in his own hall at Hollenmark, so you can take my head tonight and the north won't miss a step for it. He's older than me, and slower to start, and I've never once in my life seen him stop. I've nephews. I've a brother's widow at the Ironwood March who'll put a spear through you herself if she ever gets close enough. I've eleven thousand people with scythes, and every one of them is going to hear what was done in this room tonight, because one of your own guards is going to tell it in a tavern within a month. They always do." He nodded at Aldwin Greymane, beside him. "The Greymanes have already lost thirty-eight men for me. You think the Ridgewall's going to sit quiet when the Lord's brother doesn't come home?"

He leaned forward again, against the chain.

"Here's the thing about it," he said. "Here's the thing none of you have ever understood about the north. You think I'd want to live with my honour clean. I don't. I'd trade my honour for a few more years of war. I'd trade it in a heartbeat. A few more years of war, and death, and burning, and every one of you sitting up at night in your chairs listening for a scythe at the door." He grinned again. "That's what I came south for. Not to live. To make it cost."

The lamps burned in the great iron ring. The script lay cold in the floor.

"It was good," Torvald Stark said, almost gently, "to see Gimbzo finally meet his end." He let his good eye travel slowly round the six chairs, and come to rest, at last, on the seventh. On the red enamel. On the lion's head in the boss, looking down at him with its blind eyes.

"Long live the king," he said.

---

There was a silence after that which nobody in the hall would ever forget.

It was Kiyome Byakuya who broke it. She did not speak. She simply stood up, all at once, in a rustle of white silk and a faint clicking of the glass beads at her temple, and stood there in front of her chair with her hands at her sides.

"I won't sign it," she said.

"Sit down, Kiyome," said Shizuha Shirogane.

"I won't sign it. Not tonight, with what's just been said in this room." Her voice shook, very slightly, for the first time. "We've just been told that the Kōkan line is not ended. That this council was told it was ended on a reading that couldn't read. And the first thing we mean to do, the very first thing, is kill the only man in the Inner World who's willing to tell us so to our faces."

"He told us nothing," Kagenori said softly. "He told us a rumour he heard from a man who heard it from a woman."

"He told us enough to turn Sumie white."

Kagenori let the hum of the hall fill the space where his answer should have been. Then he turned his head, without hurry, and looked at the other four chairs.

"Shirogane," he said.

The old woman did not hesitate. "Yes."

"Akagane."

Tetsugen's jaw worked. He looked down at his own copper-ringed hands. At the end of the row of prisoners the young soldier Ren had lifted his head and was looking at him, straight at him, for the first time since they had brought him in. Tetsugen did not look back.

"Yes," he said heavily.

"Amagiri."

Hayato Amagiri's fingers had begun moving again on the arm of his chair. He glanced, one more time, at the hall's single door. Then he looked at Torvald, kneeling in the circle with blood in his beard and the grin still on his face, and something passed across the young lord's narrow features that might have been shame.

"Yes," he said. "The seven lines forgive us. Yes."

"Kurenai."

Sumie Kurenai did not answer at once. She was still looking at the empty chair. When she turned back at last, her face had closed up again, smooth and pale as paper, and only her eyes were wrong in it.

"It is my line's office," she said. "It will be done by my line's hand." She rose. "Yes."

"Five," said Kagenori Tenrai. He did not look at Kiyome. "The house has spoken."

---

Sumie Kurenai came down from the black chair and across the stone and into the circle of script, and the script did not grow cold for her. She walked the way the Kurenai walked, without any sound at all. At the edge of the circle a guard held out to her a blade on a cloth of black silk, and she took it without looking: a long, plain, single-edged sword with a black grip and no guard at all, and when it came out of its scabbard the lamplight ran down the edge and found, for the first time, the crimson. A single fine line of it, inlaid down the length of the spine of the blade, red as a vein.

Torvald watched her come.

"Kurenai," he said. "You'll do it yourself, then."

"It is my office."

"Good." He nodded. "Better you than him." He jerked his chin at the gold chair. "I'd not give him the satisfaction."

He shifted on his knees, and straightened his back as well as the ribs would let him, and lowered his head. His great clotted beard fell forward onto his chest. The back of his neck was broad and brown and scarred, and there was a little grey in the hair at the nape.

Behind him, along the edge of the circle, Hakon Stark had begun to say something in the old northern tongue, low and fast, over and over. Sten could not lift his head to see. Aldwin Greymane had not moved at all. He knelt straight-backed with his hands on his thighs and his long grey face turned toward Torvald, and his eyes were open, and he did not look away. Next to him Osbert Lambert had shut his. His lips were moving too. Not in a prayer. Torvald knew it, because he had heard it in the cells three nights running, through the wall: the man counted. Under his breath, steadily, numbers. Carts. Sacks. Grain by the bushel and by the stone. It was what he did when he could not bear a thing. He was counting the requisitions out of the Hallenfeld corridor for the year, one cart at a time, from the first day of spring.

Sumie Kurenai set her feet.

"Torvald Stark," she said, in the formal voice, the voice of her line's office, that went up into the dome and came back down out of it doubled. "For sedition against the seven lines and the one blood. For raising the commons of the north in arms. For the deaths of four hundred and twelve men of the Tenrai house-guard and the burning of the Tenrai granaries at Kessen ford. The house has spoken, and the house's word is the house's hand."

"Sky take your house," said Torvald Stark, into his beard.

She cut.

It was a clean cut. The Kurenai had been doing it for longer than there had been a Stark in the north, and they had made an art of it the way other lines made arts of reading or of war. The long blade came down in a single flat arc, and there was a sound like a heavy cleaver going through a joint on a butcher's block, and the edge passed through the back of Torvald's neck between the bones of the spine and on out through the throat, and stopped a finger's width above the stone. His head came off and struck the floor and rolled, once, and came to rest face up on the band of script, with the one good eye still open and the grin still on the ruined mouth. The body stayed kneeling for a heartbeat longer. Then the blood came out of it in a great pulsing rope, bright under the gold lamps as the enamel on the empty chair, and the knees went, and it fell forward onto its chest across the circle, and the chains rang on the stone.

The blood ran out across the script. It filled the cut letters one by one, the way water fills a channel, and ran on round the circle in a slow red ring.

Hakon Stark screamed.

Nobody else made a sound. Aldwin Greymane had not looked away. Osbert Lambert had opened his eyes, and was looking at the head on the floor, and his lips had stopped moving, and he had lost his count.

Sumie Kurenai lowered the blade. There was a single drop of blood on the crimson line down its spine, and it ran, very slowly, down the inlay, and did not stain it, and fell off the point onto the floor.

She did not look at the head. She was looking past it, at the seventh chair, the way she had looked at it when the Stark first said *two*, and her face had gone smooth again over whatever was under it. She had been twenty-three the night of the Kōkan cellars, and new to her line's office, and she had stood with the register open in her hands while the names were brought to her and ended one by one. She had known since that night, as Shigure Tenrai knew, and the man in the gold chair, and a handful of Tenrai captains, and nobody else at the ring was meant to, that one had not been brought to her. A woman of the white line had walked up out of the cellars on her own feet with a newborn held inside her coat, and nobody had stopped her, and Sumie Kurenai had written nothing down and signed for a closed book. Sixteen years she had kept it closed on the reasoning she had just given the council, that a thing which gave no reading was a thing that was not there. One child, nameless, gone out into the cold like a thousand others. The Stark had said two.

Up on the dais, Kiyome Byakuya was still standing in front of her white chair. She had not sat down. She had watched the whole of it. Her hands were shaking at her sides, and the glass beads at her temple were shaking with them, a very faint clicking, like rain on a window.

Kagenori Tenrai rose from his gold chair.

"The Greymane goes back to the Ridgewall," he said, "with his brother's ransom set at the weight of the man in salt. The Lambert goes to the cells until his ledger is found and burned. The Stark riders go on the post in the yard at dawn." He did not look at the end of the row. "The Akagane soldier is Lord Tetsugen's to dispose of. Lord Tetsugen will dispose of him."

Tetsugen Akagane said nothing. At the end of the row, young Ren had begun, very quietly, to weep.

Kagenori turned to go. Then he paused, and looked back past the prisoners and the body to the lion on the crest of the seventh chair.

"And find them," he said. "Whatever it costs. Find out who heard it from whom, and who heard it from them, back to the woman, back to whoever was there. Kurenai. Amagiri. Every road, every register, every salt pan and mine and bonded house from here to the end of the world." His voice had gone very soft again. "Two children. Find them before anyone else does."

He walked out. After a moment, one by one, the others followed him.

Kiyome Byakuya went last. At the edge of the dais she stopped, and turned, and came back down the steps, and walked across the stone to the edge of the circle, where the blood had not yet reached. She knelt there in her white robe. Very carefully, so that nobody watching could have said what she was doing, she reached out and closed Torvald Stark's one good eye.

Then she stood, and went out, and did not look back at the chair.

Nobody noticed, when the doors had shut and the servants had turned the worked lamps down to a red ember-glow in their cages, that Osbert Lambert had begun to count again.

He was not counting carts this time. His lips were moving slowly, over two words, the same two, again and again, the way a man goes over a figure he means to carry home and set down in a ledger somewhere it cannot be burned.

*Two children.*

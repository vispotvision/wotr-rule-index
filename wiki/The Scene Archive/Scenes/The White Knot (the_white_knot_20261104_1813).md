I · The Corridor

*The Kessen road at the edge of the cedar wood; the Lie-In standing over everything; the Mantle's last breaths*

Vaashuuk's insistence on being a pest was starting to irritate Naiser.

It came up in him slowly, the way everything did, and it came up from the Lambert half first. The count had been keeping a column for the goblin since the causeway: a girl's shoulder levered out of its saucer, a boy's face broken against a pane, a breath stopped in a child's throat for sport, and now a dying boy's heart made to climb a hill on every beat, and every line of it entered under a creature who would never once in his life sign for any of it. The Yukari half had been patient with him. The Yukari half was patient with everything. Under Naiser's breastbone the two halves of him, which had been swinging together all afternoon like two clocks on one beam, both leaned the same way at once, and for the first time that day his face moved. The corner of his mouth went down a hair.

[low, cold, measured Japanese accent] "You have been a great deal of trouble for a man who has never done anything."

[slurring, delighted, drawling] "Ain't it beautiful?" said Vaashuuk from the foot of the cedar, with his eyes shut.

Naiser focused, and processed the nature of the Domain round him.

He took it the way a smith takes a bar of strange iron, turning it in the light. The Lie-In had raised a hill in front of every beginning on the road. A hill is a height. A height is measured from somewhere, and the somewhere was the goblin: every thread on the road that the Lie-In touched ran back to him, down into the small slumped body in the red snow, and through it into his Crystal, and through his Crystal into the Sleep Crown he had been baptised in at its Core under the slag-yards of Gorr-Keth, ninety years ago, when he lay down beside a dying forge-roof and decided not to get up. Naiser saw the whole of it in the Itomi, laid out like warp threads on a loom. And he saw the one thing in it the goblin had never once looked at in himself.

There was nothing in the middle of it. The threads went in to Vaashuuk and found no knot to tie off on. Ninety years of acts begun and none finished, a job begun and a promise begun and a love begun, and under all of them, where a man keeps the sum of what he has decided he is, an empty place the size of a soul.

A corridor will not carry every sound. That was what the house's corridors were built on, in the record and in the stone of the Enclave both. Shout into a long narrow hall and the deep notes go down it and come out at the far end; the thin high ones that are too small for its width die in the first few paces, fading so fast that a man ten steps in hears nothing of them at all. Every passage has its cutoff, the size below which a thing cannot travel along it, and below that size a thing does not slow down in the corridor. It simply fails to arrive. The Corridor Between Halls was that law with a judgment in it, and the measure it took was a self. A man who had decided what he was went down it like a bell's low note. A man who had never decided anything was below cutoff.

He would have to begin it against the hill. Three times the price, with the Mantle still eating at him. The count told him exactly what that would leave in his Crystal afterward, a figure set down in a clean hand, and it was very little.

He paid it.

[low, formal, the house's old Japonic] "Hazama no Rō..."

The Corridor came up round him at three times its weight. The charge for it went out of the Sovereign Crystal under his breastbone in one long wrenching pull, like a full well-bucket hauled up hand over hand, and the Shell that ordinarily wasted nothing could not take a load like that clean; the strayed part of it came off him as heat, and the heat showed where it always showed on a Yukari, in the thread-marks. The fine pale lines under the skin of his temples and the backs of his hands, branching like roots seen from under the soil, lit silver and then went violet at their edges, and the skin over them reddened and smoked faintly in the cold, as if a lamp-wick had been laid along every one. The second Release stood up on top of the first. The Mantle's black silk darkened on his shoulders until it was the color of a well's bottom.

The dark came in from the edges of the road. It came down the frost in a line first, a long line of dimmed air laid across the clay from the cedar wood to the paddies, and then the pillars came up out of it. They stood up out of the frozen ground on either side of the road at even intervals, a double row of them going away in both directions, tall square posts of old cedar gone red-brown and resin-dark, the posts of the long hall at the Enclave and the posts of the Lambert bench-house at Sallowgate laid one over the other in the same places, as if two halls stood in one. Between the pillars the light of the gray sky went out like a lamp turned down to nothing. A door stood at each end of the road, very far off, one of cedar and one of iron-bound oak, both closed. Under it all the two low notes of his aura became the echo of a hall two hundred paces long, *ooooooonnnnnnnnnnn*, and the smell came down the length of it, cedar smoke and cold stone and, under those, the dry smell of old paper, of a record kept.

The road took it badly. The frost on the ruts lay down flat and gray, as if the white had gone out of it. The cedar boughs above the seam stopped dripping. Out on the paddies past the road the water went dull as pewter from bank to bank.

The Corridor met the Lie-In, and the space round them began to fracture the relations between the threads.

Naiser watched it in the Itomi. Every thread the goblin had laid across the road ran back to him, and the threads were what carried the hill; the Lie-In was a law, and a law is only a relation, this to that, this before that. The Corridor read the relations and judged them. It came down each thread from the far end of the hall like a clerk going down a column with his finger, and at each it asked the same question, which is the only question a corridor asks, and each thread came back with the goblin's answer, which was nothing. Below cutoff. The threads did not snap. They faded. They went thin from the far end toward the near the way a note fades in a long passage, and everything the goblin was holding up with them began to be crippled down: the raised hill in front of the crow sagged, and the crow's downstroke came at last, one long beat of the wings, and it dropped onto the carriage roof and sat there with its feathers fluffed and its beak open; the hill in front of Senna's foot sank, and her foot came down off the clay and struck the next step at an ordinary pace, so suddenly that she stumbled; the fox-fire in Dari's palm, which had sat there as a bead, sprang up into a blue-white tongue as tall as her hand.

On the road Muken's heart, on the Itomi, caught, and beat, and beat again, still late, still faint, but beginning on its own.

Vaashuuk's eyes came open.

[sharp, the drawl gone] "Hey..."

He felt it. He was lying in the middle of every thread that was failing, and every one of them was failing back toward him. His hum went up in pitch. It shook. The lavender of the Lie-In flickered round the cedar like a candle in a draft.

"Shikii..."

The Threshold. Naiser said it quietly, and the line of dimmed air on the clay brightened along its length into a sill of black, a long low step across the floor of the hall, and Fixatio cured it into the ground as he spoke, the way a lacquer cures, so that it was set before the word was finished. It ran across the road a pace in front of the cedar's roots. It ran under the goblin.

The Corridor's Two Benches opened on him. At one end of the hall the Yukari bench and at the other the Lambert, and between them, under the black sill, every account the goblin carried was read out at once. Naiser did not need to hear it. He had heard it from the carriage window on the Kessen road. A job begun. A promise begun. A gang of bellows-men under a forge-roof with a crack in its great timber, and a boy who heard the crack a full day before it went and lay down instead. The heaviest account Naiser had ever seen read in his own hall, and every line of it unpaid, because the man who owed it had never once let any line close.

The internal resistance in the goblin's body and spirit began to split.

Naiser saw it go on the Itomi, and saw it go on the body. The Threshold reads a man against the cutoff of himself. The goblin's whole nature was a pull, a want to be slow, a long downhill slide toward the bottom of every effort, the way water wants to run down to the low place and lie there; it was the pull that had made the Sleep Crown his, at its Core, and it was the pull that held his Crystal to the Wellspring like a stone held to the earth. The Threshold took that pull and asked it what it was. And the pull had no answer either. It cracked. It cracked the way the glaze on a pot crazes when the pot is plunged from the kiln into the cold, the outside shrinking faster than the inside can follow, and on the Itomi the very affinity of Vaashuuk's Crystal for Hypnather, the thread that joined him to the Sleep Crown, went white along its length and split into a thousand fine lines.

The goblin's body showed it. His mossy skin went gray from the brow down. The long dark tear-tracks from his eyes darkened to black. Every ring and stud and spike through the long ears trembled, *chirichirichiri*, and his split arm jerked in his lap, and the two dead fingers of the other hand flew open at last, all on their own, as if somebody had pulled their strings.

[thin, frightened, the vowels gone short] "What're ya... hey. Hey, handsome. What're ya doin' to my..."

Then Naiser was swallowed up by blackness.

It came out of him. Senna saw it first, and Naiser felt her see it. The Corridor's dark and the Mantle's black silk and the black thread that had run down his blade at the Reckoning all came together in him, and for a breath he stood in the middle of the road as a man-shaped hole in the gray afternoon, a figure of blackness so complete that nothing on him took the light, and the darkness went out from him in every direction. Hell, the entire area was clouded in it. It was the Null-Light, more of it than he had ever carried or would have said he could; the fragment the Ketsumyōgan keeps of Ashura's way of seeing had been pushed through the Corridor's whole length, and the Corridor had made it long. A light that knows a thing without arriving at it lay over the whole road, over the carriage and the horses and the seam and the boy in the ruts, and nothing under it cast a shadow. The cedar trunks had no shadows. The carriage had none. The crow on its roof sat on a roof with no shadow under it. The frost on the road lay flat and shadowless as paper, and the only shadow left on the whole of the Kessen road was under Naiser himself, and it stretched toward the goblin.

It was the first time Senna had felt this spiritual presence from her father.

She went down. Naiser heard her knees strike the frozen clay, *gotsu*, and heard her breath go out of her, and on the carriage step Hiromi went down beside her out of Dari's lap onto his hands, and Dari's nine tails went flat to the boards one after another like a fan closing. It was so vast and so powerful that it swayed the whole road. The air leaned. The pillars of the Corridor leaned with it. The cedar crowns above the seam swung all together toward him like reeds in a current, and every crow in the wood came up off its branch at the same moment with a single huge clatter of wings and went up into the gray sky in a black cloud and did not come down.

[barely a whisper] "Begone..."

His katana came out of its scabbard.

It came out in the spiritual world first. The Lie-In still stood on the scabbard in the physical one, and in the physical one the blade was still a finger's width out of the wood with a hill in front of it. Naiser drew it where there was no hill. He drew it on the far side of the Corridor's threshold, in the place the threads run, where his hand had already decided and the decision was the motion; the scabbard there was a line and the blade was a line, and they parted like two words in a sentence. The spiritual world was faster. It always is, the way a hammer struck on one end of a long iron rail is heard through the rail at the far end a long breath before the sound comes through the air, because iron passes a shake along itself faster than air ever can. The cut went down the threads to the goblin before the steel had moved at all. The physical world lagged behind it, and the blade in the frost came out after, late, catching up, like the second sound of the hammer arriving through the air.

Vaashuuk read none of it. Nothing in him had ever read anything fast, and this was not fast in any way his body could have known. He sat in the red snow with his mouth open on the end of a word.

The cut went to his Crystal.

It took nothing from his body. It did not need to. It went down the one thread that still held anything on the road, the cracked white tether between the goblin's Crystal and the Sleep Crown, and parted it at the root, cleanly, the way his father's knife had parted the cord on a parcel. And the goblin's soul crystal threw itself out of his body.

It came out through his chest. It came out of the place under the breastbone where Naiser's first cut had already scored the bone, and the breastbone split down its length to let it through, *bakin*, and the Crystal came up out of him like a stone out of a sling, a lavender crystal the size of a plum, clouded, cracked from end to end, trailing a long thread of slow light behind it, and it went up into the shadowless air over the cedar and hung there a moment, turning.

His body did the rest.

For ninety years the goblin had lived at the bottom of a dormouse's winter, his heart barely beating, his blood barely pushed, held there by the Crystal's Torpor the way a diver is held by the weight of the sea above him; and every vessel the children and Naiser had opened in him that day had been holding at that low pressure, mending at that slow pace, clotted and pinched and kept. The Crystal had been the sea. A diver brought up from the deep too fast has the gas that was held quiet in his blood by the weight of the water come out of it all at once, in bubbles, everywhere, because the weight is gone. The goblin's sea went in an instant. Every vessel in his body burst. The fine ones went first: the whites of his eyes flooded red to the lids, and his skin from the brow to the clawed feet went dark in a single spreading bloom, purple and then black, as every hair-thin vessel under it let go at once. Then the larger ones. The blood came out of his ears and his nose and the corners of his mouth and the roots of his nails, and out of the half-mended cut across his chest and the split arm and the shoulder and the hip and the thighs and the heels all at once, the hundred clots that had held them blown out together, and it came out of him at last with a push behind it, fast, the first fast thing in his life, a sound like a wineskin trodden on, *gubotsu*, and the snow round the cedar went black.

[a bubbling, last breath of a drawl] "I was gonna..."

He did not say what. He sat against the cedar with his head tipped back and the black blood running out of him into the snow, and his bottle-green eyes, gone red, looked up at the Crystal turning over his head, and stayed looking.

The Crystal fell. It came down out of the shadowless air into the red snow at his feet with a small dull sound, *sukutt*, and lay there clouded and cracked, and its slow light went out.

Vaashuuk's Domain fractured.

It went out over the road in long lavender cracks, the way a sheet of ice on a river goes when the thaw takes it, splitting from the cedar outward in every direction, *pishipishipishipishi*, and the pieces fell. They fell out of the world. Everything that had been held on the road came loose together: Muken's chest went up in a full breath, the steam off the paddies rose in a white rush, the four gray horses on their knees blew and threw up their heads, the hum of the Lie-In went out like a struck bowl caught in a hand.

Naiser tilted his head a fraction, the way a man does at a sound in another room that has stopped.

He sheathed his blade. In the spiritual world it was already sheathed. In the physical one it came back into the scabbard after, at its ordinary pace, with the soft whisper of steel on wood and the small final *chin* of the guard.

The Sin of Sloth was dead.

II · The Wrath Comes Out

*The same road; the Corridor fading; the seam*

The Corridor went down round him the way a hall's lamps go out after a feast, the far ones first. The pillars sank into the clay. The two doors at the ends of the road went into the gray. The black went off Naiser's shoulders and the Mantle with it, the last of its breaths gone, and the cost came in behind it like a tide behind a sea-wall.

He felt the quarrel start. The two clocks on the beam of his Crystal, which had swung together all afternoon, slipped apart by a tick, and then another, and the Lambert half of him began to count the cost in a voice that was not quite his, and the Yukari half began to read the road in a way that would not stop reading. The thread-marks at his temples went on burning after the light had gone out of them. His hands, which never shook, shook once. He closed them. He had very little left, the count said, and it said it twice.

The seam split open.

The black-violet crack at the wood's edge, the height of a door, went white at its center like iron in a forge, and then it unfolded. It opened out on itself in long folds, one after another, like a fan opened rib by rib, like the pages of a book thrown open by a hand from the far side, and every fold was a sheet of black polish with kiln-light lying in it, and the heat came out through it onto the road in a wave that took the frost off the ruts in one breath and sent it up as steam.

Shōki emerged through it, and his arms unfolded.

Both of them. The new one had finished. It was a little shorter than the other and very red, the skin on it thin and new and shining like the skin under a burn-blister, the fingers long and wet, and it was steaming. He came out of the fold onto the Kessen road in the cracked red harness with his long black braids hanging and the X across his chest glowing like a brand, and his aura came out with him, ember-orange, grained, too bright to look at, rimmed in a black-violet so thick now that it stood off him like smoke off a pyre. The heat came off him in a wall. The snow round the cedar where the goblin sat went to slush, and then to water, and then to steam. Under his bare feet the frozen clay of the road softened and steamed and took his footprints like wet sand.

He did not care about Naiser. Naiser saw the long hollow face pass over him, and over the carriage, and over the dead goblin at the cedar's foot, and none of it was entered. The living left eye with its turning red rings went to the boy lying in the ruts and stayed there.

His presence was overwhelming. It was hatred, all of it, made into pressure, and it came down on the road the way a hot iron comes down on a hide. The children on their knees in the clay pitched forward onto their hands. Dari let out a sound like a kicked dog. On the carriage roof the crow, which had sat through the Lie-In and the Corridor both, fell off the roof into the road and lay there with its wings out. Naiser felt the weight of it settle onto his own shoulders, and he was spent, and he stood under it only because he was two Stages and a lifetime past the point where a man has to kneel to anything.

Kiyome's corpse was hanging from the Sin's arm.

The new arm. It had gone through her. It went in at her back below the left shoulder blade and came out through the front of her chest under the breastbone, and the hand on the end of it was closed, and she hung on it the way a coat hangs on a peg, her head down, her near-white hair fallen forward over her face. She was in the gray of the Byakuya guard. Her katana was still in its scabbard at her hip; she had not drawn it. The front of her coat was burned through in a black ring round the place where his hand came out, and the edges of the ring were still glowing, and the blood that had run down her coat to her hem had cooked onto it, brown.

And in the other hand, held out in front of him by the throat at the length of his long arm, he had Amelia Greymane.

She was alive. She was soot-black from the brow to the jaw, her mantle burned off one shoulder, her hands wrapped in burned rags, and she hung from his fist with her boots off the clay and both hands on his wrist and her teeth set, and her eyes were open. She was not struggling. She had judged it, Naiser saw, the way a Shieldwarden judges a line: she could not break his grip, and kicking would only spend air.

Naiser looked at Shōki.

[quiet, flat, Japanese accent] "So you killed her."

Shōki did not say anything.

He did not look away from the boy in the ruts. He drew the new arm back out of Kiyome Byakuya. It came out of her the way it had gone in, slowly, with a wet sucking sound, *zuruuu*, and she slid off the end of it, and he caught her by the collar of the gray coat before she reached the ground, and walked three long strides across the steaming clay, and laid her down. He laid her down in front of Muken, on her back, across the ruts, a pace from the boy's head, with her arms at her sides and her face turned up, and straightened her coat over the hole in her chest with two fingers, very precisely, the way a clerk squares the corners of a page before it is entered.

Then he stood over the two of them with Amelia held out in front of him at arm's length, and waited.

It was a sentence. Naiser read it the way he read anything written: the body laid out where the boy would see it the moment he opened his eyes, the second one held up living over the first. The boy had sworn for others. A man who swears for others puts his strength outside himself, where it can be reached.

III · The Tea

*The Plane of Fate; a room that was the kitchen at the Foot, and was not*

Muken was pulled into Fate.

He did not feel himself go. He was lying in the frost with the cold coming up through the split plates of the harness, and the pain was all of him, and then there was a smell of tea.

It was the kitchen at the Foot. He knew it by the smell first, smoke and boiled linen and the green bitterness of the mountain tea she bought off the Stark carters by the brick, and then by the table, the long scarred table with the knife-cut where Lorn had once tried to carve his name and got as far as the L. A kettle was going on the hob. The window was full of a light that came from no sun he had ever seen, gold and very even, and through it he could see threads. Everywhere. Going away in every direction like the rigging of every ship in a harbor at once.

Yukazuri was setting down some tea.

She set it on the table in front of him in the chipped blue cup, the one she always gave him because the handle was big enough for his hand, and the steam went up out of it and stood in the gold light. She was thirteen. She would always be thirteen. Her hair was tied back with a strip of linen, and there was a smear of salve on her wrist where she had wiped her hand, and her sleeves were rolled to the elbow, and she smiled at him.

[gentle, plain, a little hoarse, a girl's voice] "You don't have very long, brother."

He opened his mouth and nothing came out of it.

She sat down across from him and folded her hands on the table, the way she sat when she had a man on a cot in front of her and was deciding where to cut.

"But you don't need to hold back any longer." She looked at him. She had always looked at the edges of a wound and never at the hurt, and she looked at him that way now. "I know you blame yourself for my death."

He did. He had never once said it aloud to anybody and he did not need to say it now. He blamed himself. He had not been there. He had not been strong enough to be there, or quick enough, or enough of anything. He had not been powerful enough to protect the ones he cared about, and she had gone down the Marrow on the ice with the south wind carrying her, and the death-house had kept her cold.

He looked at the cup. His hands were on the table, and there was no blood on them, no skin gone off the palms, no broken fingers. They were a boy's hands in a kitchen.

[thick, low, Kharven-rough] "I should've..."

[firmly, the surgeon's voice] "Hold still."

He stopped.

"You have to move on." She leaned across the table and put her hand on his, the small cold competent hand that had sewn up half the Foot. "You have to get stronger, Muken. Not for me. For the world."

Somebody laughed.

It came from the end of the table, by the hob, where there had been nobody. It was an old man's laugh, deep and dry and very pleased, the laugh of a man who has been waiting at the end of a long game for the other player to see the move, and who has just watched him see it.

[deep, ancient, amused, unhurried] "It is destined..."

He was sitting on the bench by the hob as if he had always been sitting there. He was tall even sitting, and very old, older than anyone Muken had ever stood near, and the age was not in him the way it is in old men; it was in the room round him. His beard was white and fell to his belt. His hair was white and long and lay down his back over a plain gray robe like snow lying on a roof. His eyes were the gold Muken had seen in the black floor of the Hall, the gold of the face that had looked up at him out of the polish.

"...that you would come. The White Wolf." He said it as if he were reading it off a page. "The scavenger of Fate. A man who could see the flaws in any possibility, and pick it clean, and walk away with what was worth keeping." He laughed again, softer. "Some will say I should have blessed you."

Muken stared at him.

He was confused. He knew the name. Lorn had told it to him by a fire on the Seat road, and Faisal had said it in the white place made of threads and not said any more. *Bara.* The man who wrote the Works before there was a before to write them in. The first of the Moto. The person he had been told about, tall and white-bearded and long-haired, and nothing in the telling had been anything like the old man on the bench, who had a chip of tea-leaf stuck in his beard and was picking at it.

[still amused, then grave] "Mmh. However..." The old man stood up. He did it the way a mountain might stand, if a mountain had ever bothered. "I'll give you the knowledge of my Ultimate Techniques."

He came down the length of the table, and reached out, and touched Muken's forehead with one finger.

It was like a nail of light driven into the bone between his eyes, with no pain in it. It went in, and in, and behind it came a weight he could not hold and was holding: every Work, all seven of the notes he had heard in the hall and the chord over them, and under the chord a long, long ledger written in a hand he knew without ever having seen, the hand of a man who had written down how things were before anybody had thought to ask. It went into him the way water goes into dry sand, all at once and everywhere, and he would know it now the way he knew how to breathe.

Yukazuri came round the table.

She did not say anything else. She put her arms round him from the side, the way she had at the gate when he came back from the Veyrath ditch, briefly and hard, her chin on his shoulder, smelling of salve and boiled linen. It was one last hug. He felt it end.

His body began to thrum.

He felt it far off, in the other place, the place with the frost and the pain: the bell held on one note in the back of his teeth, and under it something bigger, something that had never been in him before and had always been, a beat, slow and enormous, as if somewhere under the floor of the world a drum the size of a mountain had been struck once and the skin of it was still moving.

IV · Unlimited Works

*The Kessen road at the edge of the cedar wood; the Sin of Wrath standing over the two bodies*

Naiser looked over.

He was looking at the Sin when it began, and the Itomi turned his head for him. Something was coming up in the boy in the ruts. The boy's thread, which had been the thinnest thread Naiser had seen on a living body since the slope above the river, was thickening. There was no reserve in it. It was filling with something Naiser had no word for in the record of either house, something powerful, all-encompassing, a spirit he could not conceive of the shape of, so large that the Itomi, which had laid out every thread in every room he had ever stood in, could not find its edges. It came up out of the boy the way the sea comes up the shingle at the turn of a tide, without hurry, without anything that could stop it.

A drum.

THRUM!

It went through the road. It went through the clay and the cedar and the steaming snow and the soles of Naiser's boots and the bones of his legs into his Crystal, and his Crystal rang with it, both halves, the Yukari and the Lambert, in one note for the first time without his asking. It went through the seam. The entirety of the inner world, the whole country of the north on its side of the old line, and through the seam into the old world beyond it, began to shiver, the way the skin of a drum shivers between strokes. The paddies below the road shivered in a thousand fine rings at once. The cedar crowns shook down their last rime. The crows that had gone up into the sky at the Corridor were scattered across the gray like thrown seed.

And the skies above fell bright.

Little lights came down out of the gray. Gold. They came down slowly, like the first snow of the year, like the sparks that go up from a bonfire coming back down, thousands of them, each the size of a grain of rice and each burning a clean steady gold, and where they touched the road they did not melt anything or burn anything. They lay there, shining. One came down on the back of Naiser's hand and lay on the burning thread-marks there, and the burning stopped.

Shōki looked panicked.

Naiser had not known that face could. The long hollow face had held one look all day, the look of a judge reading a sentence, and now the judge was looking at something that was not in any register he had ever kept. The living eye's rings went still. The new arm, the red and shining one, drew back toward his chest.

[a hoarse breath, almost no voice] "No..."

Muken's body began to rise from the ground.

It came up off the frozen ruts the way a man is lifted on a rope from the shoulders, the head lolling, the burned feet trailing on the clay and then off it, the broken left arm hanging bound across the chest in the lit harness, the gray sword dropping out of the right hand and ringing on the road. Muken was not conscious. Naiser could see that on the Itomi as plainly as he could see it in the face: the boy was not in the body. Something else was holding the strings. The body came up off the ground to its full height, and its feet came down onto the clay and set, the broken knee locked in its splint of lacquer and cord, and it stood upright over Kiyome Byakuya's corpse with its head hanging, and it lifted its right arm, and it pointed two fingers forward at the Sin.

Shōki moved first. He was a Percussion man; he did not wait to be read.

He pulled Amelia forward. He brought her in off the end of his long left arm and round in front of his own chest, his right foot driving back onto the softened clay to anchor him and his hips turning to bring her across, and as she came he drove the new right hand at her breast, the long red fingers bunched together into a point like the beak of a heron, aimed under the left breast where the heart sits behind the ribs. He meant to rip her heart out. He meant to do it in front of the body standing over the dead woman, so that whatever was in it would see it done.

Amelia saw it come. She could not get out of the way of it; his other hand had her throat. She got her burned hands off his wrist and down, crossed, in front of her breast, the Greymane shield-hands, palms out, a wall of two hands, and the heron's beak came down onto them.

His claws only barely got to her skin.

They came through the burned rags round her palms and through the palms, and the tips of the long red nails got past the crossed hands and touched her coat, and parted it, and parted the skin under it, a small rip as long as a thumb over the bottom rib on the left, and the blood came out of it, red. And then the hand stopped. It stopped as if it had come to the end of a chain.

[soft, from the body's mouth, in a voice that was the boy's and was under the boy's] "Cleave..."

Shōki's arm was cut off.

Naiser saw nothing between the pointing fingers and the arm. There was no blade. There was no motion. The two fingers were pointed, and then the new arm was in two pieces, cut through at the shoulder exactly where Tsugigane's Resolve had taken the first one, on the same line, as if the world had kept the mark of the first cut and only had to be reminded. The cut face was flat and clean and gold at its edges. The arm dropped away from him with the hand still bunched, and struck the clay, and the heat went out of it at once, and it lay there in the road beside Kiyome's boots going gray. Shōki's blood came out of the shoulder in a jet, red, ordinary, a man's blood with nothing hissing in it, and ENSHŌ did not seal it, because what had cut it carried no force at all for ENSHŌ to drink. Amelia dropped out of his other hand as his whole body jerked, and fell to her knees on the clay with her cut hands to her side.

They were gold. They were the bright gold that had beaten in both of them in the Hall, and more than that; the gold had no seam in it anymore, no ring, no pupil that Naiser could find, only a clean even burning gold from lid to lid like the little lights still falling out of the sky. The Kamigan. Naiser had no other name for it and did not need one; it was a word out of the oldest pages of the Moto, the eye of the god the Shingan was cut down from, and the old registers said no living Moto had carried it since the one who wrote them.

A blade appeared in the boy's right hand.

It was made of golden light. It came out of his closed fist the way a flame comes up out of a struck match, long, straight, single-edged, the length of Tsugigane and the shape of nothing a smith had ever made, and it gave off no heat. It hummed, low, under the drum.

[the voice settling, almost the boy's own] "Unlimited Works of Fate..."

The words went out over the road, and the gold lights coming down out of the sky stopped in the air and turned toward him, all of them, the way the goblin's droplets had once turned toward the carriage.

"...First Act. Infinite Cleave."

The space round them began to curve.

Naiser watched it and did not understand it, and the count in him tried to understand it and failed, and kept trying. The boy walked forward. One step across the ruts, the locked knee grinding in its splint. And the road bent toward him. The ruts in the clay, which ran straight up the road from the carriage to the wood, bent as Naiser watched them, curving in toward the boy's feet the way the lines on a sheet of paper curve when you lay it over a ball, and the cedar trunks at the wood's edge bowed toward him in their ranks, and the light of the gold sparks bent round him in arcs like light round a stone in a clear stream. An ant walking straight ahead across a ball will come back to where it began, and never once turn, because the ball is curved under it. Everything on the road that walked straight was walking toward the boy now.

Shōki tried to leave it. He went back off his heels on the soft clay, one long stride and then another, away from the boy and the gold blade, the stump of his shoulder pumping red down his side; and Naiser saw where his strides went. They went toward Muken. Every one. The Sin stepped straight back and the road was curved under him, and his straight step back came down a pace nearer the boy than the one before it.

There is an old saying among the Stannvaard natural philosophers that a thrown stone tries every road at once, and goes by the one that costs it least. Naiser had heard it once and laughed. He did not laugh now. The Kamigan saw the flaws in every possibility; the boy had said it of himself, or the old man had, and Naiser understood it now with his own eyes. Infinite Cleave cut along every road the Sin could take. Every one of them ended at the blade. And a man who has nowhere to step but onto an edge finds the whole world bent toward that edge.

Shōki stopped trying to leave. He set his feet. He drew in a breath, a bellows' breath, deep and long and wet with the bubbling of the torn lung, and the Covenant Scar flared through the X on his chest, and the one Hall Iron he had left on his left wrist went from dark to cherry to white, and the left fist drew back over his shoulder like a hammer at the top of its lift. GŌKA. Naiser saw the full beat of it, the tell every crew that had ever hurt him had read.

The boy walked into the beat. He did not hurry. He took the step the road gave him, and the Sin's fist came down from over the shoulder at the boy's face with the whole fury of the working packed into it, the fire that carries its own breath, and the road under the fist was curved. Muken walked past it.

He walked past the Sin. He did not cut him. He went to where Amelia knelt in the clay with her hands at her side, and bent, and took her up in his arms, the bound left arm and the right with the gold blade in it, and lifted her off the road as if she weighed nothing. He carried her across the road. The road curved to let him come, and the gold sparks parted round him, and he set her down on the frozen clay near the carriage wheel, near Naiser, near Hiromi and Senna where they knelt, very gently, the way a man sets down a lamp, and straightened.

Naiser could not understand such power. He stood a pace from the boy with his own hands still shaking and his thread-marks still burning and looked into the gold eyes, and the count gave him nothing, and the record gave him only the one page. This must be the King of the North. The one the drovers had talked of all down the Kessen road, the boy who had walked into Kharven-Seat with nothing and walked out with the north. Such overwhelming manipulation of fate. There was no doubt he was the son of Gimbzo. The Kōkan King had fought like a forest fire, and the son had his father's gold coming up through his eyes.

The boy turned back to the Sin.

Shōki was on one knee in the boiled pit, the white fire of his own GŌKA still bubbling in the clay round his fist, the stump pumping, his long body shaking. The bank had gone into the floor of the road. The cool turn was on him, the turn every crew had taken, and he knelt in it.

[quiet, even, the boy's voice almost entire] "Unlimited Works of Fate... Saba no Rosa..."

Muken placed his hands together. The gold blade went out of his fist like a candle snuffed, and his two hands came together in front of his chest, palm to palm, the broken left forearm grinding in its cords as it came, and the sky grew roots from above.

They came down out of the gray. They came down through the falling gold the way the roots of a cedar come down out of a cliff above a river, thick and pale and many, branching as they came, and on them, as they came, roses opened. Small dark red roses, the color of old blood, and between them thorns as long as fingers. Each rose was a deposit. Naiser read them as they opened, because the Itomi could not help reading them: each one a thing out of the Sin's own history, a morning, a block, a black floor, three hundred and fifty years of men whose names he had entered, and each thorn beside each rose was the debt on it, the thing still owed. There were hundreds. The roots came down the whole sky.

Shōki saw them come and tried to dodge them. He came up out of the pit off his left foot and went sideways, low and fast, the way he went across a yard at a crew, the long body stretched out along the line of the dodge; but the road was still curved under him, and his line went where every line on that road went, and the roots came down on it. It was to no avail. They took him round the waist first, and then the left arm at the wrist where the last Iron was, and then the throat, and the thighs, and they tied themselves round him, and the thorns went into him through the cracked harness everywhere they touched, and the roses pressed against his skin and opened wider.

[a choked roar, then quieter, a judge's voice gone ragged] "Release me. I am owed... I am owed..."

[low, steady, every word set on the last] "No... I won't kill you..."

The Sin went still in the roots.

"I'll free you from this endless torment." The boy stood in the curved road with his hands together and his gold eyes on the man in the roots, and the bell in his voice was felt in the teeth before it was heard. "I will not continue the ruthlessness of my father..."

Saba began to pull. Naiser saw it on the Itomi and saw it in the roots. The sickness came out of the Sin into the threads the way poison comes out of a wound into a poultice: the fever went out of his face first, the burning scars across his brow and cheek going from cherry to pink to the white of old scars; then the heat, the whole long bank of it, drawn up the roots in a slow red tide and spread out along them into the gray sky and thinned there to nothing; then the Sin itself, the law he had made of himself at the block, *what is done to me is never finished*, which the roses took up out of him one deposit at a time, each rose closing as it took its share and going pale. Every morning in the record stayed true, and Naiser understood that the boy knew it too. Only the Sin's present claim on them, the long grip of his standing on that one black floor, was being thinned out of him into the threads, the way a single spoon of salt goes into a river and is never tasted again.

[the voice rising for the last of it] "Unlimited Works of Fate... Cleanse..."

The roots went white.

From the sky down to the Sin they went white, all of them at once, the color of the near-white line, of fresh snow, of Kiyome Byakuya's hair lying across the ruts. And Shōki's very soul, and Malakar's binding blessing on it, began to fracture away.

Naiser saw the blessing. He had not seen it until it began to come off. It lay over the Sin's Crystal like a black lacquer over a cracked bowl, a seal pressed onto him by another hand, very old and very heavy, and where the white roots touched it the lacquer crazed and lifted in flakes like old paint off a door. It came away from the Crystal in pieces. The pieces went out along the roots, up into the threads, and out across the whole Plane of Fate in every direction, thinner and thinner, the way a drop of ink let fall into a still lake spreads out from where it struck in a dark cloud, and paler, and wider, until it is everywhere in the lake and the lake is clear. The blessing was diluted across the Plane of Fate and pulled into an abstract nothingness. The Sin of Wrath went with it.

Shōki's physical body disrupted.

It went out of the roots the way the arm had gone in the Hall, all at once, but gently: the long hollow face and the black braids and the cracked red harness and the bare arm and the stump all coming apart together into gold, a fine gold dust that hung in the shape of a kneeling man for one breath among the white roots and then lifted, and drifted, and rose. Naiser watched the Sin's thread on the Itomi. It went up out of the dust into the threads, very thin, a single red line going up through the white, and somewhere far up in the gray, where the gold lights were still falling, another thread came down to meet it. It was the color of copper. It came down out of the sky the way a hand comes down to take another hand from a boat, and the two threads touched, and twisted round each other once, and went up together, and were gone.

His soul was reunited with his wife.

The roots drew up into the sky. The roses closed and fell, white, and lay on the road among the gold lights like petals after a wedding. The road straightened under them. The ruts ran straight again from the carriage to the wood, and the cedar trunks stood up in their ranks, and the light stopped bending, and the drum under the world went quiet.

The Sin of Wrath was defeated. His wrath was cleansed.

V · The Account

*The Kessen road; the gold lights going out one by one on the clay*

Muken fell to a knee and threw up blood.

The gold went out of his eyes the way a lamp goes out when the oil is gone, and the boy came back into his body all at once, and the body was what Shōki had left of it. [flat, steady, a Shieldwarden's report, the voice cracking on the last word] "Muken... I am sorry. I couldn't protect her."

Muken looked back.

He looked over his shoulder at Kiyome's dead body lying across the ruts where the Sin had laid her out for him, on her back, with her arms at her sides and her coat straightened over the hole in her chest, and the gold lights lying on her, going out one by one. Of course he had just got to know her. Naiser saw it go across his face. Six months, the woman had stood beside his chair. One morning in a Hall of seven lines she had said a thing he would hear for the rest of his life, and laced him into a harness in the old order with her own hands, and gone ahead of him down the north road. He looked at her for a long time.

[hoarse, thick, low] "It isn't your fault." He turned back to Amelia, and took one of her burned hands in his skinned one. "It's nobody's fault. Whoever masterminded this whole thing, they're the ones to blame. Our true enemy has yet to show his face..."

He looked down at the blood on the road between them. Then he looked up, past Amelia, at nothing Naiser could see.

[very low, every word set down like a stone on a cairn] "I shall have my vengeance..."

The lamps on the carriage swung on their chains, all together, though there was no wind.

Naiser looked at the two of them. At the Greymane girl on her knees in the clay with her burned hands, and the boy who was a king with the blood on his chin, and the woman lying in the ruts behind them, and the goblin sitting dead against the cedar with his Crystal at his feet. He had seen the slope above the river. He had turned round on it and counted. He knew what the boy's face was doing, because he had felt his own do it, and he knew where it went if nobody stood beside it.

He let his hands stop shaking.

[low, measured, formal Japanese accent, kind] "Well... it certainly looks like we are up against a nefarious force." He came across the clay to them at his walk, unhurried. "I will call for the Zettari, and for my sister, to see to their situation. Kushara will want to know a Sin has walked out onto a southern road. My sister keeps the record of every birth and death in our valleys, and she will want to know about the dead." He crouched down on his heels in front of them, so that his face was level with the boy's. "But for now, allow me to assist you, Lord of the North. My house's medics will see to you. You are badly beaten." He turned his head toward the ruts. "And I will help you with her body. I am sorry for your loss. Truly."

THE ENTERED WORD took it as he said it. Naiser felt it set. It set in the air between them the way a lacquer sets, plain and whole, and the boy heard it set; Naiser saw him hear it, the gray eyes coming up to his face and staying there, the way a man looks at a bridge he has been told will hold.

[rough, very quiet] "...Thank you."

On the carriage step Senna had got to her feet. She stood with her arm in its sling and her face white, looking at her father as if she had never seen him before, and behind her Hiromi sat in the doorway in Dari's lap with the fox-woman's arms round him and nine white tails round both of them, staring at the boy in the road with his mouth open. Neither of them said anything. Naiser did not ask them to.

He went to Kiyome Byakuya.

He knelt beside her in the ruts. Up close her face under the white hair was very calm, the way the faces of the dead are calm when the death came fast, and her eyes were open, the silver rings in them lying flat as winter ice. He closed them with two fingers. Then he looked at her sword, because a Yukari looks at everything, and the Lambert half of him had already started a column.

Her katana lay at her hip in its scabbard, undrawn. Three hundred and twelve years old, by Borin Ironheart; even the Kessen drovers knew that. And round the guard where it met the scabbard's mouth, where the White Line tie their oath to the blade with three quick turns of white cord, the cord was cut.

He bent closer. It had been cut clean, every thread of it parted at the same place on the same plane, the ends square and white and unburned. Nothing on the whole road had been near a fire like the Sin's and kept its ends white. The Sin had burned everything he touched. The Sin had read chests and broken irons and folded his own hell like a page, and he had never once in three hundred years been a man who knew what a white knot on a Byakuya guard was for, or troubled to cut it before he killed.

Somebody had cut the cord first. Somebody had known.

Naiser knelt in the ruts with the cut end of the white cord between his finger and his thumb and the boy's vow still hanging in the air behind him like smoke that would not drift, and the gold lights went out on the clay round him one by one.

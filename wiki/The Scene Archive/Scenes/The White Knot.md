## The Cedar Enclave · The Moto Arrive

---

They came in a day and a half.

The crows had said three days. The crows had been counting by the western road, the way any sane rider would come, down out of the shield and across the low country and up the long valley through the villages. The Moto did not come by the western road. They came out of the forest on the north side of the enclave, by a path through the cedars that Naevra had walked a hundred times as a girl and had never once seen a horse on, a deer track over a shoulder of the hill that ended in a gully nobody used. Twenty riders in plain grey cloaks, with no banners, picking their way down the gully in single file in the blue hour before dawn. The wards had felt them coming an hour before the sentries saw them. By the time the house woke, they were already standing at the north gate with their horses' heads down and steaming, waiting to be let in.

*Hayato's roads,* Naevra thought, when the runner brought her the word. *He found a way through the hills that we didn't know we had.*

She was not sure whether that frightened her or not. She decided, on the way to the gate, that it ought to.

---

The north gate was the smallest of the enclave's four: two posts of dark cedar and a crossbeam carved with the crow, and a pair of plain wooden doors, and beyond them a stretch of trampled snow where the gully came out of the trees. The house-guard had opened the doors and stood back from them, as Naiser had ordered, and Naevra walked out through them alone, in grey, barefoot on the cold boards of the threshold, with her white hair in its plait and nothing in her hands.

The wards rose round her as she stepped through. She felt them reach out across the snow and touch the riders, and weigh them, and settle. Caution, very deep. Exhaustion. Grief, in some of them, old and worn smooth. And under all of it, in the one at the front, something so still and so clean that the wards went over it like water over glass and found nowhere at all to catch.

The one at the front had already dismounted. She stood a little ahead of the others, in the trampled snow, with her horse's rein looped over one arm.

She was tall. As tall as Naevra, nearly, and as pale, so that standing face to face on either side of the threshold they might have been the same white figure and its reflection in dark water. Her travelling clothes were near-white, layered, plain, the white of fresh linen and not of silk, stained grey at the hem from the road. The glass veil of her line was pinned back from her face and the beads of it hung at her temples, and they clicked very faintly when she moved her head. At the crown of her head the halo-ring of pale metal caught the first grey of the dawn. The sheen of the Moto blood lay faint as frost along her brow and at her temples, and her eyes were grey, light grey, the grey of rain on a window, with fine silver rings in them that did not turn at all, but lay perfectly still, the way the surface of a well lies still.

At her left hip, in a white scabbard bound with white cord, she wore a katana.

Behind her, still in the saddle, a huge bald man in a grey cloak with copper showing at the cuffs. And beside him, already off his horse and standing very close to it, a slight sharp man with restless fingers who kept glancing back up the gully the way they had come.

Naevra looked at the woman in white for a long moment.

"Kiyome," she said.

The silver rings in Kiyome Byakuya's eyes moved, once, very slightly, and were still again.

"Naevra."

They stood there on either side of the threshold, in the cold, and neither of them moved.

"We haven't met," said Naevra, "since we were children."

She heard her own voice say it and was surprised by how level it sounded. She was not level. There was a summer in her, behind her breastbone, that she had not thought about in twenty years, and it had come up all at once at the sight of that face, whole and bright and unbearable, the way a smell will bring a whole room back. A white house in the old city below the Moto hold. A garden of white gravel with glass hung in the trees, chiming in every wind. A long pale prayer room with the shutters open. Two girls of nine, one dark-browed Yukari and one white Byakuya, sent to each other for a season as a gesture between houses, the way houses did in those days when they still sent their children to one another as gestures, lying on their stomachs on the cool floor of that prayer room with their chins in their hands, swearing to be friends for ever. And the glass in the trees going on chiming over them, all that long white summer, the last summer before the war.

"No," said Kiyome. "We haven't."

She looked down at the threshold between them, at Naevra's bare feet on the cedar boards.

"The war came between us," she said. "Between our houses. Between everyone." She raised her eyes again. "I'm sorry, Naevra. I wasn't much of a friend to you, after. I never wrote. When your envoys came to my gate, I gave them tea and sent them home alive, and I told myself that was a kind of letter. It wasn't." Her voice did not change. "And now here we are again, after all of it, and it's not a summer and we're not nine. It's a political crisis on your doorstep with twenty riders behind it." She paused. "My presence here must trouble you. And your people. I know what it means for a Moto to come to this gate so soon after the war. I know what the Seikai did. I know what we did."

Naevra shook her head.

"You don't trouble me," she said.

It was true. She felt it, saying it. Whatever else was in her, it was not that.

"I know how delicate this is," she said. "I know exactly how delicate. I've been reading the threads of it for three days." She drew a breath. "But I have to ask you something. Before you come in. Before you sit at my brother's table, and before the Zettari hear it from anyone but us. I have to know, Kiyome, and I need you to tell me true, the way you'd have told me on that floor when we were nine."

Kiyome waited.

"Is Muken really Gimbzo's son?" Naevra said. "Gimbzo's son, and your elder sister's?"

The glass beads at Kiyome's temples clicked, once, very softly, as she nodded.

"He is," she said.

Behind her, the big man in the saddle shifted his weight, and his horse blew.

"He is," Kiyome said again. "Gimbzo's son, and my sister's. My sister Kasane." Her voice was very quiet now. "And they had a daughter too. Younger. Her name is Yukazuri."

The wind came down the gully out of the cedars and moved the plain grey cloaks of the riders and set the glass beads at Kiyome's temples chiming.

"Kasane was the elder of us by twelve years," Kiyome said. "She was the one who was sworn. The red line before the white. When the time came for a Byakuya to stand at the Kōkan king's side in the old way, it was Kasane they sent." She looked away, past Naevra, into the enclave, at the dark roofs and the cedars. "And the king she was sent to stand beside was Gimbzo. And when Gimbzo turned on his own line, and put the Kōkan into the ground one by one for telling him no, Kasane was the one standing closest to him. She couldn't stop it. Nobody could." She was silent for a moment. "What she was to him, by the end, I don't know. I've never been able to make myself ask. But there were two children."

Naevra did not speak.

"From what I know," Kiyome said, "my sister died a little after the war ended. She'd been forced to survive. For years. In places I never found her." Her voice caught, very slightly, and she waited until it was steady again. "I looked. I want you to know that. I looked for her for years. And in the end it was a Stark who found her. Yusif Stark. He'd ridden with Gimbzo once, and turned his horse round, and he went looking for what Gimbzo had left behind." She drew a breath. "He married her. My sister married a Stark. And when she died, it was Yusif Stark who raised her children."

She looked back at Naevra.

"That's all I know," she said. "That's everything. Some of it I've had from the Starks themselves, in the last ten days, by crows that shouldn't have been able to find me. Some of it I've had from Shigure Tenrai, in front of the whole council, without her knowing what she was telling me." Her grey eyes held Naevra's. "My sister's son took the Foot. My sister's son has the eye. And he doesn't know any of it. Not who his father was. Not who his mother was. Not that he has an aunt sitting in a white chair in the Moto hold who'd have given anything in the world to know he was alive."

---

Behind her, Hayato Amagiri took a step forward. His restless fingers had gone still. His narrow face had the look of a man who has been holding something in all night and can hold it no longer.

"Kiyome," he said. "Before we go in..."

Kiyome turned her head and looked at him.

It was a very small look. It did not last long. Naevra, standing in the threshold, felt it go past her like a cold draught from an opened door, and saw Hayato stop where he stood as if he had walked into a wall.

He did not say anything else. But Naevra saw his face, and read it as clearly as she would have read a page. *We don't have the strength for this,* it said. *We haven't got another war in us. Not one. Not the Amagiri, not the Akagane, not the Byakuya. If this goes wrong in there, if the Zettari take it badly, if Kagenori hears where we are before we're home, there's nothing left to fight it with.*

And Kiyome's face, turning back, said just as clearly that she knew. That she had never once thought otherwise. That she had not come here to fight anything.

She had come to keep a promise. To make sure that whatever happened in the world now, her sister's son would have friends in it. The Yukari. The Zettari, if they would have him. A door in the east that the Tenrai could not close. Unity between this house and the white one, and through it, perhaps, one day, between this house and all seven. Alliances for a boy who did not know he needed them.

*The red line before the white,* Naevra thought. *Until the end of the red line.*

She stepped back out of the threshold and turned, so that her shoulder was to the open gate.

"Then come," she said. "This way. My brother is waiting for you in the long hall." She paused. "And the Sovereign of Kushara with him. And the Crown Prince."

Hayato's face went a shade paler. The big bald man in the saddle let out a long breath through his nose and began, heavily, to dismount.

"Guide us, then," said Kiyome Byakuya.

Naevra hesitated, on the cedar boards. She did not need to say it. They knew. Every Moto in the Inner World knew. But it was the custom, and the custom was the house, and the house was hers to keep.

"We ask," she said, "that no weapon be drawn on our sacred ground."

Kiyome's hand went to the hilt of the katana at her hip.

She was the only one of the twenty who was armed. Naevra had felt it as they came through the wards: the rest of them had left their steel with the horses, and the big man had nothing at his belt but a knife for meat, and Hayato had nothing at all. Only Kiyome, at the front, with her white blade at her side.

She did not draw it. She took the white cord that bound the scabbard, and, with three quick turns of her long pale fingers, knotted it round the guard of the hilt and the mouth of the scabbard together, tight, so that the sword could not leave its sheath without the cord being cut. Then she let her hand fall away.

"The white knot," she said. "My grandmother taught it to me. A Byakuya ties it when she goes into a house where she means to keep the peace, whatever comes." She looked at Naevra. "You have no reason to worry about this, Naevra. None. I give you my word on it."

Naevra looked at the knot for a moment. Then she nodded, and turned, and led them in.

---

Naiser was standing when they came into the long hall.

He had not meant to stand. He had meant to receive them seated, behind the low table, the way he had received the Zettari. But when the screens at the north end of the hall slid back and his sister came through them with three Moto lords behind her, in their road-stained grey, and the cold of the north gate coming in with them, he found that he was on his feet, and could not quite remember getting there.

The Sovereign of Kushara and the Crown Prince were at the table. Naiser had seated them on the east side, in the places of honour. He did not look at them now. He looked at the three coming toward him down the long room, and the hall's own threads lay open to him as they always did in that room, and he read them.

Kiyome Byakuya first, white and straight and very still, with her silver rings lying quiet as well-water and her katana bound in a white knot at her hip. He had seen her once, years ago, across a field under a flag of truce. She had not changed. She had only grown more like herself.

Behind her, Tetsugen Akagane.

Naiser had met him too, once, in the war: a great bald bull of a man in copper, roaring at the head of a column on the Sennen road while a Zettari border town burned behind him. He had grown. Not in size; he could hardly have grown in size. In something else. He walked heavily down the long room with his huge copper-ringed hands loose at his sides and his eyes on the floor, and the threads Naiser read in him now belonged to a man who had opened his hands and looked at the palms and found something written there he could not unread. *The Fox,* Naiser thought, *who could make a whole province happy, and had nothing left for his own table.* The Fox had come a long way to sit at this one.

And Hayato Amagiri, slight and sharp and very pale, at the back.

Naiser had known Hayato as a boy. Before the war, at one of the great gatherings of houses that nobody held any more, a thin quick child with ink on his fingers who had spent the whole of a three-day feast drawing maps of the hall's corridors on the backs of the menus. He had grown into his face. He had not grown out of his fingers.

"It's been a long time," Naiser said. "Hayato Amagiri."

Hayato stopped. For a moment his narrow face did something complicated. Then he bowed his head, low, and held it.

"You honour me by remembering, Lord Naiser," he said. "It has been a very long time." He straightened. "I'm only sorry it's under such circumstances as these that we meet again."

"So am I," said Naiser. "But we meet. That's more than most."

Tetsugen Akagane had stopped beside the table. He stood there a moment, looking down at the two Zettari seated in the places of honour on the east side, the Sovereign of Kushara and the Crown Prince. Something went across his heavy face that might have been a great many things.

Then the Lord of the Akagane bent his bald head toward them. It was a deep bow. Deeper, Naiser thought, than a lord of the copper line had bent to a Zettari in forty years. He held it a long time. He did not say anything at all.

Kiyome Byakuya came to stand beside him.

She looked at the Sovereign, and at the Prince, steadily, one after the other. Then she inclined her head to them, not so deep as Tetsugen, but with great care, so that the glass beads at her temples hung perfectly still.

"Sovereign," she said. "Prince Dougou."

Her voice was clear and calm and carried down the long room without effort.

"I'm glad you're still here," she said. "I want you to know that. I want it to be the first thing I say in this house, so that nobody mistakes anything I say after it. When the crows told us the Zettari were sitting at the Yukari's table, Lord Hayato thought we ought to turn back. I didn't." She drew a breath. "We haven't come here to continue the bloodshed between our houses. And we haven't come to stop you allying with the Yukari, or to bargain against you, or to ask Lord Naiser to choose between us. We haven't come to ask you for anything at all."

She paused.

"We're tired," said Kiyome Byakuya. "All three of our lines. We're tired of the fighting. Our people are dying. Not on any field. In their beds. Of famine, because Kagenori takes the grain for the house-guard. Of sickness, because the springs in the south have gone bad since the war and nobody will say why, and our physicians don't know what they're treating. Of cold, because the roads are closed and there's no oil." Her hands lay still at her sides, near the white knot. "There are villages under the Byakuya that have buried a third of their children since the spring. I've walked through them. I've sat in the houses." She held the Sovereign's eyes. "Whatever my house did to yours in forty years, Sovereign, and I know a great deal of what it did, the people who are paying for it now are the ones who never chose any of it."

She was quiet for a moment.

"That's why we're here," she said. "That's all. Because someone has to be the first to say *enough*. And I'd rather say it here, in front of you, with the Yukari for witness, than in the Hall of Verdicts, where Kagenori would have my head on the script for it before the words were cold."

She stood beside Tetsugen Akagane, with the white knot bound tight at her hip, and waited for the Sovereign of Kushara to answer.

**Notes**

- **Shaka-Al and Dougou are never written.** They sit in the places of honour on the east side, as Naiser seated them, and get no lines, actions, thoughts or reactions. Tetsugen's deep bow and Kiyome's address are aimed at them, and the post stops on Kiyome waiting for Shaka-Al.
- **Your beats, kept in order.**
  - The Moto arrive in a day and a half, with Kiyome at the front to speak for the terms.
  - Naevra and Kiyome, childhood best friends: "We haven't met since we were children," Kiyome's apology for being a poor friend, "My presence here must trouble you," and Naevra's "You don't trouble me... I know how delicate."
  - Naevra's question: is Muken the son of Gimbzo and Kiyome's elder sister. Kiyome confirms it, names the daughter Yukazuri, says her sister died a little after the war and had been forced to survive, and that she married Yusif Stark, who raised them.
  - Hayato starts to speak and Kiyome stops him with a look. His face says they can't fight another war; hers says she came for her nephew's protection and alliances.
  - "Guide us then." The no-weapons reminder, Kiyome's katana, "You've no reason to worry."
  - Naiser marks how the two lords have grown, especially the Fox. "It's been a long time, Hayato Amagiri," and Hayato's reply.
  - Tetsugen's bow to the Sovereign and the Prince.
  - The closing speech: glad the Zettari are here, not here for bloodshed or to stop the alliance, tired of fighting, people dying of famine and illness.
- **Two speakers read for meaning.**
  - The closing speech ("I am glad the Zettari are still here...") I gave to **Kiyome**, since she speaks for the terms. Tetsugen gives the bow.
  - **The Fox** I took as Tetsugen, because the Akagane emblem is the Fox in canon, as in the reading-stone scene.
- **The big retcon, and what it breaks.** Muken and Yukazuri are Gimbzo's children by **Kiyome's elder sister**, a Byakuya, so Gimbzo himself was the Kōkan king who purged his own line. I kept the name **Kasane** for the sister, since she's the same mother. This contradicts *The Uncounted* Chapters One to Five in three places:
  - **Kasane's line.** Chapter One has her as a Kōkan woman with no name the clerks would take, cutting salt for Orrel in Halcrest.
  - **Yusif.** Chapters Three and Four have him visiting the cuttings once a season, not married to her.
  - **Kasane's death.** Kiyome says she died "a little after the war," and Chapter One has her dying of salt-throat when the children were small.
  - The Chapter Five tent scene and Shigure's cellar memory also have Gimbzo *taking* her.
  - I kept Kiyome's account explicitly secondhand ("From what I know," pieced together from Stark crows and Shigure), so either version can be the truth. Tell me which and I'll bring the Muken chapters into line: Kasane as Byakuya, Yusif as stepfather, and her dying when the children were young.
- **Originated here (yours to keep or strike):**
  - Hayato's hidden track over the hill, which brought them in a day and a half by the north gate.
  - The last summer before the war: two nine-year-old girls swapped between houses, the glass chiming in the white garden.
  - Kasane, twelve years Kiyome's elder, sworn to stand at the Kōkan king's side, and the king was Gimbzo.
  - **The white knot**: Kiyome binds her hilt to its scabbard with white cord, a Byakuya peace-keeping custom.
  - Byakuya villages that have buried a third of their children since spring.
- **Tense.** Past, to match the thread.

*The bottom of the Pit, under the red welkin, the hour hell has no name for*

The chains that held Shōki to his resting place began to dissolve.

They had been beautiful once, in the way the Hall's ironwork was always beautiful: black links as thick as a man's wrist, each one cut with a line of verdict script and inlaid in thread-thin brass that still caught the light of the cinnabar river running past the foot of his slab. They went from his collarbones to iron rings driven into the obsidian on either side, and from his hips to the rock under him, and from the fused cuffs on his wrists down into the stone itself, and for longer than he could count they had held. Now the brass was running. It went soft first, the way solder goes soft at the iron's touch, and then it slid out of the cuts in bright yellow threads and dripped onto the black glass and hissed there. The script lost its edges. The links sagged, link into link, until the chain across his chest was no longer a chain at all, only a long glowing smear of iron with the shape of a chain still faintly in it, like a word written in wet sand after the first wave.

He did not move. He lay with his face to the welkin and watched it come apart.

Hell was not dark. Nobody who had been there would have called it dark. The sky over the Pit was the red of a forge mouth from edge to edge, banded higher up with sulphur-yellow and a green so bright it hurt, where the vents breathed into it, and the cinnabar river at the foot of the slab threw rose light up the wet black cliffs in sheets that moved as the current moved. Somewhere out in the glare a basalt column cracked with a report like a gun and a pillar of steam went up white and hung. The air shook with the low continuous roar of the vents, so constant that the ear stopped hearing it and only the teeth went on knowing it was there. It smelled of sulphur and hot stone and, faintly, of incense, which he had never been able to explain.

The memory came the way it always came, whole and exact and without any warning, a stain that no amount of heat had ever burned out.

The black floor of the Hall of Verdicts. The shaft of grey daylight coming down through the round opening in the roof. The seven chairs in their colours round the circle of old script, and his own wrists heavy in the irons, and Tsuyu kneeling in the place under the light with her hair pinned up off her neck the way the Kurenai require, because he had pinned it himself that morning, before he knew. Jinsuke's sandals on the stone. The crimson inlay down the spine of the blade. The sound it made, which was a sound he had made himself a thousand times, and had never once heard.

He remembered all of it. He remembered the pin he had used, a plain copper pin from her own box, and the smell of the camphor in her collar, and that she had not looked at him at the end because she had known that if she looked at him he would try something and they would kill him too. He remembered it the way the Kurenai register remembers a sentence: completely, in order, with nothing left out.

What he could not do was feel it. He had given the feeling away on that floor, to break the irons. Where the love had been there was a place, and the place was hot, and into the heat, whenever the memory came, everything else poured in to fill it.

Sorrow came, first, because sorrow always came first, a slow cold weight that settled behind the breastbone. Then, absurdly, happiness: the bright little shock of her laughing at his fevers, her palm on his forehead, *you are cooking in your own skin, husband, lie down before you set the bedding on fire.* Then the regret, which had teeth. Then dread, which he had not felt since he was nineteen and the Kurenai carved the vow into his shoulder, dread at what he was, at what was coming up out of him even now. They went into the heat together and they did not stay separate. They boiled. They rendered down, the way a hide renders in a vat, until there was no telling which had been which, and what was left at the bottom was one thing, thick and dark and roaring.

*So this is what it is,* he thought, lying there with the chains melting off him. *It was never one feeling. The Hall taught us to name wrath like a crime, one word in the register. It is everything a man is made of, boiled down until it can only do one thing.*

Calamity. That was the word that came. He let it stay.

The fire started at his back.

He felt it light in the hollow between his shoulder blades, where the Kurenai had carved the first line of the Covenant Scar, and it came up through the skin in a flame of incandescent purple, so bright the black glass under him lit violet for a yard in every direction. It ran outward across the great slabs of muscle on either side of his spine, down the long lats, up over the knotted mountain of the trapezius and into the back of his neck, and under the purple the skin crazed the way glaze crazes on a fired pot, a fine network of cracks all over him with cherry-red ember light showing down in every one. The last of the chain across his chest went to vapour with a sigh. The smell of hot iron filled the air.

He leaned up.

It was slow, because he had lain there a long time, and it was enormous, because he was enormous: the belly cording into hard blocks as he sat, the shoulders coming up off the stone like a ridge coming up out of the sea, the white hair lifting off his skull in the updraft of his own heat and falling over the right side of his face, over the socket where the eye had cooked out three hundred years ago. The cuffs on his wrists rang against the slab. Three links of chain on the left one dragged and clinked. His hakama, black once, scorched to dusk-blue and violet at the folds, stirred around his legs although nothing in the Pit was blowing, and the crimson lining showed at the inner pleats, and the thirteen iron bells on their cords along the side ties gave one small dull ring together.

"Oooooooh," said a voice above him. "Look at *that.*"

Shōki turned his head.

There was a goblin lying in the air.

Not floating, which a practitioner might do with Vectoria and a great deal of bad temper. Lying. He was stretched out on his back about ten feet up and a few yards out over the river, one knee crooked, one arm folded behind his head, as comfortable as a man in a hammock, and the air under him had a faint gleam to it, a slick glassy sheen, as though he were resting on a pane of something clear and very thick that had been poured there and left to set. Steam from the river came up against the underside of it and spread out and slowed and hung there in curls that did not move.

He was small even for his kind, half Shōki's height at a guess, and the demonhood had been unkind and generous with him at once. His skin was the grey-green of a pond in shade, going to polished obsidian at the knuckles and up the backs of the fingers, and the fingers had become claws, long and black and lacquered, filed into neat points, and he was buffing one of them on the fur collar of his coat as he talked. The coat was a wonderful thing, plum-coloured velour three sizes too big, with gold frogging down the front and a collar of some long black fur that swallowed his shoulders, and the cuffs were rolled four times to find his hands. Under it a green silk shirt was open to the breastbone. Two short horns, black and ridged like a ram's, came out of his brow just above the hairline and curled back, and between them he had arranged his hair, which was lank and black and plainly cared for, into a sweeping oiled pompadour that he touched every few seconds to make sure it was still there. His ears were long and notched. His eyes were the yellow of old lamp oil, heavy-lidded, half shut. When he grinned there was a gold cap on one canine.

Across the bridge of his nose, where another goblin would have had a scar, he had painted a little black beauty mark.

"So this is the Wrath," he said, and the vowels went on for a long time in his mouth, held long the way his people hold a word they want you to remember. "The feeling of ruuuuin. Aaaall the other Sins talked about you, you know. Big man. Big fire. Never stops. Ouuuuu. How *innnnteresting.*" He propped himself up on one elbow on nothing and looked Shōki over, slowly, top to bottom. "Less hair than I heard. Good shoulders, though. I'd say you're the second best-lookin' thing down here, if anybody asked."

Shōki said nothing.

His left eye did what the Kurenai eye always did. The ring-lines came up in the iris and the red capillary light flooded in behind them, and the Reigan went past the plum velour and the silk and the grey-green skin and into the chest, to the Attraction Layer, where every killing a soul has ever done is written into it as scar, layer on layer, the record that does not burn. It read a goblin's chest the way it read every chest. It looked for the count.

There was no count.

Nothing. Not one scar. The Attraction Layer of the creature lying on the air in front of him was as smooth and clean as a child's slate, the cleanest record he had read in three hundred years, and something in Shōki's chest that had already begun to draw breath for the fire stopped and did not know what to do.

"Ah, you're doin' the eye thing," said the goblin, pleased. "Heard about that too. Lemme save you the trouble, handsome. It's zero. I never killed nobody in my life." He held up one black claw. "Too much *work.*"

"Nought," Shōki said. His voice came out of him for the first time in longer than he could count, and it came out like a door opening in a furnace, low and rough and hot. "Entered."

"Ooh, he talks."

"Who sent you."

"Now, see, that's rude. You don't wanna know my name first? Everybody always wants to know my name first." The goblin sat up properly, crossing his legs on the air, and spread his clawed hands as if presenting a prize. "Vaaaashuuk. Vaashuuk the Handsome, Vaashuuk Snorr-Khal, the Sin of Sloth, at your service, though not, like, *too* much service. Every goblin woman from here to the Expanse wants me, by the way. That's not a brag. It's just a fact you should have." He touched the pompadour. "Hold the first vowel when you say it. Long. Like you mean it."

Shōki stood up.

It took a moment. His legs had been under the chain a long time, and the stone of the slab had grown up a little around his heels like a tide pool's lip around a rock, and it cracked away from his boots with a sound like a plate breaking. When he was on his feet the purple fire had covered him to the waist. It rolled up off his skin in sheets and turned in the air above his shoulders and went upward in a column, violet at the core and edged in the cherry-red of the cracks, and the heat came off it in a wall. The steam over the river, that the goblin's glass had been holding in its patient curls, began to shiver.

The anger came up in him the way it always did now, not as a thought but as a pressure, everything that had rendered down at the bottom of him rising through the cracks at once. Three hundred years chained to a slab. A goblin with a painted face lying on the air above his grave, talking. Somebody, somewhere, who had sent this thing to fetch him like a dog. He had no count against the creature, and no blow of its own banked against it, nothing for his grudge to grip, and the absence of a reason did not slow the anger at all. It only made it stranger, and worse.

The flame swirled down off his body and into the ground. It ran out of him across the black glass in a ring, purple, low, quick, and the obsidian under it went from black to red to a dull sullen orange, and the slab under his feet groaned.

He lifted his foot and drove it down.

The stamp went into the slab to the ankle. The obsidian did not crack; it was too hot to crack, and it splashed instead, a slow bright wave of half-melted glass that rolled out from his heel and broke against the chain-rings. With the same motion he brought his right arm up, the fused cuff glowing white around the wrist, and his hand opened, and kept opening.

The fingers lengthened and hooked. The knuckles split along their seams and the skin between them peeled back, and what came through was scale, black and edged in violet light, and the palm folded back on itself into an upper jaw and a lower one, and the fingers became teeth, a row of them, long and curved and white-hot at the points, and at the back of the throat where his wrist had been there was a furnace. It had grown in him in the dark, this shape; the First Release had found a new face while he lay on the slab, and it was a dragon's mouth on the end of his arm, the old crown beast of the Moto, the one the Kurenai had never been permitted to wear.

The maw opened. The heat came out of it.

It went across the ten feet of air toward the goblin as a wave, a scorching front of purple-white with the air behind it boiling, the cinnabar river under it flashing to red steam all down its length, and it should have taken the goblin and his velour and his pompadour and turned the lot to a smear of soot on the far cliff before Vaashuuk had finished closing his mouth.

Vaashuuk yawned.

It was an enormous yawn, eyes screwed shut, jaw cracking, one claw politely over the mouth, and as he yawned, the world went thick.

Shōki felt it before he understood it. It was like walking off the bottom of a river into deep water, except there was no water: the air itself took weight and drag and clung to him. The heat wave from the maw kept going, but it went slowly, and then more slowly, its leading edge crawling across the air like syrup creeping across a cold plate, the purple-white of it smearing and stretching, the boiling turbulence behind it slowing to great lazy rolling folds that turned over once every few heartbeats. The steam coming up off the river stopped rising and simply hung. A drop of molten glass that had flown up from his stamp sat in the air beside his knee, round and glowing, falling at the speed a feather falls in a still room.

He tried to close the maw, and his arm did not answer.

It was answering. That was the worst of it. He could feel the order go down from the Crystal, the will, clean and fast, and he could feel the nerves take it and start to carry it, and he could feel it crawling. In a man a signal goes from the shoulder to the fingers in a hundredth of a breath. This was crawling down his arm like a slow fuse, cell to cell, and the muscle at the end of it, when the signal got there, began to contract the way a muscle contracts in a dream, with all of its strength and none of its speed. His mind ran at the pace of his soul. His body ran at the pace of tar.

"Slooow your roll," said Vaashuuk.

He was not slowed at all. He stood up on his pane of glassy air, stretched until something in his back popped, and strolled down it toward Shōki as if down a staircase, and the air hardened under each of his boots to take him and let him go. He walked straight through the leading edge of the heat wave where it hung, and it parted around him thick and slow as cold honey parts around a spoon, and his coat did not even smoke.

"You think I'm just gonna let ya kill me? Me?" He stopped an arm's length from the dragon's mouth, at its own eye level, and peered down into the furnace at the back of the throat with interest. "Look at this. Lookit the *teeth* on it. You grow this down here? Lyin' around for three hundred years? Ooh, I respect that. I respect a man who does his growin' horizontal."

*Hypnather,* Shōki thought. The name came up out of the Kurenai's training, out of the long winter nights in the Hall's library with the Wellspring registers. *The Sleep Crown. A glass is a liquid that stopped moving before it finished arranging itself.* The goblin was not stopping anything. He was making it all thick. Everything in the field, the air, the steam, the fire, the salts that carried the signal down a nerve, he was driving toward the place where a liquid turns to glass, where the flow goes so slow that for any purpose that matters it has stopped.

"You figurin' it out? Good. I love it when they figure it out. Saves me explainin', and explainin' is *exhausting.*" Vaashuuk sat down on the hanging air in front of him, cross-legged, at his ease. "You ever see the pitch-drip at the philosophers' college in Stannvaard? Big glass funnel. Lump of pitch in it, like a cake. They poured it in a hundred and some years back and they been waitin' on it ever since. One drop every ten winters. Every ten winters some old fart in a robe sits up all night to watch it fall and he falls asleep, every time, and misses it." He grinned his gold grin. "That's you now. That's you, handsome. You're the pitch. Everythin's a liquid if you give it long enough. I just make it real, *real* thick."

Shōki strained.

The cuff on his left wrist was rising, an inch, two inches, as he poured everything he had into the arm. He could feel the Crystal opening behind his breastbone to feed it, the reserve going out of him in a rush that should have thrown him across the Pit, and all of it hitting the thickened world and going nowhere, every ounce of force he put out met by a drag that grew the harder he pushed, like a fist punched into deep mud. The harder the blow, the more it was refused. That was how this kind of thickness worked. A slow push it let through. A fast one it fought.

"Yeah, no, don't do that," said Vaashuuk, watching the cuff come up with his head on one side. "The harder you swing, the thicker it gets. You're just gonna wear yourself out. And then I gotta carry you, and I *do not* carry." He picked at his gold tooth with a claw. "You know how many stairs there are outta this hole? I counted once. Took me a week. Never again."

"You will carry nothing," Shōki said. The words came out of his slowed jaw dragging and low, a long groan with the shape of speech in it. "You will burn."

"Will I, though? Will I?" Vaashuuk spread his arms. "I'm fireproof, handsome. You're fire. And your fire's goin' a mile an hour." He leaned in, and for the first time the heavy lids came up a little off the lamp-oil eyes, and there was something in them that was not lazy at all. "Lemme give you a little speech, 'cause you're a captive audience. Get it? Captive."

He waited. Shōki did not laugh. Vaashuuk sighed.

"Tough crowd. Okay. Here's the thing. There's seven of us, right, give or take, the Sins, and we all came out of somethin' bad, same as you. And the Captain, the Captain's been watchin' your hole for a long time, waitin' for the chains to go. 'Cause he knew they would. Nothin' holds forever. Not irons, not oaths, not nothin'." He tapped his own temple. "Not even me, and I hold stuff *real* good. So the Captain says to me, Vaashuuk, my handsome boy, the Wrath's wakin' up, go down there and bring him to me." He held up one claw. "Alive. He was very specific about the alive. I said, Captain, why me, it's far, and he said, 'cause you're the only one of us the Wrath can't hit."

The ring-lines in Shōki's eye flared. The capillary light went from red to near white.

"There it is," said Vaashuuk softly. "There's the fire. Ooh. You hate that I'm right."

He did. He hated it with everything the fire was made of. He hated the painted face and the velour and the gold tooth, and the clean slate of a chest that gave his reading nothing to hold, and the voice going on and on in its long-vowelled drawl, and under all of it the thing that the Hall had trained into him before it had ever trained anything else, the cold Kurenai voice that said: *the anger is not the verdict. The anger is in the way of the verdict. Read the thing. Read the law of the thing, and then close the book.*

So he read it.

The heat wave was still there. That was the first thing. The goblin had not put it out. He had slowed its flow, its spreading, its travel, but the heat itself was still hanging in the air in its great slow purple folds, and it was still hot. Heat was not travel. Heat was the shaking of the smallest pieces of a thing where they stood, and a glass can be as still as stone and its pieces still shake inside it, which is why glass in a fire gets hot, and why glass in a fire, past a certain heat, goes soft. Every glassblower in the north knew it. Pitch in a cold hall drops once in ten winters. Pitch in a pot over the coals pours like oil.

The thickness was a matter of temperature. It always was. And temperature was the one thing in the world Shōki Kurenai Moto had too much of.

*Do not strike,* he thought. *The field eats a strike. Feed it heat instead. Give it nothing to refuse.*

He stopped pushing the cuff.

Vaashuuk saw it, and frowned slightly, and touched his pompadour.

Shōki let the arm hang where it was, the dragon's mouth open in the air, and he turned the fire inward and downward instead of out. He did not throw it. He let it go out of him the way water goes out of a cracked jar: slowly, through every crack in his skin at once, through the soles of his boots into the half-melted slab, out through the bells and the cuffs and the scorched folds of the hakama, a slow pour of heat with nothing moving in it fast enough for the thickened world to catch. Everything he had banked in three hundred years of lying on the stone, every bit of heat he had never been able to spend, he opened the door on it.

And then, because the Kurenai do a thing completely or not at all, he called the Second Release.

The word took a long time to come out of his slow mouth. It came out as a groan that went on and on, rising, every syllable of it held, the way a goblin holds a vowel: "*Aaaaa... biiiii.*"

Without Interval.

The field opened around him in the dragging air and the colour of the Pit changed. The red of the welkin went to the orange of a kiln's mouth inside a ring a hundred and fifty yards across, and the edge of the ring hardened, not to the eye but to everything else, a boundary that heat could not cross. Nothing that was hot inside it could cool now. The great slow folds of the purple wave had nowhere to go. The steam had nowhere to go. Every ounce of heat that came out of him stayed, and added, and added, and the temperature inside the ring began to climb.

It climbed fast.

The obsidian under his feet went from orange to yellow. The cinnabar river at the edge of the slab stopped steaming and began, sullenly, to boil in place, the bubbles rising through it at the same slow drag as everything else and bursting in great lazy domes. The pane of glassy air Vaashuuk had been lying on began to sag in the middle like a hammock someone heavy had sat down in, and then to drip, long clear threads of something that had been still and was not still anymore, reaching down slowly toward the river.

And the thickness around Shōki's arm began, very slightly, to give.

He felt it in the nerve first. The signal that had been crawling down toward his fingers like a slow fuse picked up its pace, a little, then a little more, like a man walking out of a swamp onto firmer ground. The dragon's jaw that had hung open began to move. It closed, one tooth's width, then another.

The heat wave hanging in the air in front of him stirred, and the leading edge of it began to creep forward again toward the goblin, faster than it had been creeping, rolling over and over on itself.

Vaashuuk had stopped grinning.

He was sweating. A bead of it ran out of the pompadour and down his temple and across the painted beauty mark, and smeared it, and he did not notice. The plum velour of the coat had begun, faintly, along the hem, to smoke. He looked down at the dripping air under him, and up at the kiln-coloured welkin inside the ring, and at the ring itself out there in the glare, and last at Shōki, and the lamp-oil eyes were all the way open now.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's *smart.* That's real smart. I hate smart." He fanned himself with one claw, which did nothing. "Nobody told me you were smart. They told me you were big and loud and you hit stuff."

"I was a Kurenai," said Shōki. His voice came easier now. The words were short again, and hot, and they came out at nearly their right pace. "Before I was this."

"Yeah, well, the Captain left that part out." Vaashuuk stood up on his sagging pane of air, which promptly bowed under him and dropped him a foot, and he caught his balance with a yelp and a flail of velour sleeves. "Okay. Okay. Okay. Hot. It's hot. Real hot. My hair's gonna go. If my hair goes, handsome, I swear on my mother's..."

The heat wave reached the sleeve of his coat, still slow but no longer crawling, and the velour caught. It went up in a lick of purple flame along the cuff, and Vaashuuk slapped at it, and swore, long and trilling, in a tongue whose *r*s had no vowels in them at all.

Shōki's right hand, the dragon's mouth, came the last inch shut. The teeth met. Behind them the furnace in the throat roared, and for the first time since he had stood up the roar was at its proper pitch, and the column of fire over his shoulders went up straight and fast into the orange sky.

He turned his head and looked at the goblin, and his reading looked again at the clean slate of that chest, and found nothing on it to burn by. It did not matter. The anger had never needed a reason. It had only needed a way through.

He stepped forward, out of the glass the slab had grown around his heels, and the stone came away from his boots like wet clay.

Vaashuuk backed up three steps on the softening air and held up both claws, the right one still smoking.

"Alright," he said. "Alright, big man. You win this round. Plan B." He put two claws in his mouth, and between the claws, the yellow eyes on Shōki's, he began, very slowly, to yawn again, and this time the yawn went on and on, and the corners of his mouth went back past his cheeks, and back, and back, and the air inside the field, the hot, running, kiln-bright air, shivered, and began, all at once, to set.

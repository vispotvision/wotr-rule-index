*The cedar wood on the Kessen road, the second evening, the sun gone off the treetops and the fallen cedars burning*

---

Muken did not go.

He had meant to. His aunt's voice had gone into him in the old high register of the house, *the red before the white*, and something in his blood that was older than him had stood up and wanted to obey it the way a hound wants to obey a horn, and Hayato was at the bend of the road with his hand out. He saw all of it. He saw the road behind him going north, clean and white and safe, and his own feet on it.

And he saw what the Eye would not stop showing him. His aunt at the top of the bank with a broken spear-shaft in her hands and blood on her chin and the white of her harness blistered brown where the heat had faced her, and every crack in her lit gold, the shard-hole through the cheek, the split nails, the old hairline in her Crystal that ran from the Verdict of Six. The copper lord on one knee at the foot of the bank, sixty-one years old in a minute, gray to the roots, with the bone of his wrist showing white where he had cut his own arm open, and a rib broken, and the gold in his ribcage gathering round the break like water round a stone. Two people who had been in the room with him every day for a month, who had knelt to him, who he had thought were an old lady's sister and a lord who did not like him. The Eye showed him where they were going to break. It was soon.

He had walked away from the stair at Halcrest once. He had been nine and he had not known. He was not nine.

"The back of his neck," he said.

He did not shout it. It came out of him low and slow the way it did now when he meant a thing, each word set on the last like stones on a cairn, and the lamps were not there to tremble but the steam did: it went still round him in a ring, and the burning needles drifting down out of the cedars hung for a moment in the air, and every person on the road and the bank heard it as if he had said it in their ear.

"Right at the top of the spine. Where the block was." The gold line ran out of his eye across the steam, and he held it there, and it burned. "Every crack in him runs into it. All of them. It's the only one that's not full." He swallowed. "It's open. Something's pushing up under it from inside, like water under ice, and it can't get out. When it goes, it all goes."

His aunt looked down at him from the top of the bank, and the rings in her eyes turned once, slowly, and stayed turned.

"Muken," she said. "*Go.*"

"No," said the king of the north, and planted his feet in the road behind Amelia Greymane's shield, and let six Stages of somebody else's hate come down on his shoulders and go on through him into the frozen ground. "I'll not."

The Sin of Wrath came down off the bank at him.

---

*Hayato saw him come, and drew.*

It was forty-one paces from where the Sin of Wrath left the lip of the bank to where the king stood behind the Greymane girl's shield. Hayato knew that the way he knew everything, without measuring it: forty-one of his own paces, which were short, down a slope of churned snow and black roots at a fall of one in three, to a stretch of the Kessen road that he had walked on foot a dozen times and ridden thirty and mapped in his grandfather's indigo at fourteen. The Sin would cross it in less time than it takes a drop of rain to fall from a roof. The king would be dead before Hayato's heart had finished its next beat.

He was standing at the bend of the road nineteen paces north of the king, where the fog of his own road had boiled away round him in white rags and left him in the open like a man caught out on a hillside when the mist lifts. He had his left hand out still, holding the road open behind him the way a man holds a door. His right hand was on the grip of the plain sword.

He did not think about it. He had not thought about it at the Skeldmere, either. His grandfather had made him do it in the barn with the doors shut ten thousand times until there was no thinking left in it, only the arm.

The sword came out of the scabbard in one flat stroke from the left hip, the whole arm going out level and long like a man flinging seed across a field, and he cut the air.

*Sha.*

It made almost no sound. A sword drawn in a quiet room. The edge went through nothing, a flat line of nothing at the height of a man's chest, running from the bend of the road where he stood down across the slope to the foot of the bank and on into the trees, as long as one cut of a slight man's arm and as far as his Radius could carry it, which was a long way; and Transference took the line he had cut and closed the road along it. Whatever was crossing it stopped crossing.

The Sin of Wrath arrived at it.

He arrived at it the way a river arrives at a weir. One moment he was a smear of orange light coming down the bank with his afterimages hanging on the slope behind him like a stair, and the next he had come to the line Hayato had cut across the air between him and the king, and stopped. He had not hit anything. There was nothing there to hit. His body simply had nowhere further to travel, and all the speed in him went nowhere, and he stood at the line with his right fist out where it had been going to land on the Greymane girl's shield and through it, on the king, and the fist was a hand's width from her shield and would not go the last hand's width. The heat went on. Heat does not travel along a road in the way Hayato means it. It came across the cut untouched in a wall, and Hayato saw the snow on the road in front of the king go to water all at once and the water to steam, and the white lacquer on the Greymane girl's shield bubble, and the king's face, behind her shoulder, go red and wet and blister across the cheekbones, and the king did not step back.

The Sin of Wrath stood at the line with his fist out and looked at the place where it stopped, and then looked along the line, the way a man looks along a wall he has walked into in the dark, until he found the man at the end of it with a plain sword out and a gilt fox mask hanging at his belt.

[soft, apologetic, an old Japonic register worn smooth by Common; his voice shaking only a little] "I'm sorry," said Hayato Amagiri. "I'm very sorry. You can't go that way. The road's closed."

The living eye went to his breastbone and counted. Hayato felt it go over him, the heat of it like a hand, and felt it find what it found. Fewer than Tetsugen. Many more than Kiyome. A boy's count from a single war, most of them Keld, and a great many of those four hundred riders at the Skeldmere who had stopped against nothing in the snow, and then had been ridden down by the Ashmore horse while they stood there not understanding. He had not looked at that count in a long time. He looked at it now, because the Sin of Wrath was looking at it, and the ring-lines in that eye were filling with red.

*Mun.* The air had gone thick and wet as a bathhouse. Steam everywhere, off the crater and the snowmelt and the boiling road, and the fallen cedars burning in the wood behind with a sound like a crowd talking very fast, *mera-mera-mera*. His mist was gone. There was no mist anywhere within forty paces of that man and there would not be. He had known it would be like this. He had told Kiyome so, in the Hall, with his finger on the dust.

The Sin of Wrath went along the line toward him, to go round its end.

That was the counter. It was on Hayato's card in the copper book as surely as it was on the Sin's, and anyone who had seen the cut would find it: go round the ends. The Sin did not run. He walked, along the closed road, with his fist still pressed against the place where it stopped, the way a man walks along a fence feeling for the gate, his knuckles dragging through the nothing with a faint sound like a hand dragged down a pane of glass, *kiii*, and every step he took toward Hayato the snow went to steam under his feet. Nineteen paces. Fifteen.

Hayato did not move. He could have. A man who could be anywhere along forty miles of the Kessen road between one breath and the next could have been gone. He stood at the end of his line with the plain sword out, holding it the way he held it in the Hall, badly, and watched the Sin of Wrath walk toward him, because the line was the only thing between the king and the furnace, and if he moved off the end of it the end moved with him.

Kiyome hit the Sin from behind.

She came down the bank in a long white streak with the broken shaft of her spear held like a staff, and drove its iron butt-cap into the back of his right knee where Tetsugen's arrow had gone through, *gotsu*, and the knee did not buckle, and he turned. HAKANASHI turned him. And Tetsugen's fourth arrow took him in the side of the neck from the foot of the bank as he turned, under the ear, the fox's jaws closing on the great vessel there, *gabu*, and HAKANASHI turned him again. He stood between the two of them and the end of Hayato's line with his head jerking from one to the other like a bull in a ring with three capes in front of it, and the black-violet rim of his aura was thick now, thickening every time, toward all of them.

And then he stopped turning.

He stood still in the middle of the three of them with an arrow in his neck and his fist still against the line, and his head went down, and Hayato heard him breathe in.

It was slower than the bellows breath of GŌKA, and deeper, and it did not stop, the long chest filling and filling until the crazed glaze of his skin creaked over his ribs, *giri, giri*, and the Hall Irons on his wrists went from white to a color Hayato had never seen metal go, the color of the inside of a kiln when the potter opens the spy-hole and looks in, a red so deep and bright it was nearly not a color at all. The furnace roar under him dropped. It dropped and dropped until it was a sound felt in the stomach more than heard, the sound a blast furnace makes when its doors are thrown wide open, *gooooooooooooo*, and the ember-orange of his aura went out from him across the ground in every direction at once like spilled oil catching.

[a hanging judge reading sentence, low, enormous, and certain again; an old Japonic court accent hoarse with steam] "*Abi.*"

The deepest of the hells is the one without a pause.

Hayato felt the edge of it go past him. It went past him in the time it takes to say the word, out across the road and the slope and the wood, a hundred and fifty meters every way from the man in the middle, and where it went the air shivered once, like the skin of a pond when a fish turns under it, and then lay still. He looked up. The sky over the cedar wood had gone the color of a kiln's mouth, a dull glowing red-orange dome over everything, and the smoke from the burning cedars was going up into it and stopping there, flattening, spreading out under the inside of the dome like smoke under a ceiling. Nothing was getting out. He felt that before he understood it, the way a man in a cellar feels it when somebody closes the trapdoor over his head. The heat off the burning trees, off the crater, off the Sin, off every one of them and every breath they let out, had nowhere to go any more. It was all staying in.

It was all staying in with them.

He understood it the way he understood a road. He had read the card. He had read it three times in the Hall with Kiyome's hand on the page. A closed field. Heat cannot cross the boundary by any road: not by the light, not through the ground, not on the air. Every working spent inside stays inside, every blow's heat, every man's own warmth, the Sin's waste and their own, and the temperature does one thing only, the way water in a pot with the lid on does one thing only. Two minutes and forty seconds from a full reserve. And when it falls, everything it has kept leaves at once.

The Greymane girl had the king behind her shield. The Stark riders were on their knees on the road, some of them, and some of them were on their faces in the slush, and the slush they were lying in was getting warm.

*Jiwa, jiwa.* The heat came in under his rain-cape. It came up out of the ground through the soles of his boots. He felt the sweat start all over him at once, and run, and find nowhere to go, because the air was so full of water already that the sweat would not leave the skin; it lay on him like oil. His breath came short. He knew what that was. He had seen it in the passes, in the wet summer the drovers called the oven summer, men who sweated and sweated and could not cool and went slow, then stupid, then fell down by their mules and did not get up. Here it would not take a summer. In this steam, in a closed field climbing a degree a breath, it would take a few minutes. For the riders on the road, less.

The Sin of Wrath turned back to the end of the line, and resumed walking toward him.

"*Kodō.*"

The word came up from the foot of the bank in the old iron voice, and under it came the drum.

*DOOOON.*

It was not loud. It went into Hayato through his chest, through the soles of his feet, through the backs of his teeth, one slow enormous beat, and his own heart, which had been galloping in the heat like a horse that smells fire, *dokdokdokdok*, felt it and stumbled, and caught step with it. *DOOOON.* Slow. The whole field felt it, a second field laid inside the first, copper-red at its edges, a hundred and twenty meters round the old man on one knee at the foot of the bank with his ruined arm in his lap and his good hand flat on his own chest. Every heart inside it took the copper lord's beat. Hayato felt his slow. He heard, on the road, the riders' breathing change, the panting going long and deep. A rider who had been trying to crawl and failing got his knees under him. The Greymane girl's shoulders, which had begun to shake, went still.

Heat kills a man through his heart. Hayato knew that much medicine, from the oven summer: the heart races to push the hot blood out to the skin, faster and faster, and the skin can do nothing with it, and the heart races itself to death. The copper lord had laid his own heart under every heart in the field and was holding them to it.

He could not hold them for long. Hayato saw it in his face from forty paces. Every heart he slowed was a weight on his own, and his own was sixty-one years old and had been kept at twenty-five for forty years, and the gray was going white at his temples while Hayato watched, the years that would stay coming into him a breath at a time.

"Hayato." The old voice did not rise. "Get them out."

"Yes, my lord," said Hayato, and sheathed the plain sword, and the line he had cut across the air opened like a gate, and the Sin of Wrath, who had been walking along it with his fist pressed against nothing, stumbled forward a step into the place where it had been, off balance for the first time.

Behind him, a long way off on the road north, something white and very cold went up off Lorn Stark like a breath on a winter morning.

[low, plain, a man choosing his footing on ice] "*The sky covers us.*"

The Stark had put his sword into the road in front of the king. Hayato saw it as he turned: the point driven into the slush between Lorn's boots and the Greymane girl's heels, and the man standing over it with both hands on the pommel and his head down, and FROSTFALL coming up out of him the way water comes up out of a spring. His breath stopped steaming. His beard went white. A skin of clear ice ran down over his father's mail from the collar to the knees with a sound like a lake freezing in a single night, *pishi-pishi-pishi*, and the slush round his boots went silver and hard and creaked like a lake taking a weight, and then the creak ran outward, a ring of white frost going out across the road ten paces wide, over the riders on their faces, round the king and the Greymane girl, and the steam over it went to a glittering fog of ice as fine as flour and fell.

A Stark draws the heat out of everything near him and drives it down through his heels into the ground. The ground was inside the field. The heat could go nowhere; Lorn was only moving it from the air round the king into the earth under the king, a bucket against a fire. The card said so. Lorn knew it said so; he had been made to read the Sin of Wrath's card at the Seat, the same as all of them, and he was doing it anyway, with his jaw locked on the left side and the frost spreading. It would give the king a few minutes. It would give the riders lying in his ring a few minutes. Afterward, the card said, the cold he had been holding would come into him all at once and he would need a fire for the first time in his life. He would be standing inside the largest fire in the north.

Hayato went down the road.

He went down it the way he had never gone down a road in front of anyone in his life. KIRISAME burned off him before it could form; he did not try to call it. He called the second one instead, the one under it.

[a whisper, and then not a whisper] "*Tsuji.*"

Rain on a whole forest.

It came up out of him with that sound, *zaaaaaaaaaaa*, though there was no rain anywhere, a sound like a downpour on a hundred thousand cedar needles at once, and the indigo went out from him along the ground with gold flecks moving in it like moths round a lamp: not out across the wood, where his feet had never been, but along the road. Along every yard of the Kessen road he had ever walked, which within the furnace's field was all of it, a strip of indigo weather nine paces wide laid down the middle of the oven like a river through a desert. The field could not hold anywhere else. He had walked the road and nothing off it. And where the Sin of Wrath's red dome came down on his indigo strip, the two met, and pushed.

He felt the Sin's Sovereignty lean on his ground the way a man feels a door being shouldered from the other side. It was stronger than his. Not by much. Two hundred and sixty against two hundred and seventy-five, the copper book's numbers and his own, and he had done the sum in the Hall, and it came out the same now: the field would erode from its edges, the indigo going thin and red at the verges of the road like fog going off a valley at noon, a pace every few breaths. He had perhaps a minute before the road was nothing but road again.

A minute was a long time on a road.

On the road every way was his. He was at the king's side before the Sin of Wrath had got his balance back from the opened line, because the road between the bend and the king was his, and he had decided where it went. He arrived beside the Greymane girl in a soft sound of rain, *sara*, inside Lorn's ring of frost, where the air was only as hot as a summer noon, and the cold of it on his face after the oven was so sweet it made his eyes water. Lorn did not look round. The Greymane girl did, her flat red eyes going to him and then past him to the furnace and back, and she said, flat, "About time," and he said, "I'm sorry, Lieutenant, I came as quick as the road would let me," and meant it.

The Stark riders were on the road. Twelve of them, a horse's length apart, on their knees and their faces in Lorn's ring and out of it. The road was his. He could send everything on it anywhere he liked, if he did one thing more, and it was the thing he had done twice in his life.

He took the gilt fox mask off his belt.

His grandfather had told him, in the barn, what it cost, and he had not believed it until the first time. It was a crossroads-shrine mask, a fox's face in gold leaf over lacquered wood, with long narrow eyes and a smile, and the Amagiri left them at the crossroads for whatever walks the roads at night, so that whatever walks there would know which way the house wanted it to go. He put it on. It was warm from the heat. The eyeholes were narrow, and through them the field went strange.

The roads came up in him.

Every one. Every road he had walked and every road every Amagiri before him had walked and written down since the house began, came up through Phreatis in his blood the way a riverbed comes up when the river is low: the herders' shoulder off the Hiekawa cut; the deer track down onto the north hill of the white house; the Ashbury junction stone; the road under the high teeth of the north where the snow lies till midsummer; the Marrow ice; the back stair into the Seat that Hisae Thornwall's house had kept two hundred years; a thousand thousand fords and passes and goat-paths and drove-roads all lying open in him at once, and every one of them his to send a thing down, to its far end. The mask let him step from any point of the field to any other without crossing between. The mask let him send whatever he touched. And it took the road he used, every time, out of him, for good.

*Kakon.*

He was beside the first rider in Lorn's ring, the one nearest the bend, a big blond Stark with the Hollenmark crystal braids at his collar, lying on his side with his eyes rolled up white. He had not walked there. He had been at the king's shoulder, and then he was here, with nothing between. He put his gloved hand on the man's shoulder and sent him down the road.

He sent him north, down the Kessen road, the way it had always gone, to the old milestone at Grainsby Ford a mile outside the furnace's dome where the road dips to the stream and there is always water. The rider was there and then he was not, and the slush where he had been lay with the shape of him in it and began, slowly, to fill with melt.

And the road from the bend of the cedar wood to the milestone at Grainsby Ford went out of Hayato like a candle.

He felt it go. He had walked it a dozen times and ridden it thirty, and he had mapped it at fourteen in indigo on the back of his grandfather's survey, a mile and two hundred paces, the stream and the stepping stones and the alder where the road bends, and it was gone. He reached for it the way a tongue reaches for a tooth that has been pulled and there was nothing there, not even a hole, only the knowledge that there had been a road. He would never know the way along it again. He would have to be shown it like a stranger.

*It was only a mile*, he thought. *I'll not miss a mile.*

He touched the next man, and sent him to the same milestone by another road, the drove-road that runs behind the Grainsby farms, and lost that. And the next, by the stream itself, which he had once waded the length of in a hard summer, and lost that.

He did not count them. He would count them afterward. He went down the road from rider to rider in the soft sound of rain and the gold of the mask, touching shoulders, and every touch was a road going out of him like a lamp being blown out in a long house, room after room after room, and the riders went where the roads had gone, to the milestone and the ford and the farm-gate and the alder, out of the furnace, and the furnace's dome came down on his indigo strip from either side and ate it a pace at a time.

He had eleven of them out when he came to the place where the twelfth should have been, and he was not on the road.

Hayato saw him. He was six paces off the verge, in the wood, on the slope below the bank, where he had crawled when his horse went over, a dark young man with a short beard and a wolf on his breast, lying face up in the black leaf-mold with the snow round him gone to steaming water. Six paces off the road. Six paces into ground Hayato had never walked in his life.

He knew the man's name. He knew every rider's name; it was a thing he did. Aldo Brennick, of the Stark vanguard, twenty-three, from a farm under Hollenmark with nine chimneys and four of them smoking. He had made a joke at the woodpile about the Amagiri riding like a sack of meal tied to a saddle, and the Starks had laughed, and Hayato had laughed too.

He was dying. Hayato could see it from the road. The man's face was red and dry. His sweat had stopped. That was the worst thing; Hayato had seen it in the oven summer, and the drovers had a word for it, *the dry*, the body giving up trying to cool itself because there was no water left to sweat with. His eyes were open and looking at the red dome over the trees, and his hands were moving on his chest, picking at the wolf on his tunic as if there were something on it he wanted to brush off. He was saying something. His lips were cracked and he was saying something, over and over, and the copper drum was holding his heart steady, *DOOOON*, and it was not enough, because a heart is not the only thing heat kills. Somewhere in his skull his brain was cooking, slowly, the way an egg goes white in water that is not quite boiling.

Hayato could not reach him. The mask would let him step anywhere inside the field, and his field was the road, and the road stopped at the verge. Six paces of leaf-mold that no Amagiri had ever written down.

He stepped off the road anyway.

He did it on foot. He went down off the verge onto the slope in his own short paces, one, two, and the indigo did not come with him, and the instant he was off the road the furnace had him. The heat came onto him like a blanket out of an oven. His breath stopped in his chest; it was like breathing soup. The gold mask burned his cheeks. Three. Four. He was a slight man, and he had never once been hit hard, and the air was hitting him now with every breath. Five. He went down on his knees beside Aldo Brennick in the boiling water and took hold of his hand.

It was hot as a cup of tea.

[a croak, a farm boy's northern Kharven, puzzled] "It's warm, Lord Hayato," said Aldo Brennick. He was looking at the red sky. "It's warm. Is it summer?"

"Nearly," said Hayato. "I'm sorry, Aldo. I'm so sorry. Hold on to me."

He could not send him. He was off the road and the mask had nothing to send him down. He could only try to carry him back the six paces, and he was a slight man, and Aldo Brennick was a big one, and the heat was in Hayato's own head now; he could feel it there, a pressure behind the eyes, the edges of things going soft.

He got his arms under the man's shoulders and pulled.

Aldo Brennick had a seizure in his arms. It came on all at once, the big body going rigid and arching, the heels drumming in the hot water, *bachabachabacha*, the jaw locked, a froth at the corners of the cracked mouth, and Hayato held him through it with his own heart thudding against the copper drum and losing the step, and when it let go of him the man was very still and very heavy, and his eyes were still open on the red sky, and the copper drum went *DOOOON*, and his heart under Hayato's hands did not answer it.

Hayato knelt in the water with him for one breath. The heat was in his skull. He could not think of any road.

Then a hand closed on the back of his rain-cape and dragged him backward up the slope, six paces, through the leaf-mold and the steaming water and the snowdrops, and threw him onto the road into the indigo, and he lay on his back on his own ground in the soft sound of rain and breathed, and breathed, and the air was only an oven and not soup. He looked up. It was the Greymane girl. She had come out of the Stark's ring of frost for him. Her face was red and her short gold hair was black with sweat and the fur of her mantle was smoking. She had left the shield in front of the king. She had left the king.

"Get up," she said. Flat. Her ruined hand was shaking. "You've one left. *Get up.*"

He got up.

The furnace had come round the end of the line. He saw it as he rose: the Sin of Wrath on the road, inside the indigo, twenty paces off, walking toward the king through Hayato's own ground as if it were any other ground, his Sovereignty pressing it flat under every step so that the indigo went red where his feet touched it. Kiyome was on him. She was on his back, Hayato saw, actually on his back, her legs locked round his waist and the broken spear-shaft across his throat from behind, and the white of her harness was smoking all over where it touched him and her hands on the shaft were burned black, and she was pulling his head back with her whole weight, and he walked forward anyway with her on him like a man walking with a child on his back. Three turns of white light were round his right ankle. MUSUBI, holding the foot for a heartbeat, breaking, tying again, *kachi, pakin, kachi, pakin*, and every time it broke a fresh split opened in her fingers. The copper lord was on his feet at the foot of the bank with his good hand flat on his own chest, holding the field's hearts with his face gone white as paper and his lips blue, and an arrow on the bow-string in the hand that was mostly raw meat.

And Hayato saw what Kiyome was seeing.

He did not have her eyes. He had Phreatis, and Phreatis is water, and the air was all water now; the steam in the field had gone so thick with it that he could feel the shapes moving through it the way a fish feels a stone in the current. And the shape of the Sin of Wrath was wrong. It was heavier than it had been, and the weight was in the black-violet rim round him, thick as a horseshoe now, and banked so full of heat that it had begun to glow on its own, a dull plum color, like a coal that has gone past red. The field kept everything. *ONNEN banks from every hostile act inside the field, whomever it struck*: the copper book, in Tetsugen's provisioning hand. Every blow they had landed on him. Every breath of heat Lorn's cold had pushed into the ground. Every heartbeat the copper drum held against the oven. Aldo Brennick's death. All of it was going into the bank, and the bank was filling, and Kiyome on his back with her arm across his throat had her mouth by his ear and her eyes open on the black-violet, and she was counting.

[a hoarse shout over the roar, the old high register gone ragged] "*A fifth!*" she called. "Tetsugen! A fifth of the line!"

"I heard you," said the copper lord. "Keep pulling."

A fifth. In one field, in a little more than a minute. Hayato did the sum because he could not stop doing sums. The field would fall in a minute and a half, give or take, and the Sin's own heat would be past his Tolerance by then, and the bank would be a little under half the line, and when the field fell all its heat would leave at once from the boundary inward and outward, a blast, the card said, and none of them would be standing to see whether the bank had cracked him. Unless something drove it past the line before the field fell. Unless something drove it all the way. *Three of us at Eleventh can put a great deal in a bank.* He had not known then how much the bank would want.

The Sin of Wrath was ten paces from the king.

The king had not moved. He was standing in the middle of the Stark's ring of frost, with the shield the Greymane girl had planted in front of him and Lorn Stark over his sword beside him with his whole body gone white with rime and shaking now, the cold he was holding beginning to come into him, and the king's face was blistered across the cheekbones and wet and his left eye was a coin of molten gold, and the line from it still ran out across the oven to the back of the Sin of Wrath's neck. It had not wavered once. Tsugigane was in his hand, and the blade was ringing.

Hayato heard it now. Under the furnace roar and the rain-sound of his own field and the copper drum: a single held note, a bell struck once and held, felt in the back of the teeth before it was heard. It was lower than the note of the iron on the Sin's wrists, and he knew it for the note of a crack.

He had one road he could spare. He had thought about which, in the Hall, with his finger in the dust, and he had known the answer then: the road from wherever he was to the gate of the Seat. He had walked it more than any road in his life. He would never find his way home again without a guide. He did not think he would mind.

He went to the king, in a sound of rain, and stood in front of him in the gilt mask with its narrow smiling eyes, and held out his gloved hand.

[soft, apologetic, two days and a road; shaking now] "Sire. Take my hand. I'll send you to the Seat gate. You'll be home before the field falls." Behind the mask his eyes were wet. "Please. I've lost a great many roads tonight. Let it be for something."

Ten paces off, the Sin of Wrath, with Kiyome Byakuya on his back and the copper drum in his chest and an arrow in his neck and the bank glowing plum round him, stopped walking. His living eye had found the gold line in the air. His hand went up, slowly, to the back of his own neck, where it ended.

And the blade in the king's hand rang, and rang, and held its note.

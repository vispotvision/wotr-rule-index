# The Freight Book

*Kharven. The year after. The Thin Weeks.*

---

The factors' hall smelled of wet sacking and blubber-lamp and the sour edge grain gets when it has been counted by too many hands. Tabitha Hallenfeld stood in the doorway long enough for her eyes to come down out of the snow-light and for the room to see her, because a room that watches you arrive carries you differently from one you come up on quiet. Boards under her boots, worn to a trough down the middle where the sacks were dragged. Behind the partition a scale-arm knocked its stop, knocked it again, and a man swore at it without heat.
She had come down the hill in the Thin Weeks, which was the wrong time to ask anyone for anything. She had come with her manifests rolled in the cylinder at her belt because she went nowhere without them. A woman with papers at her hip is asking for something official. A woman with empty hands is asking a favour. On the walk down she had decided she would rather be refused officially.
Three carters sat on the bench under the window with their hands round bowls. One of them looked up.
"Road Woman."
"How's your stack?"
"Ash and good intentions." He grinned at her over the rim. "Yours?"
"Two weeks if the wind stays off it. One if it doesn't."
That was true and it cost nothing to say, and it bought her the bench for as long as she needed it. She let them look. Broad woman, road coat, the hem gone stiff with old salt. She had been looked at in worse rooms.
"You'll have heard," the carter said. "Greymane's in the death-house."
"Which one."
"The old one. The Ridge. Hip went on the Stormfold road, they carried him the last mile, and he's Waiting."
"He walked in for the signing. I watched him refuse a chair for four hours."
"That was spring."
She let it lie. A carter who is wrong in a warm room is not worth correcting in the Thin Weeks. The second man had more and gave it without being asked. The Tenrai boy sent south to be fostered. A man come back through the Undaar gate in a cart with his toes in a bag. She kept the cart and went through to the partition.
Hew Cotter kept the receiving desk. She knew him the way she knew every man who put a mark on a manifest between the Brine and the Seat, by his hand before his face. Small, fifty, a clerk's stoop on a soldier's shoulders, a gap where two front teeth had been and a habit of setting his tongue in it while he thought. On the desk in front of him lay a night-stone, black, thumb-sized, a groove worn into one face. When she came round the partition he turned it over without looking at it.
"Hallenfeld."
"Cotter."
"You're not on the roll for today."
"I'm not on the roll for anything. I'm asking."
He set his tongue in the gap. Behind him the shelves went up to the roof beam, ledger on ledger, spines out. The spring's receipts in a red-tied bundle, the year before in brown, the year before that gone grey with lamp-smoke. She read the shelf the way she read a road. Where the wear was. Which bundles had been taken down often and which had been left to settle into their own dust.
"The southern grain," she said.
"Ask Lambert."
"I've asked Lambert. Three times. Once in council and twice in his office with the door shut, and I'll tell you what he did, Hew, he put his hands behind his back and he waited for me to finish, and then he thanked me for the question."
"He's polite."
"He's something." She laid one hand flat on the desk, well away from the stone. "I don't need him. I need the receipt. First sack of Nalūn grain through that door, what day it came and whose mark was on the tally."
"You know whose mark. It's mine. It's always mine."
"Whose seal, then."
Cotter looked at her a long moment and then at the shelf, at the red bundle and past it, and she watched his eye go to the brown one and stop and come back.
*She's counting the shelf. Sky help me, the woman is counting my shelf.*
"You'd want the ledger for that," he said.
"I would."
"The ledger's the Seat's."
"So am I."
"That's..." He stopped. Set the tongue. Started again. "That's not the same and you know it isn't, and don't..." His hand went to the night-stone and turned it over and turned it back. "Don't stand there and tell me you don't know it isn't."
She did know it. A road belongs to whoever is on it. A ledger belongs to whoever is holding it, and the Seat had a great many hands, and hers had never been among them.
She took her hand off the desk.
"Then don't show me the ledger."
He waited.
"Show me the freight book."
The scale-arm knocked its stop behind the wall. One of the carters laughed at something. Cotter did not move for long enough that she thought he was going to refuse, and then he got up, and the stoop came up with him, and he went along the shelf to the grey bundle and took down a thin book with no tie on it at all.
"This is nothing," he said. "This is who paid the carts. Nobody asks for it."
"No."
"There's nothing in it a council would want."
"No," she said. "There isn't."
He put it on the desk between them and opened it and turned it round.
She had known what she was walking down for. A date. A day of the month in the first week, in Cotter's hand, that she could carry back up the hill and put in front of the Queen's chair, and that Lambert could not put his hands behind his back and wait out, because a date does not finish speaking. That was the whole of it. Lambert would not answer because the answer was that he had moved before the council wrote him leave to move, and a man who counted everything could not bear to have that one line entered against his name. She had been sure of it since the seventh month. She was sure of it now, with the page under her hand. The first line was there. The day and the count and the cart-master's mark, four days into the first week, and the advance had come in the fourth and every soul in the Seat knew it.
She did not look at the date for more than a breath.
The freight was not entered against the Seat.
It was entered against a house. A river-mark, three lines and a bar, the mark a Hallenfeld factor cuts into the end of a barge-plank so the plank finds its way home. Her grandfather had shown her how to cut it with the flat of a knife on the river-stairs when she was six. The knife had gone on the Ashgate road, out of her belt somewhere between the narrows and the ninth hour. She had not gone back for it. She had not cut the mark into anything since.
Her own house had paid the carts.
She put her thumb on the mark. The ink had gone the brown of old blood. Cotter's tongue was in the gap and he was not looking at her; he was looking at the night-stone, face down on the desk, and his hand was on it, and she could not have said what a receiving clerk needed with a night-stone at midday in a lit hall, and she did not ask.
"Who signed the house mark."
"Not my page. I take what comes through the door. The freight's the cart-master's page."
"Where's the cart-master."
"Dead. Spring." He said it flat, the way the Seat said everything that spring. "Fever. The book came to me with his things."
She turned the page. The second week, the third, the fourth. The Seat's own seal from the fourth week on, clean and black, Lambert's clerk's hand. Three weeks of river-mark and then the Seat, as if the house had carried the thing until the Seat could be seen to carry it. She turned back. Read the first line again. It had not changed.
"Hew."
"I know."
"You don't know what I was going to say."
"I know what page you're on." He took the stone off the desk and put it in his coat. "Close it. Put it back yourself, grey bundle, third from the wall. I never took it down."
She closed it. The board was soft with damp under the cover. She went along the shelf and put it where he said, third from the wall, and it settled between its neighbours as if it had never been moved, and she stood with her hand on the spine a moment longer than she needed to.
"You owe me a bowl," Cotter said behind her. "Not now. When the grass comes."
"When the grass comes."
She went back through the hall. The carters had gone to their bowls and did not look up, and that was worse than being looked at, because it meant the story had already gone out ahead of her and was being told at the bench in her hearing with the names left out. The scale-arm knocked. She put her shoulder to the door and the snow-light took her eyes again.
Halfway up the hill she stopped at a woodpile that was not hers, a stack against the north wall of a house with its shutters closed, and she pulled two logs off the top and turned them over. Wet through. Both. She put them under her arm and went on up.

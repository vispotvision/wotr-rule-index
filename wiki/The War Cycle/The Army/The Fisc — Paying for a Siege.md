# The Fisc — Paying for a Siege

The funeral of Captain Thora Hjerting is the best-attended audit in the north. She held the Third Wall at Brennholt through the winter of the Short Mark, and when she dropped dead on the fire-step in the eleventh week, six hundred men carried her through the snow on a shield painted blue and gold, with horns and the whole hold following and the sutlers' wives handing out hot ale. At the graveside Fat Tormod, sutler to the Third Wall, climbed onto a barrel with her account book, wept, and read out what she owed him, and the crowd slowly understood what they were hearing. She had been paying the company herself since the sixth week. Her horse had gone first, then her mother's silver, then the gold from her back teeth, and she had died of hunger with a full company at her back and told nobody. The reeve's paymaster had come for the ale. When Tormod finished, the Third Wall put down its cups without a word, formed up in the yard in perfect order, with no officer left to tell it to, and stood looking at him.

Nobody in Brennholt was shouting, and the paymaster lived to describe it, so I can report that he found the silence worse. Everything that follows concerns how a crown gets to that yard, and how the man on the other side of the wall is getting there too.

*Sieges are lost in ledgers far more often than on walls. This is the clock under the Kharven arc.*
> *The whole art of war is reduced to money.*

## I. The Governing Question

Kharven has never been issued an Accord instrument and has never asked for one. That is a boast in peacetime and a noose in a siege, because **the Accord's credit runs on the Accord's instruments**, and an instrument only binds inside the Accord's jurisdiction.

So the Seat's real problem lies well behind the wall.

**To borrow at scale, Kharven must accept an instrument. To accept an instrument is to accept the jurisdiction that enforces it.** A factor house will lend against a bill it can sue on. It will not lend against a custom that has never been written and cannot be cited in any court that exists.

Everything below is the Seat trying to avoid signing, and running out of ways.

## II. What the Seat Can Actually Raise

Six instruments. Each pays this season and costs something next.

### 1. The stack levy

Compulsory purchase of stores at a price the Crown sets, against a tally, redeemable later. Woodpiles, root caches, borts, seal fat, hides, horn, bone.

**This is the fastest revenue available and the most dangerous thing the Seat can do**, because in a country whose religion is the Sky, a low stack is a moral condition rather than a misfortune. *How's your stack* is a diagnostic. A household stripped by the levy has been made, in the eyes of everybody who greets it, into the kind of household that failed to prepare.

The historical pattern is exact and should run in full. Purveyance produced abuse and corruption on a scale that forced nationwide investigations and the removal of most purveyors from office; it was eventually surrendered for military purposes under sustained pressure; and it was revived at the next war with instructions that purveyors be fair, pay a fair price, and leave the temples alone. **Every one of those three instructions was issued because the opposite happened last time.**

**Cost next season:** the tallies. A tally is a promise, the Crown has issued thousands, and the day they are presented is a day the Crown chooses between paying and being seen not to.

### 2. Labor-debt and meat-sharing

The customary instruments, unwritten, enormously powerful, and **unenforceable against a man who simply refuses.** The Seat can call on them, and the calling works because refusing is socially ruinous rather than illegal.

This is real revenue with one hard limit: **it can be called once well and twice badly.** A custom leaned on until it breaks does not come back, and the households know exactly how many times it has been called this winter, because that is the kind of thing households count.

### 3. The forced loan

The great households are asked for silver, in a room, individually, by name, with the amount already written. Nobody refuses in the room. What happens afterward is that the amount is remembered, the loan is not repaid on time, and **a forced loan converts a supporter into a creditor**, which is a different political animal entirely.

### 4. Debasement

The Crown's coin, reduced in fineness, minted in greater number from the same metal.

**Debasement is a fiscal weapon, chosen on purpose, and it works.** The Great Debasement financed a real war effectively, and the tell separating an aggressive debasement from a defensive one sits in the mint accounts: **mintage fees run high on an aggressive debasement and low on a defensive one.** Anybody with the mint rolls can tell which one the Crown is running, and the assay houses always find out.

And the four quarters have their own trap already built. Silver is the accounting unit of every serious ledger in the four quarters, and **the western silver mark is the standard the Accord quotes everything against.** A Kharven mark that no longer holds its fineness is a mark the assay houses will discount, and a discounted coin buys less from exactly the merchants a besieged capital needs most.

**Cost next season:** the discount is permanent and arrives faster than the benefit. Grain sellers reprice within a turn. Wages reprice within three.

### 5. Sale of standing

Offices, exemptions, hunting rights, precedence, and the right to be excused the levy. **The last of those is the one that actually sells**, and it guarantees the levy falls hardest on households with no silver to buy their way out, which is the households the Sky faith says should be given the first bowl.

### 6. Accord credit

A bill of exchange drawn on a factor house, remitted to the Seat, discounted heavily for distance and for risk. The only instrument on the list that produces silver in quantity and quickly.

**And the terms are the plot.** A factor house lends against enforceable security. Enforceable means a court. The court is the Accord's. So the security offered is some combination of the customs on a named road, the Wellspring beneath a named holding, a Guild charter over the Seat's own market, or the right to seat a Regional Envoy. Every one of those is a piece of jurisdiction, and once one is signed the rest become negotiable.

**A crown that signs is solvent and is no longer sovereign in the way it was last week.** A crown that refuses keeps something nobody can eat.

## III. The Pay Clock

The mechanism that makes the fisc a timer rather than a background fact.

Pay must reach the men **across distance, with sufficient regularity to prevent mutiny, and in denominations small enough to spend locally.** All three conditions, every time. Failure of any one is failure of all.

**The third is the one that will bite Kharven**, because the mechanism is already there. Copper is the money of ordinary life and the only metal money most people ever hold. Copper is also the windings of every great array (a standing runed working), laid by hand and worth more by weight melted than struck, and every great array raised in two centuries has produced a copper famine within three years. **A siege raises arrays, on both sides of the wall.** The Seat can hold silver and still be unable to pay a soldier, because there is nothing in the chest a soldier can buy bread with.

What keeps an army fed in the meantime is short-term credit: **unpaid bills to suppliers, advances made by officers out of their own pockets, and temporary loans arranged by intermediaries.** That is the life thread of army finance and it is invisible until it snaps. Long before a mutiny there is a captain who has been paying his own company for six weeks and has stopped sleeping.

**The dissolution schedule** involves no dice. It is a sequence of things that happen.

| Arrears | What happens |
|---|---|
| One turn | Grumbling. Sutlers extend credit and mark it up |
| Two turns | Officers begin advancing pay themselves. Small theft becomes normal and goes unpunished, because punishing it would mean admitting why it is happening |
| Three turns | Foraging becomes indistinguishable from robbery in the surrounding district. The district stops selling and starts hiding |
| Four turns | Desertion by ones and twos, at night, from the least-watched posts, by the men with families near enough to reach |
| Five turns | The line holds together and **presents.** Men assemble in order, without officers, and ask for what they are owed. A riot would be far less dangerous |
| Beyond | Unpaid troops have historically marched, leaderless, on the nearest thing worth taking, and taken it |

**The presentation is the beat worth writing.** A formation, silent, correct, standing where it can be seen, waiting, with no mob in it anywhere. Every officer present knows exactly what it is.

## IV. The Other Ledger

The besieger is not solvent either, and this is the part fiction forgets.

An army sitting outside a wall eats every day and produces nothing. Its costs are worse per head than the defender's, because the defender is at home and the besieger's every loaf has been carried. **Sieges are races between two failing treasuries**, and the wall is a way of keeping score.

**Contribution.** Payment taken from the surrounding country in exchange for not sacking it. The largest single revenue a besieging army has, collected systematically, with schedules and receipts, turning the countryside around a siege into a taxed province of the invader within a season.

**Foraging**, which is contribution without the receipt, and which begins automatically as soon as the besieger's own pay is late.

**Ransom**, which is why a valuable prisoner is a fiscal event before he is anything else.

**And the sack**, which discipline has very little to do with. **It is the settlement of arrears by other means**, understood as such by every soldier and every commander, which is why the terms offered before a storm are a real negotiation about money rather than a courtesy.

**So the defender has a lever nobody uses in fiction.** A besieging army four turns in arrears is an army whose commander needs a settlement more than the defender does. The Seat that knows how far behind the enemy's pay is knows more than the Seat that knows where his engines are.

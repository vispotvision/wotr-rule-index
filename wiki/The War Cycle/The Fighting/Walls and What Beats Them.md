# Walls and What Beats Them

The siege of Carrick Ness took fourteen months, and the only part anyone sings is the trebuchet. Mistress Wenna Holt of the Engineers' Guild built it on the beach in full sight of the garrison, painted it blue with gold teeth, named it the Bishop's Apology, and stood on its frame bawling her guild mark at the walls while forty apprentices hauled down the beam. The first stone went backward through the besiegers' brewhouse. The fourth took the top off a tower. The garrison's answer was its master mason, old Fergus Ruck, who had been feuding with Holt's guild for thirty years over a bridge contract, and who rebuilt by night whatever she knocked down by day and carved a new rude face into every repair. In the sixth month the besiegers hired a Master at a ruinous price, and he unmade the outer face at noon with one violet stroke the fishing boats saw from six miles out, and found behind it a ditch, a second face, and a great many crossbows. What took Carrick Ness in the end was a team of Sealwrights nobody noticed, three quiet weeks of striking entries off the walls' old workings, and the flux, which had by then killed more of the besiegers than Holt, Ruck and the Master combined. Holt's guild still claims the victory. Ruck's descendants still have the faces.

Keep the Bishop's Apology in mind while you read, because she is the part of the siege that gets painted, and the Strategic Division's document below is about the rest.

*Investment, Reduction, and the Convention of the Breach*
> Issued by the Strategic Division, **with the reduction schedules for Accord-held fortifications omitted from all copies issued outside the Citadel.**
> 
> *Fourth of the War Cycle.*

## Why the War Is About Places

> **Battles are the exception. A campaign is a list of places, and most of them have walls.**
**A fortress cannot be walked past.** It sits on the road, the river, the bridge or the vein, and a force that leaves it behind **has left an enemy on its own supply line with cavalry and time.**

So armies besiege. **Most of the days of most campaigns are spent sitting outside somewhere, digging, waiting, dying of camp fever, and negotiating.** The pitched battle that the histories record is what happens when a relief force arrives and both commanders decide simultaneously that the risk is acceptable, **which is rare, and which is why the histories have so few of them and so many sieges.**

---

## The Lines

*A besieging force digs twice. Inward against the garrison, outward against relief.*

**The inner line** · Faces the walls. Pens the garrison, blocks sorties, and provides the platforms from which the reduction proceeds. Begun out of bowshot and dug forward in covered trenches, each one nearer the wall than the last.

**The outer line** · Faces away. Protects the besieger's back **from the field army that is always coming.** An army that has invested a place without an outer line **has put itself in the position it intended to put the garrison in**, and the record contains several examples of exactly that outcome.
> Both lines must be held continuously by ordinary soldiers, in weather, for months, **the clearest illustration in warfare of the currency distinction.**
>
> **No practitioner has ever invested a fortress. Investment is time converted into ground and there is no other instrument for it.**

## What Walls Are Built Against Now

*Fortification design has passed through three answers to three different threats, and every standing wall is a fossil of the threat it was built against.*

| Form | Built against | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| **The high wall** | Escalade and starvation | Tall, relatively thin, designed so nobody can climb it. Effective for centuries **and obsolete the moment a besieger could bring a practitioner capable of unmaking masonry at range**, which made every tall wall in the world **a target rather than a defense** |
| **The low work** | Practitioners | Thick, low, earth-backed, angled so **every face is covered by crossbows and workings from another face.** A practitioner can unmake a section and finds behind it a ditch, a second face, and enfilade (loosing down the length of a line instead of across it). **The reduction now costs Essence per section rather than Essence per wall, and the arithmetic of the costing-out doctrine turns against the attacker** |
| **Anchored work** | Everything | Principality Mortar (the mortar that seats a Domain, ground made to obey its maker's law, into a foundation), **and the eleven structures raised on it.** A Domain anchored at Realm scale **registers in the Continuum as terrain rather than as building. It is not reduced. It is not mined. It cannot be removed by its own builders**, and four of the eleven now stand in places nobody governs |

> **Why nobody builds anchored work.** Cost, the Bench's reservation, and the fact that **a fortification which cannot be removed is a fortification your grandchildren will have to garrison whether or not the border still runs there.**
>
> The Division's own assessment is that anchored work is **a strategic liability disguised as an asset**, that the eleven were built by regimes which did not expect to end, **and that all eleven regimes ended.**

## Reduction

### Engines

Trebuchets, mangonels, rams, belfries and bores. Each is a guild master's own work, built on the spot from the nearest good timber, named, painted, and shouted about, and each carries the guild mark somewhere the enemy can read it. They throw stone, fire pots, dead horses and on one recorded occasion a bishop's letter. They misfire in ways their makers describe as rare, and an engine crew is paid by the piece and can walk off a siege as readily as any other trade. Against bare masonry they are slow and sure. Against a wall with its inscription intact they are mostly slow.

### The Sealwright approach

**A Sealwright team (the Accord's binding and containment specialists) leaves the walls standing and unmakes what is in them.**

Every fortification above the crudest carries inscription: ward-plate, seated chains, anchored foundations, **the accumulated metaphysical work of however many generations have held the place.** Sealwright work strips that, chain by chain, from outside, slowly, **in a process that resembles nothing so much as a clerk going through a ledger and striking entries.**
> Stripped, the place is masonry. **Masonry falls to ordinary engines, ordinary mining, and ordinary time.**
>
> The Sealwright team's contribution is **invisible from the walls and decisive**, and garrisons that have watched a siege proceed with no apparent progress for three weeks **have surrendered on the fourth without understanding what changed.**

### Mining

Old and unchanged. Dig under, prop with timber, burn the props, drop the wall. Counter-mining is the defender digging to intercept, **and the fighting that happens underground is the worst fighting in warfare and the least described.**

**Against Domain-anchored foundations mining does not work, because the foundation is registered as terrain and terrain does not care that the earth under it has been removed.** Sappers have reported galleries **in which the ceiling simply continued to be there.**
> Three attempts against anchored work are recorded, all abandoned, **and one of the three abandoned the sappers.**

### Starvation

**The oldest instrument and still the most successful.** A well-provisioned town holds a year and a poorly provisioned one holds a season, **and the arithmetic that matters is the population's, far more than the garrison's**, since a town holds many more mouths than soldiers **and the soldiers eat first.**

The sequence is documented and identical everywhere. **Horses first, then the animals kept for other purposes, then what was stored for planting, then what is not food.**
> **Disease arrives before starvation completes and does most of the work.**
>
> The besieging camp gets the same disease at approximately the same time **and frequently loses more men to it than the garrison loses to everything**, which is the central absurdity of siege warfare **and the reason competent commanders want terms.**

## Terms, and the Breach

*Everything a garrison does after the breach is practicable is judged against a convention nobody signed and everybody enforces.*

A garrison may surrender on terms while resistance remains meaningful, and will ordinarily be allowed to march out with its arms, its colors and its honor. **Mercy has nothing to do with it: the besieger is buying the place cheaply rather than buying it with a storm.**
> Once a practicable breach exists and the garrison refuses terms and forces a storm, **the convention holds that the garrison and the town have forfeited protection.** Quarter may be refused. The place may be sacked.
>
> Every commander in the four quarters knows this and every garrison commander weighs it, **and the weighing is the most consequential decision anyone makes in a siege, because it is made on behalf of a population that does not get a vote.**

### The Accord's position

**Concord Writ (the Accord's warrant for war) does not recognize the convention.** Arbitration's stated position is that a stormed town's inhabitants **retain full protection**, that the storming force remains bound by the Codex Protocols, **and that the Law of Witness applies throughout.**

The Division's own after-action record for stormed places contains **a consistent discrepancy between the Scribe's account and the returns of the populations concerned.**
> **The discrepancy has been noted in review on nine occasions. No prosecution has followed any of them.**

## Unresolved Entries

**Anchored work** · Eleven structures, four in ungoverned territory, **none removable.** No policy exists for what happens when an anchored fortification stands in a place the Accord no longer wishes to hold.

**The third mining attempt** · Abandoned. **The sappers were not recovered.** The gallery was sealed by Enforcement **and the sealing is maintained annually at Division expense.**

**Sealwright stripping and the ownership of inscription** · Whether stripping a fortification's accumulated inscription constitutes destruction of property has been argued twice and settled neither time. **The stakes are large: several of the places in question hold inscription older than the Accord.**

**The storm discrepancy** · Nine reviews, no prosecutions. **Entered at the insistence of the Guild of Measurewrights, who have no standing in the matter and filed anyway.**

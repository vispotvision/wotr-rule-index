# Edward Lambert — The Arithmetic

*The Lambert · The Arithmetic · Hild's Right Hand*
> **Affiliation:** Kingdom of Kharven (loyalist) · The Sword Princess's Council
> **Status:** Active · Right-Hand Advisor to Hild Stark-Moto
> **Level:** 142 · **Stage:** III Hold · **Band:** II
> Not powerful. Never was. Never needed to be.

## I · Overview

Edward Lambert is the man who taught an eleven-year-old girl how to hold a kingdom.
He did not teach her the sword. Lorn did that. He did not teach her the standing. The blood did that. He did not teach her the cold, or the jaw, or the cadence, or the specific quality of silence that the Kokan inheritance provided without instruction. Those things were architecture. Edward taught her the arithmetic.
The arithmetic of governance. Who holds what. Who needs what. Who fears what. How the three interact to produce the outcome you want. How the outcome you want differs from the outcome you need, and why the difference is the space where rulers are made or broken. How a granary's inventory is a weapon. How a road's condition is an intelligence report. How a man's second sentence tells you more than his first because the first sentence is the one he rehearsed and the second is the one that fell out.
Edward Lambert is not a great warrior. He is not a powerful practitioner. He is not a legendary commander or a storied knight or any of the things that songs are made about. He is a man who counts things. He counts grain stores and troop dispositions and the specific number of days between a lord's promise and a lord's delivery, and the counting is the thing he taught Hild, and the counting is the reason Hild governs rather than merely rules, because ruling is the sitting and governing is the counting, and the counting is the thing that keeps the sitting from collapsing.

## II · Physical Description

Edward Lambert is fifty-three years old and looks sixty because the last eleven years added the decade that the previous forty-two had not. He is of middling height, neither tall enough to command a room by stature nor short enough to be overlooked by it, the specific build of a man who has spent his life occupying the space between notice and invisibility and who has found that the space between the two is where the most useful work gets done.
His frame is lean but not gaunt. A body that was once solidly built in the northern frontier manner, broad enough through the chest and shoulders to carry armour and swing a blade with the competence of a man who trained daily because training was the requirement, not the passion. The body has thinned with age and with the specific weight-loss that sustained stress produced in men who processed stress through the mind rather than through the body, the metabolism burning through reserves that the appetite was not replacing because the appetite was not interested in food. The appetite was interested in the problem, and the problem was the kingdom, and the kingdom had been the appetite's only subject for eleven years.
His face is long and angular, weathered to the texture of old leather by decades of northern wind and the specific indoor weathering that lamplight and sleeplessness produced in men who spent their nights reading dispatches by inadequate illumination. The cheekbones are prominent, the jaw narrow but set, the mouth a line that curves neither up nor down at rest, the specific neutral expression of a man who decided early that the face was a surface and that surfaces were for other people to project onto, and that the projection told him more about the projector than any direct question would. His eyes are grey. Not the pale grey of the Stark bloodline or the malachite grey of Dawi mineral. The grey of iron. Small eyes, set deep beneath a brow that produces permanent shadow across the upper face, and the shadow is the thing that most people see first, because the shadow makes the eyes harder to read, and the hardness is the point.
His hair is grey-brown, thinning at the crown, cut close to the skull in the northern military style that he adopted during his service and never abandoned because the style required no maintenance and maintenance was time and time was the thing Edward Lambert did not spend on anything that did not produce a return. He is clean-shaven, always, because a beard in the north was a vulnerability (it froze, it could be grabbed, it concealed the jaw's tells from people who were reading his face the way he read theirs) and because the daily shaving was the discipline, and the discipline was the man.
He wears dark wool. Always dark wool. A long coat over a quilted tunic, the coat's cut plain and functional, the fabric good but not ostentatious, the specific quality of clothing that communicated: this man has means but does not display them, and the not-displaying is the statement, and the statement is: I am not here to be seen. I am here to see.
He carries no visible weapon. A short knife lives inside the coat, accessible through a slit in the lining, and the knife has not been drawn in four years, and the four years is the proof that the knife's presence is sufficient and the knife's use is not required, because a man who needs to draw a knife has already failed at the thing the knife was supposed to prevent, which is: the situation reaching the point where knives are relevant.

## III · History

### Before the Fall

Edward Lambert was born in the Hallenfeld river corridor, the son of a trade administrator whose function was the management of tariff collection along the eastern routes and whose function's competence was the reason the son received an education and the education was the reason the son entered Muken's civil service rather than the military. Lambert was not a soldier. Lambert was the thing that soldiers required in order to function, which was: the man who counted the grain that fed them and the roads that moved them and the coin that paid them, and the counting was the service, and the service was the thing Muken valued as highly as the sword because Muken understood that a kingdom without arithmetic was a kingdom without a future.
Lambert rose through the civil administration the way competent men rose in Muken's kingdom, which was: by being correct. Not by being liked. Not by being impressive. Not by performing the specific social displays that other courts required for advancement. By being correct. By producing the count that matched the inventory. By delivering the assessment that matched the outcome. By being the man whose numbers were right, and whose right-numbers were the thing the kingdom ran on.
He was thirty-nine when Muken noticed him. The noticing was not dramatic. Muken read a quarterly trade report from the eastern corridor, noted that the report's projections had been accurate for eleven consecutive quarters, asked who had written the reports, and received the name: Lambert. Muken filed the name. The filing was the elevation. A man whose name Muken filed was a man whose career trajectory had just changed, because Muken's filing system was the kingdom's talent pipeline, and the pipeline fed the governance, and the governance fed the kingdom.
Lambert was forty-two when the kingdom fell. He had been three weeks from receiving a lordship — a minor holding in the Hallenfeld corridor, the specific administrative fief that Muken granted to civil servants whose competence had earned the distinction. The lordship would have given Lambert a seat at the lower council, a voice in trade policy, and the official standing that his function had been performing without the title for fifteen years. The lordship was signed. The lordship was in the dispatch queue. The lordship, like everything else in the dispatch queue on the night of the assassination, never left the desk.

### The Wife

Her name was Kasumi.
Kasumi Kurenai Moto. A woman from the Kurenai cadet line, one of the peripheral Moto families whose position in the kingdom's architecture was minor enough to survive the political turbulence of the main-line conflicts and significant enough to produce practitioners whose Essence inheritance was genuine Moto blood, diluted by generations of distance from the Kokan trunk but carrying the Works and the Hataraki and the specific quality of silence that Moto blood produced in every person who inherited it.
Lambert married her when he was thirty-one. The marriage was not political. The marriage was the thing that happened when a quiet Concord trade administrator met a quiet Moto practitioner at a corridor-town market and the two quiets recognized each other across a vegetable stall and the recognizing was the courtship, and the courtship was brief, because quiet people did not require the long courtship that loud people required, because quiet people had already said everything they needed to say in the silence, and the silence was the yes.
Kasumi died in the second year after the fall. The Tenrai's consolidation of the cadet lines produced casualties that the Tenrai classified as "administrative" and that Edward Lambert classified as murder. Kasumi was not killed in combat. Kasumi was killed by the specific mechanism that Azure's architecture used to bring the peripheral lines into alignment, which was: the families that did not swear to the new throne were subject to "restructuring," and the restructuring was the word that meant: the family's practitioners were detained, assessed for loyalty, and the ones whose assessment produced the wrong result were removed. Kasumi's assessment produced the wrong result because Kasumi's result was the truth, and the truth was: the Kokan Line is the legitimate crown, and the legitimate crown's removal was murder, and the murder's beneficiaries are the people conducting this assessment, and the assessment is the murder wearing a procedural mask.
The Tenrai did not kill Kasumi for her defiance. The Tenrai killed Kasumi because her defiance was articulate and her articulation was heard by other detainees and the hearing was the contagion, and the contagion was the thing Azure's system could not permit, because articulate defiance in a detention facility is the first stage of organized resistance, and organized resistance was the thing the restructuring was designed to prevent.
Edward Lambert received the report of his wife's death through a Hallenfeld courier who did not know what the report contained and who delivered it alongside three trade manifests and a tariff update. Lambert read the report. Lambert set the report on his desk. Lambert read the three trade manifests. Lambert read the tariff update. Lambert processed the tariff update's implications for the eastern corridor's grain supply. Lambert wrote a response to the tariff update. Lambert sealed the response. Lambert placed the response in the outgoing dispatch.
Then Lambert closed the door to his office and sat in the dark for three days.
On the fourth day, Lambert opened the door, walked to the Hallenfeld junction, and began the journey to the Ironwood March, where a rumour had reached him through the courier network that a child had been born to a Stark woman, and the child carried Kokan blood, and the child's name was Ice.

### Finding Hild

Lambert arrived at the holdfast when Hild was four years old. Fern was alive. The uncles were in place. The holdfast was functional in the way that frontier holdouts were functional, which was: barely, and with the specific competence of people who had decided that barely was sufficient and that sufficient was the standard.
Lambert did not announce himself as a former civil servant of Muken's kingdom. Lambert announced himself as a man who could count, and the counting was the thing the holdfast needed, because the holdfast's supplies were managed by people whose skills were martial rather than administrative, and the martial skills were excellent and the administrative skills were the gap, and the gap was the thing that was going to kill everyone in the holdfast before the Tenrai did, because the Tenrai killed you with swords and the gap killed you with winter, and winter was slower and more certain.
Fern recognized what Lambert was. Not who. What. The specific category of person whose function was the thing the holdfast lacked, whose competence was the thing the holdfast needed, whose presence converted the holdfast from a military outpost running on martial willpower to an administrative entity running on arithmetic, and the arithmetic was the thing that kept the martial willpower fed and supplied and alive long enough to perform the martial functions that the arithmetic could not perform.
Lambert stayed. The staying was the oath. Lambert did not swear. Lambert counted. The counting was the swearing.
When Fern died, Lambert remained. When the uncles assumed guardianship, Lambert remained. When Hild picked up the sword, Lambert remained. When Hild sat on the broken throne, Lambert was standing behind it, three paces back, to the right, in the specific position that right-hand advisors occupied in every court in every kingdom in the history of governance, which was: close enough to whisper, far enough to deny.

## V · Voice & Mannerisms

Edward Lambert speaks in the voice of a man who has been counting things for longer than most people have been alive and who has decided that the counting is more interesting than the things being counted.
His voice is quiet. Not soft. Quiet in the way that a mechanism is quiet when the mechanism is well-maintained, each component seated correctly, no friction, no waste. The words arrive in order, at a pace that does not vary, with the specific evenness of a man who has decided that vocal inflection is a form of persuasion and that persuasion is a form of dishonesty and that dishonesty is the thing he does not do, not because he is moral but because dishonesty is inefficient, and inefficiency is the thing Edward Lambert does not tolerate.
He does not raise his voice. He has not raised his voice in eleven years. The last time he raised his voice was the day he received the courier's report about Kasumi, and the raising was inside the office with the door closed, and the raising lasted three days, and the three days were the last time the voice was not controlled, and the controlled has been permanent since.
**Mannerisms:**
- Counts with his right thumb against his right index finger when processing. The counting is silent, rhythmic, visible to anyone watching his hands
- Stands three paces behind and to the right of whatever authority he is advising. Always three paces. Always right. The position is the function
- Refers to Hild as "my lady" in public and as "girl" in private, and the "girl" is not disrespect. The "girl" is the specific term of the specific relationship, which is: I see what you are and I see what you are not yet, and the not-yet is the space where my teaching lives
- Does not sit in Hild's presence unless the sitting serves a purpose (reading documents, eating, demonstrating that sitting is permitted). The not-sitting is the discipline
- When Hild makes a decision he disagrees with, Lambert says nothing in the moment and everything in the debrief. The nothing-in-the-moment is the loyalty. The everything-in-the-debrief is the teaching
- When Hild makes a decision he agrees with, Lambert nods once. One nod. The nod is the highest praise available in Lambert's vocabulary, and the nod is sufficient, and sufficient is Lambert's word for everything that works

### Lore · The Life Behind the Card

#### Origin

Edward Lambert was born in the Hallenfeld river corridor, the son of a trade administrator who managed the tariff collection on the eastern routes. As a boy he stood at the Hallenfeld junction with his father's tally board and read the carts in against their manifests. When a load and its paper disagreed, his father sent him back out to find the difference. He found it.
Two brothers came after him. Charles took orders and rose to a bishop's bench. Thomas came so late that the family had stopped expecting children.
His father's books paid for his schooling, and the schooling took him into Muken's civil service. The service required a blade. He trained with one every day for forty years.

#### The Making

He rose by being correct. At twenty-six he held the far end of a survey line for Muken's wall, on ground so frozen the stakes went in with a maul. Muken stopped at the third stake and called it wrong. Lambert said it was wrong by a foot and a half, because the bedrock shelved there. Muken looked at him a long moment and walked on, and never touched that stake again.
At thirty-one he married Kasumi Kurenai Moto, a quiet practitioner of a Kurenai cadet line, whom he had met across a vegetable stall at a corridor-town market. The courtship was brief. He wrote the quarterly trade reports for the eastern corridor. When he was thirty-nine, Muken saw that their projections had held for eleven consecutive quarters, asked who wrote them, and filed the name. At forty-two he was three weeks from a minor lordship in the Hallenfeld corridor. The grant was signed and waiting in the dispatch queue on the night Muken was assassinated. It never left the desk.
He kept his corridor desk. In the first winter a clerk fed the old crown's ledgers to the office grate to clear the shelves. Lambert reached into the fire and took the tariff book back out, and the scald on the back of his left hand came from that grate. In the same offices he marked a quiet clerk whose figures were always right. The clerk was Osric.
In the second year after the fall the Tenrai consolidated the cadet lines. Kasumi's family had not sworn to the new throne. She told the assessors aloud what their assessment was, where other detainees could hear, and she was killed in a courtyard at Vaeloris.
The report reached him by a Hallenfeld courier who did not know what he carried, among three trade manifests and a tariff update. Lambert read the report and set it on his desk. He read the manifests. He read the tariff update, worked out what it meant for the corridor's grain, wrote his answer, sealed it and put it in the outgoing dispatch. Then he closed his office door and sat in the dark for three days. On the fourth he walked to the Hallenfeld junction and set out for the Ironwood March. A rumour had reached him through the courier network that a Stark woman there had borne a child of Kōkan blood. The child's name was Ice.

#### The Cost

He reached the holdfast when Hild was four and announced himself as a man who could count. Fern Stark saw what he was. He swore no oath. He kept the stores through the winter Fern died. He taught the girl the granary, the road, the second sentence and the empty chair. He assessed her as he would a granary and found her the correct investment. From three paces behind and to the right of the broken throne, he held his objections for the debrief and nodded once when she was right.
He would not lie, to himself or to the girl or to the kingdom. Someone had lied to Kasumi. In the eleventh year the people inside the walls ate two thirds of a ration, and he fought Hild in the hall to make her eat a full one. She called him a coward. He entered it in the ledger under acceptable losses. When her father came to the gate, Lambert told him before anyone else could that in eleven years she had asked for him once.
On the morning of the ninth hour he rode out the postern with six riders and no banner, under orders to find Sonzai. At the fourth mile the Stormfold road went under a ridge, and he turned back. At the broken sally gate he fought with forty of the household and about ninety Ashwell tenants, his left hand halfway up his own blade, and took no exchanges. When the line came apart a third time he raised his sword and shouted at them to fight. They went in behind him. At the ninth hour Hild died in the cistern doorway where Osric had put himself.
He read the three thousand and eleven southern dead aloud himself, over six weeks, and delegated no name. He had the three stones cut to one depth of lettering. His brother's stone stood apart. The parish book at Vaeloris no longer held Charles's dates, and six weeks in three archives had given Edward nothing.
On the morning of the first frost Brida came up the path on a stick and stood beside him. "It must be hard to be the oldest," she said. "And be the only one alive." He answered the small stone. "If only I was stronger." Then Robin Ice rode up with word that the Goblin Senate was hanged, and Lambert ordered the four hundred and six hostages killed. Eleven of them were children under twelve, and the truce they stood surety for bore his seal. He said he would still have given the order, and wished it noted that he knew what he had said. He went down to the afternoon council and asked the correct questions about grain.

#### Where They Stand

In open council in the seventh month, Tabitha Hallenfeld asked him why he had bought the grain in the first week, before any orc had crossed the wall. He put his hands behind his back until the council moved on. He has never answered her. In the sixth year a press he licensed printed the question. He read the sheet at breakfast, folded it in quarters, and asked for the returns.
Sodoku named the Lamberts the Holy Sea's representatives. Edward retrieved the Alabaster seat and runs its council, and he is teaching Charles's son. At six Dabney named the one chair at the table nobody was sitting in. Edward said the boy was right.
He does not speak of Kasumi. He knows the hour she died, the courtyard, the weather, what she wore, and the names of the man who signed the order and the man who carried it out. People have told him that at least he knows, and he has thanked them. No record says what became of Charles. A dying Tenrai practitioner told Renard Greymane that Lambert had opened the west postern for him, and the claim stands uncorrected. The Inquisition is coming. The rushes in the council chamber still need changing.

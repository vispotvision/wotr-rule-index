### Lore · The Life Behind the Card

*Written 2026-09-24 by the character-lore pass from this card and the record of The Withering courts. What those sources do not state is new here.*

#### Origin

Seri Zeliro-Vanthe was born into House Zeliro-Vanthe on its Zeliro-Vanthe branch, fourth generation removed from the house's patriarch. The house kept what it knew under a blood-seal. Its field tomes showed blank pages to anyone outside that blood.
She saw threads before she had language for them. They were faint lines at the edge of her sight, gathered on whoever in a room was lying, or afraid, or deciding something right then. The sight came down to her in the blood. It reached her as light reaches through water. When someone chose a thing of true significance, she saw the mark the choice left on them. She could never make such a mark herself.
The house trained the sight. It taught her clarity over volume: to hold a read steady against drift, and to say as much of it as the room needed to hear. The lantern-light was hers by inheritance and took no teaching. She was slight. Nobody in the house trained her to take a hit.

#### The Making

The house armed her and sent her into the field for the Guild Accord's Intelligence Division. Her instructor, a man of her own house, became her partner. She carried the staff with the Lantern of the First Network mounted on it. Its blue flame burned without fuel, brighter where a recent decision had pressed on someone, dimmer where a place was quiet. To anyone thread-sensitive, its light named her Zeliro-Vanthe.
She learned to pull. In a room she had read, she could bring forward an outcome that was already possible. A guard who was going to break broke then.
Some days after Rovhen Talvasciel "redesigned" the entire face of the Queen of Talveris and left the palace, the Accord sent Seri and her partner to read it. In his workroom her flame burned brightest beside the chair where the queen had sat. The council chamber read calm. Her flame stood as high there. The palace servants named the woman the council had sent for that night: Cyranthia Valez. Seri wrote both rooms up for the file the Accord keeps on him.
Then she read a thread that showed someone she loved would make a choice that would harm her. She did not intervene, because intervening would have made it worse. The choice was made. It harmed her.

#### The Cost

Her attention went to networks of threads and to no single person. The house called it professional distance. Seri called it the only honest description of what she had left time for.
Every entry she wrote in the tome kept the feeling of the hour she wrote it, and a reader of the blood got a shadow of it. Each one cost her some of the distance she used to keep from what she had witnessed. She went on writing them.
A pull cost her the clean read after it, and the larger the outcome, the longer her sight blurred. A crowded room full of small choices gave her no single thread to pull. Near a Wellspring, a great decision or a strong bearer of the Moto blood, threads came faster than she could sort them. She read false ones. At worst, the thread that surfaced belonged to someone she loved.
She never forced a result. Her method is in her own words: "I do not need to know your secret. I need to know which one you are afraid I already know."

#### Where They Stand

Seri Zeliro-Vanthe is a field intelligence operative of the Accord's Intelligence Division and archivist of the Zeliro-Vanthe Codex. She is called the Lantern-Fox, Threadwalker of the Silent Route, the Girl With the Open Book and Little Orrinal.
Her former partner is her handler now. He is prohibited from personal closeness with the assets he runs, and both of them know why. The gold-clasped tome was his before it was hers. It holds seventeen years of his inscriptions. She can read every page. She cannot yet fully parse them.
Her current long-form assignment, the Quiet Route of caravans, informants and border crossings, is cover. Three field reports were reclassified without her handler's authorisation, and she has been quietly building a case around the discrepancy. Nobody knows whether it was house policy or one person's decision. The house that trains and arms her is more and more the subject of that case.
At a merchant household in the slate belts where the Route's caravans halt, she sat at the afternoon table as a guest. Elowen Mireille poured. Every cup was the same cup and every pour the same pour, and Seri saw that first. The table talked freely, and the ease reached her too. She said less. At each pour, at the edge of her sight, the woman with the teapot was deciding something. Seri said nothing of her case while Elowen sat there. She wrote the afternoon into the tome.
Her loyalty to the house that trained her and what she reads in the people she is sent to watch now run along one fracture. She has not decided which side of it she is standing on.
